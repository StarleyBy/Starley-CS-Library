# Executive Summaries: Key Questions in Cardiac Surgery

## Chapter 1: Cardiac Anatomy

### Coronary Arteries
*   **Coronary artery system** originates from the aortic root. Left coronary artery (left main stem) divides into left anterior descending (LAD) and circumflex (Cx) arteries. Right coronary artery (RCA) terminates as the posterior descending artery (PDA) and posterior left ventricular artery.
*   **Left main coronary artery** (10–40 mm) courses between pulmonary trunk and left atrial appendage, typically bifurcating into LAD and Cx. Trifurcation (with ramus intermedius) occurs in some patients.
*   **LAD artery** runs in anterior interventricular groove to the apex. Branches: diagonal arteries (2–6, supply anterolateral LV), septal perforators (3–5, supply anterior 2/3 of septum), right ventricular branches. Divided into proximal, middle, and distal thirds.
*   **Circumflex artery** runs in left atrioventricular groove. Branches: obtuse marginal arteries (lateral LV, anterolateral papillary muscle), left atrial branches, SA nodal artery (45%), AV nodal artery (10–15%), PDA (10–15%).
*   **Right coronary artery** runs in right atrioventricular groove to inferior surface. Branches: SA nodal artery (55%), infundibular (conus) branch, acute marginal branch, anterior RV branches, AV nodal artery (85–90%), PDA (85–90%), posterior LV artery.
*   **Coronary dominance** refers to origin of PDA: right dominant (80–85%), left dominant (10–15%), codominant (~5%). Left dominance more common in males and bicuspid aortic valve.

### Coronary Veins
*   Majority drain via **coronary sinus** into right atrium. Coronary sinus is continuation of great cardiac vein.
*   **Great cardiac vein** runs with LAD and Cx. **Middle cardiac vein** runs with PDA. **Small cardiac vein** runs with RCA and acute marginal artery.

### Conduction System
*   **Sino-atrial (SA) node** — elliptical subepicardial structure lateral to SVC–right atrial junction (sulcus terminalis). Blood supply: RCA (55%) or circumflex (45%).
*   **Atrioventricular (AV) node** — within triangle of Koch (bounded by tendon of Todaro, septal leaflet hinge, coronary sinus). Blood supply: RCA (85–90%) or circumflex (10–15%).
*   **Bundle of His** penetrates central fibrous body to membranous septum. **Left bundle branch** divides into anterior and posterior fascicles. **Right bundle branch** runs on right side of septum to moderator band.

### Heart Valves
*   **Aortic valve** — three semilunar cusps: right, left, non-coronary. **Aortic root** includes sinuses of Valsalva, sinotubular junction, and ventriculo-arterial junction. Nodule of Arantius thickens leaflet free edge.
*   **Mitral valve** — anterior (aortic) and posterior (mural) leaflets, subdivided into scallops (A1–A3, P1–P3). Annulus is kidney-shaped (posterior 2/3, anterior 1/3). Tension apparatus includes papillary muscles (anterolateral from Cx, posteromedial from RCA) and chordae tendineae (primary, secondary, tertiary).
*   **Tricuspid valve** — three leaflets: anterior (largest), septal, posterior (smallest). Papillary muscles: anterior (from moderator band), medial (of Lancisi, through which right bundle branch passes), posterior.

### Pericardium
*   Two layers: fibrous and serous (visceral + parietal). Pericardial space contains 5–10 mL fluid.
*   **Transverse sinus** — behind aorta/pulmonary trunk, in front of SVC/left atrial appendage.
*   **Oblique sinus** — posterior to left atrium, between pulmonary veins.

### Vessels for Conduits and Access
*   **Left internal mammary artery (LIMA)** — originates from subclavian artery, runs 2 cm lateral to sternal edge, terminates as superior epigastric and musculophrenic arteries.
*   **Radial artery** — terminal branch of brachial artery. Nerves at risk: lateral cutaneous nerve of forearm (C5–6), superficial radial nerve (C7–8).
*   **Long saphenous vein** — from medial malleolus to saphenofemoral junction (3 cm inferior and medial to femoral artery pulsation).
*   **Short saphenous vein** — 2 cm posterior to lateral malleolus, drains into popliteal vein.
*   **Common femoral artery** — at mid-inguinal point (between ASIS and symphysis pubis). Femoral vein medial, femoral nerve lateral.

### Other Structures
*   **Phrenic nerve** — C3–C5, runs anterior to scalenus anterior, medial to internal mammary artery, on lateral pericardium. Right passes through IVC opening (T8), left pierces diaphragm.
*   **Descending thoracic aorta** — continuation of aortic arch, passes through aortic hiatus at diaphragm.
*   **Embryology** — heart tube forms from fusion of ventral aortae; endocardial cushions form atrioventricular canals and valves; atrial septum forms from septum primum and secundum.

## Chapter 2: Cardiac Physiology

### Invasive Monitoring
*   **Arterial line** — continuous BP monitoring, blood gas sampling. Waveform analysis: dP/dt (contractility), diastolic decay (SVR), dicrotic notch (aortic valve closure).
*   **Pulse oximetry** — measures SpO₂ based on differential absorption of red (660 nm) and infrared (910 nm) light by oxy- vs. deoxyhemoglobin. Inaccurate in hypothermia, vasoconstriction, non-pulsatile flow, carboxyhemoglobin.
*   **Central venous catheter** — CVP monitoring, drug/fluid administration, parenteral nutrition, pacing wires. Normal CVP waveform: a wave (atrial systole), c wave (tricuspid bulging), x descent (atrial diastole), v wave (venous return), y descent (tricuspid opening).
*   **Pulmonary artery (Swan-Ganz) catheter** — measures PAP, PAWP, CO (thermodilution), SvO₂. Complications: arrhythmias, pulmonary artery rupture, valve damage.
*   **Mixed venous saturation (SvO₂)** — 60–80%. Low SvO₂ = ↓ O₂ delivery (low CO, anaemia, hypoxia) or ↑ O₂ consumption (fever, pain, shivering). High SvO₂ = ↓ extraction (sepsis, shunting) or wedged catheter.

### Cardiac Cycle and Mechanics
*   **Cardiac cycle** — systole (isovolumetric contraction, rapid/slow ejection) and diastole (isovolumetric relaxation, rapid/slow filling, atrial systole). Atrial systole contributes up to 30% of ventricular filling.
*   **Preload** = LV end-diastolic volume (Frank-Starling law). Influenced by compliance, blood volume, atrial systole, diastolic duration.
*   **Contractility** — increased by sympathetic stimulation, inotropes; decreased by β-blockers, acidosis, hypoxia. Bowditch effect: ↑ HR → ↑ contractility.
*   **Afterload** = LV end-systolic pressure (resistance to ejection). Influenced by SVR, aortic valve area, LV wall stress (Laplace’s law).
*   **Cardiac work** = area within pressure-volume loop.

### Inotropes and Vasopressors (Tables 3–4)
| Receptor | Effect |
|----------|--------|
| α₁ | Vasoconstriction |
| β₁ | ↑ Inotropy, ↑ chronotropy, ↑ dromotropy |
| β₂ | Vasodilation, bronchodilation |
| DA₁ | Renal/splanchnic vasodilation |

**Inotropes:** adrenaline (α,β₁,β₂), dopamine (α,β₁,β₂,DA₁ dose-dependent), dobutamine (β₁,β₂), noradrenaline (α,β₁), milrinone (PDE3 inhibitor, increases cAMP). Mechanism: ↑ cAMP → ↑ Ca²⁺ influx → ↑ contractility.

### Coronary and Myocardial Physiology
*   **Coronary blood flow (CBF)** = (DBP – LVEDP) / coronary vascular resistance. 70–80% of LV flow occurs in diastole.
*   **Myocardial oxygen delivery** = CBF × arterial O₂ content. Arterial O₂ content = (1.34 × Hb × SaO₂) + (0.0031 × PaO₂).
*   **Poiseuille’s law:** Flow ∝ r⁴. Halving radius → 16× resistance increase.
*   **Oxygen dissociation curve** — right shift (Bohr effect) by acidosis, hypercapnia, hyperthermia → ↑ O₂ release to tissues.

### Electrophysiology
*   **Resting potential** (–90 mV in ventricles) maintained by Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase and open K⁺ channels.
*   **Action potential phases:** 0 (Na⁺ influx), 1 (K⁺ efflux), 2 (Ca²⁺ influx, plateau), 3 (K⁺ efflux, repolarisation), 4 (resting).
*   **Pacemaker cells** (SA/AV node) — phase 4 spontaneous depolarisation (automaticity); slower phase 0.
*   **Excitation-contraction coupling:** Ca²⁺ influx → troponin-C binding → tropomyosin shift → myosin cross-bridge formation → contraction.

### Haemostasis and Coagulation
*   **Haemostasis:** vascular phase (vasospasm), platelet phase (aggregation), clotting phase (fibrin formation).
*   **Clotting cascade** — intrinsic (contact activation), extrinsic (tissue factor), common pathway (prothrombin → thrombin → fibrin). Vitamin K required for factors II, VII, IX, X, protein C/S.
*   **Regulators:** protein C/S, antithrombin III, prostacyclin, tissue factor pathway inhibitor.
*   **Fibrinolysis** — plasmin (from plasminogen via tPA, urokinase) breaks down fibrin.
*   **Thromboelastogram (TEG)** — R time (fibrin initiation), K time and α angle (fibrinogen-platelet interaction), MA (platelet aggregation), A60 (fibrinolysis).

### Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP)
*   **Mechanism:** inflation in diastole → ↑ coronary perfusion; deflation before systole → ↓ afterload.
*   **Improves MVO₂ supply/demand ratio** by ↑ diastolic pressure-time index and ↓ tension-time index.
*   **Indications:** myocardial ischaemia, low cardiac output, acute MR, VSD, bridge to transplant.
*   **Contraindications:** severe AR, aortic dissection, AAA, severe peripheral atherosclerosis.

## Chapter 3: Cardiac Pharmacology

### Antiplatelet Agents
*   **Aspirin** — cyclo-oxygenase inhibitor → reduces thromboxane A₂ → ↓ platelet aggregation. Dose: 75–150 mg prophylaxis, 300 mg therapeutic.
*   **Clopidogrel** — ADP receptor antagonist → irreversible platelet inhibition. Loading 300 mg, maintenance 75 mg daily. Used with aspirin in ACS (CURE) and after DES.
*   **Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa blockers** (abciximab, tirofiban) — prevent fibrinogen cross-bridge formation → inhibit final common pathway of platelet aggregation. Used in ACS and PCI.

### Anticoagulants
*   **Heparin** — activates antithrombin III → inhibits thrombin and factor Xa. Reversed with protamine. Doses: prophylactic 5000 U SC bd, therapeutic 75 U/kg + 18 U/kg/h, CPB 300 U/kg.
*   **LMWH** (dalteparin, enoxaparin) — greater anti-Xa activity, longer half-life, no monitoring required. Lower risk of HIT and osteoporosis.
*   **Warfarin** — vitamin K antagonist → inhibits factors II, VII, IX, X. Reversed with vitamin K, FFP, or prothrombin complex concentrate. Target INR 2.0–3.0 (mechanical mitral 2.5–3.5).
*   **Fibrinolytics** (streptokinase, tPA) — convert plasminogen to plasmin → degrade fibrin clots. Contraindications: recent stroke/surgery/bleeding, aortic dissection.

### Antifibrinolytics
*   **Aprotinin** (withdrawn) — serine protease inhibitor (plasmin, kallikrein). BART trial showed increased mortality, stroke, renal failure.
*   **Tranexamic acid** — lysine analogue → binds plasminogen → inhibits fibrinolysis. Dose: 2 g induction + 2 g in pump prime + 1 g post-CPB.

### Statins
*   **HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors** (simvastatin, atorvastatin) — ↓ cholesterol synthesis. Pleiotropic effects: plaque stabilization, anti-inflammatory. Dose: 10–80 mg daily.

### β-Blockers
*   **Mechanism:** ↓ heart rate, contractility → ↓ myocardial O₂ demand. Antiarrhythmic membrane stabilization.
*   **Examples:** bisoprolol (cardioselective, 1.25–10 mg), metoprolol (25–100 mg bd), carvedilol (3.125–50 mg bd) — beneficial in heart failure.
*   **Cautions:** asthma, PVD, diabetes, heart block.

### Calcium Channel Blockers
*   **Mechanism:** inhibit Ca²⁺ influx → ↓ contractility, ↓ conduction, vasodilation.
*   **Classes:** dihydropyridines (amlodipine, nifedipine — vasodilation), non-dihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem — negative chronotropy/inotropy).
*   **Cautions:** poor LV function, heart block, severe AS.

### RAAS Inhibitors
*   **ACE inhibitors** (ramipril, lisinopril) — inhibit angiotensin I → II conversion → ↓ vasoconstriction, ↓ aldosterone. Side effect: dry cough.
*   **ARBs** (candesartan, losartan) — block angiotensin II receptor. No cough. Alternative when ACEi not tolerated.

### Nitrates and Vasodilators
*   **Nitrates** (GTN, isosorbide mononitrate) — smooth muscle relaxant → venodilation (↓ preload), coronary vasodilation.
*   **Nicorandil** — nitrate + K⁺ channel opener → coronary and venous dilation.
*   **Sodium nitroprusside** — arterial vasodilator → ↓ afterload. Light-sensitive. Risk of cyanide toxicity.

### Inotropes and Vasopressors
| Drug | Mechanism | Dose | Notes |
|------|-----------|------|-------|
| **Dopamine** | DA₁ (1–5), β₁ (5–10), α (>10 µg/kg/min) | 1–20 µg/kg/min | Dose-dependent effects |
| **Dobutamine** | β₁ > β₂ → inotropy + vasodilation | 2.5–10 µg/kg/min | Can give peripherally |
| **Adrenaline** | β₁, β₂, α (dose-dependent) | 0.01–1 µg/kg/min | Cardiac arrest: 1 mg |
| **Noradrenaline** | α > β₁ → vasoconstriction + inotropy | 0.01–1 µg/kg/min | Preferred in septic shock |
| **Milrinone** | PDE3 inhibitor → inodilator | 0.125–0.75 µg/kg/min | Useful in RV failure, pulmonary hypertension |
| **Phenylephrine** | α₁ agonist → pure vasoconstrictor | 0.5–3 µg/kg/min | On CPB: 0.5–1 mg bolus |

### Diuretics
*   **Loop diuretics** (furosemide, bumetanide) — inhibit Na⁺/K⁺/2Cl⁻ transport in thick ascending limb. Potent, cause hypokalaemia.
*   **Thiazides** (bendroflumethiazide) — inhibit distal convoluted tubule. Mild diuresis.
*   **Potassium-sparing** (amiloride, spironolactone) — amiloride blocks Na⁺ channel; spironolactone aldosterone antagonist.
*   **Mannitol** — osmotic diuretic. 0.5 g/kg in CPB prime.

### Antiarrhythmics
*   **Digoxin** — inhibits Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ → weak inotropy; ↑ AV node refractoriness. Therapeutic level 0.8–2 ng/mL. Reversed with Digibind®.
*   **Amiodarone** — prolongs action potential duration. Loading: 1.2 g/24h IV. Long-term: pulmonary fibrosis, thyroid dysfunction, photosensitivity.
*   **Lidocaine** — Class Ib antiarrhythmic. Shortens action potential. Maximum dose: 5 mg/kg plain, 7 mg/kg with adrenaline.
*   **Magnesium** — prolongs AV node refractory period. 5 g IV for tachyarrhythmias.

## Chapter 4: Electrocardiography

### ECG Basics
*   **ECG paper speed** — 25 mm/sec (0.04 sec/small square, 0.2 sec/large square). Height: 0.1 mV/small square, 0.5 mV/large square.
*   **Lead placement** — limb leads (I, II, III) bipolar; augmented limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF) unipolar. Chest leads V1–V6 positioned from right sternal edge (4th ICS) to midaxillary line (5th ICS).
*   **Heart rate calculation** — 60 / RR interval (seconds) or 300 / number of large squares between R waves.
*   **Normal values** — PR interval 0.12–0.2 sec, QRS duration 0.08–0.12 sec, QTc ≤0.42 sec, P wave <2.5 mm tall, <0.11 sec wide.

### Rhythm Assessment
*   **Sinus rhythm** — P wave precedes each QRS, rate 60–100 bpm.
*   **Atrial fibrillation** — absent P waves, irregularly irregular QRS, fibrillatory waves.
*   **Atrial flutter** — sawtooth flutter waves in V1, atrial rate ~300 bpm, ventricular rate 150 bpm (2:1 block).
*   **Ventricular tachycardia** — broad QRS (>0.12 sec), rate >100 bpm, AV dissociation.
*   **Ventricular fibrillation** — chaotic waveform, no cardiac output.

### Axis Determination
*   **Normal axis** –30° to +90° (positive I, positive aVF). **LAD** –30° to –90° (positive I, negative aVF). **RAD** +90° to +180° (negative I, positive aVF).
*   **Causes of LAD:** LBBB, left anterior hemiblock, inferior MI, LVH, WPW.
*   **Causes of RAD:** RBBB, left posterior hemiblock, RVH, pulmonary embolus, lateral MI, ASD secundum.

### Chamber Enlargement
*   **Left atrial enlargement (P mitrale)** — wide P wave >0.11 sec, biphasic in V1 (negative terminal component).
*   **Right atrial enlargement (P pulmonale)** — tall P wave >2.5 mm in II, peaked in V1.
*   **LVH (Sokolow)** — S V1 + R V5/V6 >35 mm. Lateral T-wave inversion (strain).
*   **RVH** — R V1 + S V6 >11 mm, R/S V1 >1, right axis deviation.

### Ischaemia and Infarction
*   **Ischaemia** — ST depression, T-wave flattening/inversion.
*   **Infarction** — ST elevation (hyperacute), pathological Q waves (>0.04 sec or >25% of QRS), T-wave inversion.
*   **Localisation:**
    *   Inferior (RCA): II, III, aVF
    *   Anterior (LAD): V2–V4
    *   Lateral (Cx/LAD): I, aVL, V5–V6
    *   Posterior (Cx): tall R waves, ST depression in V1–V3 (mirror image)

### Pericarditis
*   **Stage I:** global concave ST elevation.
*   **Stage II:** pseudonormalisation.
*   **Stage III:** T-wave inversion.
*   **Stage IV:** normalisation.

### Electrolyte Abnormalities
*   **Hyperkalaemia** — peaked T waves, wide QRS, small/absent P waves → sine wave → VF.
*   **Hypokalaemia** — flattened T waves, prominent U waves, long QT.
*   **Hypercalcaemia** — short QT.
*   **Hypocalcaemia** — long QT.

### Conduction Blocks
*   **1° AV block** — PR >0.2 sec.
*   **2° AV block Mobitz I (Wenckebach)** — progressive PR prolongation until dropped beat.
*   **2° AV block Mobitz II** — fixed PR interval with intermittent non-conducted P waves.
*   **3° AV block (complete)** — complete AV dissociation, escape rhythm.
*   **RBBB** — QRS >0.12 sec, rSR’ in V1, wide S in V6.
*   **LBBB** — QRS >0.12 sec, QS/rS in V1, broad R in V6, left axis deviation.
*   **Left anterior hemiblock** — left axis deviation, qR in I/aVL, rS in II/III/aVF.
*   **Bifascicular block** — RBBB + LAHB or RBBB + LPHB.
*   **Trifascicular block** — bifascicular block + 1° AV block.

### Pre-excitation Syndromes
*   **WPW syndrome** — short PR (<0.12 sec), delta wave, wide QRS. Type A (positive delta V1), Type B (negative delta V1).
*   **LGL syndrome** — short PR (<0.12 sec), no delta wave, normal QRS.

### Long QT Syndrome
*   **QTc >0.46 sec.** Causes: congenital (Romano-Ward), drugs (sotalol, amiodarone), electrolyte disturbance (hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia), MI. Risk of torsade de pointes.

### Exercise ECG (Bruce Protocol)
*   **Indications:** atypical chest pain, functional assessment, post-MI prognosis, exercise-induced arrhythmias.
*   **Contraindications:** severe AS, unstable angina, uncontrolled hypertension, severe left main stenosis, acute myocarditis, decompensated HF.
*   **Positive test:** ST depression >2 mm (downsloping, persisting >5 min), ST elevation, chest pain with ST changes, failure of BP to rise, ventricular arrhythmias.

## Chapter 5: Echocardiography

### Basic Principles
*   **Echocardiography** uses piezo-electric crystals (2.25 MHz in adults) that emit ultrasound waves. Distance is calculated by time delay; tissue density by signal intensity. Patient in left lateral decubitus position.
*   **Doppler echocardiography** uses the Doppler effect: flow towards probe → increased frequency; away → decreased frequency. Turbulent flow shows as “filled-in” signal.
*   **Aliasing** occurs when Nyquist limit is exceeded (pulsed-wave Doppler only). High-velocity jets require continuous-wave Doppler.
*   **M-mode** — single beam, 1D image. **2D** — cross-sectional imaging. **3D** — volumetric. **Colour-flow mapping** — pulsed-wave Doppler superimposed on 2D (BART: Blue Away, Red Towards).

### Standard Views
*   **Parasternal long axis** — probe at left sternal edge, marker to right shoulder. Shows RV, LV, LA, Ao.
*   **Parasternal short axis** — marker to left shoulder. Levels: aortic valve (trileaflet), mitral valve tips (fish mouth), papillary muscles, apex.
*   **Apical 4-chamber** — marker to left shoulder. Shows all four chambers.
*   **Apical 5-chamber** — angulated anteriorly, adds aortic outflow.
*   **Subcostal** — probe below xiphoid. Useful in obesity/lung disease.
*   **Transoesophageal (TOE)** — superior image quality for posterior structures, valve morphology, endocarditis, aortic dissection.

### Normal Values (Table 1–2)
| Parameter | Normal Range |
|-----------|--------------|
| LVEF | 50–80% |
| LVEDD | 3.5–5.6 cm |
| LV mass index | 44–102 g/m² |
| Aortic valve area | 2.5–3.5 cm² |
| Mitral valve area | 4.0–6.0 cm² |
| Peak velocity (aortic) | 0.9–1.7 m/s |

### Quantification
*   **Bernoulli equation:** ΔP = 4v² (pressure gradient from velocity).
*   **Continuity equation:** A₁ × VTI₁ = A₂ × VTI₂. Used to calculate valve area (e.g., aortic valve area = (Area<sub>LVOT</sub> × VTI<sub>LVOT</sub>) / VTI<sub>AoV</sub>).
*   **Simpson’s method:** LV volume = sum of slices (π(D/2)² × thickness). EF = (LVEDV – LVESV) / LVEDV.
*   **PISA (proximal isovelocity surface area):** effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA) calculated from converging hemisphere.
*   **Pressure half-time:** MVA = 220 / PHT (ms). Used in mitral stenosis and aortic regurgitation.

### Valvular Stenosis
*   Assessed by: peak velocity, peak/mean gradient, valve area (planimetry, continuity equation).
*   **Aortic stenosis:** dimensionless index (VTI<sub>LVOT</sub>/VTI<sub>AoV</sub>). Normal = 1, severe <0.25. MACS >2 cm normal, <0.8 cm severe.
*   **Mitral stenosis:** planimetry, pressure half-time, pulmonary artery systolic pressure.

### Valvular Regurgitation (Table 3)
*   **Colour-flow grading:** Grade 1 (mild) – jet adjacent to valve; Grade 4 (severe) – fills almost entire receiving chamber with flow reversal.
*   **Vena contracta:** narrowest part of regurgitant jet.
*   **Aortic regurgitation:** pressure half-time <300 ms, deceleration rate >3 m/s² indicates severe.
*   **Mitral regurgitation:** pulmonary vein systolic flow reversal indicates severe.

### Ventricular Function
*   **Regional wall motion:** normal, hypokinetic, akinetic, dyskinetic, aneurysmal.
*   **Diastolic dysfunction (4 grades):** I (E<A) – impaired relaxation; II (E>A) – pseudonormal; III (E>>A) – reversible restrictive; IV (E>>>A) – fixed restrictive.
*   **LVH:** LV mass index >136 g/m² (men), >112 g/m² (women). Asymmetrical septal hypertrophy: IVSd:LVPWd >1.5 (HCM).

### Haemodynamics
*   **Pulmonary artery systolic pressure (PASP):** RVSP = 4v²<sub>TR</sub> + JVP. PASP = RVSP (if no pulmonary stenosis). Normal <25 mmHg.
*   **Mitral inflow pattern:** E wave (early filling), A wave (atrial systole). E/A ratio >1 normal; <1 indicates diastolic dysfunction.

### Pericardial Conditions
*   **Pericardial effusion:** terminates at AV groove, does not extend beyond descending aorta.
*   **Tamponade:** large effusion, RV/RA diastolic collapse, exaggerated respiratory variation (>25% in tricuspid inflow).
*   **Constrictive pericarditis:** thickened pericardium, septal bounce, exaggerated respiratory variation, dilated IVC.

### Cardiomyopathies
*   **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM):** asymmetrical septal hypertrophy (IVSd:LVPWd >1.5), systolic anterior motion (SAM) of mitral valve, LVOT obstruction, diastolic dysfunction.
*   **Restrictive cardiomyopathy:** diastolic dysfunction, concentric thickening, reduced cavity dimensions, endomyocardial fibrosis (echobright endocardium).

### TOE Indications
*   Aortic dissection (sensitivity 98%), mitral valve assessment, infective endocarditis (abscess detection), intraoperative assessment (valve repair, de-airing, LV function), intracardiac masses, septal defects.
*   **Contraindications:** oesophageal disease, upper GI bleeding, severe cervical arthritis/instability.

## Chapter 6: Cardiac Catheterisation

### Basic Principles
*   **Coronary angiography** — invasive injection of radio-opaque dye into coronary ostia to delineate coronary anatomy using multiple 2D cine images.
*   **Access routes:** femoral, brachial, radial, ulnar arteries (modified Seldinger technique). 6Fr (2 mm) catheters allow vessel size estimation.
*   **Views described by X-ray detector position:** RAO (right anterior oblique), LAO (left anterior oblique), AP (anteroposterior), with cranial/caudal tilt.

### Standard Angiographic Views
*   **Left coronary system:** RAO 20° caudal 20°, AP caudal 30°, AP cranial 40°, LAO 50° cranial 30°, LAO 50° caudal 30° (‘spider view’).
*   **LAD identification:** longest vessel, descends to apex, septal arteries branch at 90°.
*   **Circumflex identification:** closest to thoracic spine and descending aorta.
*   **Right coronary system:** LAO 30°, RAO 30°.
*   **Left ventricle:** RAO view (anterobasal, anterior, apical, inferior, inferobasal), LAO view (lateral, septal, inferior).

### Indications and Contraindications
*   **Indications:** chronic stable angina, ACS, positive ETT, ischaemic cardiomyopathy, pre-op assessment (>40 years or multiple risk factors), cardiac arrest survivors.
*   **Contraindications:** acute Type A dissection, aortic valve endocarditis with vegetation, decompensated HF, active infection, renal dysfunction (contrast nephropathy), contrast allergy.
*   **Complications:** local vascular (3%), contrast nephropathy (0.5%), emergency surgery (0.1%), CVA (0.05%), MI (0.05%), mortality <0.05%.

### Coronary Artery Assessment
*   **Significant stenosis:** >60% luminal diameter (84% cross-sectional area reduction). Left main stem: >50% luminal diameter.
*   **Area reduction formula:** area ∝ r² → 50% diameter = 75% area, 70% diameter = 91% area, 90% diameter = 99% area.
*   **Fractional flow reserve (FFR):** ratio of distal to proximal coronary pressure. Normal = 1.0, ischaemic <0.80 (>90% accuracy). FAME study showed FFR-guided revascularisation improves outcomes.
*   **TIMI flow:** Grade 0 (no flow), Grade 1 (reduced, incomplete filling), Grade 2 (slow but complete filling), Grade 3 (prompt antegrade flow).
*   **Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS):** transcatheter ultrasound probe — direct imaging of luminal diameter, vessel wall, atheroma, calcification. Useful for equivocal stenosis and endoluminal calcification.
*   **Intracoronary pressure wire:** measures FFR; adenosine used to assess flow limitation.

### Valvular Assessment
*   **Mitral regurgitation (Sellers’ classification):** I (incomplete LA opacification, clears each beat) → IV (complete LA opacification in one beat, becomes denser, reflux into pulmonary veins).
*   **Aortic regurgitation (Sellers’ classification):** 1+ (small jet, no LV opacification) → 4+ (LV opacification greater than aorta).
*   **LV function:** good (EF >50%), moderate (30–50%), poor (<30%). Regional wall motion: normal, hypokinetic, akinetic, dyskinetic, aneurysmal.

### Haemodynamic Calculations
*   **Fick principle:** Flow = O₂ consumption / (arteriovenous O₂ concentration difference). O₂ content = (Hb × 1.34 × O₂ sats) + (0.003 × PaO₂).
*   **Shunt ratio (Qp:Qs):** = (PV O₂ sats – PA O₂ sats) / (Ao sats – RA sats). Normal = 1:1. PV sats assumed 100%.
*   **Gorlin equation (aortic valve area):** AVA = CO / (44.5 × LVET × HR × √mean gradient). Constant 44.5 for aortic/pulmonary, 31 for mitral/tricuspid.
*   **Square root sign (‘dip and plateau’):** abnormal ventricular diastolic filling — rapid early filling followed by sudden halt. Associated with constrictive pericarditis and restrictive cardiomyopathy (NOT tamponade).

## Chapter 7: Radiological Imaging

### Chest Radiography
*   **CXR** uses 0.1 mSv ionising radiation. Views: AP, PA, lateral. PA gives more accurate cardiac size.
*   **Technical assessment:** penetration (vertebral bodies just visible), projection (scapula position), rotation (clavicles equidistant from spinous processes), inspiratory effort (6th anterior/10th posterior ribs above diaphragm).
*   **Anatomical assessment:** airway (trachea position), breathing (lungs, hila — left <2.5 cm higher than right), circulation (heart size, mediastinum), diaphragm (right <3 cm higher than left, costophrenic angles), extrathoracic structures (bones, prosthetic materials).

### Key Pathological Findings on CXR
*   **Pleural effusion:** concave density replacing convex diaphragm, layering on decubitus films.
*   **Pulmonary oedema:** prominent pulmonary vasculature, peripheral alveolar opacification, Kerley B lines.
*   **Pericardial effusion:** globular enlargement of cardiac silhouette (cannot distinguish from dilation).
*   **Traumatic aortic rupture:** widened mediastinum, left apical cap, tracheal deviation, obliterated aortopulmonary window, left haemothorax, fractures of 1st/2nd ribs.

### Computed Tomography (CT)
*   **Principle:** X-ray beams rotate around patient in spiral path. Multiple slices per rotation.
*   **Houndsfield units (HU):** air –1000, lung –500, fat –100, soft tissue +20 to +80, bone +1000.
*   **Windows:** lung (WL –500, WW 1500), mediastinal (WL +25, WW 350).
*   **Constrictive pericarditis:** thickened pericardium (>4 mm), calcification (50%, inferior diaphragmatic surface), dilated SVC/IVC.
*   **Traumatic aortic rupture:** intimal flap, contrast extravasation, pseudoaneurysm, peri-aortic haematoma.
*   **Aortic aneurysm:** dilated lumen, luminal thrombus, atherosclerosis.
*   **Aortic dissection:** intimal flap separating true/false lumens, peri-aortic haematoma, left haemothorax (Type B).

### Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)
*   **Principle:** aligns hydrogen atoms with magnetic field; radio waves cause signal emission; ECG gating for cine images.
*   **Indications:** thoracic aortic disease (no radiation), hibernating myocardium, cardiac tumours, constrictive pericarditis, valve/ventricular function.
*   **Contraindications:** pacemaker/ICD, cochlear implant, cerebral aneurysm clip, ocular metallic FB, neural stimulator, shrapnel, pregnancy. **Safe** with coronary stents, sternal wires, joint replacements, most prosthetic valves.
*   **Viable myocardium criteria:** resting wall thickness >6 mm, delayed hyperenhancement <25% transmurality, dobutamine stress thickening >2 mm. >50% hyperenhancement = non-viable.

### Positron Emission Tomography (PET)
*   **Principle:** radio-isotope decay emits positron → interacts with electron → gamma rays detected. 18FDG (fluorodeoxyglucose) trapped in cells after phosphorylation.
*   **Indications:** myocardial perfusion (13N ammonia, 15O water, 82Rb), myocardial viability (18FDG + 13N ammonia).
*   **Viability pattern:** mismatch = reduced perfusion + normal metabolism (hibernating). Match = reduced perfusion + reduced metabolism (infarct).

### Nuclear Medicine
*   **Myocardial perfusion scanning (201Thallium):** potassium analogue, uptake proportional to blood flow. Stress vs rest: reversible defect = ischaemia; fixed defect = infarct; fixed defect with redistribution = hibernating.
*   **MUGA (multi-gated acquisition):** 99Tc labels RBCs; gated to cardiac cycle. Gold standard for LVEF assessment.
*   **SPECT:** combines nuclear medicine with CT (3D reconstruction). Detects gamma rays directly (unlike PET).

## Chapter 8: Cardiopulmonary Bypass

### Aims and Components of CPB
*   **Principal aims:** facilitate cardiac/aortic surgery by excluding heart/lungs from circulation, providing gas exchange, systemic perfusion, temperature control, and blood salvage.
*   **Circuit components:** venous cannula → venous reservoir → pump (roller/centrifugal) → heat exchanger → oxygenator → arterial filter → arterial cannula.
*   **Prime volume:** 1.5–2 L (Hartmann’s, gelofusin, mannitol, heparin, ± blood if Hb <10 g/dL).

### Venous Drainage and Cannulation
*   **Venous drainage methods:** Ross basket, two-stage single cannula (34–42Fr), bicaval cannulation (24–36Fr), femoral venous cannula.
*   **Venous reservoir:** open (rigid — allows vacuum-assisted drainage) or closed (collapsible — reduces blood-air interface).
*   **Arterial cannulation:** usually distal ascending aorta. Alternative sites: arch, innominate, subclavian, axillary, femoral, LV apex. Epi-aortic probe recommended for atheroma detection.
*   **Complications of arterial cannulation:** bleeding, dissection, air embolisation, plaque disruption, malposition.

### Oxygenators and Heat Exchangers
*   **Membrane oxygenators:** hollow fibres (120–200 µm, 0.3–0.8 µm pores) — less microemboli, better gas control. Bubble oxygenators rarely used.
*   **Heat exchanger:** controls body temperature; located before oxygenator to reduce gaseous emboli. Warming gradient 5–10°C; blood not heated above 40°C.

### Pumps (Table 1)
| Feature | Roller Pump | Centrifugal Pump |
|---------|-------------|------------------|
| Occlusivity | Nearly occlusive | Non-occlusive |
| Afterload dependence | Independent | Sensitive |
| Prime volume | Low | High |
| Potential for air embolism | Yes | No |
| Blood trauma | Yes | Low |

### Anticoagulation
*   **Heparin:** 300–400 U/kg IV (2 mg/kg for MECC). Target ACT >480 s for CPB initiation, >400 s for pump suckers.
*   **ACT monitoring:** every 30 min on CPB; additional heparin (5000–10,000 U) to maintain ACT >480 s.
*   **Heparin resistance:** consider antithrombin III deficiency; give FFP or recombinant antithrombin.
*   **Protamine reversal:** 1 mg per 100 U heparin (max 3 mg/kg). Slow infusion over 15 min.
*   **Horrow classification of protamine reactions:**
    - **Class I:** hypotension from rapid administration (histamine release).
    - **Class IIa (true anaphylaxis):** anti-protamine IgE; treat with adrenaline, hydrocortisone, chlorphenamine.
    - **Class IIb/IIc (anaphylactoid):** complement-mediated, less severe.
    - **Class III:** catastrophic pulmonary vasoconstriction (thromboxane A₂); treat with adrenaline, milrinone, reinstitute CPB.

### Haematocrit and Flow
*   **Optimal haematocrit:** similar to temperature in °C.
*   **CPB haematocrit = (blood volume / (blood volume + prime volume)) × pre-CPB haematocrit**.
*   **O₂ delivery = 10 × pump flow × [1.34 × Hb × SaO₂ + 0.003 × PaO₂]**.
*   **Flow rate:** 2.4 L/min/m² at 37°C; reduce by 7% per 1°C cooling. MAP 55–65 mmHg (65–75 mmHg in high-risk patients).
*   **BSA (m²) = √([height(cm) × weight(kg)] / 3600)**.

### Venting and Special Situations
*   **Venting indications:** prevents cardiac rewarming and ventricular distension. Sites: aortic root, through aortic valve, right superior pulmonary vein, LV apex, main PA.
*   **MECC (minimal extracorporeal circulation):** removes venous reservoir and cardiotomy suckers → reduces blood-air interface and systemic inflammatory response; reduces prime volume via retrograde/antegrade autologous priming.
*   **PLSVC (persistent left superior vena cava):** occurs in 0.3–0.5%. Drains into coronary sinus (90%) or LA (10%). Requires bicaval cannulation + snaring or additional coronary sinus cannula.

### Complications of CPB
*   **Systemic inflammatory response:** contact activation → cytokine, kallikrein, complement cascades → increased capillary permeability, organ dysfunction.
*   **Coagulopathy:** platelet dysfunction, dilution/consumption of factors.
*   **Cerebral emboli:** gas (vents, cardiotomy reservoir, rapid warming, cavitation) and particulate (thrombus, atheroma, cellular debris, bone, fat).
*   **Safety devices:** low reservoir alarm, high arterial pressure alarm, bubble detector, backup generator, backup oxygen supply.

## Chapter 9: Cardiopulmonary Bypass Scenarios

### Commencing CPB
*   **Key monitoring parameters:** heart decompression, venous drainage, arterial line pressures, systemic pressures, arterial blood O₂, venous reservoir levels.
*   **After 2 minutes of stable bypass:** discontinue ventilation, commence systemic cooling, apply aortic cross-clamp as appropriate.

### Failure of Heart to Arrest
*   **Causes:** aortic regurgitation, incomplete aortic cross-clamp, high-grade proximal coronary stenosis, low potassium in cardioplegia, poor flow through cardioplegia line (kinking, clamping, cannula trapped in clamp).

### Poor Venous Return / Empty Reservoir
*   **Causes:** systemic vasodilation, volume loss (bleeding, pleural pooling, iatrogenic dissection), inadequate venous drainage (kinked line, airlock, cannula malposition, caval snares, IVC tear, inadequate height differential).
*   **Venous ‘chattering’:** caused by inadequate volume or excessive siphon effect. Treatment: add volume, reduce height differential, partially clamp venous line.

### Massive Arterial Air Embolism
*   **Immediate steps:** stop pump, clamp arterial/venous lines, steep Trendelenburg position.
*   **Remove arterial cannula** and aspirate air at entry site. Remove venous cannula.
*   **Retrograde cerebral perfusion** via SVC (400–500 mL/min, ~3 min) until bubbles stop.
*   **Cerebral protection:** mannitol, dexamethasone, ice packs around head.
*   **Resume antegrade circulation** with deep hypothermia (18–20°C) for 30 min.
*   **Maintain** high perfusion pressures (70–80 mmHg), 100% FiO₂. TOE guidance for de-airing.

### Iatrogenic Aortic Dissection
*   **Signs:** spreading haematoma distal to cannulation site, boggy mass in oblique sinus, failure of blood to rise in arterial cannula, poor swing on arterial line, high line pressures, profound hypotension with poor venous return.
*   **Management:** stop pump immediately, clamp lines, insert cannula into right atrium for volume infusion, insert arterial cannula into peripheral artery or uninvolved distal aorta.
*   **After DHCA (18°C):** open ascending aorta, inspect, repair with direct suture, patch, or interposition graft.
*   **Survival:** 66–85% if recognised early, ~50% if discovered postoperatively.

### Hypotension on CPB
*   **Causes:** low pump flow, damped arterial line trace, low SVR (sepsis, pre-op vasodilators, cytokines from pleural blood), kinked/clamped arterial cannula, iatrogenic dissection, selective arch vessel cannulation.

### Hypertension on CPB
*   **Causes:** high pump flow, selective brachiocephalic cannulation (with right radial arterial line), increased SVR (vasopressors, hypothermia, inadequate anaesthesia).

### Oliguria and Haematuria
*   **Oliguria causes:** pre-renal (inadequate flow, hypovolaemia, renal vasoconstriction, dissection), renal (pre-op dysfunction), post-renal (obstructed catheter).
*   **Haematuria causes:** urinary tract trauma with heparinisation, haemolysis (cardiotomy suction, prolonged CPB, acquired red cell fragility, congenital anomalies).

### Weaning from CPB — Before Weaning
*   **Patient factors:** temperature (peripheral 34°C, core >36.5°C), ECG (rate >60 bpm, rhythm, isoelectric ST), BP (MAP >60 mmHg).
*   **Surgical factors:** operation complete, haemostasis checked, LV/RV function adequate, snares released (bicaval), pericardial/pleural cavities decompressed.
*   **Anaesthetic factors:** ABG (PaO₂ >15 kPa, PaCO₂ <6 kPa, base excess >–4, K⁺ <5.5), TOE (no air), ventilation (lungs expanded).

### Weaning from CPB — Steps
*   Perfusionist gradually occludes venous line, reduces pump flow → heart fills and ejects independently.
*   Wean slightly underfilled to reduce myocardial O₂ demand.
*   Adjust preload with 50–100 mL boluses.
*   Monitor: CVP, MAP, PAP, cardiac index, rate/rhythm, contractility.
*   Once stable and TOE confirms successful repair → remove venous cannulae.
*   Protamine given (aortic cannula left in for rapid transfusion if reaction occurs).

### Post-Weaning Hypotension
*   **Causes:** reduced preload (hypovolaemia, bleeding, swabs compressing heart, ‘hitched up’ pericardium), reduced contractility (myocardial stunning, incomplete revascularisation, inadequate protection, coronary air embolism, graft failure), vasodilation (SIRS), arrhythmias (including inadequate pacing).
*   **Management:** treat underlying cause, volume transfusion, haemostasis, inotropes/vasopressors, rest on CPB, IABP.

### De-Airing After AVR
*   Carbon dioxide insufflation throughout procedure.
*   Before tying aortotomy: Trendelenburg, partially occlude venous line to fill heart, gently agitate heart, inflate lungs.
*   Remove cross-clamp, tie aortotomy, turn on aortic root vent (300 mL/min).
*   TOE guidance for de-airing.

## Chapter 10: Adjuncts to Cardiopulmonary Bypass

### Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA)
*   **Indications:** aortic arch surgery (dissection, aneurysms), bloodless field requirement (thoracoabdominal aneurysms, renal tumours with IVC/RA invasion, complex congenital, pulmonary thromboendarterectomy), impossible aortic clamping (porcelain aorta, distal arch aneurysms), massive haemorrhage control.
*   **Safe duration (Table 1):** 36°C → 1 min; 32°C → 5 min; 28°C → 10 min; 24°C → 20 min; 20°C → ~30 min; 16°C → ~40 min.
*   **Cerebral metabolism:** at 20°C, metabolism is ~20% of normothermic (5% drop per 1°C). Mortality/adverse neurologic outcomes increase after ~40 min at 16°C.
*   **Monitoring:** nasopharyngeal temperature, internal jugular venous saturation (>95% = minimal extraction), EEG (not always reliable), near infrared spectroscopy (INVOS® or Foresight®).
*   **Complications:** coagulopathy/platelet dysfunction, systemic inflammatory response (capillary leak, oedema, organ dysfunction), neurological injury (watershed ischaemia, emboli, SIRS).

### DHCA Protocol for Thoracic Aorta
1. Cool core (nasopharyngeal) to 15–18°C on CPB.
2. Maintain steady state for 10–20 min before arrest.
3. Clamp arterial line, drain volume to reservoir, stop pump.
4. After procedure: Trendelenburg position.
5. Restart circulation antegrade at ~500 mL/min.
6. Cold reperfusion for ~10 min at 15–18°C before rewarming (0.2–0.5°C/min).
7. **Avoid cerebral hyperthermia** during rewarming.

### Cerebral Protection Adjuncts
*   **Ice packs around head** — uniform cooling, prevent rewarming.
*   **Pharmacological:** dexamethasone (100 mg), mannitol (1 mg/kg) — efficacy debated. Thiopentone not used (negative inotropy).
*   **Normoglycaemia** — hyperglycaemia contributes to brain injury.
*   **Antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP):** 10 mL/kg/min, 10–18°C, 30–70 mmHg. Requires complete Circle of Willis.
*   **Retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP):** 250–400 mL/min, 10–18°C, 25–40 mmHg. Developed for massive air embolism. Effectiveness unclear but may enhance cooling and wash out emboli.

### Acid-Base Management During Hypothermia
*   **pH-stat:** add CO₂ to keep PaCO₂ at 5 kPa, pH at 7.4 (at 37°C). Advantage: more rapid, homogenous cooling. Disadvantage: increased cerebral embolism risk.
*   **Alpha-stat:** no CO₂ added; allow pH and PaCO₂ to drift (alkalotic during cooling). Advantage: reduced cerebral embolism risk.
*   **Recommended strategy:** pH-stat on cooling (enhances perfusion, facilitates cooling), alpha-stat on rewarming (reduces microembolic load).

### Haematocrit Management
*   As temperature decreases, metabolic rate ↓, blood viscosity ↑.
*   **Target haematocrit ≈ temperature (°C).** At 20°C → haematocrit ~20%.

### Blood Conservation
*   **Pharmacological:** aprotinin (withdrawn — BART trial: increased mortality, renal impairment, heart failure, MI), tranexamic acid (reduces blood loss, transfusion, re-exploration), epsilon-aminocaproic acid, recombinant erythropoietin.
*   **Aprotinin (IMAGE trial):** ↓ mediastinal blood loss, ↓ transfusions, ↓ platelets/products, ↓ re-exploration. Side effects: thrombotic risk, reduced graft patency (poor run-off), hypersensitivity (within 6 months), increased mortality (BART).
*   **Autologous pre-donation:** rarely used due to contraindications (anaemia, unstable angina, AS, HF).
*   **Intra-operative cell salvage:** washes, filters, centrifuges shed blood → concentrated RBCs (Hb 20 g/dL, Hct 60%). Eliminates platelets, clotting factors. Reduces transfusion requirements.
*   **Autotransfusion of washed shed mediastinal fluid:** requires ≥500 mL drainage.
*   **Jehovah’s Witness strategies:** pre-op (erythropoietin, stop antiplatelets/anticoagulants 7 days), intra-op (pharmacological adjuncts, meticulous haemostasis, cell salvage, off-pump/MECC, fibrin glue), post-op (autotransfusion, low threshold for re-exploration).

### Blood Transfusion
*   **Complications:** blood-borne pathogens (HIV, HBV, HCV), transfusion reactions (haemolytic, allergic, febrile), cost, increased bacterial infections, reduced immune function.
*   **Triggers:** Hb <8 g/dL (general), <10 g/dL (elderly/high-risk).

## Chapter 11: Myocardial Protection

### Principles of Myocardial Protection
*   **Aortic cross-clamping** deprives coronary arteries of oxygenated blood → myocardial damage must be minimised.
*   **Methods to reduce myocardial O₂ demand:**
    - **Unloading the heart** (cardiopulmonary bypass) — 6 mL O₂/100g/min
    - **Stopping the heart** (cardioplegic diastolic arrest) — 1 mL O₂/100g/min (90% reduction)
    - **Cooling the heart** (hypothermia) — 7% reduction per 1°C
*   **Ischaemia-reperfusion injury:** damage following restoration of blood supply → triggered by oxygen-free radicals, calcium influx, leucocytes, lactate accumulation, ATP depletion. Results in arrhythmias, myocardial stunning, cell death.

### Routes of Cardioplegia Delivery
*   **Antegrade (aortic root):** 60–100 mmHg, 250 mL/min → arrest in 30–60 s. Unpredictable with CAD; contraindicated in severe AR (ventricular distension).
*   **Antegrade (direct coronary ostia):** used during aortic valve/root procedures.
*   **Retrograde (coronary sinus):** 30–50 mmHg, 150 mL/min → arrest in 2–4 min. Alternative: direct right atrial delivery with SVC/IVC cannulation.
*   **Retrograde cannula insertion:** purse-string 4 cm below RAA; guide past Thebesian valve; confirm position by palpation, manometry (20–30 mmHg), aspiration of dark blood.

### Retrograde Cardioplegia
*   **Advantages:** useful in severe AR, significant CAD, coronary ostial stenosis; does not interrupt operation.
*   **Disadvantages:** less predictable distribution; concerns about RV protection when used alone.

### Cardioplegia Solutions
*   **Crystalloid (St. Thomas’ solution):** Na⁺ 110, K⁺ 16, Ca²⁺ 1.2, Mg²⁺ 16, Cl⁻ 160 mmol/L.
*   **Blood cardioplegia (4:1 blood:St. Thomas):** plus procaine, glutamate, aspartate, oxygen-free radical scavengers, adenosine.
*   **Blood advantages:** provides oxygen/nutrients, buffering capacity, oncotic proteins (↓ oedema), even distribution, faster arrest, free radical scavengers (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione, vitamins C/E).
*   **Efficacy:** equal in normal ventricles; blood cardioplegia may benefit unstable angina or LV dysfunction.

### Mechanism of Cardioplegic Arrest
*   **Extracellular solutions (St. Thomas):** ↑ extracellular K⁺ → prevents repolarisation.
*   **Intracellular solutions (Bretschneider):** ↓ extracellular Na⁺ → blocks depolarisation.
*   **Magnesium:** blocks myosin phosphorylase, delays Ca²⁺ flux, inhibits excitation-contraction coupling.

### Terminal Warm Blood Cardioplegia (‘Hot Shot’)
*   Given immediately before removing aortic cross-clamp.
*   **Effects:** washes out anaerobic metabolites (lactic acid); provides substrate resuscitation (oxygen, ATP). Improves post-ischaemic metabolism and contractility.

### Cross-Clamp Fibrillation
*   **Sequence:** CPB with mild hypothermia (32–34°C) → decompress LV → electrical fibrillation → apply cross-clamp during minimal flow → perform distal anastomosis → remove clamp → DC cardioversion → proximal anastomosis → repeat.
*   **Principles:** electrical fibrillation prior to clamping → global ischaemia with fibrillation → anoxic arrest. Protection from unloading, reduced work, ischaemic pre-conditioning. LV decompression prevents distension/subendocardial ischaemia.
*   **Advantages:** simple, inexpensive, shorter clamp/CPB times, facilitates vessel identification.
*   **Disadvantages:** aortic trauma, thromboembolism (especially in arteriopaths).
*   **Efficacy:** similar to cold blood cardioplegia for enzyme release and LV function.

### Ischaemic Pre-Conditioning
*   Repeated brief sub-lethal ischaemia followed by reperfusion protects myocardium during subsequent prolonged ischaemia (preserves ATP, improves contractile recovery).
*   **First window:** immediately after insult, lasts ~12 hours.
*   **Second window:** 24–36 hours after insult.
*   **Mechanism:** adenosine receptors.
*   **Applied in** cross-clamp fibrillation (sequential graft construction) and OPCAB.

### OPCAB Myocardial Protection
*   Intracoronary shunts
*   Ischaemic pre-conditioning
*   Construct LIMA-to-LAD anastomosis first

### Redo Surgery with Patent LIMA
*   LIMA balloon (pre-operative fluoroscopic guidance)
*   Dissect and temporarily occlude LIMA
*   Moderate hypothermic fibrillatory arrest (without occluding LIMA)
*   Cooling (28°C) with continuous retrograde or intermittent antegrade cold blood cardioplegia (without isolating LIMA)

## Chapter 12: Aortic Valve Disease

### Aortic Stenosis (AS) — Natural History and Pathophysiology
*   **Asymptomatic period** — long latent period; sudden death rare.
*   **Symptom onset → reduced survival:** angina 5 years, syncope 3 years, dyspnoea 2 years.
*   **Pathophysiology:** pressure overload → LV hypertrophy (Laplace’s law: WS = P × r / 2Th) → diastolic dysfunction, ↑ LVEDP, ↑ myocardial O₂ demand, ↓ coronary flow.
*   **Causes:** <70 years — bicuspid (50%), rheumatic (25%), calcific (18%); >70 years — calcific (48%), rheumatic (23%), bicuspid (21%).
*   **Symptoms:** angina, syncope, dyspnoea, heart failure.
*   **Signs:** pulsus tardus et parvus, sustained apex, single S₂, paradoxical splitting, S₄, crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur (late peaking = severity).

### AS Severity (AHA Guidelines — Table 1)
| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| AVA (cm²) | 1.5–2.5 | 1.0–1.5 | <1.0 |
| Mean gradient (mmHg) | 15–25 | 25–40 | >40 |
| Peak velocity (m/s) | <3.0 | 3.0–4.0 | >4.0 |

### Low-Flow, Low-Gradient AS
*   **Definition:** EF <40%, mean gradient <30 mmHg or peak velocity <3.5 m/s, AVA <1.0 cm².
*   **Dobutamine stress echo (5–20 µg/kg/min):** distinguishes severe AS (↑ stroke volume, ↔ gradient, ↔ AVA) from primary contractile dysfunction (↑ stroke volume, ↔ AVA by >0.2 cm² or to 1.2 cm²). Severe AS benefits from AVR (operative mortality 5%); primary dysfunction does not (mortality 32%).

### Indications for AVR in AS (AHA)
*   **Class I:** severe AS with symptoms; severe AS with LV dysfunction (EF <50%); severe AS in patient undergoing other cardiac surgery.
*   **Class IIa:** moderate AS in patient undergoing cardiac surgery.
*   **Class IIb:** severe AS with abnormal exercise response (<20 mmHg rise); rapid progression; extremely severe AS (mean gradient >60 mmHg, peak velocity >5 m/s, AVA <0.6 cm²).

### Asymptomatic AS Management
*   Exercise test to confirm truly asymptomatic.
*   Surveillance echocardiography: mild — every 5 years; moderate — every 3 years; severe — every year.

### Aortic Regurgitation (AR) — Natural History
*   Asymptomatic with good LV function: 6% per year develop symptoms or LV dysfunction.
*   Asymptomatic with LV dysfunction: 25% per year develop symptoms.
*   Symptomatic: 10% mortality per year.

### AR Severity (AHA Guidelines — Table 3)
| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jet width (% LVOT) | <25 | — | >65 |
| Vena contracta (cm) | <0.3 | 0.3–0.6 | >0.6 |
| Regurgitant volume (mL) | <30 | — | >60 |
| Regurgitant fraction (%) | <30 | — | >50 |
| EROA (cm²) | <0.1 | 0.1–0.3 | >0.3 |
| Deceleration rate (m/s²) | <2.0 | 2.0–3.0 | >3.0 |
| Pressure half-time (ms) | >400 | 300–400 | <300 |

### Causes of AR
*   **Leaflet:** calcific degeneration, rheumatic, bicuspid, endocarditis.
*   **Annulus/root/aorta:** aneurysm, dissection.
*   **Acute AR:** aortic dissection, endocarditis, trauma.

### Signs of AR (Eponyms)
*   **Widened pulse pressure:** Duroziez’s (to-and-fro femoral murmur), Quincke’s (capillary pulsation), Traube’s (pistol-shot), de Musset’s (head bobbing), Corrigan’s (water-hammer pulse), Mueller’s (uvula pulsation), Hill’s (leg systolic > arm by ≥20 mmHg).
*   **Other:** hyperdynamic displaced apex, S₃, decrescendo diastolic murmur (L sternal edge), Austin Flint murmur.

### Indications for AVR in AR (AHA)
*   **Class I:** severe AR with symptoms; severe AR with LV dysfunction (EF <50%); severe AR in patient undergoing other cardiac surgery.
*   **Class IIa:** severe AR with LV dilation (ESD >55 mm, EDD >75 mm).
*   **Class IIb:** severe AR with abnormal exercise response; rapid progression; LV dilation (ESD >50 mm, EDD >70 mm); moderate AR in patient undergoing cardiac surgery.

### Asymptomatic AR Management
*   Surveillance echocardiography: mild — every 5 years; moderate — every 3 years; severe — every year.

### Concomitant CABG with AVR (AHA)
*   **Class I:** coronary stenosis >70%.
*   **Class IIa:** coronary stenosis 50–70%.

### Surgical Anatomy — Structures at Risk (Figure 7)
*   Anterior mitral leaflet (beneath non-coronary and left coronary cusps)
*   Membranous septum (beneath non-coronary and right coronary cusps)
*   Bundle of His (beneath commissure between non-coronary and right coronary cusps)
*   Left and right coronary ostia

### Surgical Approaches
*   Oblique (J-shaped), transverse, or greater curve aortotomy.

### Prosthesis Implantation Techniques (Figure 8)
*   **Interrupted sutures:** slower, lower paravalvular leak risk; everting (intra-annular) vs non-everting (supra-annular — larger EOA).
*   **Semi-continuous:** faster, higher leak risk.

### Patient Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM)
*   EOA indexed to BSA: mild >0.85, moderate 0.65–0.85, severe <0.65 cm²/m².
*   Leads to persistent gradients, reduced LV mass regression, reduced symptom improvement.

### Small Aortic Root Options
*   Prostheses with improved EOA (supra-annular, stentless)
*   Aortic root enlargement (patch)
*   Annular enlargement: Nicks’ (non-coronary sinus), Manougian’s (commissure between left and non-coronary sinuses), Konno
*   Aortic root replacement, apico-aortic conduit

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbomedics Top Hat® gradient (mmHg) | 30 | 20 | 18 | 16 | 10 |

### Randomised Trials
*   **Veterans Affairs (15 years):** mechanical better survival, lower re-operation (3% vs 29%); less bleeding with bioprosthesis.
*   **Edinburgh (20 years):** no survival difference; higher re-operation with bioprosthesis; more bleeding with mechanical.

### Freedom from Structural Valve Deterioration (15 years, age >70)
*   Mechanical 97%, homograft 85%, bovine pericardial 85%, porcine 80%, stentless 80%, Ross 74% (aortic)/80% (pulmonary).
*   SVD at 10 years: 40% (0–40 years), 30% (40–69 years), 10% (>70 years).

### Bicuspid Aortic Valve
*   1–2% of population, autosomal dominant.
*   Associated with: short LMCA, left dominant circulation, anomalous ostia, aortopathy (dissection/aneurysm), coarctation, earlier AS.

### Valve Choice: Mechanical vs Bioprosthetic
*   No difference in survival, thromboembolism, or endocarditis.
*   **Mechanical preferred:** already on warfarin, young (<60 years) who want to avoid re-operation.
*   **Bioprosthetic preferred:** age >65 years, want to avoid warfarin (contraindication, child-bearing age, active lifestyle, recent GI bleed, future malignancy surgery).

### Pregnancy and Mechanical Valves
*   Warfarin crosses placenta → embryopathy (5–10%, lower if <5 mg/day), abortion, prematurity.
*   Heparin does not cross placenta → no embryopathy but may cause uteroplacental bleeding.
*   **Suggested strategy:** warfarin weeks 1–6 → UFH weeks 6–12 → warfarin weeks 12–36 → UFH from week 36 → stop before delivery → restart UFH 4–6 hours after placental separation.

### Minimally Invasive AVR
*   Upper hemisternotomy (J-shaped to 4th ICS)
*   TAVI: transapical (antegrade via left anterior thoracotomy) or transfemoral (retrograde)

### Transapical TAVI Steps
1. GA, RV pacing wire (femoral vein), pig-tail catheter (femoral artery)
2. Left anterior thoracotomy (6th ICS, locate LV apex with TOE)
3. Pericardial incision anterior to phrenic nerve
4. LV apex purse-string, stab incision, balloon dilator across valve
5. Rapid pacing (200 bpm) → balloon dilation (45 atm, 5 s)
6. Deploy crimped stented valve, repeat rapid pacing and balloon dilation
7. TOE to check position and paravalvular regurgitation

## Chapter 13: Mitral Valve Disease

### Mitral Regurgitation (MR) — Natural History and Pathophysiology
*   **Asymptomatic period** — chronic MR well tolerated if LV function preserved.
*   **Poor prognostic features:** symptoms >1 year, AF, age >60, EF <50%, LVEDV >100 mL/m², LVESV >60 mL/m², LVESD >5 cm, LVEDD >7 cm.
*   **Pathophysiology:** volume overload → eccentric LV hypertrophy → Frank-Starling maintains SV → eventual contractile decline → ↑ LVESV, ↑ filling pressures, pulmonary congestion. EF <60% indicates systolic dysfunction (afterload-reducing effect of MR).

### Carpentier Functional Classification (Figure 1)
| Type | Leaflet Motion | Examples |
|------|---------------|----------|
| I | Normal | Annular dilation, leaflet perforation |
| II | Excessive | Myxomatous degeneration, chordal rupture |
| IIIa | Restricted opening | Rheumatic fever |
| IIIb | Restricted closure | Ischaemic cardiomyopathy |

### Causes of MR
*   **Chronic:** myxomatous degeneration (Type II), rheumatic fever (Type IIIa), ischaemic cardiomyopathy (Type IIIb + Type I).
*   **Acute:** chordal rupture (Type II), endocarditis (Type I), papillary muscle rupture post-MI (Type II).

### Ischaemic MR
*   **Acute:** infarcted/ruptured papillary muscle (Type II).
*   **Chronic:** restricted P2/P3 motion (LV dilation displacing papillary muscles — Type IIIb) + annular dilation (Type I) + reduced annular contraction + reduced closing forces.

### Symptoms of MR
*   Fatigue, weakness (low output), dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND, eventually pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure.

### Signs of MR
*   Displaced volume-loaded apex, apical thrill, S₃, pansystolic murmur radiating to axilla, diastolic flow murmur.

### MR Severity (AHA Guidelines — Table 1)
| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jet area (% LA) | <20% | 20–40% | >40% |
| Vena contracta (cm) | <0.3 | 0.3–0.7 | >0.7 |
| Regurgitant volume (mL) | <30 | 30–60 | >60 |
| Regurgitant fraction (%) | <30 | 30–50 | >50 |
| EROA (cm²) | <0.2 | 0.2–0.4 | >0.4 |

For ischaemic MR: regurgitant volume >30 mL, EROA >0.2 cm² = severe.

### Indications for Mitral Valve Surgery in MR (AHA)
*   **Class I:** severe MR with symptoms (NYHA II–IV) or LV changes (ESD >40 mm, EF <60%).
*   **Class IIa:** severe MR, asymptomatic, good LV function, repair likelihood >90%; severe MR with AF or pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg rest, >60 mmHg exercise).
*   **Class IIb:** severe MR, poor LV function (EF <30%), symptoms (NYHA III–IV) despite maximal medical therapy.

### TOE Indications in Severe MR (AHA)
*   **Class I:** intra-operative (pre-bypass for repair feasibility, post-bypass for repair success).
*   **Class IIa:** pre-operative to guide repair likelihood (>90%).

### Mitral Valve Prolapse (Barlow’s Disease)
*   >2 mm billowing beyond annular plane. Associated with Marfan syndrome.
*   Histology: myxomatous proliferation in zona spongiosa → chordal thinning/elongation.
*   Surgery per AHA guidelines for non-ischaemic MR.

### Mitral Stenosis (MS) — Natural History
*   Symptoms on exertion when MVA <2.5 cm², at rest when <1.5 cm².
*   Progression: MVA reduces 0.1–0.3 cm²/year. Rheumatic fever to signs: 10–20 years; signs to mild symptoms: 10–20 years; mild to decompensation: 5–10 years.
*   10-year survival: 80% (NYHA I/II), 10–15% (NYHA III/IV). Pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg) → mean survival 3 years.

### Pathophysiology of MS
*   Restricted LV inflow → ↑ LA pressure → back pressure to pulmonary circulation → dyspnoea → pulmonary hypertension → RV failure → LV failure.
*   Tachycardia/AF worsen haemodynamics (reduced diastolic filling period).

### Causes of MS
*   Majority: rheumatic fever (Lancefield group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus).
*   Rare: parachute mitral valve, endocardial fibroelastosis, carcinoid.
*   Pathophysiology mimics: cor triatriatum, LA myxoma, pulmonary vein stenosis.

### Symptoms of MS
*   Fatigue, dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND.
*   LA distension: AF (thromboembolism), Ortner’s syndrome (hoarseness — L recurrent laryngeal nerve), dysphagia, left lung collapse.
*   Pulmonary hypertension/RV failure: peripheral oedema, ascites, haemoptysis.

### Signs of MS
*   Low volume pulse, irregular pulse, tapping non-displaced apex, opening snap, loud S₁, mid-diastolic rumbling murmur (apex).
*   Pulmonary hypertension: mitral facies, central cyanosis, loud P₂, TR (pan-systolic at R sternal edge), PR (Graham Steel — early diastolic on inspiration).

### MS Severity (AHA Guidelines — Table 2)
| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| MVA (cm²) | >1.5 | 1.0–1.5 | <1.0 |
| Mean gradient (mmHg) | <5 | 5–10 | >10 |
| PAP systolic (mmHg) | <30 | 30–50 | >50 |

### MVA Calculation Methods
*   Planimetry (2D echo short axis)
*   Pressure half-time: MVA = 220 / PHT
*   Continuity equation: MVA = (Area<sub>LVOT</sub> × VTI<sub>LVOT</sub>) / VTI<sub>MV</sub>
*   Gorlin equation: MVA = CO / (38 × HR × DFP × √mean gradient)

### Indications for Mitral Valve Surgery in MS (AHA)
*   **Class I:** moderate–severe MS, symptoms (NYHA III–IV), PMBV not suitable.
*   **Class IIa:** severe MS, severe pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg) or symptoms (NYHA I–II), PMBV not suitable.
*   **Class IIb:** moderate–severe MS, recurrent embolic events on anticoagulation.

### Wilkins Mitral Stenosis Score (Figure 4)
*   Parameters: leaflet mobility, leaflet thickening, leaflet calcification, subvalvular thickening.
*   Each scores 0–4 (maximum 16). Wilkins score **>9** → unlikely to be amenable to PMBV.

### Percutaneous Mitral Balloon Valvuloplasty (PMBV)
*   **Indications (AHA Class I):** moderate–severe MS, symptoms (NYHA II–IV) or pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg rest, >60 mmHg exercise).
*   **Indications (AHA Class IIa):** moderate–severe MS, symptoms (NYHA III–IV), non-pliable valve or high surgical risk.
*   **Requirements:** Wilkins score ≤8, no LA thrombus, no moderate–severe MR.
*   **Technique (Figure 5):** femoral vein → RA → trans-septal → LA → across MV → balloon inflation.
*   **Results:** MVA increases from ~1 to ~2 cm².
*   **Complications:** mortality 1%, cardiac perforation 1%, stroke 2%, severe MR 3%.
*   **Best results:** thin, mobile, non-calcified leaflets, young age, no subvalvular fusion/calcification.

### Closed Mitral Valvotomy (Figure 6)
*   Left posterolateral thoracotomy → pericardiotomy anterior to phrenic nerve → finger in LA → Tubbs dilator via LV apex → guided through MV orifice → opened along intercommissural line.

### Surgical Approaches to Mitral Valve (Figure 7)
*   **Standard left atriotomy (Waterson’s groove)**
*   **Bi-atrial trans-septal** — risk to SA nodal artery
*   **Superior roof incision** — risk to LMCA, non-coronary sinus, SVC
*   **Bi-atrial Dubost incision** — good access but difficult closure
*   **Right anterolateral thoracotomy** — minimal access/redo

### Intra-operative Mitral Valve Assessment
*   Inspect each scallop (P1, P2, P3, A1, A2, A3) with nerve hooks.
*   Pre-repair: distend LV with cold saline → identify regurgitation jet.
*   Post-repair: static testing (cold saline) + TOE.

### Structures at Risk During Mitral Valve Surgery (Figure 8)
*   Circumflex coronary artery
*   Coronary sinus
*   Aortic valve (left and non-coronary cusps)
*   AV node and conducting bundles

### Systolic Anterior Motion (SAM) (Figure 9)
*   Leaflet tips displaced anteriorly into LVOT → Venturi effect → MR + LVOT obstruction.
*   More common in HOCM and small elderly women with asymmetric septal hypertrophy.
*   **Prevention:** avoid excess anterior leaflet tissue, excess posterior leaflet height (>1.5 cm) (displaces coaptation line anteriorly).
*   **Exacerbated by:** hypovolaemia, excess inotropes, tachycardia.
*   **Post-repair SAM treatment:** conservative (volume, afterload, rate, inotropes); surgical — remove ring, sliding annuloplasty, incomplete ring (Cosgrove®), larger ring (↑ AP diameter), low profile prosthesis (St Jude®).

### Surgical Treatment Options for Mitral Valve Disease (Figures 11–12)
| Pathology | Options |
|-----------|---------|
| Posterior prolapse | Quadrangular/triangular resection, sliding annuloplasty, artificial chords |
| Anterior prolapse | Triangular resection, artificial chords |
| Bileaflet prolapse | Leaflet resection, artificial chords, Alfieri stitch, replacement |
| Partial papillary rupture | Reimplantation |
| Complete papillary rupture | Replacement |
| Leaflet perforation | Bovine pericardial patch |
| Annular dilation | Complete flexible ring (Physio®) |
| Ischaemic MR | McCarthy-Adams IMR® ring, downsizing annuloplasty, replacement |
| Rheumatic MS | Replacement |

### Sliding Annuloplasty (Figure 13)
*   Indicated after excessive posterior leaflet resection or to reduce SAM risk (posterior leaflet height >1.5 cm or long anterior leaflet).

### Mitral Annuloplasty Rings (Figure 14)
| Ring | Features |
|------|----------|
| Carpentier-McCarthy-Adams IMR® | For ischaemic MR |
| Cosgrove-Edwards® | Incomplete, flexible — for SAM risk |
| Carpentier-Edwards Physio® | Complete, semi-flexible — most frequently used |
| Carpentier Classic® | Rigid, complete — adjustable AP diameter |

### Benefits of Annuloplasty Ring
*   Corrects annular dilation, prevents further dilation, increases leaflet coaptation, reinforces suture lines.

### Advantages of Mitral Valve Repair vs Replacement
*   Greater freedom from mortality, SVD, re-operation, endocarditis, thromboembolism, haemorrhage.
*   Preserves subvalvular apparatus → better LV geometry → less LV dysfunction.

### Therapeutic Options for Ischaemic MR
*   CABG
*   McCarthy-Adams IMR® ring (reduced AP diameter, concavity at P2/P3)
*   Downsizing annuloplasty
*   Mitral valve replacement

|-----------|---------|

| Mechanical prosthesis | 98% |
| Anterior leaflet repair | 78% |

| Closed commissurotomy | 70% |

| Porcine bioprosthesis | 75% |
| Bovine pericardial | 80% |

1.  Re-establish CPB
2.  Remove prosthetic valve

## Chapter 14: Tricuspid Valve Disease

### Tricuspid Regurgitation (TR) — Classification
*   **Normal leaflet motion** — annular dilation (secondary to pulmonary hypertension, RV failure, mitral valve disease), infective endocarditis.
*   **Excessive leaflet motion** — myxomatous degeneration.
*   **Restricted leaflet motion** — rheumatic fever, Ebstein’s anomaly.

### Ebstein’s Anomaly (Figure 1)
*   Dysplasia of tricuspid valve leaflets with:
    - Fused/perforated/absent leaflets, abnormal chordae
    - **Apical displacement** of septal leaflet into RV body
    - Thin **atrialised portion** of RV
    - Severe RV dysfunction and dilation
    - TR
    - RVOT obstruction (displaced leaflets)
*   Associated rhythm disturbances: SVT, VT, WPW syndrome.

### Symptoms of TR
*   Dyspnoea, fatigue (↓ cardiac output)
*   Peripheral oedema, ascites (systemic venous congestion)

### Signs of TR
*   ↑ JVP with prominent **V wave**
*   Peripheral oedema, ascites
*   **Pan-systolic murmur** at left sternal edge, ↑ with inspiration (Carvallo’s sign)
*   S₃ (gallop rhythm)
*   RV heave
*   Jaundice (hepatic congestion)

### Quantification of TR (Echocardiography)
*   **Severe TR:** vena contracta **>0.7 cm** (AHA)
*   Systolic flow reversal in hepatic veins
*   Additional: colour-flow jet area (relative to RA), PISA radius, RA size

### Tricuspid Stenosis (TS) — Causes
*   Rheumatic fever (most common)
*   Carcinoid heart disease
*   Congenital TS

### Symptoms of TS
*   Dyspnoea, fatigue (↓ cardiac output)
*   Peripheral oedema, ascites (systemic venous congestion)

### Signs of TS
*   ↑ JVP with prominent **A wave**
*   Peripheral oedema, ascites
*   Tricuspid opening snap
*   **Diastolic rumbling murmur** at left sternal edge, ↑ with inspiration
*   Split S₁

### Quantification of TS (Echocardiography)
*   **Severe TS:** tricuspid valve area **<1.0 cm²** (AHA)
*   Mean tricuspid diastolic pressure gradient
*   TVA calculation: continuity equation or planimetry

### Indications for Tricuspid Valve Surgery (AHA)

**In absence of mitral valve disease:**
*   **Class IIa:** severe TR in symptomatic patients
*   Considerations: annulus size (>40 mm), RV function/size, pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg rest, >60 mmHg exercise), RA size, AF

**In patients undergoing mitral valve surgery:**
*   **Class I:** severe TR
*   **Class IIb:** less than severe TR with pulmonary hypertension or dilated annulus (>40 mm)

### Surgical Options for Annular Dilation
*   **Bicuspidisation/plication** — running suture along posterior annulus to shorten/eliminate posterior leaflet
*   **De Vega annuloplasty (Figure 2):** two arms of running suture woven along annulus from anteroseptal to posteroseptal commissures, tightened over Hegar dilator
*   **Ring annuloplasty (Figures 3–4):** interrupted mattress sutures, sized ring implanted
*   **Avoid** AV node and Bundle of His (triangle of Koch)

### Structures at Risk During Tricuspid Valve Surgery (Figure 5)
*   AV node (centre of triangle of Koch) and Bundle of His (apex of triangle of Koch)
*   Aortic valve (non-coronary cusp)
*   Coronary sinus
*   Right coronary artery
*   Atrioventricular nodal artery

### Tricuspid Valve Annulus Sizing (Figure 6)
*   Annuloplasty sizers measuring anterior leaflet
*   Fixed sizes: 30–32 mm (women), 32–34 mm (men)
*   Ring has **gap** at apex of triangle of Koch to avoid suturing conducting system

## Chapter 15: Infective Endocarditis

### Epidemiology and Pathogenesis
*   **Native valve endocarditis** — incidence 2–4 cases per 100,000 persons/year.
*   **Prosthetic valve endocarditis** — 0.5–1% per year.
*   **Distribution:** left-sided valves (mitral > aortic); tricuspid 5%; pulmonary rare.
*   **Pathogenesis:** endothelial disruption + valvular lesions (AS, MR) → non-laminar flow → platelet-fibrin thrombus → bacterial colonisation → vegetation → spread/embolisation.

### Clinical Features
*   **Infection:** fever, night sweats, rigors, weight loss, malaise, anaemia, clubbing, splenomegaly (chronic).
*   **Immune complex deposition:**
    - Roth spots — retinal boat-shaped haemorrhage with pale centre
    - Splinter haemorrhages — thin reddish-brown lines in nailbed
    - Osler’s nodes — painful pulp infarcts on fingers/toes
    - Janeway lesions — painless palmar/plantar erythema
    - Vasculitis (brain, skin, kidney), arthralgia
*   **Cardiac:** new or changing murmur, heart block, heart failure, pyopericardium.
*   **Emboli:** cerebral, retinal, splenic, mesenteric, renal, lower limb (fragile in fungal endocarditis).

### Common Organisms

**Native valve:**
*   Streptococcus (45%) — viridans (milleri, oralis, mitis, mutans, salivarius) or bovis
*   Staphylococcus aureus/epidermidis (35%)
*   Enterococcus faecalis (10%)
*   HACEK, anaerobes, Coxiella burnetii (Q fever), Chlamydia, fungi

**Prosthetic valve (<2 months):**
*   Staphylococcus aureus/epidermidis (45–50%)
*   Gram-negative bacilli, fungi

**Prosthetic valve (>2 months):**
*   Streptococcus viridans (45%)
*   Staphylococcus aureus/epidermidis (35%)
*   Enterococcus faecalis (10%)

### HACEK Organisms
Gram-negative fastidious bacilli requiring 10% CO₂:
*   Haemophilus (aphrophilus, not influenzae)
*   Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans
*   Cardiobacterium hominis
*   Eikenella corrodens
*   Kingella kingae

### Antibiotics (Empirical/Organism-Specific)
| Organism | Antibiotic |
|----------|------------|
| Unknown (Streptococcus suspected) | Benzylpenicillin + gentamicin |
| Unknown (Staphylococcus suspected) | Vancomycin + gentamicin |
| Streptococcus viridans | Benzylpenicillin + gentamicin |
| Staphylococcus aureus | Vancomycin + gentamicin |
| Enterococcus | Amoxycillin + gentamicin |
| HACEK (β-lactam resistant) | Ceftriaxone |

### Causes of Culture-Negative Endocarditis
*   Prior antibiotic therapy
*   Slow-growing/fastidious organisms (HACEK, nutritionally variant Streptococci, Brucella, Legionella, Corynebacterium, Listeria, Bartonella)
*   Fungal endocarditis
*   Obligate intracellular parasites (Coxiella burnetii, Chlamydiae)
*   Non-infective (marantic) endocarditis

### Marantic (Non-Infective) Endocarditis
*   Mucinous adenocarcinoma (pancreas, lung, upper GI)
*   Lymphoma, bladder carcinoma
*   Anti-phospholipid syndrome (Hughes’ syndrome)
*   Acute rheumatic fever
*   Libman-Sacks endocarditis (SLE, scleroderma)

### Duke’s Diagnostic Criteria
**Major criteria:**
1. Three positive blood cultures (12h apart) with typical organisms (S. viridans/bovis, S. aureus, Enterococci, HACEK)
2. Evidence of endocardial infection (vegetation, abscess, prosthetic valve dehiscence, new regurgitation)

**Minor criteria:**
1. Predisposing factors (cardiac lesion, IV drug abuse)
2. Fever >38°C
3. Embolic/vascular phenomena (splinter haemorrhages, vasculitis)
4. Immunological phenomena (Osler’s nodes, Roth spots)
5. Serology consistent with endocarditis
6. Blood cultures compatible but not typical
7. Echocardiographic findings (not covered by major criteria)

**Diagnosis:**
*   **Definite (specificity 99%, sensitivity >80%):** 2 major; 1 major + 3 minor; 5 minor; or pathological evidence
*   **Possible:** findings consistent but insufficient
*   **Rejected:** alternative diagnosis, resolution within 4 days of antibiotics, no evidence at surgery/autopsy

### Indications for Surgery — Native Valve Endocarditis (AHA)
**Class I:**
*   Valvular regurgitation/stenosis leading to heart failure
*   AR/MR with raised LVEDP, LAP, or moderate pulmonary hypertension
*   Fungal or highly resistant organisms
*   Local invasion → heart block, annular/aortic abscess, fistula, leaflet perforation

**Class IIa:**
*   Recurrent emboli with persistent vegetation despite antibiotics

**Class IIb:**
*   Mobile vegetation >10 mm

### Indications for Surgery — Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (AHA)
**Class I:**
*   Heart failure, valve dehiscence, worsening regurgitation/obstruction, local invasion

**Class IIa:**
*   Persistent bacteraemia or recurrent emboli despite antibiotics, or relapsing infection

**Class III (contraindication):**
*   Uncomplicated prosthetic valve endocarditis with sensitive organism

### Surgical Principles
*   Debridement of infected tissues
*   Closure of cardiac defects
*   Valve repair (preferred if <50% affected) vs replacement
*   Aortic homografts for root abscesses
*   IV drug abusers with tricuspid endocarditis: valvectomy without replacement (only if no significant pulmonary hypertension)

### Aortic Peri-Annular Abscess Treatment
*   Bovine pericardial patch
*   Aortic valve homograft

### Outcomes
| | Operative mortality | 5-year survival |
|---|---|---|
| Native valve | 5–10% | 80% |
| Prosthetic valve | 10–20% | 60% |

### Antibiotic Prophylaxis for Dental Procedures
| Patient | Regimen |
|---------|---------|
| Standard | Amoxycillin (3 g oral 1h pre-op or 2 g IV 30 min pre-op) |
| Penicillin allergy | Clindamycin (600 mg oral 1h pre-op) |
| Previous endocarditis | Amoxycillin (2 g IV 30 min pre-op) + gentamicin (120 mg IV 30 min pre-op) |
| Previous endocarditis + penicillin allergy | Vancomycin (1 g IV 1h pre-op) + gentamicin (120 mg IV 30 min pre-op) |

**Note:** NICE guidelines (UK) do not recommend routine prophylaxis for prosthetic valve patients — controversial; many surgeons still advocate.

## Chapter 16: Thoracic Aortic Disease

### Normal Anatomy and Classification
*   **Aneurysm definition:** permanent localised dilation ≥150% of normal diameter.
*   **Aortic layers:** intima (endothelial + elastic), media (elastin, collagen, smooth muscle), adventitia (connective tissue).
*   **Aneurysm classification:** true (all 3 layers) vs false/pseudoaneurysm (adventitia only); saccular vs fusiform; atherosclerotic, dissecting, mycotic; ascending, arch, descending.
*   **Crawford classification (thoracoabdominal):** Type I (distal descending to suprarenal), II (distal descending to infrarenal), III (lower descending into abdomen), IV (below diaphragm — AAA), V (mid-descending to above diaphragm).

### Natural History and Risk Factors
*   **Expansion rate:** <5 cm → 0.2 cm/year; >5 cm → 0.8 cm/year (Laplace’s law).
*   **Rupture risk:** 4–5 cm → 0.3%/year; >6 cm → 3.6%/year.
*   **Median rupture size:** ascending 6 cm, descending 7 cm.
*   **Mean time from symptoms to rupture:** 2 years.
*   **5-year survival (untreated):** thoracic 39%, thoracoabdominal 23%.
*   **Pathophysiology:** cystic medial degeneration (loss of smooth muscle, elastin fragmentation) → loss of elasticity and tensile strength.
*   **Predisposing factors:** age, atherosclerosis, bicuspid aortic valve, hypertension, connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos), dissection, trauma, aortitis.
*   **Rupture risk factors:** aortic diameter, pain/symptoms, age, COPD, smoking.

### Marfan Syndrome
*   Autosomal dominant, fibrillin gene mutation (chromosome 15).
*   **Diagnosis (≥2 features):** tall thin (arm span > height), arachnodactyly, high arched palate, scoliosis/pectus, joint hypermobility, spontaneous pneumothorax, ectopia lentis, floppy MV, aortopathy.

### Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome
*   Autosomal dominant. **Type IV** → Type III collagen defect → aortopathy. Less common than Marfan.

### Clinical Features of Aortic Aneurysms
*   Asymptomatic (incidental), pain (anterior chest/back — sudden suggests dissection/rupture), compression (hoarseness — recurrent laryngeal, dysphagia — oesophagus, stridor — trachea), fistula (haematemesis, haemoptysis).

### Indications for Intervention
*   **Size criteria:**
    - Ascending >5.5 cm (>4.5 cm if AVR/CABG; >4.0–5.0 cm in Marfan or ratio of maximal area/height >10)
    - Arch >5.5 cm
    - Descending >5.5 cm
    - Thoracoabdominal >6.0 cm
*   **Other:** symptoms of expansion/compression/fistula, rapid expansion >1 cm/year, recurrent emboli.

### Indications for Aortic Root Replacement
*   Annulo-aortic ectasia, sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (most common non-coronary), root abscess, Type A dissection extending into root, small root (if root-sparing not possible).

### Surgical Principles and Outcomes
*   **Operative mortality:** root/ascending 2–5%, arch 5–10%, thoracoabdominal 10–20%.
*   **Neurological risk:** stroke 5–10%, paraplegia 5–10% (descending).
*   **5-year survival post-surgery:** ascending 82%, arch 70%, descending 65%, thoracoabdominal 65%.

### Aortic Root Replacement Options (Figures 8–9)
*   **Valve-sparing (normal valve):**
    - David (reimplantation) — stabilises annulus
    - Yacoub (remodelling) — not for connective tissue disorders or annulus >30 mm
*   **Valved conduit (diseased valve):**
    - Classic Bentall (inclusion) — rarely used
    - Modified Bentall — excise root, reimplant coronary buttons
    - Cabrol — interposition graft for coronary reimplantation (tension)
    - Homograft — for root abscess
    - Ross — pulmonary autograft (aortic) + pulmonary homograft

### Ascending Aneurysm Surgical Options (Figure 10)
*   Ascending replacement (Dacron® graft)
*   Reduction aortoplasty
*   Supporting aortoplasty (mesh wrap)

### Acute Aortic Syndrome (Figure 11)
*   Includes: aortic dissection, penetrating aortic ulcer, intramural haematoma, leaking aneurysm.
*   Management: ascending → surgery; descending → medical (but stent/surgery if rupture, unremitting pain, malperfusion).

### Intramural Haematoma (Figure 12)
*   Blood within media without intimal tear. Aetiology: vasa vasorum rupture, plaque haemorrhage, progression from penetrating ulcer.
*   **Higher rupture risk** than dissection (closer to adventitia).

### Penetrating Aortic Ulcer (Figure 13)
*   Focal intimal defect at atherosclerotic plaque → progressive erosion → intramural haematoma, dissection, rupture, pseudoaneurysm.
*   **Surgery indicated:** descending ulcers >20 mm diameter + >10 mm depth; ascending ulcers.

### Aortic Dissection — Classification (Figure 14)
*   **Timing:** acute (<14 days), sub-acute (14 days–2 months), chronic (>2 months).
*   **Stanford:** Type A (ascending involved), Type B (ascending not involved).
*   **DeBakey:** I (whole aorta), II (ascending only), IIIa (descending only), IIIb (descending + abdominal).

### Pathophysiology of Dissection
*   Intimal tear → blood tracks into media → propagates spirally → false lumen. Multiple re-entry tears in descending aorta.
*   **Entry tear locations:** ascending (65%, ~2 cm above non-coronary sinus), descending (20%, left anterolateral), arch (10%, opposite innominate).

### Predisposing Factors for Dissection (ABCDE)
*   A: age, atherosclerosis, aneurysm
*   B: bicuspid aortic valve (fibrillin deficiency), blood pressure (hypertension)
*   C: connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos)
*   D: degenerative (cystic medial degeneration)
*   E: trauma, surgery, iatrogenic, pregnancy

### Clinical Features of Type A Dissection
*   Tearing retrosternal chest pain radiating to back/neck
*   Organ malperfusion (myocardial ischaemia, stroke, abdominal pain)
*   Dyspnoea (AR, tamponade, haemothorax)
*   BP differential between arms, AR murmur, absent pulses

### Investigations for Acute Type A Dissection (Figure 15)
| Modality | Sensitivity | Limitations |
|----------|-------------|-------------|
| TOE | 98% | Operator-dependent, blind spot (distal ascending/proximal arch) |
| CT angiography | 95% | Contrast nephropathy |
| MRI | 99% | Not always available (gold standard) |
| Aortography | 80% | Rarely used, may precipitate rupture |

### Monitoring for Type A Dissection Surgery
*   Arterial lines: pre-arch (right radial), post-arch (femoral/left radial)
*   Urinary catheter, CVC, nasopharyngeal temperature, cerebral oximetry (NIRS)

### Right Axillary Artery Cannulation (Figure 16)
*   Patient: supine, upper body 20°, tilted left. 5 cm infraclavicular incision. Mobilise axillary artery (avoid brachial plexus).
*   18Fr cannula directly (Seldinger) or via 8 mm Dacron® graft.
*   **Not suitable** if dissection extends into brachiocephalic artery.

### Surgery for Type A Dissection — Principles (Figures 17–18)
*   Resect entry tear
*   Aortic root: repair sinus segments or replace
*   Aortic valve: resuspend or replace
*   Arch: hemi-arch or total arch replacement
*   Distal anastomosis: obliterate false lumen
*   **Options:** ascending graft ± AVR ± root replacement (Bentall/Cabrol) ± valve-sparing (David/Yacoub) ± arch replacement
*   **Mortality:** without surgery ~90%; with surgery 10–20%; 5-year survival 60%.

### Management of Type B Dissection (Figure 19)
*   **Medical (first-line):** labetalol (α + β blocker) — reduce force of contraction, shear stress, BP.
*   **Rationale for medical management:** medical mortality 10% vs operative 27% (paraplegia 24%); no survival benefit with surgery.

### Indications for Intervention in Acute Type B Dissection (Figures 20–21)
*   Rupture/impending rupture (large mediastinal haematoma, large haemothorax)
*   Extension with unremitting pain
*   Limb/visceral/spinal cord malperfusion (metabolic acidosis, ↑ lactate, oliguria, paraesthesia/paraplegia)

### Endovascular Stent Grafting — Indications
*   Acute/chronic Type B dissection, traumatic aortic injury, descending aneurysms, coarctation, arch aneurysms (with branched grafts/fenestration/debranching).

### Stent Graft Requirements and Complications (Figure 22)
*   **Requirements:** landing zones >1.5 cm normal aorta, femoral arteries >7 mm, relatively straight descending aorta.
*   **Complications:** endoleak/migration 10–15%, local vascular 5–10%, mortality 5–10%, aortic trauma 5%, stroke/paraplegia 5%.

## Chapter 17: Coronary Artery Disease

### Pathophysiology
*   **Atherosclerosis stages:** linear fatty streak → fibrolipid plaque → plaque evolution (rupture → thrombosis). Soft plaques (low fibrin) more lethal than hard plaques.
*   **Risk factors:** smoking, hyperlipidaemia, hypertension, diabetes, male gender, age, family history.
*   **Myocardial ischaemia:** imbalance between O₂ supply and demand.
*   **MI:** ischaemic cardiomyocyte loss from progressive atherosclerosis or unstable plaque disruption.

### Angina Treatment
*   Conservative: smoking cessation, diet, BP/diabetic control
*   Medical: antiplatelet, statin, nitrate, β-blocker, CCB, nicorandil
*   PCI: balloon, BMS, DES
*   Surgery: CABG, TMR

### CCS Classification
*   I: no limitation, no symptoms with ordinary activity
*   II: slight limitation, angina with vigorous activity
*   III: marked limitation, angina with routine activity
*   IV: inability to perform any activity without angina, or angina at rest

### Acute Coronary Syndrome (Table 1)
| | Troponin I (ng/mL) | CK/CK-MB | ECG |
|---|---|---|---|
| UA | <0.6 | Normal | Transient ST/T changes or normal |
| NSTEMI | 0.6–1.5 | <2x normal | Transient ST/T changes or normal |
| STEMI | >1.5 | >2x normal | ST elevation or Q waves |

### UA/NSTEMI Management
*   Initial: diamorphine, β-blocker/nitrate/CCB, aspirin + clopidogrel, LMWH, statin
*   High-risk factors (age >65, hypertension, diabetes, prior MI, LV dysfunction, ongoing pain, shock, ECG changes, elevated enzymes) → glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors + urgent angiography + revascularisation

### STEMI Diagnosis (ESC/ACC)
*   Rise and fall of cardiac enzymes + at least one of: ST elevation (>0.1 mV, >0.2 mV in V1–3), pathological Q waves (>0.04 s or >25% QRS), ischaemic chest pain >20 min

### STEMI Management
*   Initial: O₂, diamorphine + metoclopramide, aspirin, β-blocker, statin
*   Reperfusion: primary PCI (if available) or thrombolysis (if within 12 hours of pain, or 24 hours with ongoing pain)

### CABG Indications (AHA) — Selected Key Points
*   **Class I:** left main >50%, left main equivalent, 3VD (especially if EF <50%), 1–2VD with proximal LAD and EF <50%, post-MI mechanical complications, cardiogenic shock within 36 hours
*   **Class III (contraindication):** 1–2VD without proximal LAD and no ischaemia, borderline stenosis (50–60%) with no ischaemia, insignificant stenosis (<50%)

### ESC Guidelines for Revascularisation (Table 3)

*   Additive score approximates percentage mortality risk
*   Factors: age, female, COPD, extracardiac arteriopathy, neurological dysfunction, previous cardiac surgery, creatinine >200 µmol/L, active endocarditis, critical pre-op state, unstable angina, LV dysfunction, recent MI, pulmonary hypertension, emergency, non-CABG surgery, thoracic aorta surgery, post-infarct septal rupture

*   Hybrid: off-pump LIMA-LAD + PCI to remaining lesions
*   PCI alone

### Post-Infarct VSR (Figures 4, Table 6)
*   Emergency repair + CABG (AHA Class I)
*   Pre-op optimisation: dobutamine, diuretics, IABP
*   Operative technique: left ventriculotomy, bovine pericardial patch excluded from LV, two-layer closure with Teflon® strips

*   Positioning: deep pericardial sutures, apical suction, stabilising device

## Chapter 18: Heart Failure

### Definition and Clinical Presentation

*   **Heart failure (HF):** Complex clinical syndrome resulting from structural or functional cardiac disorder impairing ventricular filling or ejection, leading to inadequate cardiac output for metabolic demands.
*   **Left heart failure symptoms:** Dyspnea, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, weight loss, cold peripheries.
*   **Right heart failure symptoms:** Peripheral edema, ascites, hepatic congestion, distended varicose veins.
*   **Key signs:** Peripheral cyanosis, pulsus alternans (severe cases), raised JVP, displaced apex beat, third heart sound (gallop rhythm), hepatomegaly, pleural effusions.

### Classification Systems

*   **NYHA functional classification (I–IV):** I = no limitations; II = slight limitation (comfortable at rest); III = marked limitation (comfortable only at rest); IV = symptoms at rest.
*   **AHA staging (A–D):** A = at risk (HTN/CAD); B = asymptomatic with structural changes (LVH/systolic dysfunction); C = symptomatic with structural changes; D = refractory HF.

### Pathophysiology

*   **Neuroendocrine activation:** Sympathetic nervous system, renin-angiotensin system, release of noradrenaline, ADH (vasopressin), and endothelin → fluid retention and increased afterload.
*   **Natriuretic peptides (ANP/BNP):** Released in response to fluid overload; promote natriuresis/diuresis, vasodilation, inhibit ADH/aldosterone — but overwhelmed by neuroendocrine activation.

### Etiology

*   **Increased preload (volume overload):** Mitral/aortic regurgitation, septal defects
*   **Reduced contractility:** CAD, dilated/restrictive cardiomyopathy, myocarditis
*   **Increased afterload (pressure overload):** Aortic stenosis, hypertension, coarctation
*   **Impaired rhythm:** Atrial/ventricular tachyarrhythmias
*   **Impaired filling:** Tamponade, constrictive pericarditis, mitral stenosis
*   **High-output failure:** Anemia, sepsis, AV fistula, hyperthyroidism, pregnancy

### Myocardial Viability

*   **Viable myocardium:** Cardiac muscle amenable to revascularization (hibernating, stunned, ischemic)
*   **Hibernating myocardium:** Hypocontractility during chronic hypoperfusion that recovers after revascularization
*   **Myocardial stunning:** Dysfunction after ischemia-reperfusion (may last up to 2 weeks)
*   **Identification of hibernating myocardium:**
    *   Dobutamine stress echo: biphasic response (↑ contractility with low dose, not with high dose)
    *   MRI: no thinning (<6 mm), delayed hyperenhancement
    *   PET: normal <sup>18</sup>FDG uptake with decreased <sup>13</sup>N-ammonia/<sup>15</sup>O-water uptake
    *   <sup>201</sup>Thallium: increased uptake on late redistribution

### Surgical Treatments for HF

*   **CABG indications in HF:** CAD with reasonable targets, myocardial ischemia, viability >20% of LV. CASS Registry: 5-year survival 41% (medical) vs. 62% (surgical) for EF <25%. Contraindications: poor targets, pulmonary hypertension (>60 mm Hg), severe RV dysfunction.
*   **Cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT/Biventricular pacing):** Indicated for HF ≥6 weeks on maximal therapy, NYHA III/IV, EF <35%, QRS >150 ms.
*   **ICD indications:** Primary prevention after MI with EF <35% + non-sustained VT or inducible VT; secondary prevention for hemodynamically significant VT with EF <35%.
*   **Batista procedure (partial left ventriculectomy):** Resects posterolateral LV wall to reduce volume and wall stress (Laplace's law) — no sustained benefit shown.
*   **Dynamic cardiomyoplasty:** Latissimus dorsi wrap around ventricles with pacemaker stimulation — C‑SMART trial showed no survival benefit at 12 months.
*   **Surgical ventricular restoration (SVR):** Excludes non-functioning LV, restores elliptical shape. RESTORE registry (n=1198): 30-day mortality 5.3%, 5-year survival 69%, EF improved from 29% to 39%.
*   **Passive ventricular constraint devices (CorCap®):** Reduce further dilation and wall stress. Acorn trial: improved QOL, LV volumes, sphericity index.

### Cardiac Transplantation

*   **Survival:** 1 month 93%, 1 year 85%, 5 year 72%, 10 year 55%. 25% mortality on waiting list; 50–60% receive heart within 2 years.
*   **Heart Failure Survival Score (HFSS) components:** Ischemic cardiomyopathy, heart rate, LVEF, MAP, conduction delay, VO₂ max, serum sodium. 1-year survival: low risk (>8.1) 93%, medium risk (7.2–8.1) 73%, high risk (<7.2) 43%.
*   **Definite indications (ISHLT):** High-risk HFSS, VO₂ max <10 mL/kg/min, NYHA IV refractory, recurrent hospitalization, refractory ischemia with EF <20% not amenable to revascularization, refractory VT.
*   **Contraindications:** Age >65, active infection, diabetes with end-organ damage, recent malignancy (need 5-year disease-free), excessive obesity, Cr >250 mmol/L or CrCl <50 mL/min, bilirubin >25 mmol/L, irreversible pulmonary hypertension (PAP >60 mm Hg or PVR >5 Wood units or TPG <18 mm Hg), drug abuse within 6 months, non-compliance risk.
*   **Donor criteria:** Brain stem death, consent, no CAD or prior MI, no other cardiac disease, no malignancies (except CNS tumors), no refractory infection.
*   **Immunosuppression:** Induction (ATG, azathioprine, corticosteroids); maintenance (cyclosporine A/tacrolimus + azathioprine/MMF + corticosteroids); newer agents (OKT3, daclizumab, basiliximab, sirolimus).

### Left Ventricular Assist Devices (LVADs)

*   **Classification:** Pulsatile (HeartMate I, Novacor) vs. non-pulsatile/continuous flow (HeartMate II, Jarvik 2000, DeBakey VAD); location (implantable, paracorporeal, percutaneous).
*   **Uses:** Bridge to transplantation, bridge to recovery, destination therapy.
*   **Complications:** Mortality 5–10% at 30 days, bleeding, infection (driveline/pocket), RV failure, thromboembolism, hemolysis.
*   **REMATCH trial (n=129, NYHA IV, EF <25%, VO₂ max <12):** LVAD vs. medical therapy — 1-year survival 52% vs. 25%, 2-year survival 23% vs. 8%. More adverse events with LVAD. Conclusion: acceptable alternative for non-transplant candidates.

### Key Clinical Trials

| Trial | Population | Intervention | Outcome |
|-------|------------|--------------|---------|
| **MADIT** | Prior MI, EF <35%, non-sustained VT or inducible VT | ICD vs. medical | 27% mortality reduction with ICD |
| **MUSTIC** | NYHA III/IV, EF <35%, QRS >150 ms | CRT (3 months on/off) | Improved QOL, 23% ↑ exercise tolerance, reduced hospitalizations |
| **STICH (hypothesis 2)** | CAD, EF <35%, anterior akinesia/dyskinesia | CABG + SVR vs. CABG alone | No difference in death or cardiac hospitalization |
| **HeartMate II** | Advanced HF, transplant-ineligible | Continuous-flow (HM II) vs. pulsatile (HM XVE) | 2-year survival 58% vs. 24%; fewer adverse events with continuous-flow |

## Chapter 19: Chapter 19: Arrhythmia Surgery

### Atrial Fibrillation: Classification and Epidemiology

*   **Atrial fibrillation (AF):** Uncoordinated atrial activity at a rate of 300–500 bpm.
*   **Classification:** Isolated AF (single episode); recurrent AF (≥2 episodes) — paroxysmal (<7 days, spontaneous termination), persistent (requires drugs or DC cardioversion), permanent (does not revert to sinus rhythm).
*   **Incidence:** 1% of general population; increases with age — 0.2–0.3% at 25–35 years, 3–4% at 55–65 years, 6–9% at 65–90 years. Most common arrhythmia (33% of all arrhythmias).

### Causes and Risk Factors

*   **Common causes:** Idiopathic, mitral valve disease (→ LA dilation), ischemic heart disease, hypertension, post-cardiac surgery, alcohol, thyrotoxicosis.
*   **Post-operative AF risk factors:** Age, β-blocker withdrawal, electrolyte imbalance (K⁺, Mg²⁺), hypoxia, ischemia, pericardial effusion, infection.

### Pathophysiology

*   **Triggers:** Focal areas of automaticity (mainly around pulmonary veins)
*   **Substrate:** Atrial abnormality (inflammation/fibrosis)
*   **Modulating factors:** Autonomic nervous system
*   **Maintenance:** Multiple macro re-entry circuits within both atria
*   **Treatment principle:** Paroxysmal AF → pulmonary vein isolation (targets triggers); Persistent AF → Cox-maze procedure (targets macro re-entry circuits)

### Sequelae of AF

*   Palpitations and anxiety
*   Hemodynamic compromise — 30% loss of cardiac output; long-term may cause cardiomyopathy
*   Thromboembolism — stasis → stroke or pulmonary embolism

### Anticoagulation Guidelines (AHA Class I Indications)

| Clinical Scenario | Anticoagulation |
|------------------|-----------------|
| Prosthetic mechanical valves | Warfarin (INR 2.5–3.5) |
| 1 high-risk factor | Warfarin (INR 2.0–3.0) |
| >1 moderate-risk factor | Warfarin (INR 2.0–3.0) |
| 1 moderate-risk factor | Aspirin 81–325 mg or Warfarin (INR 2.0–3.0) |
| Low-risk factors | Aspirin 81–325 mg |

*   **High-risk factors:** Prior thromboembolism (stroke, TIA, systemic embolism), rheumatic mitral stenosis
*   **Moderate-risk factors:** Age >75 years, hypertension, heart failure, LVEF <35%, diabetes mellitus
*   **Low-risk factors:** Female gender, age 65–75 years, CAD, thyrotoxicosis
*   **Lone AF age <60 years:** No anticoagulation recommended

### Treatment Options for AF

*   **Pharmacological:** Chemical cardioversion and rate control (amiodarone, digoxin, β-blockers, calcium channel blockers)
*   **DC cardioversion**
*   **Catheter ablation:** 60% success rate; complications include pulmonary vein stenosis, systemic embolization, pericardial effusion, tamponade, phrenic nerve paralysis
*   **Surgical ablation:** 75–80% success rate

### Surgical Ablation Modalities

| Modality | Mechanism |
|----------|-----------|
| **Cut and sew** | Surgical incisions (Cox maze III lesion set) |
| **Radiofrequency ablation** | Alternating current 350 kHz–1 MHz, 70–80°C for 1 minute, 3–6 mm lesion; transmurality assessed by conductance/impedance |
| **Microwave** | High-frequency electromagnetic radiation → water molecule oscillation |
| **Cryoablation** | Nitrous oxide at –60°C for 2 minutes → visible "iceball" |
| **Ultrasound** | High-frequency sound waves (2–20 MHz) → thermal heating and membrane disruption |
| **Laser** | Monochromatic, phase-coherent beam → heating and cellular destruction |

### Maze Procedure

*   **Principle:** Creates blind alleys with one entrance and one exit, directing electrical impulse from SA node to AV node by interrupting conduction routes and re-entrant pathways.
*   **Advantage over Guiraudon corridor procedure:** Better maintenance of atrial transport.
*   **Left atrial appendage excision:** Often performed to reduce blood stasis and thrombus formation.
*   **Predictors of failure:** Large left atrium (>5 cm) or longstanding AF (>5 years).

### Left Atrial Lesion Set (for mitral valve surgery)

*   Around right superior and inferior pulmonary veins
*   Around left superior and inferior pulmonary veins (divide ligament of Marshall)
*   From LSPV to LAA (LAA then excluded with suture)
*   From RIPV to LIPV
*   From connecting lesion between LIPV/RIPV to posterior mitral annulus
*   From left atriotomy to posterior mitral annulus

### Right Atrial Lesion Set (for full maze)

*   Right atrial free wall → SVC
*   Right atrial free wall → IVC
*   Right atrial free wall → anteroposterior tricuspid commissure
*   Right atrial appendage → anteroseptal tricuspid commissure
*   Right atrial appendage → right atrial free wall (leaving 2–3 cm gap for conduction)

### Atrial Flutter

*   **Mechanism:** Single macro re-entry circuit
*   **Presentation:** Variable block (2:1, 3:1, or 4:1) — atrial rate 300 bpm → ventricular response 150, 100, or 75 bpm
*   **Isthmus:** From IVC opening along Eustachian valve and coronary sinus to tricuspid valve
*   **Treatment:** Amiodarone, calcium channel blockers, DC cardioversion, catheter ablation, or surgical ablation of the flutter isthmus

### Vaughan-Williams Classification of Antiarrhythmic Drugs

| Class | Mechanism | Drugs |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| **I** | Fast sodium channel blockade | Ia: quinidine, procainamide, disopyramide; Ib: lidocaine, phenytoin; Ic: flecainide, propafenone |
| **II** | β-sympathetic blockade | β-blockers, bretylium, guanethidine |
| **III** | Potassium channel blockade | amiodarone, sotalol, bretylium |
| **IV** | Slow calcium channel blockade | verapamil, diltiazem, adenosine |

## Chapter 20: Pericardial Disease, Cardiac Tumours and Cardiac Trauma

### Pericardial Tamponade

*   **Clinical features:** Haemodynamic compromise, dyspnoea, tachypnoea, low pulse volume, **pulsus paradoxus** (fall in systolic BP >10 mm Hg on inspiration), raised JVP with prominent x descent but no y descent, muffled heart sounds, oliguria/anuria.
*   **Pulsus paradoxus mechanism:** Increased inspiratory venous return → right heart filling → interventricular septum displaces leftward (rigid pericardium) → reduced left heart filling → decreased cardiac output and BP.
*   **ECG findings:** Low voltage QRS complexes, **electrical alternans** (heart swinging in fluid-filled sac).
*   **CXR:** Symmetrical globular enlargement of the heart ("water bottle" shape).
*   **Echocardiography:** Large pericardial collection, swinging heart, early diastolic RV collapse, late diastolic RA collapse, marked respiratory variation (tricuspid >40%, mitral >25%).
*   **Treatment:** Paraxiphoid pericardiocentesis, subxiphoid drainage, thoracotomy with pericardial window, or re-opening of sternotomy (post-op).

### Constrictive Pericarditis

*   **Causes:** Idiopathic (most common in developed world), infection (TB, viral, fungal), mediastinal radiotherapy, post-cardiac surgery (0.2–0.3%), rare (tumour, drugs, sarcoidosis, amyloidosis, uraemia, trauma, MI).
*   **Clinical features:** Symptoms of left and right heart failure, raised JVP, ascites, peripheral oedema, hepatosplenomegaly, displaced apex beat, muffled heart sounds, narrowed pulse pressure, Kussmaul's sign.
*   **Diagnostic findings:**
    *   CXR: pericardial calcification, bilateral pleural effusions
    *   Echocardiography: impaired diastolic filling, thickened pericardium, dilated RA/IVC/hepatic veins, increased E:A ratio
    *   CT/MRI: thickened pericardium >4 mm (normal 1–2 mm)
    *   Cardiac catheterisation: equalisation of LVEDP and RVEDP (<5 mm Hg), square root sign (dip and plateau), RVEDP/RVSP ratio >1:3
*   **Differentiation from restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM):**
    *   RCM more likely: pulmonary hypertension (PAP >50 mm Hg), reduced LV function (EF <40%), infiltrative process on biopsy
    *   CP more likely: equalisation of LVEDP/RVEDP (<5 mm Hg), Kussmaul's sign, RVEDP/RVSP ratio >1:3, thickened pericardium on imaging
*   **Pericardiectomy:** Complete removal of thickened pericardium and epicardium from ventricles and diaphragm, preserving both phrenic nerves. Operative mortality 10–15%, 5-year survival 70%.

### Restrictive Cardiomyopathy (SAILS)

*   **S**cleroderma
*   **A**myloidosis
*   **I**ron storage diseases (haemochromatosis)
*   **L**oeffler's eosinophilic endocarditis / endomyocardial fibrosis
*   **S**arcoidosis

### Cardiac Tumours

*   **Classification:** Primary tumours (75% benign, 25% malignant); secondary tumours (metastatic or direct spread) are **30 times more common**.
*   **Benign tumours:**
    *   **Myxoma** (most common benign in adults): 75% left atrium, 20% right atrium, 5% ventricles/valves
    *   **Lipoma:** Well-encapsulated, obese elderly females, right atrium
    *   **Fibroelastoma:** Cardiac valves (mitral/aortic), frond-like projections, embolisation risk
    *   **Rhabdomyoma:** Most common benign in children, multicentric, ventricles
*   **Malignant tumours:**
    *   **Angiosarcoma:** Atria, presents with caval/valvular obstruction, distant metastases common, median survival ~6 months
    *   **Rhabdomyosarcoma:** Multicentric, all locations, median survival ~12 months
*   **Carney's syndrome:** Autosomal dominant familial atrial myxoma; male predominance, earlier onset (childhood), multicentric (65%), recurrence (40%); associated with adrenocortical nodules, Sertoli cell tumours, spotty pigmentation.

### Cardiac Myxoma — Surgical Principles

*   **Approach:** Median sternotomy, bicaval cannulation, minimal cardiac manipulation
*   **Incision:** Left atriotomy, transseptal, or Dubost bi-atrial incision
*   **Resection:** En bloc with clear margins; interatrial septum defect closed with pericardial patch
*   **Irrigation:** Saline washout of residual cells (avoid cell-saver or bypass suction)
*   **Recurrence rate:** <5% (inadequate resection, tumour seeding, multicentric tumours)

### Traumatic Aortic Rupture

*   **Pathophysiology:** Deceleration injury → strain at anatomical fixation points. 80–90% at **aortic isthmus** (between left subclavian artery and ligamentum arteriosum). 80–90% die at scene; 50% of survivors die within 48 hours.
*   **Clinical features:** Consider in severe blunt thoracic trauma with upper rib (1st–3rd), sternal, or scapula fractures; retrosternal/interscapular pain; haemodynamic compromise; peri-aortic haematoma causing dysphagia, stridor, hoarseness.
*   **CXR findings:** Widened (>8 cm) superior mediastinum, obliterated aortic knob, left apical pleural cap, left haemothorax, tracheal/oesophageal deviation to right, left hilum displaced downwards.
*   **CT findings:** Aortic lumen disruption, contrast extravasation, peri-aortic haematoma.
*   **Surgical options:**
    *   **Clamp and sew:** No heparin, but paraplegia risk if clamp time >30 minutes
    *   **Gott shunt:** Heparin-bonded tubing from ascending aorta/LV apex to descending aorta/femoral artery
    *   **Left heart bypass:** Heparin-bonded tubing from LAA to femoral artery with centrifugal pump (~2 L/min), no systemic heparin
    *   **Total cardiopulmonary bypass:** Femoral vein to femoral artery, systemic heparin required
    *   **Endovascular stenting:** Consider as alternative

### Emergency Resuscitative Thoracotomy (ATLS)

*   **Indication:** Penetrating trauma (not blunt) within 10 minutes of PEA
*   **Approach:** Left anterolateral thoracotomy through 4th intercostal space
*   **Therapeutic options:** Pericardial evacuation, open cardiac massage, descending aortic clamping

### Blunt Cardiac Trauma

*   **Management:** ATLS guidelines, monitor with serial ECGs, echocardiography, cardiac enzymes, CXR, CT
*   **Potential injuries:** Left pleuropericardial tears, right atrial disruption (caval junctions), atrial/ventricular septal defects, aortic regurgitation

### Penetrating Cardiac Trauma

*   **Diagnosis:** Clinical signs (haemodynamic compromise, Beck's triad, Kussmaul's sign, pulsus paradoxus) and echocardiography
*   **Treatment:** Prompt decompression (paraxiphoid pericardiocentesis) then operative closure
*   **Access:** Left anterolateral thoracotomy (emergency) or median sternotomy (if time permits)
*   **Technique:** Open pericardium, evacuate clot, open cardiac massage, direct suture of lacerations (Teflon® strips for buttressing). Coronary artery lacerations may require ligation and bypass grafting.

## Chapter 21: Cardiac Anaesthesia and Intensive Care Management

### Anaesthetic Monitoring and Principles

*   **Standard monitoring:** ECG (limb leads + V5), arterial oxygen saturation, end-tidal CO₂, invasive/non-invasive arterial BP, CVP, core/peripheral temperature, urine output, cardiac output (Swan-Ganz, LiDCO), pulmonary artery pressure, TOE.
*   **Pre-operative preparation:** Fast 6–8 hours, premedication (benzodiazepine + opiate) to reduce anxiety-induced tachycardia/hypertension.
*   **Three components of anaesthesia:**
    *   **Hypnosis:** propofol (IV), isoflurane (inhaled)
    *   **Analgesia:** fentanyl or remifentanil (preferred over morphine — fewer cardiac effects, short-acting)
    *   **Muscle relaxation:** non-depolarising (vecuronium, rocuronium — 2–3 min onset, 20–60 min duration, reversed by neostigmine) or depolarising (suxamethonium — 5–10 min, not reversible)
*   **Goal:** Minimise myocardial oxygen consumption by controlling heart rate, BP, preload, and afterload.

### ASA Classification

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| I | Healthy patient |
| II | Mild systemic disease, no functional limitation |
| III | Moderate systemic disease with definite functional limitation |
| IV | Severe systemic disease, constant threat to life |
| V | Moribund, not expected to survive 24 hours |

### Intubation Grades

*   **Cormack-Lehane:** I (vocal cords visible), II (partially visible), III (only epiglottis), IV (neither visible)
*   **Mallampati (pre-op assessment):** I (faucial pillars, soft palate, uvula visible), II (pillars + soft palate), III (only soft palate), IV (none visible)

### Transfer to ICU and Monitoring

*   **Before transfer:** Haemodynamically stable, no bleeding, infusions checked, portable monitoring, oxygen tank, self-inflating bag, IV boluses available, defibrillator.
*   **ICU monitoring:** ECG, SpO₂, EtCO₂, arterial BP, CVP, temperature, urine output, mediastinal drainage, cardiac output, pulmonary artery pressure.

### Mediastinal Bleeding

*   **Surgical causes:** Anastomotic suture lines, cannulation sites, conduit side branches, sternal sites, raw surfaces (IMA bed, pericardium, thymus).
*   **Coagulopathy causes:** Residual heparin, thrombocytopenia (CPB reduces platelets 20–50%), platelet dysfunction (aspirin, clopidogrel, CPB), factor depletion (warfarin, liver disease, haemodilution), fibrinolysis (thrombolytics, CPB).
*   **Management thresholds:**
    *   **Protamine:** if APTTR >1.3 or ACT >110% baseline
    *   **Platelets:** if <100 × 10⁹/L or TEG dysfunction — 1 unit/10 kg (increases count by 7–10 × 10⁹/L)
    *   **FFP:** if INR >1.3 — 2–4 units (5–10 mL/kg)
    *   **Cryoprecipitate:** if fibrinogen <150 mg/dL — 0.1 U/kg (1–2 pools)
    *   **RBCs:** if Hb <8 g/dL — 1 unit increases Hb by 1 g/dL
    *   **Re-exploration:** tamponade, haemodynamic instability, or bleeding >500 mL/hr ×1 h, >400 mL/hr ×2 h, or >300 mL/hr ×3 h

### Low Cardiac Output Syndrome

*   **Causes:**
    *   Reduced preload: hypovolaemia, tamponade, tension pneumothorax, tachyarrhythmias
    *   Impaired contractility: negative inotropes, hypoxia/acidosis, stunning, ischaemia, mechanical (valve dysfunction)
    *   Altered afterload: vasoplegia (SIRS, rewarming, protamine) or excessive afterload (hypothermia, pressors)
*   **Management:**
    *   Target cardiac index >2.5 L/min/m²
    *   Optimise preload (filling pressures 10–14 mm Hg)
    *   Inotropes: epinephrine, dopamine, milrinone
    *   Afterload management: vasodilators (GTN, nitroprusside) or vasoconstrictors (noradrenaline, vasopressin)
    *   IABP for refractory cases

### Right Ventricular Failure

*   **Causes:** Poor myocardial protection, stunning, ischaemia (RCA), pre-existing RV dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension (left heart disease, pulmonary disease, PE, ARDS)
*   **Management:**
    *   Preload: volume, AV sequential pacing, sinus rhythm
    *   Contractility: epinephrine, milrinone
    *   Afterload: correct hypoxia/hypercarbia/acidosis, sildenafil, milrinone, inhaled nitric oxide (20–40 ppm), nesiritide, RVAD

### Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP)

*   **Indications:** Elective (poor pre-op function), peri-operative ischaemia, mechanical complications of MI, low cardiac output refractory to inotropes, bridge to revascularisation/VAD/transplant
*   **Contraindications:** Aortic regurgitation, aortic dissection, severe peripheral atherosclerosis

### Pacemaker Classification (NASPE/BPEG)

| Position | Meaning | Codes |
|----------|---------|-------|
| I | Chamber paced | O, A, V, D, S |
| II | Chamber sensed | O, A, V, D, S |
| III | Response to sensing | O, T, I, D |
| IV | Programmability | O, R |
| V | Multisite pacing | O, A, V, D |

*   **Typical temporary settings:** Rate 90 bpm, atrial output 5 mA, atrial sensitivity 1.0 mV, ventricular output 5 mA, ventricular sensitivity 2.0 mV, AVD 150 ms, mode DDD.

### Ventilation

*   **Modes:** Volume-preset (AC, IMV, SIMV); pressure-preset (PCV, PS)
*   **Typical settings:** SIMV or AC, FiO₂ 100% → wean to 40%, TV 6–10 mL/kg, RR 12–18/min, minute volume 100–120 mL/kg/min, PEEP 5 cmH₂O, I:E 1:2, peak pressure <35 cmH₂O
*   **Fast-tracking exclusion:** Poor LV function, redo sternotomy, severe respiratory dysfunction (FEV₁ <75%), severe pulmonary hypertension (PAP >60 mm Hg), severe renal/hepatic insufficiency, CVA, difficult intubation, prolonged operation, high-dose inotropes, ongoing bleeding

### Extubation Criteria

*   **Cardiac:** CI >2.2 L/min/m², MAP >70 mm Hg, no significant arrhythmias
*   **Respiratory:** PaO₂ >10 kPa, SaO₂ >92% on FiO₂ <50%, PEEP <5 cmH₂O, RR <20/min, pressure support <10 cmH₂O
*   **Other:** Mediastinal drainage <50 mL/hr, Hb >8 g/dL, urine output >1 mL/kg/hr, awake, able to lift head, cough, core temperature >36°C
*   **Poor weaning predictors:** TV <5 mL/kg, minute volume <10 L/min, max inspiratory pressure <-20 cmH₂O, shallow breathing index >100

### Respiratory Impairment

*   **Causes of poor gas exchange:** Atelectasis (anaesthesia, IMA harvesting, reduced surfactant, pain, secretions), pulmonary oedema (cardiogenic, SIRS, transfusion), pleural effusion, bronchospasm, pneumothorax, phrenic nerve injury (iced slush, trauma, IMA harvest)
*   **ARDS criteria:** Acute onset, PaO₂:FiO₂ <200 mm Hg, bilateral infiltrates, no cardiogenic oedema (PCWP <18 mm Hg), reduced compliance, predisposing factor (CPB, sepsis, aspiration, trauma)

### Fluid Balance and Renal Function

*   **CPB effects:** Increased capillary permeability, vasodilation, reduced oncotic pressure → interstitial fluid excess; prime volume (1.5–2.0 L), RAAS activation, ADH, cortisol → total body fluid increase.
*   **Renal risk factors:** Pre-op (Cr >150 μmol/L, age, LV dysfunction, emergency, low output, HTN, DM, nephrotoxins), intra-op (prolonged CPB, hypoperfusion, aprotinin), post-op (ATN, vasoconstriction, sepsis)
*   **Cockcroft-Gault formula:**
    *   Men: $\frac{(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{wt (kg)}}{72 \times P_{\text{Cr}}}$
    *   Women: $0.85 \times \frac{(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{wt (kg)}}{72 \times P_{\text{Cr}}}$
*   **Renal replacement therapy:** Haemodialysis (diffusion) or haemofiltration (convection — removes larger molecules up to 20,000 MW); requires double-lumen central access

### Hypothermia

*   **Causes:** Anaesthetic-induced vasodilation, reduced metabolism, increased heat loss, lowered thermoregulatory threshold (3–4°C), neuromuscular blockade prevents shivering, cold gases/IV fluids, active CPB cooling
*   **Effects:** Vasoconstriction (↑ afterload, ↑ MVO₂), arrhythmias, coagulopathy, left-shifted ODC (↓ tissue O₂ delivery), ↑ stress response (hyperglycaemia)

### Postoperative Complications

*   **Hypertension causes:** Anxiety/pain, inotropes, hypothermia, hyperdynamic syndrome (post-AVR/CABG), abnormal baroreceptors, autonomic neuropathy
*   **Acidosis:** Metabolic (hypoperfusion → lactic acidosis, renal failure, sepsis, hyperglycaemia) or respiratory (CO₂ retention)
*   **Hyperkalaemia:** Excess K⁺ administration, K⁺-sparing diuretics, ACEI/ARB, renal failure, tissue ischaemia. Treatment: calcium gluconate, insulin/dextrose, β-agonist, furosemide, dialysis
*   **Hypokalaemia:** Diuresis, insulin, alkalosis, NG drainage. Treatment: K⁺ supplementation, K⁺-sparing agents
*   **Hyponatraemia:** Hypervolaemic (excess 5% dextrose, renal failure), euvolaemic (SIADH), hypovolaemic (thiazides, ACEI, diarrhoea)
*   **Hypernatraemia:** Water depletion (diabetes insipidus, diarrhoea), sodium excess (normal saline), drugs
*   **Oliguria:** Pre-renal (hypovolaemia, low CO, renal artery disease), renal (pre-existing disease, nephrotoxins, CPB, sepsis), post-renal (catheter blockage)

### Enteral Feeding

*   **Requirements:** 25 kcal/kg/day (catabolic: 30 kcal/kg/day)
*   **Start:** If not extubated or eating within 48 hours
*   **Renal failure:** Low-protein feeds (except on RRT → high-protein), high-energy low-volume, low-electrolyte

### Sternal Wound Infection

*   **Prevention:** Antibiotic prophylaxis (cefuroxime or vancomycin for MRSA)
*   **Superficial:** Drainage, VAC pump
*   **Deep/mediastinitis:** Debridement, VAC or irrigation, antibiotics, reconstruction (sternal rewiring, pectoral flaps, latissimus dorsi, rectus abdominis, omentum) after infection controlled

### Anticoagulation (AHA Guidelines)

| Indication | Target INR |
|------------|-----------|
| Coronary endarterectomy | 2.0–3.0 |
| Atrial fibrillation | 2.0–3.0 |
| Mechanical aortic valve | 2.0–3.0 |
| Mechanical mitral valve | 2.5–3.5 |
| Bioprosthetic valve | Aspirin alone, or warfarin 3 months (INR 2.0–3.0) then aspirin |

### Secondary Prevention of CAD

*   **Aspirin:** Antiplatelet
*   **Statin:** Reduces cholesterol deposition
*   **β-blocker:** Reduces LV work (contractility, afterload)
*   **ACEI:** Reduces afterload, promotes reverse remodelling

## Chapter 22: Postoperative Management

### Hypertension and Arrhythmias

*   **Causes of postoperative hypertension:** Anxiety/pain, inotropes (dopamine, noradrenaline, adrenaline, vasopressin), hypothermia-induced vasoconstriction, hyperdynamic syndrome (post-AVR/CABG), abnormal baroreceptor function (aortic dissection), autonomic neuropathy (diabetes).
*   **Common arrhythmias:** Sinus tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, heart block (especially post-valve surgery), ventricular/atrial ectopics, ventricular tachycardia, sinus bradycardia.
*   **Causes of sinus tachycardia:** β-blocker withdrawal, fever/sepsis, anxiety/pain, hypovolaemia, inotropes, anaemia.

### Acidosis

*   **Metabolic acidosis causes:** Hypoperfusion → lactic acidosis (hypovolaemia, low CO, myocardial dysfunction, excessive vasoconstriction, sepsis); increased metabolic activity (adrenaline, sepsis); renal impairment; splanchnic/limb ischaemia; endocrine (hyperglycaemia, thyrotoxic crisis).
*   **Respiratory acidosis:** CO₂ retention.
*   **Management:** Identify underlying cause, optimise cardiac output, reduce vasopressors, restore normothermia, optimise renal function, consider sodium bicarbonate (50 mL 8.4%) or dialysis.

### Electrolyte Disturbances

**Hyperkalaemia:**
*   **Causes:** Excess K⁺ administration, K⁺-sparing diuretics, ACEI/ARB, renal failure, tissue ischaemia.
*   **Treatment:** Re-check K⁺, cardiac monitoring, stop K⁺-sparing drugs and supplements, calcium resonium; shift K⁺ into cells (insulin/dextrose, NaHCO₃, β-agonist); increase excretion (furosemide, dialysis); calcium gluconate (membrane stabiliser).

**Hypokalaemia:**
*   **Causes:** Diuresis without supplementation, insulin, alkalosis, excessive NG drainage.
*   **Treatment:** Re-check K⁺, cardiac monitoring, K⁺ supplementation, K⁺-sparing medications, reduce insulin/β-agonists, reduce furosemide.

**Hyponatraemia:**
*   **Hypervolaemic:** Excess 5% dextrose, renal failure → fluid restriction, replace dextrose with saline, loop diuretics.
*   **Euvolaemic:** SIADH (urinary Na >20 mmol/L, serum Na <130 mmol/L, serum osmolality <270 mOsm/kg, urinary osmolality >100 mOsm/L) → demeclocycline, vasopressin antagonist (conivaptan), sodium supplements.
*   **Hypovolaemic:** Thiazides, ACEI/ARB, diarrhoea/vomiting → stop offending drugs, fluid replacement with normal saline.

**Hypernatraemia:**
*   **Causes:** Pure water depletion (diabetes insipidus), water depletion exceeding sodium depletion (diarrhoea), sodium excess (normal saline), drugs (amphotericin, phenytoin, lithium, gentamicin).
*   **Treatment:** Check urine/plasma Na and osmolality, treat underlying cause, 5% dextrose, vasopressin for diabetes insipidus.

### Hypoxia

*   **Causes of poor gas exchange:** Atelectasis (anaesthesia, CPB, IMA harvesting, pain, secretions), pulmonary oedema (cardiogenic, SIRS, transfusion), pleural effusion, bronchospasm, pneumothorax, phrenic nerve injury (iced slush, direct trauma, IMA harvest).
*   **Inadequate oxygen delivery:** Low FiO₂, inadequate ventilator settings, misplaced ETT, low cardiac output, anaemia, increased demand (shivering, sepsis).

### Oliguria

*   **Pre-renal:** Hypovolaemia, low cardiac output, renal artery disease (aortic dissection).
*   **Renal:** Pre-existing renal disease, nephrotoxins (gentamicin, NSAIDs), CPB-induced nephropathy, sepsis.
*   **Post-renal:** Catheter blockage/kinking.
*   **Management:** Ensure catheter patent, optimise myocardial function (preload, contractility, afterload, dysrhythmias, IABP), diuretics (bolus → infusion), renal-dose dopamine (1–5 μg/kg/min), dialysis for hyperkalaemia, acidosis, fluid overload.

### Enteral Feeding

*   **Requirements:** 25 kcal/kg/day (catabolic: 30 kcal/kg/day)
*   **Initiation:** If not extubated or eating within 48 hours
*   **Standard feeds:** 1–1.2 kcal/mL; high-energy: 1.5–2 kcal/mL
*   **Renal failure:** Low-protein feeds (except on RRT → high-protein), high-energy low-volume, low-electrolyte
*   **Administration:** 16–20 hours with 4–8 hour rest period
*   **TPN:** If not absorbing or contraindication to enteral feeding

### Sternal Wound Infections

*   **Prevention:** Antibiotic prophylaxis (cefuroxime 30 min pre-incision, vancomycin for MRSA risk), infection control.
*   **Superficial:** Drainage, VAC pump.
*   **Deep/mediastinitis:** Debridement, VAC or irrigation, targeted antibiotics, reconstruction after infection controlled (sternal rewiring, pectoral flaps, latissimus dorsi, rectus abdominis, omentum).

### Anticoagulation (AHA Guidelines)

| Indication | Target INR |
|------------|-----------|
| Coronary endarterectomy | 2.0–3.0 |
| Atrial fibrillation | 2.0–3.0 |
| Mechanical aortic valve | 2.0–3.0 |
| Mechanical mitral valve | 2.5–3.5 |
| Bioprosthetic valve | Aspirin alone, or warfarin 3 months (INR 2.0–3.0) then aspirin |

### Secondary Prevention of CAD

*   **Aspirin:** Antiplatelet — reduces coronary thrombosis risk
*   **Statin:** HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor — reduces cholesterol deposition in coronaries and grafts
*   **β-blocker:** Reduces LV work (contractility, afterload)
*   **ACEI:** Reduces afterload, promotes LV reverse remodelling
