## <span class="t-red">C</span>hapter <span class="ov-gold">1</span>: <span class="t-green">History of Cardiac Surgery</span>

<div class="details-content">


<div class="admonition admonition-tip">
  <div class="admonition-title">Origins and Trauma</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">
<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">First Heart Wound Surgeries:</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

 The field began with the repair of cardiac wounds. 
- **Dr. Daniel Hale Williams** (1893) performed the first successful documented surgery involving the pericardium and heart, though he did not suture the heart itself. 
- **Dr. Ludwig Rehn** (1896) performed the first successful suture of a human heart wound.
  </div>
</div>


<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">World War II Advancements:</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">
    
- **Dr. Dwight Harken** demonstrated the feasibility of cardiac surgery by successfully removing 134 missiles from the mediastinum and cardiac chambers during WWII without a single death.
  </div>
</div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-tip">
  <div class="admonition-title">Development of Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB)</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">
<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">The Heart-Lung Machine</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

**The invention of the heart-lung machine** was the pivotal breakthrough enabling open intracardiac surgery. Key contributors include **John Gibbon**, who performed the first successful open-heart surgery using a CPB machine on May 6, 1953, to close an atrial septal defect.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Pioneering Programs</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">
    
- **C. Walton Lillehei** used controlled cross-circulation (using a parent as a “heart-lung machine”) in 1954 to successfully repair ventricular septal defects. 
- **John Kirklin** at the Mayo Clinic established the first successful series of patients using a mechanical heart-lung machine in 1955.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Myocardial Protection</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

**The development of cardioplegia** was crucial for protecting the heart during surgery. While early solutions were toxic, the reintroduction of hypothermic blood cardioplegia became the standard for myocardial preservation.
  </div>
</div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-info">
  <div class="admonition-title">Congenital Heart Surgery</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

*   **Palliative Procedures:** Early successes in congenital heart disease were achieved with palliative shunts. The **Blalock-Taussig shunt** (1945) for tetralogy of Fallot was a landmark procedure that dramatically improved outcomes for “blue babies.”
*   **Corrective Repairs:** With the advent of CPB, surgeons began performing definitive repairs for a wide range of congenital defects, including atrial and ventricular septal defects, tetralogy of Fallot, and transposition of the great arteries.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-tip">
  <div class="admonition-title">Valvular Heart Surgery</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">
    
<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Closed and Open Techniques:</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

Early attempts at valvuloplasty were performed with blind techniques. The development of CPB allowed for direct vision repair or replacement. The **Starr-Edwards caged-ball valve** became the first successful prosthetic valve, establishing a standard for mechanical valves.
  </div>
</div>
<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Bioprostheses and Repair</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

- Tissue valves, initially from homografts and later from porcine xenografts fixed with glutaraldehyde, were developed to avoid the need for lifelong anticoagulation. 
- Concurrently, techniques for mitral valve repair, refined by **Alain Carpentier**, became a highly successful alternative to replacement.
  </div>
</div>

  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-tip">
  <div class="admonition-title">Coronary Artery Surgery</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Direct Revascularization</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

- The development of <em>selective coronary angiography</em> by **Mason Sones** enabled the precise diagnosis of coronary obstructions. 
- The first clearly documented coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) was performed by **Robert Goetz** in 1960.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Establishment of CABG</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">
    
**Rene Favaloro** popularized the use of the saphenous vein graft, while **V. I. Kolessov** and **George Green** championed the internal mammary artery graft. By 1969, **W. Dudley Johnson** had refined the techniques, establishing CABG as a mainstream treatment for coronary artery disease.
  </div>
</div>

  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-icu">
  <div class="admonition-title">Arrhythmia and Pacemaker Development</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Surgical Ablation</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">
    
The **Cox Maze procedure**, developed by **James Cox**, became the gold standard for the surgical treatment of atrial fibrillation by creating a maze of incisions to direct electrical signals.
  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Pacing and Defibrillation</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">
    
- **Paul Zoll** pioneered external cardiac pacing in 1952. 
- **C. Walton Lillehei** advanced the field with direct myocardial pacing. 
- The first totally implantable pacemaker was developed by **Elmquist, Senning**, and **Chardack**. 
- **Morton Mirowski** developed the implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) to prevent sudden cardiac death.
  </div>
</div>

  </div>
</div>

<div class="admonition admonition-surgery">

  <div class="admonition-content">

<div class="admonition admonition-note">
  <div class="admonition-title">Heart Transplantation</div>
  <div class="admonition-content">

    
The first human-to-human heart transplant was performed by **Christiaan Barnard** in 1967. While initial outcomes were poor, the development of better immunosuppressive drugs like **cyclosporine** led to the establishment of heart transplantation as a standard therapy for end-stage heart failure.
  </div>
</div>


<div class="admonition admonition-note">

The development of the intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP), ventricular assist devices (VADs), and the total artificial heart provided essential support for patients in cardiogenic shock, often as a bridge to transplantation.

## Chapter 2: Surgical Anatomy of the Heart
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Location and Spatial Relationships

*   **Shape and position:** Heart is a three-sided pyramid in the middle mediastinum. One-third lies right of midline, two-thirds left. Long axis: left epigastrium to right shoulder. Short axis (atrioventricular groove plane): closer to vertical than horizontal.
*   **Surfaces:** Acute margin (inferior, sharp angle between sternocostal and diaphragmatic surfaces), obtuse margin (superior, diffuse), and unnamed posterior margin.
*   **Relations:** Anterior: sternum and costal cartilages (ribs 3–5). Lateral: lungs (right lung reaches midline; left lung has cardiac notch). Posterior: esophagus, tracheal bifurcation, bronchi. Inferior: diaphragm.
*   **Pericardium:** Visceral epicardium encases heart; parietal pericardium lines fibrous sac. Transverse sinus (between aorta/pulmonary trunk posteriorly and interatrial groove anteriorly). Oblique sinus (cul-de-sac behind left atrium).
*   **Mediastinal nerves:**
    *   **Phrenic nerve:** Anterior scalene → posterior to internal thoracic artery at thoracic inlet. Right: lateral SVC; left: lateral to left-sided SVC if present. Vulnerable during IMA dissection and venous cannulation.
    *   **Vagus nerve:** Posterior to phrenic. Right: gives recurrent laryngeal around right subclavian. Left: crosses aortic arch, gives recurrent laryngeal around arterial ligament.

### Surgical Incisions

*   **Median sternotomy:** Most common approach. Jugular notch to xiphoid. Extensions: neck for aortic arch exposure; third intercostal space for proximal descending aorta.
*   **Clamshell (bilateral transverse thoracosternotomy):** Fourth or fifth intercostal space. Bilateral submammary incision with transverse sternal division. Used for bilateral lung transplants, heart-lung transplants, aortic arch/descending aorta.
*   **Anterolateral thoracotomy:** Right side for tricuspid/mitral valves, right coronary artery. Left side for circumflex bypass or mitral valve exposure.
*   **Posterolateral thoracotomy:** Left side for distal aortic arch and descending thoracic aorta; requires femoral cannulation for CPB.

### Atrial Anatomy

*   **Right atrium:** Three parts: appendage (pectinate muscles), vestibule (smooth wall inserting into tricuspid valve), venous component (receives SVC, IVC, coronary sinus). Terminal groove marks junction of appendage and venous component.
*   **Sinus node:** Located at anterior-superior terminal groove (SVC-atrial appendage junction). Blood supply: RCA (55%) or circumflex (45%). Nodal artery courses along anterior interatrial groove; variable at cavoatrial junction.
*   **Atrial septum:** True septum confined to oval fossa. Superior rim (septum secundum) is infolding between right atrium and right pulmonary veins. Inferior rim continuous with sinus septum (separates IVC and coronary sinus).
*   **Triangle of Koch:** Borders: tendon of Todaro, septal leaflet of tricuspid valve, coronary sinus orifice. Contains atrioventricular node and conduction axis. Apex marks penetration of His bundle.
*   **Left atrium:** Appendage (pectinate muscles confined within), vestibule (continuous with mural leaflet of mitral valve), venous component (receives four pulmonary veins). Approaches: interatrial groove (right of and parallel to), dome (between atrial appendages), or trans-septal via right atrium.

### Ventricular and Valvular Anatomy

*   **Tricuspid valve:** Three leaflets (septal, anterosuperior, inferior). No discrete collagenous annulus; separated from atrial/ventricular myocardium by fibrofatty tissue of atrioventricular groove. Entire parietal attachment encircled by right coronary artery.
*   **Mitral valve:** Two leaflets: aortic (anterior, short, square, in fibrous continuity with aortic valve) and mural (posterior, shallow, two-thirds of circumference). Dense collagenous annulus. Circumflex artery adjacent to left half; coronary sinus adjacent to right half. Dominant circumflex may course extensively within left atrioventricular groove.
*   **Right ventricle:** Three components: inlet (tricuspid valve), apical trabecular (thin wall, vulnerable to perforation), outlet (infundibulum, supports pulmonary valve). Supraventricular crest separates tricuspid and pulmonary valves.
*   **Pulmonary valve:** Semilunar, no discrete annulus. Three rings: sinotubular ridge, ventriculoarterial junction, basal attachments to infundibulum. Semilunar attachments cross musculoarterial junction.
*   **Left ventricle:** Inlet (mitral valve), apical trabecular (fine trabeculations), outlet (supports aortic valve). Left bundle enters outflow tract posterior to membranous septum.
*   **Aortic valve:** Semilunar, central position related to all cardiac chambers. Fibrous continuity with mitral valve (aortomitral curtain). Noncoronary leaflet related to right atrium; right coronary leaflet related to central fibrous body. Konno-Rastan procedure: incision through commissure between right and left coronary leaflets; enlarges aortic root via ventricular septal defect patch.

### Coronary Arteries

*   **Right dominance (85–90%):** RCA supplies inferior interventricular artery (posterior descending).
*   **Left main coronary artery:** 10–20 mm length. Divides into left anterior descending (LAD) and circumflex.
*   **LAD:** Anterior interventricular groove to apex. Branches: diagonals (anterolateral LV), septal perforators (anterior 2/3 septum, perpendicular orientation is angiographic marker), right ventricular branches.
*   **Circumflex:** Left atrioventricular groove. Branches: obtuse marginals (lateral LV, posteromedial papillary muscle). Supplies sinus node in 40–50%; atrioventricular node in left dominance.
*   **RCA:** Right atrioventricular groove, acute margin, crux (U-turn). Branches: acute marginals (anterior RV), inferior interventricular artery (inferior septum), right posterolateral artery (posterior LV). Supplies sinus node in 50–60%; atrioventricular node in right dominance.

### Coronary Veins

*   **Coronary sinus:** Drains 85% of coronary venous blood. Lies in posterior atrioventricular groove; empties into right atrium at lateral border of triangle of Koch.
*   **Tributaries:** Great cardiac vein (anterior interventricular vein → left atrioventricular groove), middle cardiac vein (inferior interventricular groove), small cardiac vein (right atrioventricular groove).
*   **Anterior right ventricular veins:** Drain directly into right atrium or coalesce into small cardiac vein.
*   **Thebesian veins:** Small tributaries draining directly into cardiac chambers (primarily right atrium and ventricle).

## Chapter 3: Cardiac Surgical Physiology
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Cellular Activation & Action Potential
*   **Resting Membrane Potential:** Determined mainly by K+ gradient (via Na<sup>+</sup>/K<sup>+</sup> ATPase). Typical value ~ -90 mV.
*   **Phases (Myocyte):**
    *   **Phase 0 (Depolarization):** Fast Na<sup>+</sup> channels open (inward current).
    *   **Phase 1 (Early repolarization):** Transient K<sup>+</sup> current.
    *   **Phase 2 (Plateau):** L-type Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels open (inward Ca<sup>2+</sup> sustains contraction). Inactivation gates close slowly.
    *   **Phase 3 (Repolarization):** Ca<sup>2+</sup> channels close; delayed rectifier K<sup>+</sup> channels open.
    *   **Phase 4 (Resting):** Maintained by K<sup>+</sup> channels (iK<sub>1</sub>).
*   **Pacemaker Cells (SA/AV Node):** No fast Na<sup>+</sup> channels. Phase 4 shows spontaneous diastolic depolarization (pacemaker potential) driven by funny current (If) and Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx.
*   **Refractory Periods:**
    *   **Absolute:** No new action potential possible (Na<sup>+</sup> channels inactivated).
    *   **Relative:** Only strong stimuli can excite (some Na<sup>+</sup> channels recovered).

### Key Ion Channels & Pumps
*   **Na<sup>+</sup>/K<sup>+</sup> ATPase:** Pumps 3 Na<sup>+</sup> out, 2 K<sup>+</sup> in; contributes -10 mV to resting potential.
*   **Na<sup>+</sup>/Ca<sup>2+</sup> Exchanger (NCX):** Primary Ca<sup>2+</sup> extrusion mechanism (3 Na<sup>+</sup> in / 1 Ca<sup>2+</sup> out). Can reverse in ischemia (Ca<sup>2+</sup> overload).
*   **Ryanodine Receptor (RyR):** SR Ca<sup>2+</sup> release channel; activated by Ca<sup>2+</sup> influx ("calcium-induced calcium release").
*   **SERCA:** SR Ca<sup>2+</sup> uptake pump; regulated by **Phospholamban** (inhibits; phosphorylation by PKA relieves inhibition → lusitropy).

### Regulation of Function
*   **Beta-1 Receptors:** Gs → ↑ cAMP → PKA → ↑ Ca<sup>2+</sup> channel opening, ↑ SR Ca<sup>2+</sup> release, ↑ SERCA activity. Results in:
    *   Positive **chronotropy** (HR)
    *   Positive **inotropy** (contractility)
    *   Positive **dromotropy** (conduction velocity)
    *   Positive **lusitropy** (relaxation)
*   **Beta-Blockers:** Reduce MvO<sub>2</sub>; cause receptor upregulation.
*   **Phosphodiesterase Inhibitors (Milrinone):** ↓ cAMP breakdown → ↑ inotropy + vasodilation (inodilator); synergistic with beta-agonists.
*   **Adenosine:** A1 receptor → ↓ cAMP, opens K+ channels (hyperpolarization); used to slow AV conduction.
*   **Acetylcholine (Parasympathetic):** Gi → ↓ cAMP; opens K+ channels → hyperpolarization (slows HR).
*   **Acidosis:** ↓ Ca<sup>2+</sup> release, ↓ myofilament Ca<sup>2+</sup> sensitivity, desensitizes beta-receptors → ↓ contractility.
*   **Other Regulators:** Angiotensin II (vasoconstriction, aldosterone), NO (vasodilation, regulates Ca<sup>2+</sup> handling), Endothelin-1 (vasoconstriction).

### The Sarcomere & Contraction
*   **Sliding Filament Theory:** Ca<sup>2+</sup> binds to **TnC** → tropomyosin shifts → exposes actin binding site → myosin head forms cross-bridge (ATPase activated) → power stroke.
*   **Length-Tension Relationship (Frank-Starling):** ↑ Sarcomere length (up to 2.2-2.4 μm) → ↑ Ca<sup>2+</sup> sensitivity of TnC → ↑ force. Best clinical correlate: ↑ Preload → ↑ Stroke Volume.

### Ventricular Mechanics (Pressure-Volume Loops)
*   **Key Loops Points:**
    *   **Aortic Valve Opens:** End of isovolumic contraction.
    *   **Aortic Valve Closes:** End-systole (dicrotic notch).
    *   **Mitral Valve Opens:** Start of filling.
*   **ESPVR (End-Systolic Pressure-Volume Relationship):**
    *   **Slope (Ees):** Gold standard index of **contractility**. ↑ Slope = ↑ Inotropy.
*   **EDPVR (End-Diastolic Pressure-Volume Relationship):**
    *   **Slope:** Index of **compliance** (lusitropy). ↑ Slope = Stiffer ventricle.
*   **Preload Recruitable Stroke Work (PRSW):** Load-independent contractility index (stroke work vs. EDV).

### Preload, Afterload, Compliance
*   **Preload:** End-diastolic volume (surrogate: filling pressure). Determined by venous return and atrial systole.
*   **Afterload:** Ventricular wall stress during ejection. Simplified as: **Wall Stress (σ) = (P × r) / (2 × w)** (Laplace). ↑ Radius or ↑ Pressure → ↑ Wall Stress → ↑ MvO<sub>2</sub>.
*   **Compliance (dV/dP):** Stiffness (dP/dV) is increased in hypertrophy, fibrosis, ischemia, tamponade.

### Coronary Blood Flow
*   **Left Coronary Flow:** **Phasic** (mostly in diastole) due to systolic compression.
*   **Subendocardium at Risk:** Highest wall tension, perfused only in diastole. Vulnerable to hypotension, tachycardia (↓ diastole), high LVEDP (compression).
*   **Autoregulation Plateau:** Flow constant between MAP ~60-120 mmHg.
*   **Poiseuille's Law (Stenosis):** Resistance ∝ **1 / r⁴**. 90% stenosis → resistance **256x** > 60% stenosis.
*   **Coronary Flow Reserve:** Maximal increase in flow (3-4x normal).

### Myocardial Protection & Injury
*   **Stunning:** Post-ischemic dysfunction despite restored flow; weeks to recover (↓ myofilament Ca<sup>2+</sup> sensitivity).
*   **Hibernation:** Chronic hypoperfusion → downregulation; reversible with revascularization.
*   **Preconditioning:** Brief ischemia protects against subsequent prolonged ischemia.
*   **Reperfusion Injury:** Ca<sup>2+</sup> overload, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, no-reflow.

### Heart Failure Physiology
*   **Systolic Failure:** ↓ Ees; LV dilates (right shift on PV loop); EF ↓.
*   **Diastolic Failure:** ↑ EDPVR (stiff); high filling pressures; preserved EF.
*   **Maladaptive Responses:** Chronic neurohumoral activation (RAAS, SNS) → fibrosis, remodeling, beta-receptor downregulation, arrhythmias.
*   **BNP:** Secreted in response to stretch; diagnostic marker.

## Chapter 4: Cardiac Surgical Pharmacology
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Cellular Pharmacology Overview
*   **Intracellular calcium** is the central mediator of both myocardial contractility (via troponin C) and vascular smooth muscle tone (via calmodulin).
*   **cAMP**: Increases intracellular Ca²⁺ in myocytes (↑ inotropy, ↑ chronotropy) but decreases Ca²⁺ in vascular smooth muscle (vasodilation).
*   **cGMP**: Decreases Ca²⁺ availability in vascular smooth muscle → vasodilation; stimulated by nitric oxide (nitrates, nitroprusside).

### Antiarrhythmics (Vaughan Williams Classification)
| Class | Mechanism | Key Drugs | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **I** | Na⁺ channel blockade | Lidocaine (Ib), Procainamide (Ia) | Ib: little effect on normal tissue, slows conduction in ischemic tissue |
| **II** | β-blockade | Metoprolol, Esmolol | Decrease HR, contractility, AV conduction |
| **III** | K⁺ channel blockade | Amiodarone, Sotalol | Prolong action potential duration, refractory period |
| **IV** | Ca²⁺ channel blockade | Diltiazem, Verapamil | AV nodal depression; negative inotropes |

#### Amiodarone (Key Drug)
*   **Properties**: All 4 Vaughan Williams classes; noncompetitive antiadrenergic; highly lipophilic.
*   **Pharmacokinetics**: Vd ~60 L/kg; elimination half-life 40–60 days; slow onset (weeks for oral).
*   **IV Dosing (loading)**: 150 mg over 10 min → 60 mg/h × 6h → 30 mg/h.
*   **Oral Dosing**: 800–1600 mg/d × 10d → 600–800 mg/d × 4–6 wk → 200–600 mg/d maintenance.
*   **Side effects** (chronic): Pulmonary toxicity (most serious), bradycardia, hypotension.
*   **Efficacy**:
    *   **ARREST trial**: Amiodarone 300 mg IV improved survival to hospital admission in shock-resistant VF (44% vs 34% placebo).
    *   **Dorian trial**: Amiodarone superior to lidocaine for survival to admission in out-of-hospital VF (22.8% vs 12.0%).
    *   **Prophylaxis for post-op AF**: Reduces incidence (25–35% vs 47–53% placebo); may reduce hospital stay.

#### Atrial Fibrillation (Post-Cardiac Surgery)
*   **Risk factors**: Advanced age, prior AF, valvular surgery.
*   **Prophylaxis**: β-blockers (first-line). Amiodarone for high-risk patients.
*   **Acute rate control**: IV amiodarone, diltiazem, or β-blockers.

### Inotropic Agents
| Agent | Mechanism | Hemodynamic Effects | Key Points |
|-------|-----------|---------------------|------------|
| **Calcium** | Direct Ca²⁺ entry | ↑ contractility, ↑ SVR, impairs diastole | Routine use post-CPB not recommended; may blunt epinephrine response |
| **Epinephrine** | β₁ + β₂ + α | ↑ HR, ↑ contractility, vasoconstriction (high dose) | Potent inotrope; may impair splanchnic perfusion |
| **Norepinephrine** | α + β₁ | Vasoconstriction, ↑ contractility | First-line for vasodilatory shock |
| **Dopamine** | D₁ (low), β₁ (mid), α (high) | Dose-dependent: renal vasodilation → inotropy → vasoconstriction | "Renal dose" dopamine not evidence-based for renal protection |
| **Dobutamine** | β₁ >> β₂ | ↑ contractility, mild vasodilation | Increases HR; may increase coronary flow proportionally to MvO₂ |
| **Milrinone** | PDE-III inhibitor | Inotropy + vasodilation (inodilator) | Effective when catecholamines fail; load over 15–30 min to avoid hypotension |
| **Levosimendan** | Ca²⁺ sensitizer + PDE-III + KATP | ↑ contractility, vasodilation, no ↑ MvO₂ | Approved in Europe; under study in US for cardiac surgery |

#### Clinical Trial Data
*   **Feneck**: Dobutamine and milrinone equally effective for low-output syndrome post-CABG; combination may be superior.
*   **LIDO study**: Levosimendan showed mortality benefit over dobutamine in severe low-output failure.

### Vasopressors (Vasodilatory Shock)
*   **Mechanisms of vasodilation**:
    *   **Nitric oxide**: Activates guanylate cyclase → ↑ cGMP → vasodilation.
    *   **KATP channels**: Activated by ↓ ATP, acidosis, lactate → hyperpolarization → ↓ Ca²⁺ influx.
    *   **Vasopressin deficiency**: Common after CPB.
*   **Treatment algorithm**:
    1.  Norepinephrine (first-line).
    2.  Add vasopressin (0.01–0.1 U/min) if norepinephrine inadequate.
*   **Argenziano study**: Post-CPB vasodilatory shock patients had inappropriately low AVP levels; low-dose vasopressin restored BP and reduced norepinephrine requirements.

### Vasodilators
| Drug Class | Mechanism | Key Uses |
|------------|-----------|----------|
| **Nitroglycerin** | NO donor (requires metabolic conversion) | Coronary vasodilation; no coronary steal; venodilation |
| **Nitroprusside** | Direct NO donor | Arterial + venous vasodilation; reflex tachycardia; cyanide toxicity with prolonged use |
| **Clevidipine** | Dihydropyridine Ca²⁺ blocker | Arterial vasodilation; ultra-short acting; titratable |
| **Nicardipine** | Dihydropyridine Ca²⁺ blocker | Arterial vasodilation; longer duration |
| **Milrinone** | PDE-III inhibitor | Inodilator; pulmonary vasodilation |
| **Sildenafil** | PDE-V inhibitor | Pulmonary vasodilation; no inotropic effect |

### Beta-Adrenergic Receptor Blockers
*   **Mechanism**: Competitive inhibition at β-receptors; effects depend on basal sympathetic tone.
*   **Key Drugs**:
    *   **Cardioselective (β₁)**: Metoprolol, Atenolol, Esmolol (IV, ultra-short acting).
    *   **Nonselective (β₁ + β₂)**: Propranolol.
    *   **Mixed (α₁ + β)**: Labetalol (α:β = 1:3 oral, 1:7 IV), Carvedilol (1:10).
*   **Indications**: Hypertension, angina, post-MI, HF (carvedilol, metoprolol, bisoprolol), supraventricular tachycardia, aortic dissection.
*   **Toxicity**: Bradycardia, hypotension, AV block, bronchospasm (β₂ blockade). Treatment: atropine, catecholamines.
*   **Perioperative management**: Continue β-blockers perioperatively; abrupt withdrawal increases risk of ischemia.

### Diuretics
| Class | Site of Action | Mechanism | Key Drugs |
|-------|----------------|-----------|-----------|
| **Loop** | Thick ascending loop of Henle | Inhibit Na⁺-K⁺-2Cl⁻ cotransport | Furosemide, Bumetanide |
| **Thiazide** | Distal convoluted tubule | Inhibit Na⁺-Cl⁻ cotransport | Hydrochlorothiazide |
| **Potassium-sparing** | Collecting duct | Aldosterone antagonist (spironolactone) or Na⁺ channel block (amiloride, triamterene) | Spironolactone, Amiloride |
| **Osmotic** | Proximal tubule | Osmotic retention of water | Mannitol |
| **Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor** | Proximal tubule | Inhibit Na⁺-H⁺ exchange | Acetazolamide (for metabolic alkalosis) |

#### Key Points
*   **Furosemide dosing**: Highly variable; start with 5–10 mg IV in elderly/renal impairment; up to 40–80 mg.
*   **Mannitol**: Used in CPB prime (25–50 g); increases renal blood flow; risk of volume overload.
*   **Acetazolamide**: Treats metabolic alkalosis from prolonged diuretic use.

### Airway Management Pharmacology
#### Drugs to Facilitate Intubation
| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| **Propofol** | 1–2 mg/kg (titrate) | Hypotension from vasodilation |
| **Etomidate** | 0.15–0.3 mg/kg | Minimal cardiovascular effects; inhibits steroid synthesis |
| **Succinylcholine** | 1 mg/kg | Onset 30–60 sec; causes K⁺ release; avoid in hyperkalemia |
| **Rocuronium** | 0.5–1 mg/kg | Onset 60–90 sec; no cardiovascular effects |

#### Sedation for Mechanical Ventilation
*   **Propofol**: 25–75 μg/kg/min; rapid awakening; vasodilation.
*   **Dexmedetomidine (α₂ agonist)**: 0.2–0.7 μg/kg/h; preserves respiratory drive; arousable sedation.
*   **Midazolam**: 1–4 mg/h; amnestic; less predictable awakening.
*   **Fentanyl**: 1–4 μg/kg/h; analgesic; blunts airway reflexes.

#### Bronchospasm Treatment
*   **Inhaled β₂ agonists**: Albuterol 2.5 mg/3 mL q4–6h.
*   **Ipratropium**: Anticholinergic; 0.5 mg/3 mL q4–6h.
*   **IV steroids**: For severe bronchospasm (dexamethasone 8 mg then 4 mg q6h).
*   **Heliox**: 80% helium/20% oxygen; reduces turbulent flow in upper airway obstruction.

#### Airway Edema
*   **Racemic epinephrine**: 2.5 mL of 1% solution via aerosol; vasoconstriction.
*   **Heliox**: Reduces work of breathing through narrowed airway.
*   **Dexamethasone**: 8 mg IV then 4 mg q6h × 4–8 doses.

## Chapter 5: Cardiovascular Pathology
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Myocardial Hypertrophy & Remodeling
*   **Pressure overload** (hypertension, AS): **Concentric hypertrophy** — increased wall thickness, no dilation.
*   **Volume overload** (regurgitation, DCM): **Eccentric hypertrophy** — chamber dilation, increased radius.
*   **Ventricular remodeling:** Regional (post-MI) or global changes; at cellular level: pressure → parallel sarcomere addition (widening); volume → parallel + series addition (widening + lengthening).
*   **Deleterious mechanisms:** Ischemia vulnerability (inadequate vascular proliferation), fibrosis, impaired energetics, reduced adrenergic responsiveness, apoptosis.
*   **Hypertrophy** is an independent risk factor for sudden death and cardiac mortality.

### Cardiomyopathies
| Type | Key Features | Genetics/Notes |
|------|--------------|----------------|
| **Dilated (DCM)** | 4-chamber dilation; cardiomegaly 2–3× normal; systolic dysfunction | 25–50% familial; cytoskeletal/sarcomeric gene mutations |
| **Hypertrophic (HCM)** | Massive hypertrophy, no dilation; asymmetric septal hypertrophy (septum/free wall ratio >1.3); myocyte diameter >40 μm (normal 15–20 μm) | >1500 mutations in ≥11 sarcomeric genes (β-MHC, MyBP-C most common); complications: AF, sudden death, LVOT obstruction (SAM of mitral valve) |
| **Restrictive (RCM)** | Heart failure with preserved EF; biatrial dilation | Causes: amyloidosis, Fabry's, postirradiation, eosinophilic endomyocardial disease |
| **ARVC** | Dilated, thinned RV; fatty infiltration, myocyte loss, fibrosis; right-sided failure; sudden death in athletes | Mutations in cell-adhesion genes (desmosomal) |

### Coronary Artery Disease & Atherosclerosis
*   **Pathogenesis:** Endothelial dysfunction → monocyte adhesion, smooth muscle proliferation, lipid accumulation, foam cells, ECM production, calcification.
*   **Endothelial dysfunction causes:** ↓ NO, ↑ lipoprotein permeability, prothrombotic surface, leukocyte adhesion.
*   **Vulnerable plaque:** Thin fibrous cap, abundant macrophages (MMP production), large necrotic lipid core — prone to rupture even at <50% stenosis.
*   **Hemodynamic stenosis:** 75% luminal area reduction limits exertion; 90% limits resting flow.
*   **Acute plaque rupture** is the key mechanism triggering ACS.

### Ischemic Myocardial Injury (Timeline)
| Event | Time |
|-------|------|
| Anaerobic metabolism | Seconds |
| Loss of contractility | <2 min |
| ATP 50% | 10 min |
| ATP 10% | 40 min |
| Irreversible cell injury | 20–40 min |
| Microvascular injury | >1 h |

| Pathologic finding | Onset |
|-------------------|-------|
| Wavy fibers | 1–3 h |
| TTC staining defect | 2–3 h |
| Histologic necrosis | 6–12 h |
| Gross changes | 12–24 h |

*   **Wavefront phenomenon:** Necrosis begins in subendocardium, progresses outward; 50% of area at risk necrotic by 3–4 h; transmural extent complete by 6–12 h.
*   **Healing:** PMNs 6–12 h → macrophage peak 3–5 days → collagen begins 7–10 days → dense scar by 6–8 weeks.

### Reperfusion, Stunning, Hibernation, Preconditioning
*   **Reperfusion window:** Irreversible injury ~20 min; salvage possible ≤3–4 h; clinical goal **door-to-balloon 90 min**.
*   **Reperfusion injury:** Contraction bands (Ca²⁺ overload), hemorrhage, no-reflow, arrhythmias.
*   **Myocardial stunning:** Reversible dysfunction hours–days despite restored flow; seen after PCI, CPB, unstable angina.
*   **Myocardial hibernation:** Chronic dysfunction with reduced flow; viable myocardium; reversible with revascularization.
*   **Ischemic preconditioning:** Brief ischemia protects against subsequent prolonged ischemia.

### Myocardial Infarction Complications
*   **Transmural (Q-wave) MI:** Full-thickness necrosis from plaque rupture + thrombosis.
*   **Subendocardial (non-Q-wave) MI:** Inner 1/3–1/2 of wall from global ischemia or interrupted wavefront.
*   **Mortality:** Declined from ~30% (1950s) to ≤7% today; 50% of MI deaths occur within first hour.
*   **Cardiac rupture syndromes (post-MI):**
    1.  **Free wall rupture** (most common) → tamponade
    2.  **Ventricular septal rupture** → acquired VSD
    3.  **Papillary muscle rupture** → acute severe MR
    *   Timing: most within 24 h – 5 days post-MI.
*   **Infarct extension:** New necrosis in same distribution.
*   **Infarct expansion:** Disproportionate thinning/dilation without new necrosis → aneurysm substrate.
*   **Cardiogenic shock:** Indicates large infarct (>40% LV).

### Revascularization
*   **PTCA mechanism:** Plaque fracture (dominant), compression, vessel wall expansion.
*   **Restenosis:** 30–50% at 4–6 months; driven by smooth muscle proliferation and ECM production.
*   **Stents:**
    *   **BMS:** In-stent restenosis 50% at 6 months.
    *   **DES:** Sirolimus/paclitaxel reduce restenosis; risk of late stent thrombosis requiring prolonged DAPT.
    *   **RBS:** Biodegradable; under investigation.
*   **CABG:** Improves survival in left main CAD, 3-vessel disease, reduced LV function. Hospital mortality ~1% (low-risk).
*   **Saphenous vein graft patency:** ~60% at 10 years. Failure mechanisms: early thrombosis → intimal hyperplasia (1 mo–1 yr) → atherosclerosis (>1–3 yr).
*   **IMA graft patency:** >90% at 10 years; superior due to minimal preexisting disease, artery-to-artery anastomosis, preserved nutrient supply.

### Valvular Heart Disease
#### Calcific Aortic Stenosis
*   Most common valvular disease in Western countries; prevalence ~3% after age 75.
*   **Morphology:** Calcified masses in cuspal fibrosa; bicuspid valves present earlier (6th–7th decade) vs tricuspid (7th–9th decade).
*   **Pathogenesis:** Active process (inflammation, lipid infiltration, osteoblastic VIC phenotype), not passive degeneration.
*   **Clinical threshold:** Symptoms (angina, syncope, HF) herald poor prognosis; AVR indicated.

#### Mitral Valve Prolapse
*   Prevalence ~2%; most common indication for mitral repair/replacement.
*   **Anatomic changes:** Intercordal hooding, leaflet thickening, annular dilation (posterior leaflet predominance).
*   **Histology:** Myxomatous degeneration (proteoglycan accumulation in spongiosa).
*   **Complications:** Progressive HF, endocarditis, thromboembolism, sudden death, AF.

#### Infective Endocarditis
| Feature | Acute | Subacute |
|---------|-------|----------|
| Organism | *S. aureus* | *S. viridans* |
| Valve | Often normal | Preexisting disease |
| Course | Days–weeks | Weeks–months |
| Pathology | Destructive, ulcerative | Indolent, vegetations |

*   **Duke criteria:** Standardized diagnosis integrating blood cultures, echo, clinical findings.
*   **Complications:** Valvular insufficiency, ring abscess, fistulae, embolization.

#### Prosthetic Valve Complications
| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| Thromboembolism | Thrombosis, systemic emboli; mechanical valves require lifelong anticoagulation |
| Infection | Prosthetic valve endocarditis (early: *S. epidermidis*; late: *S. viridans*, *S. aureus*) |
| Structural dysfunction | Mechanical: fracture (Bjork-Shiley); Bioprosthetic: calcification (50% fail by 15–20 yr) |
| Nonstructural | Paravalvular leak, pannus overgrowth, suture impingement |

### Cardiac Transplantation
*   **Indications:** Idiopathic DCM (50%), end-stage ischemic heart disease (40%).
*   **Survival:** 1-year ~90%, 5-year ~70%.
*   **Rejection grading (ISHLT 2004):**
    *   0R: None
    *   1R: Mild (focal lymphocytic infiltrate ± 1 focus myocyte damage)
    *   2R: Moderate (multifocal infiltrate + myocyte damage)
    *   3R: Severe (diffuse infiltrate + myocyte damage ± vascular injury)
*   **Antibody-mediated rejection (AMR):** pAMR grading based on histology + C4d staining.
*   **Graft vasculopathy:** Concentric intimal proliferation; affects epicardial + intramural vessels; leading cause of late mortality; distinct from atherosclerosis (diffuse, concentric, immunologic).
*   **Posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD):** ~2% of recipients; EBV-associated B-cell proliferation; reduced immunosuppression is first-line therapy.

### Cardiac Assist Devices
*   **Complications:**
    *   **Hemorrhage:** Perioperative coagulopathy, hepatic dysfunction, CPB effects.
    *   **Thrombosis:** Pump thrombosis (HeartMate II incidence increasing); requires anticoagulation.
    *   **Infection:** Driveline infections; biofilm-forming organisms (*S. epidermidis*, *S. aureus*).
    *   **Structural:** Inflow obstruction from reverse remodeling, allosensitization.
*   **Reverse remodeling:** LVAD support may allow myocardial recovery; subset of patients weaned (bridge to recovery).

### Arrhythmias: Ablation & Devices
*   **RF ablation:** Produces coagulation necrosis with contraction bands; healing proceeds as infarct (neutrophils → macrophages → granulation → scar).
*   **Pacemaker/ICD complications:** Lead displacement, perforation, infection, lead fracture, device malfunction.
*   **Fibrosis at electrode tip** determines stimulus threshold; corticosteroids at tip reduce fibrosis, extend battery life.

### Cardiac Tumors
| Tumor | Features |
|-------|----------|
| **Myxoma** | Most common primary; left atrium (80%); pedunculated, can cause obstruction; Carney complex (PRKAR1α mutation) |
| **Papillary fibroelastoma** | Valve surface (aortic > mitral); sea anemone-like; embolic source |
| **Lipoma** | Mature adipocytes; lipomatous hypertrophy of IAS (nonneoplastic) |
| **Rhabdomyoma** | Most common in children; associated with tuberous sclerosis; spider cells |
| **Angiosarcoma** | Most common malignant; right atrium; rapidly progressive |

### Biomaterials & Tissue Engineering
*   **Biomaterial-tissue interactions:**
    *   Blood-surface: thrombosis, thromboembolism (Virchow's triad)
    *   Foreign body reaction: fibrous encapsulation, chronic inflammation
    *   Healing: pannus ingrowth from anastomoses; intimal hyperplasia (SMC migration/proliferation)
*   **Device infection:** Biofilm formation (S. epidermidis, S. aureus); often requires device removal.
*   **Tissue engineering applications:**
    *   Vascular grafts: cell-seeded biodegradable scaffolds; pulsatile conditioning improves properties
    *   Myocardial regeneration: stem cells (resident cardiac, bone marrow); paracrine effects may predominate
    *   TEHV: living valves from autologous cells; decellularized scaffolds; challenges include immune response, durability, translation

## Chapter 6: Computed Tomography of the Adult Cardiac Surgery Patient: Principles and Applications
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Technical Principles of Cardiac CT

*   **Temporal resolution:** The metric measuring imaging speed. For a single-source scanner, temporal resolution = one-half the gantry rotation time (typically 150 ms). Dual-source scanners achieve 75 ms by using two independent X-ray sources positioned 90° apart.
*   **ECG gating:** Simultaneous acquisition of ECG tracing and CT data enables reconstruction using only a short segment of the R-R interval. Prospective gating limits exposure to a small part of the cycle (usually mid-diastole near 75%), reducing radiation. Retrospective gating acquires full-cycle data for cine evaluation of valve and ventricular function.
*   **Beta-blockade:** Recommended for most coronary CT examinations to lower heart rate (<60 bpm), improving image quality. Dual-source scanners reduce dependence on heart rate control.
*   **Patient irradiation:** ECG-gated cardiac CT delivers higher radiation than other body parts. For patients with life expectancy <10-15 years, radiation risk is less relevant; for younger patients, prospective gating and current modulation should be used.
*   **Contrast administration:** Iodinated contrast delivered via dual-injection system with saline chaser. Proper timing ensures left heart and coronaries enhanced while right heart filled with saline to reduce artifacts.

### Native Coronary Artery Assessment

*   **Negative predictive value (NPV):** Coronary CTA with 64+ detector rows has very high NPV, effectively excluding coronary artery disease (CAD) in low- to intermediate-risk patients.
*   **Surgical application:** Particularly useful for noncoronary cardiac surgery (e.g., isolated mitral valve repair) to exclude significant CAD without arterial puncture.
*   **Stenosis definition:** Significant stenosis generally defined as >50% luminal narrowing by quantitative coronary angiography.

### Bypass Graft Imaging and Reoperative Surgery

*   **Graft patency:** Cardiac CT provides noninvasive assessment of graft patency with excellent sensitivity and specificity for occluded vs. patent grafts. Metallic surgical clips may cause artifact limiting evaluation of graft stenosis.
*   **Reoperative planning:** Preoperative CTA identifies relationships of LIMA grafts, right ventricle, and aorta to the sternum. Studies show modification of surgical strategy in 1 in 5 redo cardiac surgery patients based on CT findings.
*   **Critical finding:** Patent LIMA graft adherent to sternal table or right ventricle adherent to sternum mandates cardiopulmonary bypass prior to reentry.

### Valve Assessment and TAVR Planning

*   **Valve area:** Planimetric valve area by CT correlates well with echocardiography and MRI. Useful when echocardiography is technically poor or results are discrepant.
*   **Mechanical valves:** CT permits high-resolution functional evaluation, measurement of opening angles, and diagnosis of pannus in-growth without artifact.
*   **TAVR planning:** CT is the reference standard for aortic annulus measurement (area, dimensions), coronary ostia height, leaflet length, and iliofemoral access vessel assessment (diameter, calcification, tortuosity).

### Aorta and Great Vessels

*   **Cross-clamping:** Noncontrast CT best defines aortic calcification, identifying safe sites for cross-clamping and cannulation to avoid embolic stroke.
*   **Aortic aneurysm:** ECG-gated CT provides motion-free measurement of aortic root, ascending aorta, and arch involvement. 3D volume rendering defines pathology relative to valve, sinotubular junction, and coronary ostia.
*   **Aortic dissection:** CTA has nearly 100% sensitivity and specificity. ECG-gating defines proximal extent, relationship to coronary arteries, and aortic valve involvement.

### Heart Failure and Transplantation

*   **LVAD assessment:** CT superior to echocardiography for evaluating in-device thrombus, hemorrhage, cannula obstruction, and tamponade (IVC dilatation, RV compression, azygos reflux).
*   **Cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV):** CTA offers high NPV for excluding significant CAV in major coronary segments (>1.5 mm), reducing need for invasive angiography.
*   **Pacemaker/defibrillator:** ECG-gated CT identifies lead malposition and possible cardiac perforation.

### Pericardium and Cardiac Masses

*   **Constrictive pericarditis:** CT best demonstrates calcification extent, pericardial thickening, and effusion. 3D volume rendering aids preoperative planning for pericardial stripping.
*   **Cardiac masses:** CT evaluates extra-cardiac thoracic extension, calcification, and fat-containing lesions. MRI preferred for tissue characterization when not contraindicated.

## Chapter 7: Risk Assessment and Performance Improvement in Cardiac Surgery
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Foundational Concepts in Quality Assessment

*   **Donabedian framework:** Quality in health care is defined by improvement in patient status after accounting for severity of illness, comorbidity, and services received. Quality is measured across three domains: **structure** (facilities, personnel, volume), **process** (actions leading to outcomes, e.g., IMA use, antiplatelet therapy), and **outcome** (operative mortality, morbidity, patient-reported outcomes).
*   **Performance measures:** Quantitative entities indicating performance. Outcome measures (mortality, renal failure, stroke) are most important for patients but require risk adjustment. Process measures (beta-blockade at discharge, antibiotic prophylaxis) are actionable but may have weak links to outcomes. Structural measures (database participation, ICU facilities) are easily tabulated but often difficult to alter.
*   **Patient-reported outcomes (PRO):** Growing importance in elderly populations where symptom relief and quality of life, rather than cure, are goals. CABG provides excellent health-related quality of life 10–15 years postoperatively, even in patients >80 years.

### Statistical Tools and Risk Adjustment

*   **Basic statistics:** Three broad uses—recap (summarize data), relate (compare groups using t-tests, chi-square), and regress (multivariable analysis for risk adjustment).
*   **Logistic regression:** Most common method for predicting dichotomous outcomes (death, complications). Provides observed/expected (O/E) ratios for provider performance assessment.
*   **Kaplan-Meier method:** Estimates time-related survival probability. Log-rank statistic compares survival curves. Hazard is the instantaneous risk of an event; Cox proportional hazards regression identifies predictors of long-term survival and calculates hazard ratios.
*   **Comorbidity indices:** Charlson Index, CIRS, ICED, and Kaplan Index are validated measures of comorbidity, though none derived specifically from cardiac surgery populations.

### Major Risk Models and Databases

*   **STS Adult Cardiac Surgery Database:** Voluntary registry capturing >90% of US cardiac centers. Provides benchmark data, risk-adjusted outcomes, and supports 27 risk models for CABG, isolated valve, and combined procedures. NQF-endorsed measures include composite scores, risk-adjusted mortality for all major procedures, and process measures (IMA use, antiplatelet therapy, beta-blockade).
*   **Regional systems:** New York, Pennsylvania, Northern New England, EuroSCORE, VA, and Canadian risk models each use varying numbers of risk factors but show similar predictive capability—much predictive ability resides in a relatively small number of factors.
*   **Limitations:** Risk models predict average population outcomes but not individual patient outcomes. Even with perfect risk adjustment, random variation accounts for much observed mortality differences; sensitivity for detecting poor-quality providers is <20% with >50% predictive error for outliers.

### Study Designs and Evidence Hierarchy

*   **Randomized controlled trials (RCTs):** Gold standard for causal inference, championed by Archie Cochrane (father of evidence-based medicine). Advantages: absence of confounding and selection bias. Disadvantages: small, highly selected populations; costly; time-consuming.
*   **Observational studies:** Represent "real-world" patients with large sample sizes. Propensity score matching creates pseudo-randomized groups approximating RCT results when well-designed.
*   **Practice guidelines:** Compiled evidence-based recommendations with graded classes (I–III) and levels of evidence (A–C). Class I: beneficial/useful; Class II: conflicting evidence (IIa favors, IIb less established); Class III: not useful/harmful.

### Performance Assessment Applications

*   **Operative mortality:** Most studied outcome, particularly for CABG. Risk factors show consistency across models (age, gender, urgency, EF, renal dysfunction, prior CABG, left main disease).
*   **Morbidity and resource utilization:** Non-fatal complications account for ~40% of CABG costs, affecting only 10–15% of patients (Pareto principle). STS morbidity models for stroke, renal failure, reoperation, prolonged ventilation, and mediastinitis guide quality improvement.
*   **Failure-to-rescue:** Mortality rate in patients experiencing postoperative complications. Reflects ICU capabilities, nursing care, and structural measures rather than preoperative risk alone.
*   **Composite measures:** Combine multiple quality indicators (outcome, process, structure) into single scores for more robust performance assessment.

### Quality Improvement Case Studies

*   **Northern New England Cardiovascular Study Group (NNECVDSG):** Voluntary consortium using feedback, continuous quality improvement training, and site visits. Reduced CABG mortality from 4.3% to below expected across all acuity categories.
*   **Total Quality Management (TQM):** Deming and Juran principles applied to health care: process-focused, customer (patient)-supplier relationships, understanding variation, Pareto principle (80/20 rule), quality reduces cost.

### Controversies and Limitations

*   **Risk aversion:** Public reporting may lead providers to avoid high-risk patients. Evidence mixed—New York state analyses showed increased severity over time, but outmigration concerns persist.
*   **Administrative vs. clinical data:** Claims data underestimate comorbidity, misclassify procedures, and lack critical prognostic variables (LV function, vessel disease). Clinical databases (STS) provide superior accuracy.
*   **Hierarchical vs. logistic regression:** Multilevel models "shrink" low-volume provider estimates toward the mean, reducing false outlier identification. Used by STS and Massachusetts for provider profiling.
*   **League tables vs. funnel plots:** League tables imply false distinctions; funnel plots show volume-related uncertainty and identify true outliers with appropriate confidence intervals.

### Future Directions

*   **Effectiveness vs. safety:** Evidence-based effectiveness interventions may save more lives than safety-focused error reduction. Effectiveness outcomes are easier to measure.
*   **Guidelines vs. standards:** Guidelines reflect available evidence; standards operationalize guidelines into bedside practice with monitored outcome measures.
*   **Human factors research:** Structured observation of operating room behavior, modeled on aviation industry, can identify error patterns and improve outcomes.
*   **Electronic medical records:** CPOE systems show mixed results—may reduce errors but can introduce new ones. Veterans Health Affairs system demonstrates successful implementation.
*   **Public reporting:** STS partnership with Consumers Union publishes composite CABG measures; transparency has public appeal but effect on consumer behavior remains uncertain.

## Chapter 8: Simulation in Cardiac Surgery
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Definition and Historical Context

*   **Simulation:** A "technique, not a technology, to replace or guide real experiences with guided experiences that evoke or replicate substantial aspects of the real world in a fully interactive manner."
*   **Historical roots:** Early applications include Sim One (anesthesia training, 1960s), Resusci Anne (cardiac life support), and Harvey (cardiology teaching). The first surgical model simulator was created by Howard in 1868 for hernia repair.
*   **Modern era:** Began in late 1980s with realistic mannequins for crisis management training in anesthesia. Task trainers for surgical skills emerged concurrently.

### Rationale for Simulation in Cardiac Surgery

*   **Ethical imperative:** Practicing outside the OR is critical to master tasks without exposing patients to risk (Ziv et al.).
*   **Skill acquisition:** Deliberate practice via simulation increases speed and accuracy for coronary anastomosis and mitral valve annuloplasty, reducing operating time related to training.
*   **Teamwork and emergencies:** Preferred modality for improving communication, practicing rare emergencies, and working out complex problems in a nonthreatening setting.
*   **Proficiency assessment:** Potential for objective evaluation of technical competence for certification and clinical advancement.

### Key Simulator Platforms

*   **Ramphal beating-heart simulator (2005):** High-realism porcine model developed to address case volume shortages. Adapted by US leadership and incorporated into Boot Camp and Senior Tour programs.
*   **Boot Camp:** Three-day simulation curriculum sponsored by TSDA, ABTS, and JTSE for first-year cardiothoracic residents. Standardized evaluations show improvement in both speed and accuracy of basic techniques.
*   **Part task trainers:** Chamberlain Group (synthetic grafts, IMA, mitral valve models), Limbs and Things, and Wet Lab facility provide synthetic and tissue-based simulators for component skill training.
*   **Mannequin simulators:** Full-body simulators with physiologic features (heart sounds, pulses, pupillary response) placed in realistic OR settings for team training and crisis management.

### Basic Skills Training Curriculum

*   **Progressive approach:** Component tasks taught first on low-fidelity models → integrated into higher-fidelity simulations → full procedures on beating-heart model.
*   **Curriculum components (7-week syllabus):** Cardiopulmonary bypass (cannulation, cardioplegia, initiation/weaning), CABG (large and small vessel anastomoses), valve replacement (aortic and mitral), air embolism management, and acute intraoperative aortic dissection.
*   **Deliberate practice structure:** Trainees practice repeatedly on dry porcine hearts, then integrate skills on perfused beating-heart model under expert supervision.

### Teamwork Training and Crisis Resource Management

*   **Anesthesia Crisis Resource Management (ACRM):** Adapted from aviation Crew Resource Management by Gaba and colleagues (1990s) to teach anesthesiologists crisis handling skills.
*   **Communication failures:** Second most common factor in surgical errors after technical performance. Root-cause analysis of closed claims identifies poor communication as significant contributor to preventable adverse events.
*   **Teamwork training outcomes:** Formal training improves staff attitudes, teamwork quality, and reduces observed clinical errors. CRICO/RMF offers malpractice premium reductions (10%) for surgeons participating in biannual 6-hour simulation-based team training.
*   **Cardiac surgery applications:** Stevens et al. developed comprehensive program with bypass simulator engaging entire OR team. Post-training focus groups reported improved patient care, increased briefings, and desirable behavior changes.

### Simulation as an Evaluation Tool

*   **Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS):** Validated for general surgery (Martin et al., 1997) and colorectal surgery (de Montbrun et al., 2013).
*   **JTSE Coronary Artery Assessment Tool:** 13 parameters scored on Likert scale (1 = poor, 5 = outstanding). Lee et al. (2013) demonstrated high interrater reliability and internal consistency for assessing coronary anastomosis competence.
*   **Performance rating components:** Graft orientation, bite appropriateness, spacing, needle holder use, forceps use, needle angles, needle transfer, suture management, knot tying.

### Adult Learning Principles

*   **Kopta's three stages:** Perception → integration → automation.
*   **Collins' apprenticeship framework:** Teacher roles evolve as student progresses from novice to expert.
*   **Deliberate practice:** Ericsson's research shows expert performance requires ~10,000 hours of deliberate practice with coaching—simulation offers opportunity to acquire this without direct patient exposure.

### Future Directions

*   **Competency-based training:** Systematic curricula where residents complete simulation with coaching before entering OR (Baker et al., 2012).
*   **3-D printing:** Patient-specific, anatomically correct models for operative planning, endovascular stent planning, and resident education (e.g., VSD repair).
*   **New technology integration:** Simulation for OR opening, new procedure introduction, and new technology adoption before clinical use.
*   **Expanded pathology repertoire:** Need for more technically accurate and biologically diverse simulations to develop pre- and intra-procedure judgment.

## Chapter 9: The Integrated Cardiovascular Center
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Evolution of Cardiovascular Care Organization

*   **Cardiovascular disease burden:** Leading cause of death in the US, with 235.5 deaths per 100,000 (2010). Despite 31% mortality reduction from 2000–2010, costs reached USD315.4 billion annually.
*   **Historical roots of subspecialties:** Cardiac surgery emerged from general surgery (Dwight Harken, WWII); interventional cardiology emerged from radiology (Charles Dotter, first angioplasty; Andreas Gruentzig, first coronary balloon angioplasty). These distinct origins led to siloed departmental structures.
*   **Convergence:** Increasing specialization created overlapping clinical interests across departments, fostering symbiotic relationships between cardiac surgeons, cardiologists, cardiovascular radiologists, and anesthesiologists, ultimately forming dedicated cardiovascular centers.

### Advantages of Integrated Cardiovascular Centers

*   **Improved clinical care:**
    *   Volume-quality relationship: Higher procedural volumes in cardiac surgery (>125 CABG/year) and interventional cardiology (>200 angioplasties/year, >36 primary angioplasties/year) correlate with lower mortality and complications.
    *   Specialized expertise: Multidisciplinary management, co-located services, and participation in multicenter trials provide access to latest treatments.
*   **Enhanced efficiency:**
    *   Patient flow: Coordination of preoperative tests (echocardiography, catheterization, anesthesia evaluation) minimizes hospital visits.
    *   Practice management: Merged groups achieve cost reductions (e.g., 13% expense reduction in one vascular surgery/interventional radiology merger).
*   **Cost reduction:** Integrated care reduces time off work, redundancy, and administrative overhead.

### The Integrated Cardiovascular Center of the Future

*   **Clinical integration:**
    *   Multidisciplinary outpatient clinics: Shared physician workspaces with patients seen in designated cubicles; joined consultations across specialties.
    *   Shared interpretation: Cardiologists and radiologists in joint reading rooms, combining clinical knowledge with imaging/informatics expertise.
    *   Multidisciplinary conferences: High-risk preoperative conferences assess risk/benefit ratios with input from surgeons, cardiologists, and anesthesiologists.
    *   Hybrid operating rooms: Combine OR features with catheterization/endovascular capabilities, enabling simultaneous surgical and interventional procedures.

*   **Educational integration:**
    *   Free-standing residencies: Cardiothoracic and vascular surgery programs independent of general surgery training.
    *   Cross-specialty rotations: CT surgery residents rotate with cardiology; cardiology fellows observe heart procurements and implants; TAVR fellowships require catheterization suite training.

*   **Research integration:**
    *   Basic research: Differences between subspecialties diminish; shared animal facilities and core equipment reduce costs.
    *   Translational research: Multidisciplinary teams (stem cell, tissue engineering, bioengineering, valve hemodynamics, 3-D printing) transcend departmental boundaries.
    *   Clinical outcomes research: Jointly maintained databases with multi-specialty follow-up.

### Hybrid Procedures and New Paradigms

*   **Hybrid coronary revascularization:** LIMA to LAD (minimally invasive or open) combined with PCI of non-LAD vessels.
*   **Minimally invasive valve + PCI:** Converts high-risk valve/CABG operations into lower-risk isolated procedures.
*   **Hybrid arrhythmia procedures:** Combined endomyocardial and epicardial approaches.
*   **Hybrid aortic procedures:** Open and endovascular approaches for arch and descending aortic pathologies.
*   **TAVR:** Hybrid OR as optimal setting for transcatheter aortic valve replacement.
*   **The Hybrid Paradigm:** Cardiovascular subspecialists increasingly identify as "cardiovascular proceduralists," transcending traditional boundaries.

## Chapter 10: Preoperative Evaluation for Cardiac Surgery
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Risk Assessment Framework

*   **Individual risk-benefit analysis:** Decisions must weigh procedural risks (mortality, major morbidity) against expected benefits (longevity, symptom relief, functional capacity).
*   **Limitations of scoring systems:** Traditional risk scores do not adequately account for procedure-specific impediments, major organ system compromise, and frailty.
*   **Heart Team approach:** Multidisciplinary participation (surgeons, cardiologists, imaging, anesthesia, geriatrics) optimizes patient selection and shared decision making.

### Patient Characteristics and Conditions

*   **Age:** Perioperative mortality similar across ages, but 1-year mortality higher in patients >75 years. Octogenarians have nearly double mortality (4.1% vs. 2.3%) and >60% experience ≥1 nonfatal complication. Nonagenarians can achieve 95% 30-day survival with careful selection.
*   **Gender:** Female gender associated with higher postoperative mortality, possibly due to smaller coronary arteries, later referral, and smaller body surface area. Quality-of-life benefits similar after surgery.
*   **Race:** Black race associated with increased postoperative complications (prolonged ventilation, reoperation for bleeding, renal failure) but mortality differences small after risk adjustment.
*   **Diabetes:** Independent predictor of in-hospital mortality, especially with target organ damage (vascular disease, renal failure). Insulin-dependent patients have increased risk of renal failure, sternal wound infection. Strict glucose control reduces mortality and mediastinitis.
*   **Renal function:** Graded increase in mortality/morbidity with worsening renal function. Even mild dysfunction (Cr 1.47–2.25 mg/dL) increases operative and long-term mortality, postoperative dialysis, and stroke.
*   **Pulmonary function:** COPD increases mortality and complications (arrhythmias, reintubation, pneumonia, prolonged ICU stay). Preoperative optimization includes smoking cessation, antibiotics, bronchodilators, and inspiratory muscle training.

### Surgical Considerations

*   **Reoperation:** Traditionally higher mortality due to older age, extensive disease, and technical challenges (sternal reentry, adhesions, in situ grafts). With experience, mortality now approaches primary CABG.
*   **Prior radiation:** Thoracic radiation (Hodgkin's, lymphoma, breast cancer) associated with poorer outcomes. Extensive radiation linked to longer radiation-to-operation interval, poorer pulmonary function, more severe aortic regurgitation, and left main stenosis.
*   **Surgical complexity:** Mortality and morbidity increase with procedure complexity: isolated CABG (2.3% mortality, 14.4% composite) → isolated valve (AVR 3.2%, MVR 5.7%, MVP 1.6%) → combined procedures (AVR+CABG 5.6%, MVR+CABG 11.6%).
*   **Minimally invasive techniques:** Benefits include earlier extubation, less discomfort, lower wound infection, less blood loss, shorter recovery. Off-pump CABG reduces AF, blood loss, and wound infections but mortality benefit not clearly established. Hybrid procedures (PCI + minimally invasive CABG) show promising early outcomes.

### Risk Scores

*   **EuroSCORE:** 17 risk factors with additive and logistic models. Additive model overestimates low-risk mortality, underestimates high-risk. Logistic model more accurate for combined procedures.
*   **STS Score:** Extensive data entry; models for CABG, valve, combined procedures with mortality and morbidity endpoints (stroke, renal failure, prolonged ventilation, sternal wound infection). Similar discriminative capability to EuroSCORE.
*   **Limitations:** Models derived from large databases; do not capture surgeon experience, unmeasured comorbidities, or frailty. Risk factors change over time, leading to under- or overestimation.

### Frailty Assessment

*   **Definition:** Syndrome of decreased physiological reserve and vulnerability to stressors. Prevalence 10–60% depending on population and measurement tool.
*   **Core domains:** Slowness (gait speed), weakness (handgrip strength), low physical activity, exhaustion, shrinking (weight loss, muscle mass).
*   **Key measures:**
    *   **5-m gait speed:** Slow (<0.83 m/s) associated with 3-fold increase in postoperative mortality/major morbidity, adds incremental value to STS score.
    *   **Handgrip strength:** Men <30 kg, women <20 kg indicate weakness.
    *   **Frailty ABCs study:** Slow gait + high-risk score = 43% mortality/morbidity; normal gait + low-intermediate risk = 6%.
*   **Biology:** Systemic immune, hormonal, endocrine dysregulation → inflammatory cytokine up-regulation, decreased testosterone, insulin resistance → sarcopenia.

### Heart Team and Shared Decision Making

*   **Origins:** SYNTAX and PARTNER trials established multidisciplinary approach for coronary disease and aortic stenosis. Now Class I recommendation for severe aortic stenosis; required for TAVR reimbursement in US.
*   **Expanded membership:** Imaging, neurology, pulmonology, anesthesia, geriatrics, palliative care, social work, IT, administration, finance.
*   **Goals:** Optimize patient selection, utilize synergistic skill sets, standardize care pathways, establish post-discharge follow-up, deliver patient-centered care.
*   **Shared decision making:** ~70% of patients prefer shared role in treatment decisions. Resources: Ottawa Decision Aids, Mayo SDM Resource Center, Option Grids.

### Futility

*   **Definition:** Lack of medical efficacy (therapy unlikely to produce intended clinical result) OR lack of meaningful survival (judged by patient values).
*   **TAVR context:** Some patients die soon after or have minimal quality-of-life improvement despite successful procedure. Futility decisions should be collaborative; declining invasive treatment does not equate to abandonment of care.

## Chapter 11: Cardiac Anesthesia
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Myocardial Oxygen Supply-Demand Balance

*   **Oxygen content formula:** O₂ content = (1.34)(Hb)(%O₂ saturation) + (0.003)(PaO₂). Maximizing oxygen release requires high hemoglobin, high saturation, warm temperature, and increased 2,3-DPG.
*   **Coronary perfusion pressure (CPP):** CPP = aortic diastolic pressure – left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP). Left ventricular endocardial flow occurs entirely during diastole.
*   **Optimization goals:** Normal to increased diastolic BP, low LVEDP, slow heart rate (maximize diastole); avoid tachycardia, minimize LVEDP, reduce chamber size and contractility to decrease demand.

### Preoperative Assessment and Monitoring

*   **Risk calculators:** STS Risk Calculator and EuroSCORE II predict mortality and complications based on age, gender, EF, urgency, renal function, and other variables.
*   **Medications:** Beta-blockers and statins recommended preoperatively (reduce atrial fibrillation, ischemia, mortality). ACE inhibitors/ARBs controversial due to intraoperative hypotension.
*   **Monitoring:**
    *   Standard ASA monitors plus arterial line, central venous catheter, urinary catheter.
    *   **BIS monitoring:** Reduces intraoperative awareness in high-risk patients (OR 0.24).
    *   **Pulmonary artery catheter (PAC):** Class I indication for cardiogenic shock; declining use but still employed in >75% of CPB cases.
    *   **Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE):** Routine for valvular procedures, recommended for CABG; monitors volume status, ventricular function, ischemia.

### Anesthetic Techniques

*   **Fast-track balanced anesthetic (contemporary standard):** Induction with propofol/thiopental/etomidate/midazolam; maintenance with volatile anesthetic (isoflurane, sevoflurane, desflurane) plus low-dose opioid (fentanyl/sufentanil/remifentanil).
*   **Volatile anesthetics provide preconditioning:** Meta-analysis shows reduced mortality vs. propofol-based technique (OR 0.51).
*   **High-dose opioid technique (historical):** Fentanyl 50–100 mcg/kg with pancuronium; now less common due to delayed extubation, lack of preconditioning.
*   **Regional techniques:** Thoracic epidural and intrathecal opioids possible but risk of epidural hematoma with heparinization limits use.

### Hemodynamic Optimization

*   **Vasoactive agents:** Epinephrine (α/β₁/β₂), norepinephrine (α>>β), dopamine (dose-dependent), dobutamine (β₁), milrinone (inodilator, phosphodiesterase inhibitor), vasopressin (V₁), levosimendan (calcium sensitizer, not available in US).
*   **Low cardiac output syndrome risk factors:** Renal disease, regional wall motion abnormalities, CABG + MV surgery, reoperation, MR, prolonged cross-clamp, EF <40%, CI <2.5 L/min/m², LVEDP ≥20 mm Hg.

### Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG) Procedural Considerations

*   **Heparinization:** 300–400 units/kg, target ACT ≥480 seconds before CPB.
*   **Weaning from CPB:** Preparation requires core temperature ≥36°C, lung recruitment, resumption of ventilation, heart rate 80–100 bpm. Process requires "sterile cockpit" environment.
*   **Off-pump CABG (OPCAB):** Uses stabilization device on beating heart. Meta-analyses show no cognitive benefit; all-cause mortality slightly higher (3.7% vs. 3.1%); use declining.

### Valvular Heart Disease

*   **Aortic stenosis (AS):** Hemodynamic goals: ↑ preload, ↑ afterload, normal heart rate. Avoid tachycardia (encroaches on diastolic filling, increases O₂ consumption). Avoid bradycardia <50 bpm. TEE preferred for preload assessment over PAC.
*   **Aortic regurgitation (AR):** Hemodynamic goals: ↑ preload, ↓ afterload, ↑ heart rate. Faster rate reduces diastolic regurgitant time. Acute AR: high LVEDP limits CPP.
*   **Mitral stenosis (MS):** Hemodynamic goals: ↑ preload (avoid reduction), normal heart rate, avoid tachycardia. Treat atrial fibrillation with rate control, not necessarily conversion. Pulmonary vasodilators for RV dysfunction.
*   **Mitral regurgitation (MR):** Hemodynamic goals: ↓ afterload (promotes forward flow), avoid bradycardia, normal heart rate. Preoperative LVEF <60% identifies reduced systolic performance. LV end-systolic diameter >36 mm increases risk of postoperative EF <40% (6.5-fold).
*   **Tricuspid regurgitation (TR):** Support RV contractility (milrinone, dobutamine), mildly elevated heart rate, reduce pulmonary vascular resistance.

### Thoracic Aortic Surgery

*   **Aortic root/ascending aorta:** TEE critical for diagnosis, aortic valve assessment, true lumen identification for cannulation, and guiding endovascular approaches. Target BP <140/90 (elective) or SBP 100–120 mm Hg (acute dissection).
*   **Aortic arch repair:** Requires hypothermic circulatory arrest. Neuroprotection strategies: profound/deep/moderate/mild hypothermia (consensus classification). Selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP) with moderate hypothermia is emerging standard.
*   **Neuromonitoring:** EEG determines metabolic suppression (EEG silence indicates adequate cooling). Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) monitors cerebral oxygen saturation; contralateral drop with unilateral ACP suggests incomplete circle of Willis.
*   **Descending aorta:** Spinal cord protection strategies: left heart bypass, CSF drainage (target pressure 10 mm Hg), reimplantation of intercostal arteries, mild-moderate hypothermia. SSEP/MEP monitoring detects ischemia.

### Heart Failure Surgery

*   **Heart transplantation:** Preoperative TEE to rule out thrombus; maintain inotropic support until CPB. Donor characteristics affecting prognosis: age >50 years, LVH >14 mm, ischemic time >210 min, donor-specific antibodies.
*   **Ventricular assist device (VAD) implantation:** 40% develop vasoplegia requiring vasopressin/methylene blue. Preventing RV failure (25–50% incidence) is key; monitor septal position (TEE). RV failure management: sinus rhythm, avoid volume overload, inotropes, pulmonary vasodilators.
*   **Total artificial heart (TAH):** Central venous catheter tip must not enter right atrium (risk of valve entrapment). PAC contraindicated after implantation.

### Electrophysiology and Catheterization Lab Procedures

*   **Pacemaker modes:** NASPE/BPEG code: I (chambers paced), II (chambers sensed), III (response to sensing), IV (rate modulation), V (multisite pacing). "Mode switch" prevents rapid ventricular pacing during atrial fibrillation.
*   **ICDs:** Respond to VT with antitachycardia pacing (ATP) or shock. Magnet application turns off arrhythmia detection (no effect on pacing).
*   **Catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation:** Requires general anesthesia; avoid paralysis (phrenic nerve monitoring). Success rates 80% (paroxysmal) to 50–70% (heart failure). Complications: tamponade, atrial-esophageal fistula, pulmonary vein stenosis.
*   **Ventricular tachycardia ablation:** High-risk patients often require inotropic support, IABP, or percutaneous LVAD. Access may be retrograde aortic, transseptal, or epicardial subxiphoid.
*   **TAVR:** Indicated for STS risk score >8%. Devices: Sapien XT (balloon-expandable, rapid pacing required), CoreValve (self-expanding). Alternative access: transapical, transaortic, subclavian.
*   **MitraClip:** For high-risk degenerative MR; mimics Alfieri stitch; requires TEE guidance.

## Chapter 12: Echocardiography in Cardiac Surgery
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Basic Principles and Safety

*   **Physics of ultrasound:** Images are constructed from high-frequency sound waves transmitted from a piezo-electric crystal transducer. Sound travels well through water/blood, reasonably through tissue, poorly through air. Structures perpendicular to the beam produce the most accurate images.
*   **Doppler principles:** Blood flow velocity is derived from frequency shift of reflected sound. The simplified Bernoulli equation (ΔP = 4 × velocity²) calculates pressure gradients. Pulse wave (PW) Doppler has excellent spatial resolution; continuous wave (CW) Doppler has excellent velocity resolution.
*   **TEE safety:** Complication rate 0.2% in 7200 intraoperative cases. Most common: odynophagia (0.1%), upper GI hemorrhage (0.03%), esophageal perforation (0.01%). Absolute contraindications: perforated viscous, prior esophagectomy, esophagogastrectomy.
*   **Epicardial ultrasound:** Recommended for epiaortic scanning prior to aortic cannulation to detect atheromatous disease. ASE guidelines define standard views for epicardial imaging when TEE contraindicated.

### Coronary Artery Disease Assessment

*   **Wall motion scoring:** 16-segment LV model (ASE). Segments graded: normal/hyperkinetic, hypokinetic, akinetic, dyskinetic.
*   **Stress echocardiography:** Sensitivity 88%, specificity 76%. Dobutamine stress identifies viable myocardium (biphasic response: improvement at low dose, deterioration at high dose).
*   **Mechanical complications of MI:**
    *   LV free wall rupture: catastrophic acute rupture vs. subacute with hemopericardium. Pseudoaneurysm has narrow neck.
    *   Ventricular septal rupture (VSR): 50-66% anterior infarctions; distal septum in anterior MI, basal inferior septum in inferior MI.
    *   Acute MR: papillary muscle rupture (posteromedial > anterolateral due to single blood supply).

### Mitral Valve Disease

*   **MR classification (Carpentier):** Type I (normal leaflet motion), Type II (excessive motion), Type IIIa (restricted diastolic), Type IIIb (restricted systolic).
*   **Severe MR quantification:**
    *   Organic MR: EROA ≥ 40 mm², regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL, vena contracta > 7 mm.
    *   Functional MR: EROA ≥ 20 mm², regurgitant volume ≥ 30 mL (lower thresholds due to worse prognosis).
*   **Prognostic triggers for MV surgery:** Symptoms, LVEF < 60%, LVESD > 40 mm, pulmonary hypertension (PASP > 50 mm Hg), new atrial fibrillation.
*   **Mitral stenosis:** Wilkins' score (leaflet mobility, subvalvular thickening, leaflet thickening, calcification; each 1–4). Score > 8 predicts poor PMBV success.
*   **MVA calculation:** Planimetry (gold standard), pressure half-time (PHT = 220/MVA), continuity method.

### Aortic Valve Disease

*   **Severe AS classification:**
    *   High-gradient AS: AVA ≤ 1 cm², Vmax ≥ 4 m/s, mean gradient ≥ 40 mm Hg.
    *   Low-flow low-gradient AS with reduced LVEF (<50%): AVA ≤ 1 cm², Vmax < 4 m/s. Dobutamine stress distinguishes true severe from pseudo-severe.
    *   Paradoxical low-flow AS (normal LVEF): Indexed AVA ≤ 0.6 cm²/m², stroke volume index < 35 mL/m², small LV cavity.
*   **Continuity equation:** AVA = (CSALVOT × VTILVOT) / VTIAV. Dimensionless index = VLVOT/VAV ≤ 0.25 indicates severe stenosis.
*   **Severe AR parameters:** Jet width/LVOT diameter > 65%, vena contracta > 6 mm, PHT < 200 ms, holodiastolic flow reversal in descending aorta (end-diastolic velocity > 20 cm/s), EROA ≥ 30 mm², regurgitant volume ≥ 60 mL.

### Tricuspid Valve and Prosthetic Valves

*   **Functional TR:** Most common cause. Annular dilatation (> 40 mm or > 21 mm/m²) predicts progression after left-sided valve surgery.
*   **Severe TR parameters:** Vena contracta > 7 mm, EROA ≥ 40 mm², regurgitant volume ≥ 45 mL, systolic flow reversal in hepatic veins.
*   **Prosthetic valve assessment:** Expected gradients/EOA vary by valve type/size. DVI (dimensionless velocity index) < 0.25 suggests obstruction. PHT > 200 ms suggests stenosis; PHT < 130 ms with elevated E wave suggests regurgitation.
*   **PVE (prosthetic valve endocarditis):** TEE superior for detecting annular abscesses; TTE better for anterior aortic valve and LV side of mitral prosthesis.

### Aortic and Pericardial Diseases

*   **Aortic dissection:** TEE sensitivity 98-100%, specificity 95-100%. Intimal flap separates true from false lumen. Reverberation artifacts mimic flap but move in parallel with aortic wall.
*   **Cardiac tamponade:** RA inversion (sensitive, specific), RV collapse (more specific), exaggerated respiratory variation in mitral/tricuspid inflow (inspiration: tricuspid E > 60% increase, mitral E > 30% decrease).
*   **Constrictive pericarditis:** Septal bounce, respiratory variation in flow (mitral inflow decreases > 25% with inspiration), preserved/elevated mitral annular velocities (e′ > 7 cm/s), lateral e′ < septal e′.

### Perioperative Echocardiography Guidance

*   **Communication:** Surgeon-echocardiographer collaboration essential. Predictions based on echocardiographic data guide surgical technique (ring size, SAM potential, coronary damage risk).
*   **Epicardial views:** ASE guidelines define standard views (AV short/long axis, LV basal/mid SAX, LV long axis, two-chamber, RV outflow tract) for when TEE contraindicated.
*   **Intracardiac air detection:** Post-CPB views include left atrial dome, pulmonary veins, left atrial appendage, LV apex.

## Chapter 13: Extracorporeal Circulation
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Perfusion Systems and Circuit Components
*   **CPB Circuit Basics:** Blood drains by gravity from the SVC/IVC to a venous reservoir, is pumped through a membrane oxygenator (gas exchange), and returned to the systemic circulation via an arterial cannula (aorta, femoral, or axillary artery).
*   **Venous Cannulation:** Bicaval, single atrial, or two-stage (cavoatrial) cannulas are used. A persistent left SVC (0.3–0.5% of population) requires careful management to avoid cerebral venous hypertension.
*   **Assisted Venous Drainage (AVD/VAVD):** Negative pressure applied to the venous line permits smaller cannulas but increases risk of air entrainment, hemolysis, and cerebral air embolism.
*   **Arterial Cannulation:** The ascending aorta is the preferred site. Aortic atherosclerosis (particularly "porcelain aorta") mandates alternative sites (axillary, femoral) to prevent atheroembolic stroke. Pressure gradients >100 mm Hg cause hemolysis.
*   **Oxygenators:** Modern hollow-fiber membrane oxygenators produce fewer microemboli and cause less blood element activation than bubble oxygenators. Polymethylpentene membranes are preferred for prolonged ECMO.
*   **Pumps:** Centrifugal pumps are afterload-sensitive, nonocclusive, and safer for massive air embolism; roller pumps are inexpensive but can generate excessive pressures and spallation.

### Coagulation and Inflammation
*   **Thrombin Generation:** CPB continuously generates thrombin despite high-dose heparin. The wound (tissue factor) is a major source, not just the circuit. Thrombin drives both thrombosis and consumptive coagulopathy.
*   **Heparin Limitations:** Heparin requires antithrombin III, does not inhibit clot-bound thrombin, and only partially suppresses thrombin formation. Alternatives for HIT/HITT include bivalirudin and argatroban.
*   **HIT/HITT:** IgG antibodies to heparin-PF4 complex cause severe thrombocytopenia and thrombosis 5–15 days post-exposure. After CPB, 50% have antibodies; 1% develop HITT. Management requires stopping all heparin and using direct thrombin inhibitors.
*   **Inflammatory Response:** Complement (C3a, C5a), neutrophils, monocytes, and endothelial cells are activated. C5a and C5b-9 mediate organ injury. Hypothermia reduces inflammation; normothermia increases cytokine release.
*   **Consumptive Coagulopathy:** The equilibrium between procoagulants (thrombin) and anticoagulants is disrupted. Management requires ACT, platelet count, and judicious use of antifibrinolytics, platelets, and FFP.

### Organ Damage and Protection
*   **Neurologic Injury:** Major causes are atherosclerotic microemboli from aortic manipulation, air embolism, and hypoperfusion. Risk increases with age, aortic atherosclerosis, and prolonged CPB. Neuroprotective strategies: mild hypothermia (32–34°C), hematocrit >25%, and epiaortic scanning to avoid atherosclerotic aorta.
*   **Cardiac Injury:** Stunning, reperfusion injury, and edema contribute to post-CPB dysfunction. Adequate venting and cardioplegia are critical.
*   **Lung Injury:** CPB increases pulmonary capillary permeability, interstitial edema, and atelectasis due to microemboli and inflammatory mediators.
*   **Renal Injury:** Hemodilution improves renal flow, but perioperative low cardiac output and hypotension are major risk factors for acute renal failure (1–5% require dialysis).
*   **Gastrointestinal Injury:** Stress ulcers are now uncommon with H₂ blockers; mesenteric ischemia in elderly patients on vasopressors is rare but catastrophic.

### Key Clinical Parameters
*   **Flow Rate:** Normothermic (37°C): 2.4 L/min/m²; hypothermic (30°C): 1.8 L/min/m²; (25°C): 1.6 L/min/m².
*   **Hematocrit:** Target 25–30% during CPB; <23% associated with increased mortality.
*   **MAP:** Maintain 70–80 mm Hg in older patients; lower pressures (50–60 mm Hg) associated with worse outcomes.
*   **Temperature:** Mild hypothermia (32–34°C) is preferred to balance organ protection and avoid hyperthermic rewarming injury.
*   **ACT:** Target >400 seconds before CPB; re-dose heparin every hour (⅓ initial dose).

## Chapter 14: Transfusion Therapy and Blood Conservation
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Rationale for Blood Conservation

*   **Transfusion risks:** Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) mortality 35–58%; RBC transfusions associated with longer ICU stays, worse short- and long-term survival. Each unit transfused poses additive risk for adverse outcomes.
*   **Evidence-based practice:** Growing literature demonstrates transfusions are associated with increased morbidity and mortality. Randomized trials show lower hemoglobin thresholds tolerated in critically ill and cardiac surgical patients.
*   **Blood conservation programs:** Multimodal, multidisciplinary approaches most effective. Examples: Ovrum et al. achieved 96% of elective CABG patients receiving no allogeneic blood; Van der Linden et al. demonstrated 53% decrease in RBC utilization.

### Preoperative Risk Identification and Optimization

*   **Five consistent risk factors for transfusion:** Low preoperative hemoglobin/hematocrit, advanced age, female sex, renal insufficiency, surgery urgency.
*   **Additional risk factors:** Antiplatelet/anticoagulation therapy, insulin-dependent diabetes, decreased LV function, prolonged CPB time, complex valvular/aortic surgery.
*   **Medication management:**
    *   **Aspirin:** Reasonable to discontinue only in purely elective patients without acute coronary syndromes.
    *   **ADP receptor inhibitors (clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor):** Discontinue 3 days prior (timing based on half-life). Point-of-care testing may identify nonresponders who can undergo early surgery without cessation.
    *   **GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors (abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban):** Stop prior to surgery to decrease bleeding events.
    *   **Vitamin K antagonists (warfarin):** Discontinue 2–4 days before surgery; high-risk patients bridged with IV heparin once INR subtherapeutic.
    *   **Novel oral anticoagulants (NOACs):** Discontinue 2–5 days prior; bridging not required. For emergency surgery, defer ≥12 hours if possible; no specific antidote available.
    *   **Herbal supplements:** Thyme, rosemary, fish oil, garlic, ginger, Gingko biloba may have antiplatelet effects; stop 7 days prior.
*   **Increasing RBC mass:** Iron supplementation for iron-deficiency anemia. Preoperative erythropoietin (EPO) reduces allogeneic exposure (56% relative risk reduction in one trial). Preoperative autologous blood donation (PABD) limited by time required (≥2 weeks/unit) and patient reserve; largely supplanted by perioperative strategies.

### Perioperative Blood Conservation Strategies

*   **CPB techniques:**
    *   **Acute normovolemic hemodilution (ANH):** Removal of 1–2 units whole blood before CPB with crystalloid/colloid replacement; reinfused after heparin reversal. Nomogram for allowable removal based on weight, preop hematocrit, and CPB prime volume.
    *   **Retrograde autologous priming (RAP):** CPB circuit primed with patient's own blood, displacing crystalloid prime; reduces hemodilution and transfusions.
    *   **Mini-circuits:** Closed CPB systems with priming volumes <500 mL; reduce hemodilution, transfusion requirements, and may improve mortality. Vacuum-assisted venous drainage used but caution regarding air entrainment.
    *   **Modified ultrafiltration:** Removes water and inflammatory mediators at case conclusion; returns protein-rich concentrated blood. Recommended for blood conservation.
    *   **Autotransfusion:** Cardiotomy suction, centrifuged cell salvage, and reinfusion of residual circuit blood recommended.
*   **Operative techniques:**
    *   **Off-pump CABG:** Reduces transfusion requirements, reoperations for bleeding, and coagulopathy; however, graft patency concerns have tempered enthusiasm.
    *   **Minimally invasive approaches:** TEVAR reduces transfusions vs. open aortic surgery; TAVR associated with fewer transfusions than surgical AVR; minithoracotomy (MT) for valve surgery reduces blood loss vs. sternotomy.
*   **Topical hemostatic agents:** Fibrin sealants, FloSeal (gelatin matrix + thrombin), BioGlue (albumin + glutaraldehyde). Class IIb recommendation; may be considered as part of multimodal program. No single agent proven superior.
*   **Antifibrinolytic agents:**
    *   **Tranexamic acid (TA):** 10× more potent than EACA. Class I recommendation for reducing blood loss and transfusions. Concerns over seizure risk.
    *   **Epsilon-aminocaproic acid (EACA):** Most commonly used in US. Loading 50–150 mg/kg, infusion 25–30 mg/kg/h to maintain plasma levels >130 μg/mL. Class I recommendation.
    *   **Aprotinin:** Removed from market 2007 due to renal toxicity and higher mortality in randomized trial.

### Postoperative Management

*   **Transfusion triggers:**
    *   **On CPB with hypothermia:** Hematocrit as low as 15% tolerated; 18% in patients at risk for decreased cerebral oxygen delivery (stroke, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease).
    *   **Post-CPB (warm):** Increase thresholds by 2% (17% and 20% respectively).
    *   **Consensus guidelines:** Hemoglobin >10 g/dL: transfusion rarely of benefit. Hemoglobin <7 g/dL: most patients benefit. Hemoglobin <6 g/dL: reasonable (life-saving). TRICC trial showed restrictive strategy (Hb <7) reduced transfusions without increased mortality; TRACS trial (cardiac surgery) showed comparable outcomes with restrictive (Hct ≥24%) vs. liberal (Hct ≥30%) strategies, with 60% fewer transfusions in restrictive group.
*   **Point-of-care testing:**
    *   **Thromboelastography (TEG) and rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM):** Assess whole-blood coagulation, platelet function, fibrinolysis. May reduce blood subcomponent transfusions but evidence heterogeneous; not yet recommended for routine use.
*   **Novel blood products:**
    *   **Recombinant activated factor VII (rFVIIa):** May be considered for intractable nonsurgical bleeding unresponsive to conventional therapies. Off-label use associated with higher arterial thrombotic events (5.5% vs. 3.2%).
    *   **Prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs):** Vitamin K-dependent factor concentrates. Useful for rapid reversal of oral anticoagulants; faster and with less bleeding than FFP. Early data suggest possible benefit for post-CPB coagulopathy.

## Chapter 15: Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Historical Context

*   **Origins:** Bigelow demonstrated hypothermia in canine model (1950); Lewis performed first successful atrial septal defect repair using hypothermia (1953). Griepp's translational research ushered in modern era of aortic arch surgery using HCA.

### Basic Principles

*   **Cerebral metabolism:** Brain metabolic rate 7.5× non-nervous tissue; depends on aerobic glycolysis with only 2-minute glucose reserve. ATP depletion → ionic pump failure → Ca²⁺ accumulation → irreversible neuronal injury.
*   **Cerebral blood flow (CBF):** 750–900 mL/min (15% of cardiac output). Autoregulation maintains CBF at MAP 60–140 mm Hg. Hypothermia reduces CMRO₂ exponentially: 20°C = 76% of baseline; 15°C = 84% of baseline.
*   **Temperature classifications:**
    *   Profound hypothermia: ≤14°C
    *   Deep hypothermia (DHCA): 14.1–20.0°C
    *   Moderate hypothermia (MHCA): 20.1–28.0°C
    *   Mild hypothermia: 28.1–34.0°C
*   **pH management:**
    *   **Alpha-stat:** Maintains normal pH at 37°C; preserves cerebral autoregulation; preferred in adults (reduces embolic risk).
    *   **pH-stat:** Adds CO₂ to maintain normal pH at hypothermia; causes cerebral vasodilation, eliminates autoregulation; preferred in children (increases CBF during low-flow states).

### Pathogenesis of Brain Injury

*   **Permanent neurologic dysfunction (PND):** Focal deficit (stroke) from embolic occlusion. Risk factors: atheromatous disease, arterial cannulation site (axillary artery lowest risk). Epiaortic ultrasound and cerebral perfusion reduce risk.
*   **Temporary neurologic dysfunction (TND):** Diffuse, reversible injury (confusion, agitation, delirium). Clinical manifestation of global cerebral ischemia with necrotic/apoptotic cell death.
*   **Necrosis:** ATP depletion → anaerobic metabolism → Na⁺/K⁺ pump failure → Ca²⁺ influx → cell lysis. Risk factors: inadequate hypothermia, EEG activity at arrest, HCA >40 min without perfusion.
*   **Apoptosis:** Energy-dependent programmed cell death; occurs hours to 72 hours after reperfusion. Caspase-mediated.
*   **Excitotoxicity:** Glutamate accumulation → Ca²⁺ influx → lethal biochemical cascade.

### Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA)

*   **Cooling:** Temperature gradient ≤10°C to prevent gas emboli. Parameters: cooling ≥30 min, nasopharyngeal temperature 18°C, jugular venous saturation >95%, electrocerebral silence. Topical head ice packing advocated.
*   **Safe duration:** Calculated from CMRO₂: 5 min at 37°C → 21 min at 20°C → 31 min at 15°C. DHCA alone safe for 30 min; times >40 min increase stroke risk.
*   **Rewarming:** Cold reperfusion (low pressure) prior to rewarming improves outcomes. Maintain perfusate temperature ≤36°C; avoid hyperthermia. Higher hematocrit (30%) and normoglycemia beneficial. Permissive hypothermia post-op reduces delayed injury.

### Cerebral Perfusion Adjuncts

*   **Retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP):** Cannulate SVC, infuse blood retrograde (300–500 mL/min, SVC pressure 20–25 mm Hg). Benefits: flushes emboli, maintains hypothermia, provides metabolic support. Limitations: limited capillary flow (competent jugular valves in >70% of cadavers; azygous predominant pathway). Reduces mortality and stroke compared to DHCA alone, but TND incidence 15–25%.
*   **Selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (SACP):**
    *   **Unilateral (uSACP):** Right axillary artery cannulation; occlude innominate and left carotid; flow 10 mL/kg/min; maintain cerebral perfusion pressure 50–60 mm Hg. Provides adequate bilateral perfusion even with incomplete circle of Willis.
    *   **Bilateral (bSACP):** Direct carotid cannulation or arch vessel cannulation.
*   **Advantages over RCP:** Superior CBF, maintains near-normal cerebral metabolism, reduced TND, preserved cell structure.

### Moderate Hypothermia with SACP (MHCA/SACP)

*   **Rationale:** SACP converts total body arrest to lower body arrest; brain perfused, visceral organs protected by moderate hypothermia. Avoids complications of deep hypothermia (prolonged CPB, endothelial dysfunction, bleeding, systemic inflammation).
*   **Outcomes:** Emory series: hemiarch at 28–29°C (mortality elective 4.3%, emergent 7.7%; PND 1.9–4.6%; TND 3.8–6.2%). Total arch at 25–26°C (mortality 9.7%; PND 2.8%; TND 5.6%; mean circulatory arrest 55 min). Comparable results from multiple high-volume centers.

### Comparisons of Cerebral Protection Strategies

*   **Key findings:**
    1.  Adjunct cerebral protection (RCP or SACP) superior to DHCA alone.
    2.  No difference in PND between RCP and SACP.
    3.  SACP significantly reduces TND compared to RCP (especially for HCA >40 min).
*   **Current consensus:** No universal standard; institutional bias prevails. SACP preferred for extended/complex arch reconstructions requiring prolonged HCA.

### Pharmacologic Adjuncts

*   **Corticosteroids:** Anti-inflammatory; reduce blood-brain barrier permeability, attenuate cerebral apoptosis, improve CBF post-DHCA. Methylprednisolone 1 g after induction.
*   **Mannitol:** Osmotic diuretic; free radical scavenger; anti-apoptotic. Reduces mortality in multivariate analysis.
*   **Barbiturates (thiopental):** Reduce cerebral O₂ consumption, produce EEG silence, minimize edema. Evidence inconclusive; not recommended based on current data.

## Chapter 16: Myocardial Protection
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Ischemia/Reperfusion Injury

*   **Definition:** I/R injury manifests as reversible injury (myocardial stunning) lasting hours to days, or irreversible injury (apoptosis/necrosis) with cell death.
*   **Epidemiology:** Postoperative MI in CABG patients 3–18%; severe ventricular dysfunction/heart failure 1–15%. CK-MB and troponin elevations are common and associated with increased short-, medium-, and long-term mortality.
*   **Primary mediators:**
    *   Intracellular Ca²⁺ overload
    *   Reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated at reperfusion
    *   Nitric oxide (NO) interaction with superoxide generating reactive nitrogen species
*   **Cellular mechanisms:** During ischemia, ATP depletion → Na⁺/K⁺ pump failure → Na⁺ accumulation → reverse Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchange → Ca²⁺ overload. Lactic acidosis activates Na⁺/H⁺ exchange, exacerbating Na⁺ loading. At reperfusion, ROS production, mPTP opening, and calpain activation.
*   **Cell death pathways:**
    *   **Necrosis:** ATP depletion → cell swelling, membrane rupture, inflammation.
    *   **Apoptosis:** Energy-dependent, caspase-mediated programmed cell death; characterized by intact membrane with blebbing, nuclear condensation, DNA fragmentation.
    *   **Excitotoxicity:** Glutamate accumulation → Ca²⁺ influx → cell death.
*   **Mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP):** Opening causes loss of membrane potential, ATP hydrolysis, cytochrome c release, cell death. Regulated by cyclophilin D.

### Historical Perspectives

*   **Key milestones:** Ringer (1883) – Ca²⁺/K⁺ antagonism; Wiggers (1930) – K⁺ arrest; Bigelow (1950) – hypothermia; Melrose (1955) – potassium citrate arrest; Bretschneider (1964) – low-sodium, Ca²⁺-free solution; Hearse (1975) – St. Thomas solution; Buckberg (1979) – blood cardioplegia; Murry (1986) – ischemic preconditioning.

### Cardioplegic Solutions

*   **Crystalloid cardioplegia:**
    *   **Intracellular type:** Low Na⁺, low Ca²⁺ (Bretschneider's, Roe's)
    *   **Extracellular type:** Higher Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ (St. Thomas, del Nido)
    *   Potassium concentration 10–40 mmol/L; buffered with bicarbonate, phosphate, or histidine.
*   **Blood cardioplegia:**
    *   Most common in US. Blood: crystalloid ratios 8:1, 4:1, 2:1.
    *   Advantages: oxygenated, buffers, endogenous antioxidants, physiologic electrolytes.
    *   Induction dose: K⁺ 20–25 mEq/L; maintenance: 8–10 mEq/L.
*   **Temperature variations:**
    *   **Cold (4–10°C):** Most common; reduces metabolism, preserves ATP.
    *   **Warm (37°C):** Normothermic; potential for better metabolic recovery but concerns about ischemic tolerance.
    *   **Tepid (28–29°C):** Balance between cold and warm; may reduce anaerobic lactate release.
*   **Meta-analyses:** Cold blood vs. crystalloid cardioplegia show no significant differences in mortality, MI, or stroke. Both are safe and effective.

### Delivery Methods

*   **Antegrade:** Aortic root infusion (requires competent aortic valve). Induction rate 250–300 mL/min; maintenance 300–500 mL every 15–20 min. Contraindicated in severe AI.
*   **Retrograde:** Coronary sinus catheter; perfusion pressure ≤45–50 mm Hg to prevent rupture. Advantages: distributes to regions with severe coronary stenosis; disadvantages: suboptimal right ventricular distribution.
*   **Combined antegrade/retrograde:** More homogeneous distribution, especially for prolonged cross-clamp times or severe coronary disease.
*   **Continuous vs. intermittent:** Continuous may reduce lactate production but no clear outcome advantage; intermittent provides drier surgical field.

### Noncardioplegic Techniques

*   **Intermittent cross-clamping with fibrillation (ICCF):** Patient cooled to 30–32°C; heart fibrillates during distal anastomoses. Mortality rates: elective 0.5–0.9%, urgent 1.5–3.1%, emergent 2.3–5.6%. Advantageous in cold agglutinin disease and calcified aorta.
*   **Elective fibrillatory arrest:** Systemic hypothermia (26–30°C); perfusion pressure 80–100 mm Hg. Useful when aortic occlusion undesirable (porcelain aorta).

### Ischemic Preconditioning (IPC)

*   **Definition:** Brief periods of ischemia/reperfusion protect against subsequent prolonged ischemia. First described by Murry et al. (1986).
*   **Classic IPC:** Reduces infarct size, apoptosis, arrhythmias; lasts 1–2 hours.
*   **Delayed (late) IPC:** Appears 24 hours post-stimulus, lasts up to 72 hours; protects against infarction and stunning.
*   **Signaling pathways:** Triggers (adenosine, bradykinin, opioids) → G-protein-coupled receptors → PKC, PI3K-Akt, ERK1/2 → mK<sub>ATP</sub> channel opening, mPTP inhibition.
*   **Clinical evidence:** Meta-analysis of 22 trials (933 patients) showed IPC reduced ventricular arrhythmias and inotropic requirements but no mortality benefit.

### Postconditioning

*   **Definition:** Stuttered reperfusion (brief interruptions) during early reperfusion. Reduces infarct size 20–70% in animal models.
*   **Clinical applications:** PCI studies show reduced ST-segment changes and CK release. Limited cardiac surgery studies suggest reduced troponin release and inotropic support.

### Remote Ischemic Preconditioning (RIPC)

*   **Definition:** Brief ischemia of one organ/tissue (e.g., upper limb) protects distant organ (heart) against I/R injury.
*   **Mechanisms:** Neuronal signaling, humoral factors (adenosine, bradykinin), exosomes containing proteins and microRNAs.
*   **RIPHeart Trial (n=1385):** No effect on composite endpoint (death, MI, stroke, acute renal failure) in CABG patients.
*   **ERICCA Trial (n=1612):** No effect on 12-month cardiovascular death or perioperative myocardial injury.
*   **Conclusion:** RIPC not currently recommended for routine cardiac surgery.

### Pharmacological Cardioprotective Agents

*   **Adenosine:** Activates A₁, A₂ₐ, A₂բ, A₃ receptors. AMISTAD trials showed infarct size reduction but no clinical outcome benefit. Mixed results in cardiac surgery; short half-life limits efficacy.
*   **Sodium-hydrogen exchanger (NHE-1) inhibitors (cariporide):** EXPEDITION trial (n=5770): 18.3% relative risk reduction in death/MI but increased cerebrovascular events (mortality 1.5% → 2.2%). Effective but safety concerns.
*   **Acadesine (AICA riboside):** Purine nucleoside analog; activates AMPK, may increase adenosine. RED-CABG trial (terminated early, n=3080) showed no benefit on all-cause mortality, stroke, or need for mechanical support.
*   **Glucose-insulin-potassium (GIK):** Rationale: increases glycolytic flux, maintains GSH/GSSG balance, supports Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. Meta-analyses show mixed results; may reduce perioperative MI and inotropic requirements but no mortality benefit. Timing and patient selection critical.
*   **Cyclosporine A (CsA):** mPTP inhibitor (cyclophilin D target). Small studies suggested reduced troponin release; CIRCUS trial (n=791) in STEMI patients showed no benefit on composite endpoint (death, heart failure, LV remodeling).

### Conditioning by Stem Cells and Exosomes

*   **Cardiosphere-derived cells (CDCs):** Infusion 30 min post-reperfusion in porcine MI reduced troponin I and infarct size.
*   **Mesenchymal stem cell conditioned medium:** Active component = exosomes. Exosomes offer advantages: standardization, longer shelf life, no histocompatibility matching.

### Autophagy

*   **Definition:** Lysosomal degradation of damaged organelles and proteins; double-membrane autophagosome sequesters cargo, fuses with lysosome.
*   **Role in cardioprotection:** Upregulation of autophagy protects against I/R injury; inhibition exacerbates cell death. Mediates ischemic and pharmacologic preconditioning.
*   **Human evidence:** I/R associated with upregulation of autophagy-related genes and proteins in atrial biopsies. Impaired autophagy may increase postoperative atrial fibrillation risk.

### Myocardial Protection in Beating Heart Surgery (OPCAB)

*   **OPCAB:** Avoids CPB but may have incomplete revascularization, lower graft patency. Chowdhury et al. (2013): OPCAB puts less myocardium at risk (lower troponin release) than on-pump.
*   **Protective strategies:** Beta-blockade (esmolol, labetalol), calcium channel blockers (diltiazem), nitrates, gentle core cooling, volatile anesthetics (preconditioning). IPC, RIPC not shown to improve clinical outcomes in OPCAB.

### Challenges in Clinical Trials

*   **Preclinical models:** Young healthy animals; failure to account for comorbidities (obesity, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia, age) that impair cardioprotective responses.
*   **Trial design:** Low event rates require very large sample sizes. Surrogate markers (troponin, CK-MB) correlate with long-term mortality but FDA requires morbidity/mortality endpoints.
*   **Timing:** Agents effective pre-ischemia may fail when given at reperfusion (only clinically feasible approach for acute MI).

## Chapter 17: Postoperative Care of Cardiac Surgery Patients
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Hemodynamic Assessment and Goals

*   **Basic assessment:** Heart rate/rhythm, MAP, CVP, ECG for ischemia/conduction abnormalities. PA catheter enables measurement of PAP, PCWP, MvO₂, CO, SVR, PVR.
*   **Target goals:** MvO₂ ~60%, MAP >65 mm Hg, CI >2 L/min/m². Individualize based on comorbidities (hypertension requires higher BP; bleeding/fragile tissues require tighter control).
*   **Key formulae:** CO = SV × HR; CI = CO/BSA; SVR = (MAP – CVP)/CO × 80; PVR = (PAP – PCWP)/CO × 80; O₂ delivery = CO × (Hb × %sat × 1.39 + PaO₂ × 0.0031).
*   **Volume status:** CVP unreliable for LV preload. PCWP most accurate for left-sided filling in absence of MS. Most patients have ongoing fluid requirements postoperatively due to third spacing, vasodilation, warming, diuresis, bleeding.
*   **Oxygen-carrying capacity:** Hct 21% with SaO₂ >90% adequate for stable postoperative patient.

### Hemodynamic Management

*   **Fluid resuscitation:** Colloids achieve comparable effects with less volume; 80% of 5% albumin retained intravascularly at 1 hour. No outcome difference between colloid and crystalloid in largest RCT. Hetastarch avoided in bleeding/coagulopathic/renal impairment patients.
*   **Vasoactive agents:** Pressors (phenylephrine, vasopressin, methylene blue) for vasodilated patients with normal pump function. Vasodilators (nitroglycerin, nitroprusside, nicardipine) for hypertension or normotension with poor pump function. Inotropes (dobutamine, milrinone) for low CO despite optimized preload/afterload/rate/rhythm.
*   **Dobutamine vs. milrinone:** Dobutamine shorter acting, easier to titrate; milrinone increases CO with lower MVO₂. Both arrhythmogenic. Epinephrine/norepinephrine combine β and α effects; dopamine dose-dependent.
*   **Pacing:** Atrial pacing preferred to AV pacing preferred to ventricular pacing. Ventricular pacing causes dys-synchrony and loss of atrial contribution; useful for acutely decreasing BP. Atrial wires recommended in patients with diastolic dysfunction (AS, LVH).

### Postoperative Atrial Fibrillation (POAF)

*   **Incidence:** 30–50% after cardiothoracic surgery; higher in elderly, renal impairment, COPD. Associated with stroke, longer hospitalization, increased mortality.
*   **Prophylaxis:** Beta-blockers highest level of evidence; start/resume as soon as tolerated. Sotalol and amiodarone also effective but not superior.
*   **Treatment algorithm (Fig. 17-1):** Assess symptoms, precipitating factors (ischemia, atrial distension, electrolyte imbalances, sympathomimetics, beta-blocker withdrawal), and goals. Rate control sufficient for 90% (spontaneous conversion within 6 weeks). Metoprolol first-line (50 mg PO, then 25 mg q2–3h until rate <110). Diltiazem alternative. Electrical cardioversion for hemodynamic instability.
*   **Anticoagulation:** Coumadin for AF >24 hours. Heparin after 48 hours in high-risk patients (stroke/TIA history, low EF). INR <2 for pacemaker placement.

### Postoperative Ischemia and Infarction

*   **Etiology:** Inadequate myocardial protection, kinked/spasmed/thrombosed conduits, thrombosed endarterectomized vessels, air/atherosclerotic emboli.
*   **Diagnosis:** Unexplained poor pump function, ST changes, new bundle branch block, ventricular arrhythmias, enzyme elevation. Air embolism preferentially involves RCA (inferior ST changes), typically resolves within hours.
*   **Management:** Anticoagulation, beta-blockade, nitroglycerin. IABP to minimize inotrope requirements and O₂ demand. Timely reoperation or catheterization if significant myocardium at risk.

### Right Ventricular Failure and Pulmonary Hypertension

*   **Etiology:** Perioperative ischemia/infarction, acute PVR increase (LV dysfunction, MS, volume overload, atelectasis, hypoxia, acidosis). Chronic pulmonary hypertension due to left-sided failure, AS, MV disease, pulmonary disease.
*   **Management:** Identify reversible causes. Optimize volume status (RV Starling curve—needs adequate preload but overdistension worsens function). PEEP for atelectasis; hyperventilation reduces hypoxic/hypercapnic vasoconstriction. Inhaled NO or prostacyclin (PGI₂) provides selective pulmonary vasodilation without systemic hypotension. Milrinone beneficial; vasopressin increases SVR > PVR.

### Valve Surgery-Specific Considerations

*   **Aortic stenosis:** Hypertrophied, noncompliant LV. Requires meticulous BP control, volume, beta-blockers for dynamic outflow obstruction. Atrial wires essential (up to 30% of stroke volume dependent on atrial synchrony).
*   **Aortic regurgitation:** Dilated LV without hypertrophy; often poor postoperative function; challenging volume/afterload/inotropy optimization.
*   **Mitral regurgitation:** Afterload reduction and inotropic support required to manage afterload mismatch and unmasked LV dysfunction.
*   **Mitral stenosis:** Preserved LV function; exacerbation of preexisting pulmonary hypertension common; focus on RV function and PVR reduction.

### Cardiac Arrest and Resuscitation

*   **Incidence:** 0.7–2.9% post-cardiac surgery; survival up to 70% (better than general population due to monitored environment, reversible causes).
*   **Reversible causes:** Hypovolemia (hemorrhage), tamponade, hypoxia, electrolyte abnormalities, tension pneumothorax, pacing failure, myocardial ischemia.
*   **Special considerations:** Stacked shocks (up to 3) for VF/pulseless VT before compressions if defibrillation immediately available. Avoid chest compressions in tamponade if emergency resternotomy available. Pacing wires for asystole/bradycardia.
*   **Monitoring:** Arterial line pressure (systolic >80 mm Hg) or ETCO₂ (>10 mm Hg reflects adequate CPR; >40 mm Hg associated with ROSC).
*   **Teamwork:** Closed-loop communication; team leader coordinates; resternotomy within 5–10 minutes if unsuccessful resuscitation, especially if tamponade suspected.

### Bleeding, Thrombosis, and Transfusion

*   **Preoperative risk factors:** HIT (delay 3 months or use bivalirudin); antiplatelet agents (aspirin modest increase, clopidogrel/prasugrel/ticagrelor stop 5–7 days; GP IIb/IIIa inhibitors: eptifibatide/tirofiban short-acting, abciximab delay 24–48 h). NOACs: stop 2–5 days preoperatively; no specific antidote.
*   **Transfusion triggers (STS guidelines):** Hb <7 g/dL (Hct <21%) in stable patients; higher threshold for end-organ ischemia. TRICC trial: restrictive strategy (Hb 7–9) equivalent to liberal (10–12) with lower mortality in younger/less ill patients.
*   **Postoperative coagulopathy:** Causes: residual heparin, thrombocytopenia, factor depletion, hypothermia, hemodilution. Reexploration criteria: >400 mL/h ×1 h, >300 mL/h ×2–3 h, >200 mL/h ×4 h, or tamponade signs.
*   **Autotransfusion:** Unwashed shed mediastinal blood viable; avoid in endocarditis, hemolytic disorders, or if topical hemostatics used.
*   **Recombinant factor VIIa:** For uncontrollable bleeding; significant thrombosis risk. PCCs for warfarin reversal; lower thrombosis risk than rFVIIa.

### Respiratory Care

*   **Postoperative pulmonary pathophysiology:** Atelectasis, edema, increased A-a gradient, shunt fraction, decreased compliance due to CPB-induced inflammatory response. Complement activation, neutrophil sequestration, cytokine release.
*   **Early extubation:** Goal for stable patients with normal mental status. Contraindications: preoperative pulmonary failure, uncompensated CHF, cardiogenic shock (IABP), DHCA, persistent hypothermia (<35.5°C), hypoxia (PaO₂:FiO₂ <200), acidosis (pH <7.30), persistent bleeding, CVA, reduced mental status.
*   **Ventilator weaning:** Spontaneous breathing trial (30 min with 5 cm H₂O pressure support or T-tube) most accurate predictor of successful extubation. Daily SBTs lead to extubation 2–3× earlier than IMV or PSV weaning.
*   **Tracheotomy:** Percutaneous dilatational tracheotomy in ICU safe; early PDT (within 48 h) reduces duration of mechanical ventilation, pneumonia, and mortality vs. delayed (14–16 days).

### Renal and Metabolic Support

*   **Acute renal failure incidence:** CABG 3.14% (0.87% require dialysis); mortality 63.7% with ARF requiring dialysis vs. 4.3% without.
*   **Risk factors:** Preoperative Cr >1.5 mg/dL increases stroke, bleeding, dialysis, prolonged ventilation, death. Mortality 11.4% in chronic dialysis patients; 32% 5-year survival.
*   **Management:** Maintain MAP >60 mm Hg, urine output >0.5 cc/kg/h. No convincing evidence for renoprotective effect of diuretics, mannitol, dopamine, fenoldapam, nesiritide.
*   **Electrolytes:** Hypocalcemia common post-CPB; treat if ionized Ca²⁺ <1.1 mmol/L. Hypokalemia aggressive treatment decreases perioperative arrhythmias. Hypomagnesemia (>70% of patients) increases AF and torsade de pointes risk.
*   **Glucose control:** NICE-Sugar Study: tight control (81–108 mg/dL) associated with higher mortality than conventional (target <180 mg/dL). STS recommends target <180 mg/dL.

### Postoperative Complications

*   **Stroke:** Incidence 1–4%; predictors: calcified aorta, prior stroke, age, carotid disease, CPB duration, renal failure, PVD, smoking, diabetes.
*   **Delirium:** 50% of patients; risk factors: preexisting organic mental disorders, alcohol, advanced age, intracranial disease. Dexmedetomidine causes less delirium than benzodiazepines.
*   **Mesenteric ischemia:** Catastrophic; risk factors: prolonged CPB, pressor use, IABP, AF, PVD, HIT. Mortality 48% if surgery <6 h; 99% if >6 h.
*   **Deep sternal wound infection (mediastinitis):** 1–2% incidence; mortality ~10%. Risk factors: obesity, diabetes, COPD, renal dysfunction, prolonged CPB, transfusion, bilateral IMA harvest, hyperglycemia. Treatment: debridement, IV antibiotics, muscle/omental flap, vacuum-assisted closure.
*   **Nutrition:** Cachectic patients (albumin <3.5 g/dL, weight loss >10%) at high risk. Postoperative requirements: 25–40 kcal/kg/day.

## Chapter 18: Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP)

*   **Physiology:** Reduces LV afterload (14–19% reduction in peak systolic wall stress), increases coronary perfusion pressure via diastolic augmentation. Inflation timed to aortic valve closure (dicrotic notch); deflation timed to R wave. Balloon positioned just distal to left subclavian artery.
*   **Indications:** Cardiogenic shock, uncontrolled ischemic pain, postcardiotomy low cardiac output, high-risk PCI, post-MI VSD or acute MR, bridge to transplant/LVAD. Prophylactic use in high-risk patients (LVEF <40%, left main stenosis >70%, redo CABG) debated.
*   **Insertion:** Percutaneous via common femoral artery (Seldinger technique) or surgical cut down. 7-Fr catheter without sheath for small vessels. Alternative sites: axillary artery (via 6-mm graft chimney), iliac artery, ascending aorta.
*   **Complications:** Leg ischemia (9–25% incidence), balloon rupture (1.7%), thrombosis, bleeding, infection, false aneurysm, lymphocele, femoral neuropathy. Risk factors: female sex, PVD, diabetes, smoking, advanced age, obesity, cardiogenic shock.
*   **IABP SHOCK II trial (2013):** 600 patients with AMI and cardiogenic shock; no difference in 30-day mortality (IABP 39.7% vs. control 41.3%) → downgraded guideline recommendations. Still considered first-line mechanical support.
*   **Subclavian IABP:** 6-mm graft to axillary artery allows ambulation while awaiting transplant/LVAD; qualifies as UNOS status 1A.

### Direct Circulatory Support

*   **Indications:** Cardiac index <2.2 L/min/m², SBP <90 mm Hg, PCWP/CVP >20 mm Hg, high-dose inotropes (≥2 agents). Postcardiotomy failure (0.2–0.6% incidence) with >50% mortality without support.
*   **Centrifugal pumps:** Medtronic Bio-Pump, Rotoflow. Provide high flows, afterload dependent. Complications: bleeding, renal failure (47%), stroke (10%), leg ischemia (70% with peripheral ECLS), hemolysis. Survival: 21–41% overall; weaning 45.7%, discharge 25.3%.

### Extracorporeal Life Support (ECLS/ECMO)

*   **Circuit:** Hollow-fiber membrane oxygenator, centrifugal pump, heat exchanger, heparin-bonded circuits. Peripheral cannulation (femoral artery/vein) for rapid deployment; distal limb perfusion cannula (8–10 Fr) reduces leg ischemia.
*   **Key differences from CPB:** Longer duration, lower heparin doses, no venous reservoir, reduced inflammatory response (though cytokines rise rapidly).
*   **Limitation:** Unloads RV but not LV; may increase LV afterload and wall stress in non-ejecting hearts → left-sided vent indicated if PCWP remains high.
*   **Outcomes:** Cleveland Clinic series (n=202): survival 76% at 3 days, 38% at 30 days, 24% at 5 years. Quadrox D oxygenator (PMP membrane) improved durability (exchange rate 64% → 7%) but survival unchanged (27–33%).
*   **Cardiohelp™:** Compact system with integrated oxygenator; low priming volume; facilitates transport. Series of 20 patients: 62% survival.

### Newer Percutaneous Devices

*   **TandemHeart:** Centrifugal pump; 21-Fr transseptal inflow cannula (left atrial drainage) → excellent LV decompression. Outflow via femoral artery. Flows up to 4 L (percutaneous) to 8 L (surgical cannulae). 30-day survival 59.8%; 6-month 54.7%. Off-label for >6 hours and with oxygenator.
*   **CentriMag:** Magnetically levitated centrifugal pump; minimal friction, low priming volume. Configurable for LVAD, RVAD, or BiVAD. FDA approval: 6 hours for LVAD, 30 days for RVAD. Flows >9 L. Outcomes: 30-day survival 69%, 1-year 49% (Columbia series, n=148). Biventricular CentriMag as bridge to decision: 56% 30-day survival, 14 transitioned to durable LVAD.
*   **Impella:** Microaxial pump across aortic valve (LV support) or across pulmonary valve (RV support). Versions: 2.5 (12 Fr, 2.5 L/min), CP (14 Fr, 4 L/min), 5.0 (21 Fr, 5 L/min, surgical cut down). RP (22 Fr, >4 L/min) for RV support. PROTECT II trial (n=452): Impella 2.5 vs. IABP in high-risk PCI → superior hemodynamic support, similar 30-day MACE, better 90-day outcomes in subgroup analysis.
*   **Heartmate PHP™:** 13-Fr introducer, expands to 24 Fr in LV; 4–5 L/min flow. CE mark approved; SHIELD II trial ongoing.

### Pulsatile Pumps

*   **ABIOMED AB5000:** Pneumatically driven; vacuum-assisted drainage; <3 months support. Inflow: atrial or ventricular (36-Fr malleable cannula). Outflow: 10-mm graft (PA) or 12-mm graft (aorta). Survival to recovery/transplant/D VAD: 42% (n=50).
*   **Thoratec pVAD:** Pneumatic, pulsatile; stroke volume 65 mL. Paracorporeal placement on anterior abdominal wall. Inflow: atrial (51-Fr) or ventricular; outflow: 14-mm (PA) or 18-mm (aorta). Approved for bridge to transplant (1995) and postcardiotomy (1998). Postcardiotomy survival: 28–60% depending on center experience. Use declining with smaller continuous-flow devices.

### Patient Management

*   **Ventilatory support:** Peak inspiratory pressure <35 cm H₂O; FiO₂ weaned to <50%; PEEP 5 cm H₂O initially.
*   **Anticoagulation:** Heparin infusion (8–10 μg/kg/h) targeting PTT 45–55 seconds after hemostasis. Platelets maintained >50,000/mm³ (>100,000 if bleeding). Fibrinogen >250 mg/dL.
*   **Fluid management:** Aggressive diuresis; continuous ultrafiltration/CVVHD if diuretic-resistant.
*   **Weaning:** Attempt after 48–72 hours; reduce flow gradually (0.5–1 L/min increments). Maintain ACT >300 seconds during low flow. If recovery absent, transition to durable VAD.

### Key Concepts

*   **Bridge to bridge:** Short-term device used to stabilize patient, then transition to durable VAD as bridge to transplant.
*   **Multidisciplinary approach:** Cardiogenic shock requires collaboration between surgery, cardiology, perfusion, critical care.
*   **Early recognition:** Failure to escalate from IABP to direct support when shock persists is associated with high mortality.

## Chapter 19: Myocardial Revascularization with Percutaneous Devices
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Balloon Angioplasty Principles

*   **Mechanism:** Balloon inflation overstretches the vessel wall, fracturing the inelastic intima. Dissection plane extension can cause abrupt closure (10% incidence). Overstretching without fracture results in early recoil.
*   **Healing response:** Thrombus accumulation → inflammatory cell colonization → intimal hyperplasia (peaks at 3 months) + negative remodeling (scar contracture) → restenosis in 40–50% of PTCA procedures.
*   **Procedural mortality:** ~1% at 1 month; 66% of deaths due to low-output failure.

### Tools for Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI)

*   **Guiding catheters:** Wire-braided with thin walls for larger lumen and rigidity. Shape selection based on aortic anatomy and coronary origin (Judkins, Amplatz, XB). Guideliner™ facilitates subselective delivery for challenging anatomy.
*   **Guidewires:** 0.0254–0.0356 cm diameter; 190–300 cm length. Softest tip safest; stiffer hydrophilic wires increase success in chronic total occlusions but increase dissection/perforation risk.
*   **Balloon catheters:** "On the wire" (best trackability, requires assistant) vs. "monorail" (shorter central lumen, single operator). Compliance categories: noncompliant, semicompliant, compliant. Compliant balloons have lowest deflated profile but may not uniformly expand under high pressure.

### Antithrombotic Therapy

*   **Unfractionated heparin (UFH):** Target ACT >300 seconds. Alternative anticoagulants: bivalirudin, enoxaparin, argatroban.
*   **Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban):** Impair platelet-platelet aggregation regardless of stimulus. Reduce ACT target to 200–250 seconds.
*   **Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT):** Aspirin + P2Y₁₂ inhibitor (clopidogrel, prasugrel, ticagrelor). Duration 6–12 months; indefinite for complex lesions or first-generation DES.

### Stents

*   **Bare-metal stents (BMS):** Reduce abrupt closure (scaffolding) and negative remodeling. Intimal hyperplasia (late loss ~0.8 mm) > PTCA (0.32 mm). Restenosis 20–24% at 1 year.
*   **Drug-eluting stents (DES):** Reduce late loss by 50–100%; target vessel failure (TVF) 9.9–10.8% at 1 year (vs. 20–24% with BMS). First-generation DES require ≥12 months DAPT due to delayed endothelialization and late stent thrombosis risk.
*   **Newer-generation DES:** Faster reendothelialization; DAPT duration as short as 6 months in stable patients.

### Specialized Devices

*   **Rotational atherectomy (Rotablator):** Diamond-chip olive-shaped burr for heavily calcified lesions. Risks: microembolization, perforation, no-reflow. Contraindicated in thrombotic lesions, recent MI, or last remaining patent vessel.
*   **Aspiration devices:** Remove thrombus/atheroma; improve flow in thrombus-laden lesions and saphenous vein grafts.
*   **Embolic protection devices:** Distal filters or balloon occlusion + aspiration. Reduce periprocedural MI from 14.7% to 8.6% in saphenous vein graft interventions.
*   **Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS):** Accurate vessel sizing, lesion assessment, stent optimization. Reduces repeat procedures by >30%.
*   **Fractional flow reserve (FFR):** Ratio of distal coronary pressure to aortic pressure after adenosine. FFR ≤0.75 identifies hemodynamically significant lesions. Routine use reduces adverse events and repeat procedures.

### Circulatory Support

*   **Risk score for support:** Score each of six arterial segments (LAD, D₁, S₁, OM, PLV, PDA): 1 point for target lesion or additional lesion with >70% stenosis; 0.5 points if subtended region hypokinetic. Score >3 → consider IABP.
*   **Intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP):** Reduces hypotension and complications in high-risk patients.
*   **Percutaneous LVAD (pLVAD):** Axial-flow pump providing up to 4 L/min. Superior hemodynamic support but limited by access-site complications; reserved for highest-risk patients.

### Complications

*   **Bleeding:** 0.5–4% incidence; reduced with radial artery access.
*   **Stroke:** 0.18%; risk factors: age, LV dysfunction, diabetes, SVG intervention, prolonged procedures, IABP use.
*   **Periprocedural MI:** CK-MB elevation in up to 38% (any elevation); >3× ULN definition used clinically. Elevation >3× ULN associated with increased mortality.
*   **No-reflow/slow-reflow:** Seen in SVG interventions, rotational atherectomy, primary PCI for STEMI. Treated with intracoronary verapamil, adenosine, nitroprusside.
*   **Coronary perforation:** 0.5% incidence; higher with ablation devices (1.3%). Classified I (extraluminal crater), II (pericardial/myocardial blushing), III (contrast streaming). Treatment: reversal of anticoagulation, prolonged balloon inflation, covered stent, surgery.
*   **Radiocontrast nephropathy (RCN):** 5–6% incidence. Prevention: iso-osmolar contrast, volume expansion, N-acetylcysteine (600 mg BID × 2 days).

### Special Circumstances

*   **Acute coronary syndromes (STEMI):** Primary PCI within 6 hours superior to thrombolytics. DES reduces adverse events (9.4% vs. 17% at 300 days). Distal embolization and no-reflow common.
*   **Previous CABG (saphenous vein grafts):** Stent + embolic protection device standard of care. 4-year survival 79%, but event-free survival only 29% due to disease progression in other graft segments.

### SYNTAX Score

*   **Purpose:** Quantify procedural risk and long-term success odds for PCI vs. CABG based on angiographic characteristics.
*   **Components:** Lesion characteristics (total occlusion, bifurcation/trifurcation, aorto-ostial, tortuosity, length >20 mm, calcification, thrombus, diffuse disease) and clinical factors (LV function, renal function, lung disease).
*   **Application:** Objective decision-making tool to match patient to optimal revascularization strategy.

## Chapter 20: Myocardial Revascularization with Cardiopulmonary Bypass
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Indications for Surgical Revascularization (2011 ACCF/AHA Guidelines)

*   **Survival benefit (Class I recommendations):** Unprotected left main disease, three-vessel disease (with or without proximal LAD involvement), two-vessel disease with proximal LAD involvement.
*   **Symptom relief (Class I, Level A):** Patients with ≥1 significant coronary stenosis and unacceptable angina despite guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT).
*   **Class IIa recommendations:** Two-vessel disease without proximal LAD but with extensive ischemia; one-vessel proximal LAD disease; moderate LV dysfunction (EF 35–50%); complex three-vessel CAD (SYNTAX score >22) in good CABG candidates (CABG preferred over PCI).
*   **Class IIb recommendations:** Two-vessel disease without proximal LAD or extensive ischemia; severe LV dysfunction (EF <35%) without left main disease.

### Clinical Assessment

*   **Angiographic significance:** ≥70% stenosis (non-left main); ≥50% for left main coronary artery (LMCA). SYNTAX score quantifies lesion complexity (length, stenosis, bifurcation/trifurcation, total occlusion, calcification, thrombus).
*   **Functional assessment:** Canadian Cardiovascular Society angina classification (I–IV). Stress ECG, perfusion imaging (thallium, technetium), stress echocardiography identify ischemia and viability.

### Comparative Trials

*   **CABG vs. medical therapy (historical trials—CASS, VA, ECSS):** Survival benefit greatest in patients at highest risk (severe angina, multivessel disease, LV dysfunction). Yusuf meta-analysis (7 trials) showed enhanced survival at 5, 7, 10 years for surgical patients at highest (4.8% annual mortality) and moderate (2.5%) risk.
*   **PCI vs. medical therapy:** No definitive survival benefit; COURAGE trial (2287 patients) showed no benefit for PCI over best medical therapy for death, MI, or major cardiovascular events.
*   **PCI vs. CABG:**
    *   Early trials (balloon angioplasty, BMS): Similar survival, similar MI rates, CABG superior for angina relief and freedom from repeat revascularization.
    *   SYNTAX trial (DES): Similar mortality/MI at 1 year; higher stroke in CABG (2.2% vs. 0.6%); higher reintervention in PCI (13.5% vs. 5.9%). CABG preferred for intermediate/high SYNTAX scores (23–32, ≥33).
    *   FREEDOM trial (diabetics): CABG superior to PCI with DES for composite endpoint (death, MI, stroke) at 5 years (18.7% vs. 26.6%).
    *   Recent meta-analysis (Sipahi et al., 2014): CABG associated with significantly lower mortality, MI, and repeat revascularization at 4.1 years weighted average follow-up.

### Special Circumstances

*   **Acute coronary syndromes:**
    *   STEMI: Emergency CABG indicated after unsuccessful/complicated PCI, mechanical complications (VSD, free wall rupture, papillary muscle rupture), cardiogenic shock, life-threatening arrhythmias with left main/three-vessel disease.
    *   Timing: Mortality peaks 7–24 hours post-MI; emergency CABG within 6 hours maximizes salvage; delay 3–4 days if after 6-hour window.
*   **Left main disease:** CABG remains Class I for most patients. PCI Class IIa for low SYNTAX score (≤22) with high surgical risk (STS mortality >5%); Class IIb for intermediate SYNTAX (<33) with STS mortality >2%.
*   **Severe LV dysfunction:** STICH trial (EF <35%) showed trend toward decreased mortality with CABG (36% vs. 41%, p = NS). CABG improved composite endpoints (death, heart failure hospitalization, revascularization). CABG Class IIa for EF 35–50%; Class IIb for EF <35%.
*   **Diabetes mellitus:** BARI, FREEDOM trials demonstrate CABG survival benefit over PCI, even with DES. Complete revascularization more often achieved surgically.
*   **End-stage renal disease (ESRD):** CABG with increased operative mortality (OR 3.1) but improved survival compared to medical management; CABG may confer improved survival over PCI in some studies.
*   **Concomitant carotid disease:** Carotid disease present in ~8% of CABG patients. Combined CABG + CEA Class IIa for patients with prior TIA/stroke and ≥50% stenosis. Multidisciplinary team approach recommended (Class I). Risk of perioperative stroke from aortic atheroma > carotid stenosis.

### Bypass Conduits

*   **Internal thoracic artery (ITA):** Superior patency: LITA-to-LAD 98% at 1 year, 91% at 4 years, 95% at 10 years, 88% at 15 years (vs. SVG 61% at 10 years). Pedicled vs. skeletonized: Skeletonization reduces sternal ischemia and wound complications (especially in diabetics) without compromising patency.
*   **Radial artery (RA):** Patency 98% at 5 years to ideal targets (≥90% stenosis). Vasospasm prevented with nitroglycerin/calcium channel blockers. Target stenosis <90% increases occlusion risk.
*   **Saphenous vein graft (SVG):** Patency 87% at 1 year, 83% at 4 years, 61% at 10 years. Failure mechanisms: thrombosis (first month), intimal hyperplasia (1 month–1 year), graft atherosclerosis (>5 years). Endoscopic harvest may increase failure rates; no-touch technique (pedicled harvest) improves patency.

### Operative Technique

*   **Median sternotomy:** Primary incision. Cannulation: distal ascending aorta (20–22 Fr), right atrial appendage (two-stage venous cannula). Heparin 300 U/kg, ACT >450 seconds.
*   **Myocardial protection:** Cold blood cardioplegia (10 mL/kg induction, 5 mL/kg q20 min). Antegrade ± retrograde. LV vent for aortic regurgitation.
*   **Distal anastomosis:** 7-0 polypropylene, continuous parachuting technique. Arteriotomy 1.5× conduit diameter. Coronary endarterectomy for diffusely diseased vessels (increased risk, 3-year patency 74–80%).
*   **Proximal anastomosis:** Single cross-clamp technique preferred (reduces aortic manipulation). 5-0 (vein) or 6-0 (artery) polypropylene.
*   **Composite grafts:** Y- or T-grafts using ITA as donor for free RA or other arterial grafts. Allows total arterial revascularization without aortic anastomoses.

### Outcomes

*   **Operative mortality:** Declined to 1.9% in 2009 (24.4% reduction from 2000). Predictors: multiple reoperations (OR 4.19), first reoperation (OR 2.76), shock (OR 2.04), renal failure/dialysis (OR 1.88), insulin-dependent diabetes (OR 1.5).
*   **Complication rates (STS, 1997–1999):** Stroke 1.6%, renal failure 3.5%, reoperation 5.2%, prolonged ventilation 5.9%, sternal infection 0.63%. Stroke predictors: advanced age, aortic atherosclerosis, prior neurologic disease, diabetes, unstable angina, hypotension.
*   **Long-term outcomes (KU Leuven, n=9600):** Freedom from angina: 95% at 1 year, 82% at 5 years, 61% at 10 years, 38% at 15 years. Freedom from MI: 97% at 30 days, 94% at 5 years, 86% at 10 years. Survival: 98% at 30 days, 92% at 5 years, 81% at 10 years, 66% at 15 years, 51% at 20 years.
*   **Bilateral ITA grafting:** Improves 10-year survival (86% vs. 71% for single ITA) and reoperation-free survival (77% vs. 62%) in retrospective studies.

## Chapter 21: Myocardial Revascularization Without Cardiopulmonary Bypass
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Rationale and Historical Context
*   **Off-pump CABG (OPCAB):** Developed to avoid the systemic inflammatory effects of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and aortic manipulation, which are linked to stroke, renal failure, and respiratory failure.
*   **Usage Trends:** OPCAB use peaked in 2002 (23%) and declined to approximately 17% by 2012, due to a lack of compelling evidence from large RCTs and concerns about incomplete revascularization.
*   **Proven Benefits:** RCTs consistently show reduced transfusion requirements, lower postoperative myocardial enzyme levels, and shorter hospital stays with OPCAB.

### Surgeon Experience and Patient Selection
*   **Technical Challenge:** OPCAB is an advanced technique requiring a dedicated surgical team and experienced anesthesia. It should be performed by surgeons with specialized training in high-volume centers.
*   **Learning Curve:** Early cases should be elective, with 1–3 grafts, good target anatomy, preserved ventricular function, and no complex lateral wall targets. Relative early exclusions include left main disease, severe LV dysfunction, and urgent/emergent cases.
*   **Anesthesia Management:** Key principles include Trendelenburg positioning for autotransfusion, judicious use of alpha-adrenergic agents, close communication, and avoiding massive fluid boluses.

### Surgical Technique and Exposure
*   **Epiaortic Ultrasound:** Essential for assessing ascending aortic atherosclerosis to guide clamping strategy. Grades 3–5 (moderate to mobile plaque) mandate a no-touch or clampless technique to prevent atheroembolism.
*   **Cardiac Positioning:** Suction-based positioners (Starfish, XPOSE) and stabilizers (Octopus, ACROBAT) allow heart displacement without compression, minimizing hemodynamic compromise.
*   **Key Exposure Maneuvers:** The "deep stitch" (pericardial traction suture) elevates the heart to expose lateral and inferior walls. The right pericardium must be relaxed to prevent right heart compression.

### Grafting Sequence and Hemodynamic Management
*   **Collateralized Vessels First:** Graft the vessel receiving collaterals before the donor vessel (e.g., graft PDA before LAD if PDA is collateralized) to avoid global ischemia.
*   **Hemodynamic Instability:** If hypotension occurs during anastomosis, gently relax the positioner, use steep Trendelenburg, administer inotropes/vasopressors, or place an intracoronary shunt. Elective conversion to CPB is safe; emergent conversion carries high mortality (6–15%).
*   **Proximal Anastomoses:** Can be performed with a side-biting clamp (if aorta is disease-free) or clampless devices (Heartstring III, PAS-Port) to avoid aortic manipulation.

### Outcomes: Mortality and Morbidity
*   **Operative Mortality:** Large RCTs (ROOBY, CORONARY, GOPCABE) show no difference in 30-day or 1-year mortality between OPCAB and ONCAB. However, large retrospective registries (STS, New York) suggest a risk-adjusted mortality benefit with OPCAB, especially in high-risk patients.
*   **Stroke:** Retrospective studies and meta-analyses show a 30% relative risk reduction with OPCAB. Stroke risk decreases progressively with less aortic manipulation: no-touch aorta (0.6%) < clampless device (1.2%) < side-biting clamp (1.5%). This benefit was not consistently seen in RCTs, possibly due to unstandardized proximal anastomotic techniques.
*   **Renal Failure:** OPCAB may reduce renal morbidity, particularly in patients with pre-existing renal dysfunction or diabetes, though data remain controversial.
*   **Perioperative Morbidity:** OPCAB consistently reduces atrial fibrillation (OR 0.58), transfusion requirements (OR 0.43), inotrope use, respiratory infections, and ICU/hospital length of stay.

### Revascularization Completeness and Graft Patency
*   **Fewer Grafts:** OPCAB is consistently associated with a small but statistically significant reduction in the number of grafts performed (approximately 0.1–0.2 fewer grafts per patient) and higher rates of incomplete revascularization in RCTs.
*   **Patency Concerns:** The ROOBY Trial reported lower 1-year graft patency with OPCAB (82.6% vs. 87.8%), driven by vein graft failure. However, experienced centers (SMART Trial) achieve equivalent patency (93.6% vs. 95.8%).
*   **Intraoperative Flow Measurement:** Transit-time Doppler flow (Medistim) with flow >15 mL/min, pulsatility index <5, and diastolic fraction >50% predicts good graft patency. Values outside these ranges warrant graft revision.

### High-Risk Subgroups and Centers of Excellence
*   **High-Risk Patients:** OPCAB confers disproportionate benefit in patients with LV dysfunction (EF <30%), advanced age, renal insufficiency, prior stroke, reoperative surgery, and high STS predicted risk scores.
*   **Surgeon Volume:** Mortality decreases with increasing OPCAB volume, with thresholds for improvement at 50 cases/year and optimal outcomes above 150 cases/year.
*   **Centers of Excellence:** OPCAB should be centralized to expert surgeons at high-volume centers, incorporating minimally invasive, hybrid, and total arterial revascularization strategies.

### Minimally Invasive and Hybrid Approaches
*   **Minimally Invasive CABG:** Sternal-sparing techniques (MIDCAB, MVST, robotic TECAB) utilize off-pump principles. MIDCAB for LAD disease shows superior long-term outcomes compared to DES-PCI.
*   **Hybrid Coronary Revascularization (HCR):** Combines LIMA-LAD grafting (surgical) with PCI to non-LAD lesions. HCR reduces transfusion and improves recovery but is associated with higher repeat revascularization rates (12.2% vs. 3.7% in one study).

### Guideline Recommendations
*   **2011 ACCF/AHA:** OPCAB to reduce bleeding/transfusion (Class IIa, A) and for renal dysfunction (Class IIb, B).
*   **2014 ESC/EACTS:** OPCAB in high-risk patients at experienced centers (Class IIa, B); OPCAB in patients with ascending aortic atherosclerosis (Class I, B); routine intraoperative graft flow measurement (Class IIa, C).

## Chapter 22: Myocardial Revascularization after Acute Myocardial Infarction
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Pathophysiology and Myocardial States
- **Acute Coronary Occlusion:** Within 60 seconds of occlusion, the ischemic zone shifts from active systolic shortening to passive lengthening. Reperfusion after <20 minutes causes reversible stunning; after >40 minutes, salvageable myocardium decreases from 60–70% to ~10% by 3 hours.
- **Infarcted Myocardium:** Nonviable tissue due to prolonged ischemia; irreversible, does not benefit from revascularization.
- **Hibernating Myocardium:** A viable but chronically hypoperfused state with depressed contractility. Function can improve promptly after revascularization. Present in up to 75% of unstable angina patients.
- **Stunned Myocardium:** Post-ischemic ventricular dysfunction in viable tissue after reperfusion. Recovery is delayed but can be complete. The primary mechanism is generation of oxygen-derived free radicals.

### Diagnosis of Viable Myocardium
- **PET** is the best method for determining viability, especially in severe left ventricular dysfunction.
- **Dobutamine echocardiography** has high specificity and positive predictive value for identifying hibernating and stunned myocardium.
- **Cardiac MRI** offers superior resolution to identify transmural infarction, microvascular damage (no-reflow), and predicts functional recovery.

### Reperfusion Strategies and Guidelines
- **Goal:** Reperfusion within 12 hours of symptom onset. Time delay exponentially increases 1-year mortality.
- **Thrombolytics:** Indicated for STEMI when FMC-to-device time at a PCI-capable hospital is >120 minutes. Should be given within 30 minutes of hospital arrival. Patency rates are 50–85%.
- **Primary PCI:** Preferred reperfusion strategy when it can be performed in a timely fashion (FMC-to-device goal of ≤90 minutes). Superior to thrombolytics in reducing mortality, reinfarction, and stroke.
- **CABG:** Indicated for patients with coronary anatomy not amenable to PCI, mechanical complications, cardiogenic shock, or ongoing ischemia. Optimal timing: delay surgery for 3–7 days in transmural MI to reduce mortality risk, which is highest in the first 6–23 hours.

### Cardiogenic Shock
- **Definition:** Systolic BP <90 mm Hg with signs of hypoperfusion (CI <2.2 L/min/m², PCWP >18 mm Hg). Occurs with >40% left ventricular mass loss. Mortality remains ~80% without aggressive intervention.
- **Management:** Early revascularization (PCI or CABG) within 12 hours is a Class I recommendation and improves survival. The SHOCK trial demonstrated a 6-year survival benefit for early revascularization (32.8%) over initial medical stabilization (19.6%).
- **Mechanical Circulatory Support (MCS):** IABP use has been downgraded (Class IIa) after the IABP-Shock II trial showed no mortality benefit. VA-ECMO and percutaneous VADs (Impella, TandemHeart) are used for stabilization, end-organ preservation, and as a bridge to recovery or durable LVAD/transplant.

## Chapter 23: Minimally Invasive Myocardial Revascularization
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Off-Pump Coronary Artery Bypass (OPCAB) Grafting
*   **Definition:** CABG performed on a beating heart without cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).
*   **Rationale:** Avoids the systemic inflammatory response, cytokine release, clotting cascade activation, and microembolization associated with CPB.
*   **Technique:** Relies on vacuum-assisted stabilizers for local myocardial immobilization, pericardial stay sutures for heart positioning, and careful anesthesia to manage hemodynamic changes.
*   **Key Outcomes:** Meta-analyses show a 30% reduction in postoperative stroke with OPCAB. It is associated with reduced acute kidney injury, lower transfusion rates, and shorter hospital stay. Results are best in high-risk patients (elderly, low EF). Graft patency rates are variable in trials, with some showing inferiority and others equivalence to on-pump CABG. The CORONARY and GOPCABE trials showed no difference in composite clinical outcomes at 1 year.

### Minimally Invasive Direct Coronary Artery Bypass (MIDCAB)
*   **Definition:** A beating-heart CABG performed through a left anterolateral minithoracotomy, avoiding sternotomy.
*   **Primary Indication:** Revascularization of the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, typically with the left internal thoracic artery (LITA).
*   **Results:** Excellent early patency rates (94–98%) and long-term outcomes, with 10-year survival around 77%. Stroke rates are very low due to avoidance of aortic manipulation. Compared to PCI for proximal LAD disease, MIDCAB has lower target vessel revascularization rates and comparable hard endpoints (death, MI) at 10 years.

### Total Endoscopic Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (TECAB)
*   **Definition:** A fully endoscopic CABG performed with telemanipulation (robotic) systems.
*   **Technique:** Uses port-access and a computer-enhanced system (e.g., da Vinci) to overcome the limitations of conventional endoscopic instruments.
*   **Status:** Technically demanding with a long learning curve. Currently restricted to single-vessel (LAD) grafting in select centers.

### Hybrid Coronary Revascularization (HCR)
*   **Definition:** Combines minimally invasive surgical LITA-LAD grafting with PCI to non-LAD vessels.
*   **Goal:** Achieves complete revascularization while minimizing surgical trauma.
*   **Indications:** High-risk patients with comorbidities, those with acute coronary syndromes requiring PCI of a non-LAD culprit lesion, or patient preference after thorough discussion.
*   **Outcomes:** Feasible and safe, with shorter hospital stays compared to OPCAB. Mid-term results show MACCE rates comparable to CABG and lower than PCI, driven by superior LAD outcomes.

### Endoscopic Conduit Harvest
*   **Rationale:** Minimizes wound complications, pain, and scarring associated with open harvest of the saphenous vein and radial artery.
*   **Endoscopic Vein Harvest:** Reduces wound complications by up to 69% compared to the open technique, with no proven difference in graft patency in most studies.
*   **Endoscopic Radial Artery Harvest:** Associated with lower wound infection rates and improved patient satisfaction, with comparable graft integrity and patency.

## Chapter 24: Coronary Artery Reoperations
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Incidence and Changing Population
- **Incidence:** Cumulative incidence of reoperation is 3% at 5 years, 10% at 10 years, and 25% at 20 years after primary bypass.
- **Trend:** Reoperative procedures now represent only ~4.6% of isolated coronary bypass operations, down from 30% in 2002.
- **Patient Profile:** Today, patients typically had a successful primary operation ≥10 years ago and present with late graft failure (vein graft atherosclerosis) combined with progression of native-vessel distal CAD.

### Graft Failure Pathology
- **Vein Graft Atherosclerosis:** A distinct, friable, superficial lesion often associated with mural thrombus. Develops 3–4 years postoperatively and accounts for most late SVG stenoses. Poses a significant risk of distal coronary embolization during reoperation or PCI.
- **Intimal Fibroplasia:** A concentric, diffuse, non-friable process occurring 2–3 months after operation; rarely causes stenosis.
- **ITA Grafts:** Rarely develop late atherosclerosis and have extremely high late patency rates. Their use significantly decreases the risk of reoperation and death.

### Indications for Reoperation
- **Survival Benefit:** Strongly indicated for patients with late (≥5 years) stenoses in vein grafts supplying the LAD, especially when combined with multivessel disease or LV dysfunction.
- **Symptom Relief:** Effective for early vein graft stenoses or patients with patent ITA-LAD grafts and other ischemia-producing pathology.
- **Alternative (PCI):** Preferred for early vein graft stenoses, focal late SVG stenoses not supplying the LAD, or native coronary stenoses, particularly when conduits are limited.

### Technical Challenges and Strategies
- **Sternal Reentry:** High-risk situations include patent grafts adherent to sternum, RV/aortic enlargement, and multiple previous operations. Strategies: leave wires in place during sternal division, use right thoracotomy for dissection, or cannulate before sternotomy.
- **Myocardial Protection:** Combination of antegrade and retrograde cold blood cardioplegia is essential. Retrograde delivery avoids atheroembolism from vein grafts and protects areas supplied by in situ arterial grafts.
- **Management of Atherosclerotic Vein Grafts:** Avoid manipulation. Leave them in place when adding an arterial graft to the same vessel to prevent hypoperfusion. Replace with new vein grafts when removing them.
- **Conduit Strategies:** Use composite arterial grafts (e.g., RITA to LITA) to avoid aortic anastomoses. The radial artery and RGEA are valuable alternatives.

### Results
- **Early Mortality:** Current in-hospital mortality for reoperation is ~3.5%, compared to 1.5% for primary operations. Risk is substantially higher for emergency reoperations.
- **Late Survival:** 10-year survival for hospital survivors is ~69%. Predictors of decreased late survival: LV dysfunction, advanced age, smoking, hypertension, and left main or triple-vessel disease.
- **Multiple Reoperations:** Higher risk, but long-term outcomes remain favorable.

## Chapter 25: Surgical Treatment of Complications of Myocardial Infarction, Ventricular Septal Defect, Myocardial Rupture, and Left Ventricular Aneurysm
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Postinfarction Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD)
- **Incidence:** Occurs in 1–2% of acute MIs, typically 3–5 days post-infarction. Anterior infarctions account for 70% of cases.
- **Pathophysiology:** Transmural septal infarction leads to left-to-right shunting, often with significant right ventricular infarction, especially in inferior MIs. This results in low cardiac output and cardiogenic shock.
- **Natural History:** Without surgery, mortality is 50% at 1 week and 80% at 4 weeks.
- **Surgical Principles:** Repair via left ventriculotomy through the infarct zone. Infarctectomy (Daggett) or infarct exclusion (David) techniques are used. Closure requires prosthetic patches and buttressed sutures to avoid cutting through friable tissue.
- **Outcomes:** Operative mortality is 30–50%, with higher risk in older patients, those in shock, and those with inferior defects. For hospital survivors, 5-year survival is 40–60%. Recurrent or residual VSD occurs in 10–25%.

### Acute Ventricular Free Wall Rupture
- **Definition:** A catastrophic complication involving transmural infarction and myocardial tear, leading to tamponade and electromechanical dissociation.
- **Natural History:** Most patients die within minutes. Subacute forms may be contained by clot and pericardium, progressing to pseudoaneurysm.
- **Surgical Management:** Emergency operation is mandatory. Techniques include Teflon felt patch closure with or without biological glue, often with rapid institution of CPB (preferably via femoral vessels) before sternotomy.
- **Outcomes:** Operative mortality remains high (~50%), with limited small series reported.

### Left Ventricular Aneurysm
- **Definition:** A discrete area of abnormal diastolic contour with systolic dyskinesia or akinesia, typically following transmural anterior infarction. True aneurysms contain all layers of the wall; pseudoaneurysms are contained ruptures.
- **Natural History:** Asymptomatic patients have a 10-year survival of ~90%; symptomatic patients have a 10-year survival of ~46%. Causes of death: arrhythmia (44%), heart failure (33%).
- **Surgical Indications:** Angina, congestive heart failure, ventricular arrhythmias, pseudoaneurysm, or documented expansion. Asymptomatic aneurysms generally do not require repair.
- **Surgical Techniques:**
    - **Linear Repair:** Excision of aneurysm with linear closure over felt strips. Simpler but may distort ventricular geometry.
    - **Circular Patch Repair:** For inferior/posterior aneurysms, using a Dacron patch to close the defect.
    - **Endoventricular Patch (Dor procedure):** Intracavitary patch to exclude the infarct, preserving geometry and the LAD. Increasingly preferred.
- **Outcomes:** Operative mortality is 3–7% in modern series. The STICH trial showed no survival benefit for SVR added to CABG in patients with moderate LV dysfunction, but post-hoc analysis suggests benefit if postoperative LVESVI < 70 mL/m².

## Chapter 26: Pathophysiology of Aortic Valve Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Anatomy and Mechanics
- **Structure:** The normal AV has three semilunar leaflets (right, left, posterior coronary cusps) attached in a crown-like fashion, with the sinuses of Valsalva above each cusp. The leaflets consist of three layers: fibrosa (collagen), spongiosa (loose CT), and ventricularis (elastic fibers).
- **Function:** AV opens and closes passively in response to pressure gradients. Aortic root dilation precedes opening; vortices in the sinuses of Valsalva initiate closure. The valve presents minimal resistance to ejection.

### Aortic Stenosis (AS)
- **Etiology:** Most common valvular disease in adults. Causes: degenerative calcific (most common, age-related, shares risk factors with atherosclerosis), bicuspid AV (2% of population, presents in 5th–6th decade), and rheumatic (rare, usually with mitral disease).
- **Pathophysiology:** Progressive leaflet calcification → reduced valve area (normal 3–4 cm²). Severe AS: AVA < 1.0 cm², mean gradient > 40 mm Hg, jet velocity > 4.0 m/s. Concentric LV hypertrophy develops, leading to diastolic dysfunction, reduced coronary reserve, and subendocardial ischemia.
- **Natural History:** Long asymptomatic period; once symptoms (angina, syncope, heart failure) develop, mortality is 25%/year without surgery. Average survival: angina 5 years, syncope 3 years, heart failure 2 years.
- **Management:** AVR is definitive treatment. TAVR for high-risk/prohibitive-risk patients. No effective medical therapy; vasodilators are generally contraindicated. Asymptomatic patients with very severe AS (jet velocity > 4.5 m/s, rapid progression, or abnormal exercise test) may benefit from early surgery.

### Aortic Regurgitation (AR)
- **Etiology:** Leaflet pathology (bicuspid, endocarditis, rheumatic, myxomatous) or aortic root disease (aortic dissection, Marfan, hypertension, aortitis).
- **Pathophysiology:**
    - **Acute AR:** Sudden volume overload → rapid rise in LVEDP → pulmonary edema, cardiogenic shock. Poorly tolerated; requires emergent surgery.
    - **Chronic AR:** Eccentric LV hypertrophy and dilation maintain forward stroke volume for years. Wide pulse pressure is classic. Eventually, afterload mismatch and fibrosis lead to systolic dysfunction.
- **Indications for Surgery:** Symptoms, LVEF ≤ 50%, or progressive LV dilation (LVESD ≥ 50 mm, LVEDD ≥ 65 mm) in asymptomatic patients. Valve repair is preferred over replacement when feasible.

## Chapter 27: Aortic Valve Replacement with a Mechanical Cardiac Valve Prosthesis
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Historical Evolution
- **First-generation:** Ball-and-cage valves (Starr-Edwards) introduced in the 1960s, durable for up to 40 years but required intense anticoagulation and had suboptimal hemodynamics.
- **Second-generation:** Tilting disc valves (Bjork-Shiley, Medtronic Hall) offered lower profile but had issues with stasis and leaflet dysfunction.
- **Third-generation:** Bileaflet valves (St. Jude Medical, CarboMedics, ATS, On-X) introduced in 1977, now dominate the market. They offer excellent hemodynamics, low thromboembolism rates, and simplified implantation. The SJM Regent valve further improved effective orifice area by supra-annular design.

### Patient Selection
- **Indications:** Younger patients (<60 years), reoperations, patients already requiring chronic anticoagulation, those with good medical infrastructure and compliance.
- **Contraindications:** Noncompliance, transient lifestyle, dangerous hobbies, inability to manage anticoagulation.
- **Age Considerations:** Bioprostheses often preferred in patients >70 years; mechanical valves favored in patients <60 years. For patients aged 60–70 years, individual factors and patient preference guide decision-making.

### Surgical Technique
- Standard transverse aortotomy, annular decalcification, and interrupted pledgeted mattress sutures.
- Bileaflet valves are seated supra-annularly; orientation of pivot guards toward coronary ostia is important.
- For small aortic roots, the Regent valve allows implantation of a larger effective orifice area without annular enlargement.

### Anticoagulation Management
- **Target INR:** Individualized based on risk factors. Normal low-risk patients: INR 1.6–2.0 + aspirin. Single risk factor: INR 2.0–2.5 + aspirin. Multiple risk factors or atrial fibrillation: INR 2.5–3.5.
- **Key Principles:** Slow initiation to avoid supratherapeutic levels; home monitoring improves time in therapeutic range; aspirin is recommended adjunct unless contraindicated.
- **Bleeding Risk:** Anticoagulation-related hemorrhage is the most common complication, with 40% of events occurring in the first year.

### Outcomes
- **Structural Failure:** Virtually none with modern bileaflet valves in up to 25 years of follow-up.
- **Thromboembolism:** 0.8–2.3% per patient-year; similar to bioprostheses.
- **Anticoagulation-Related Hemorrhage:** 1.0–2.5% per patient-year; most common in first postoperative year.
- **Valve Thrombosis:** <0.3% per patient-year, usually due to inadequate anticoagulation.
- **Reoperation:** <2% at 25 years for mechanical valves, compared to much higher rates for bioprostheses after 10–15 years.
- **Patient-Prosthesis Mismatch:** Not a clinically significant issue with modern bileaflet mechanical valves.

### Special Considerations
- **Younger Patients:** Low event rates; excellent long-term survival (~88% at 20 years). Durability is paramount.
- **Stopping Anticoagulation:** Limited data support antiplatelet-only therapy in highly selected patients, but this remains investigational.

## Chapter 28: Stented Bioprosthetic Aortic Valve Replacement
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Natural History and Indications for Aortic Stenosis (AS)
- **Prevalence:** Degenerative calcific AS is most common in patients >70 years; bicuspid AS in those <70 years. Prevalence increases with age: 0.6% (55–64), 1.4% (65–74), 4.6% (≥75).
- **Symptomatic AS:** Onset of angina, syncope, or heart failure is a Class I indication for AVR. Average survival without surgery: angina 4 years, syncope 3 years, heart failure 2 years.
- **Asymptomatic Severe AS:** High-risk subsets (very severe AS: jet velocity ≥5 m/s, rapid progression, exercise-induced symptoms, LVEF <50%) benefit from early surgery.
- **Low-Gradient AS (LGAS):** True LGAS with contractile reserve benefits from AVR despite higher operative risk. Paradoxical LGAS (low-flow, normal EF) also warrants AVR when valve obstruction is the likely cause of symptoms.
- **Guidelines (2014 ACC/AHA):** AVR is recommended for all symptomatic severe AS (Stage D), asymptomatic severe AS with LVEF <50% (Stage C2), and patients undergoing other cardiac surgery. It is reasonable for very severe AS (Stage C1), exercise-induced symptoms, and low-flow/low-gradient AS with contractile reserve.

### Natural History and Indications for Aortic Regurgitation (AR)
- **Acute AR:** Life-threatening, caused by aortic dissection, endocarditis, or trauma. Urgent surgery is indicated due to poor tolerance of acute volume overload.
- **Chronic AR:** Volume overload leads to eccentric LV hypertrophy and dilation. Surgery is indicated for symptoms, LVEF ≤50%, or severe LV dilation (LVESD ≥50 mm, LVEDD ≥65 mm). Asymptomatic patients with normal LV function can be monitored until these thresholds are reached.

### Surgical Technique
- **Myocardial Protection:** Combination of antegrade (direct ostial) and retrograde cardioplegia ensures protection, especially in patients with AR, severe CAD, or small nondominant RCA.
- **Valve Excision and Implantation:** Transverse or oblique aortotomy; careful decalcification respecting conduction system (right-non commissure) and mitral-aortic continuity. Interrupted pledgeted sutures, supra-annular placement when possible.
- **Concomitant Procedures:** CABG for significant coronary stenosis (≥70%). Ascending aorta replacement when diameter exceeds 4.5 cm in patients undergoing AVR.

### Stented Bioprosthesis Devices
- **Generation:** First-generation (high-pressure fixed); second-generation (low/zero-pressure fixed: Hancock II, Perimount); third-generation (anti-mineralization, lower profile: Mosaic Ultra, Magna Ease, Mitroflow, Trifecta).
- **Hemodynamics:** Third-generation valves offer improved effective orifice areas (EOA). Small differences exist, but no valve has demonstrated superior survival or LV mass regression in head-to-head trials.

### Outcomes
- **Operative Mortality:** Isolated AVR: 3.2% (STS 2002–2006). Combined AVR/CABG: 5.6%. Outcomes are improving, especially in moderate- and high-risk patients.
- **Long-Term Survival:** Median survival after AVR: 65–69 years: 12.8 years; 70–79 years: 9.2 years; ≥80 years: 6.2 years. Survival is reduced by severe lung disease, renal failure, and LV dysfunction.
- **Structural Valve Deterioration (SVD):** For second-generation valves, freedom from SVD is ~90% at 12 years, but falls to ~70% at 15 years. Younger patients (<60 years) have accelerated SVD.
- **Antithrombotic Therapy:** Warfarin (INR 2–3) for first 3 months post-op (Class IIa), then aspirin alone (Class IIa). NOACs are contraindicated in prosthetic valves.
- **Prosthesis-Patient Mismatch (PPM):** Severe PPM (iEOA ≤0.65 cm²/m²) is associated with increased mortality and should be avoided, particularly in patients with LV dysfunction.

### Special Considerations
- **Prosthesis Selection:** Mechanical valves preferred for patients <60 years, bioprostheses for >70 years. Shared decision-making is critical for patients aged 60–70. Absolute anticoagulation requirements favor mechanical valves; contraindications favor bioprostheses.
- **TAVR:** Indicated for inoperable patients (Class I) and high-risk operable patients (Class IIa). Decision-making requires a multidisciplinary Heart Team.

## Chapter 29: Stentless Aortic Valve and Root Replacement
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Historical Context and Current Options
- **Homografts:** First used in 1962 (Ross, Barratt-Boyes). Cryopreservation became standard in 1975. Offer excellent hemodynamics and low endocarditis risk but limited availability and durability inversely related to recipient age.
- **Porcine Xenograft (Freestyle):** Third-generation tissue valve (zero-pressure fixation, AOA treatment), available "off the shelf" since 1998. Provides excellent hemodynamics and serves as a complete root replacement.
- **Pulmonary Autograft (Ross Procedure):** Living autologous valve, introduced by Ross in 1967. Offers ideal hemodynamics, no anticoagulation, and potential for long-term durability in young patients, but involves a double-valve operation with two potential failure points.

### Homograft and Porcine Root
- **Indications:** Class I for active aortic valve endocarditis with root abscess. Also suitable for small aortic roots, older patients (>60 years), and when anticoagulation is contraindicated.
- **Implant Techniques:** Subcoronary (more demanding, higher long-term reoperation rate) and full root replacement (preferred for endocarditis, small roots, and better long-term durability).
- **Outcomes:** Operative mortality 1–5% for elective cases, 8–16% for active endocarditis. Hemodynamics excellent (mean gradients 5–6 mm Hg). Freedom from reoperation ~90% at 10 years, falling to ~70% at 15–20 years. Homograft and Freestyle have similar mid-term results.

### Ross Procedure (Pulmonary Autograft)
- **Patient Selection:** Ideal for patients <50 years with life expectancy >20–25 years. Contraindications: significant pulmonary valve disease, connective tissue disorders, complex coronary anomalies.
- **Technique:** Full root replacement with autograft, external reinforcement (felt, fabric jacket) to prevent late autograft dilatation. RVOT reconstructed with pulmonary homograft.
- **Outcomes:** Operative mortality 1–3% in experienced centers. Long-term survival approximates age-matched normal population. Freedom from autograft reoperation ~75–88% at 20 years; freedom from homograft reoperation ~80–89% at 20 years. The Ross provides superior exercise hemodynamics compared to other bioprostheses.
- **Reoperation:** Safe in experienced centers with low mortality for elective cases. Options include valve-sparing root repair, Bentall procedure, or transcatheter valve-in-valve for failed homografts.

## Chapter 30: Aortic Valve Repair and Aortic Valve-Sparing Operations
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Functional Anatomy of the Aortic Root
- **Components:** The aortic root consists of the aortoventricular junction (annulus), aortic cusps, aortic sinuses (of Valsalva), and sinotubular junction. The triangles beneath the commissures are also critical for valve function.
- **Annulus:** A scalloped fibrous structure attached 45% to ventricular myocardium and 55% to fibrous structures (mitral valve, membranous septum). The bundle of His lies beneath the membranous septum.
- **Cusps:** Semilunar shape with base length 1.5× free margin length. Three cusps (left, right, noncoronary) with coaptation during diastole. Noncoronary cusp is largest; left is smallest.
- **Sinotubular Junction:** Commissures lie immediately below it; changes in its diameter directly affect cusp coaptation.

### Aortic Valve and Root Pathology
- **Aortic Stenosis:** Dystrophic/calcific degeneration in older adults; bicuspid valve (0.5–1.5% of population) is the most common congenital cause, often leading to stenosis or regurgitation.
- **Aortic Insufficiency (AR):** Can result from cusp pathology (bicuspid, endocarditis, rheumatic) or aortic root pathology (aneurysm, dissection, connective tissue disorders).
- **Aortic Root Aneurysm:** Often due to cystic medial degeneration. Associated with Marfan syndrome (FBN1 mutation, TGF-β dysregulation), Loeys-Dietz syndrome (TGFBR1/2 mutations, high early rupture risk), Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (COL3A1 mutation), and bicuspid aortic valve.
- **Genetic Syndromes:** Marfan (revised Ghent criteria, aortic root dilation), Loeys-Dietz (hypertelorism, bifid uvula, arterial tortuosity, early dissection), vascular Ehlers-Danlos (spontaneous rupture of medium arteries).

### Natural History and Indications
- **Aortic Stenosis:** Asymptomatic patients have good prognosis. Once symptoms (angina, syncope, heart failure) develop, survival is 2–3 years without surgery.
- **Aortic Insufficiency:** Symptomatic patients have poor prognosis (4 years with angina, 2 years with heart failure). Chronic AR is tolerated for years until LV dysfunction develops.
- **Aortic Root Aneurysm:** Risk of rupture/dissection increases with size. For ascending aorta, annual risk: <4 cm: 8.8%; 4–4.9 cm: 9.5%; 5–5.9 cm: 17.8%; ≥6 cm: 27.9%. Surgery recommended at 5.0–5.5 cm (lower for Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, bicuspid with risk factors).

### Techniques of Aortic Valve Repair
- **Cusp Perforation:** Patch repair with fresh or glutaraldehyde-fixed autologous pericardium.
- **Cusp Prolapse:** Shortened by plication along the nodule of Arantius.
- **Stress Fenestration:** Free margin reinforced with double-layer 6-0 ePTFE suture.
- **Bicuspid Aortic Valve:** Excision of raphe, shortening of prolapsed cusp, and subcommissural annuloplasty. Reimplantation may be preferred for dilated annulus.

### Aortic Valve-Sparing Operations
- **Ascending Aortic Aneurysm with AR:** Sinotubular junction reconstruction with tubular Dacron graft. May require cusp prolapse repair or noncoronary sinus replacement.
- **Remodeling of the Aortic Root:** Excision of sinuses, creation of neoaortic sinuses in graft, suspension of commissures. Suitable for patients with normal aortic annulus and asymmetric sinus dilation.
- **Reimplantation of the Aortic Valve (David Procedure):** Valve reimplanted inside a tubular graft with annular fixation. Ideal for annuloaortic ectasia, genetic syndromes, and acute type A dissection. Prevents late annular dilation.

### Results
- **Ascending Aortic Aneurysm with AR:** 10-year survival 54 ± 7%; freedom from reoperation 98%; freedom from moderate/severe AR 80 ± 7% (David series).
- **Aortic Root Aneurysm (David series):** 18-year survival 76.8%; freedom from moderate/severe AR 78%; freedom from reoperation 94.8%. Results similar for remodeling and reimplantation.
- **General Principle:** Aortic valve repair and valve-sparing operations provide excellent long-term outcomes in experienced centers but are technically demanding.

## Chapter 31: Surgical Treatment of Aortic Valve Endocarditis
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Predisposing Factors
- **Incidence:** Native aortic valve IE: 1.7–7.0 episodes per 100,000 person-years. Prosthetic valve IE: 0.2–1.4 episodes per 100 patient-years. Early prosthetic valve IE (<1 year) occurs in ~1.4% of AVR patients.
- **Predisposing Lesions:** Bicuspid aortic valve (most common), degenerative calcific stenosis, rheumatic disease, prosthetic valves. Normal valves can be infected by highly virulent organisms.

### Pathogenesis and Pathology
- **Pathogenesis:** Circulating organisms adhere to damaged endocardium with platelet-fibrin thrombi (nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis), forming vegetations (biofilm). Organisms produce enzymes causing tissue destruction.
- **Invasive Disease:** Progression from cellulitis to abscess to pseudoaneurysm. Aortic root invasion is particularly extensive due to high pressure. Consequence: heart block (invasion of triangle of Koch), fistulas, perforations.
- **Types of Infection:**
    - **Native valve:** Vegetations on cusps; may cause "kissing lesions" on anterior mitral leaflet.
    - **Bioprosthetic valve:** Involves cusps and/or sewing ring.
    - **Mechanical valve:** Usually involves sewing ring, causing dehiscence.
    - **Allograft:** May start on cusps or suture lines.

### Microbiology
- **Native Valve:** *Staphylococcus aureus* (virulent, acute) and *Streptococcus viridans* (subacute) are most common. Coagulase-negative staphylococci are emerging.
- **Prosthetic Valve:** Early (<1 year): coagulase-negative staphylococci, *S. aureus*, *Enterococcus*. Late (>1 year): similar to native valve.
- **Other:** HACEK group (Gram-negative, fastidious), fungi (*Candida*, *Aspergillus*—large vegetations). Culture-negative cases may be identified by PCR.

### Diagnosis (Modified Duke Criteria)
- **Major Criteria:** Blood culture positive for typical organisms; echocardiographic evidence (vegetation, abscess, new dehiscence, new regurgitation).
- **Minor Criteria:** Predisposition, fever, vascular phenomena, immunologic phenomena, microbiologic evidence.
- **Definite IE:** 2 major, or 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor criteria.

### Indications and Timing for Surgery
- **Heart Failure (Class I):** Emergency for refractory pulmonary edema/cardiogenic shock; urgent for persisting heart failure or poor hemodynamic tolerance.
- **Uncontrolled Infection (Class I):** Locally uncontrolled infection (abscess, fistula, enlarging vegetation); persisting fever/bacteremia >7–10 days; fungi or multiresistant organisms.
- **Prevention of Embolism (Class I):** Large vegetations (>10 mm) with prior emboli or other predictors of complicated course.
- **Timing:** Emergency (<24 h), urgent (few days), elective (after 1–2 weeks antibiotics). Delay surgery for 1–2 weeks after ischemic stroke, 3–4 weeks after hemorrhagic stroke, depending on hemodynamic stability.

### Surgical Treatment
- **Approach:** Median sternotomy, intraoperative TEE. Myocardial protection with antegrade/retrograde cardioplegia. Minimize field contamination.
- **Noninvasive Disease:** Complete valve excision with mechanical or biologic prosthesis. No evidence that bioprosthesis is superior in active IE.
- **Invasive Disease (Root Abscess):** Radical debridement of all infected tissue and foreign material. Reconstruction with aortic allograft (preferred), autologous pericardium for patching. Coronary buttons mobilized; no felt reinforcement.
- **Postoperative Care:** Manage coagulopathy; anticipate heart block (pacemaker may be needed); monitor for multisystem organ failure, neurologic deterioration, metastatic abscesses.

### Results
- **Overall:** In-hospital mortality for isolated aortic valve IE in experienced centers ~7–10%. Native and prosthetic valve IE have similar outcomes when invasive disease is managed aggressively.
- **ICE-PCS Data (2781 patients):** Overall in-hospital mortality 17.7%. Risk factors: prosthetic valve, older age, pulmonary edema, *S. aureus*, coagulase-negative staphylococci, paravalvular abscess.
- **Key Principle:** Aggressive debridement and allograft reconstruction for invasive disease yields excellent results.

### Prophylaxis (AHA 2008 Guidelines)
- Prophylaxis recommended only for highest-risk patients (prior IE, prosthetic valve, certain congenital heart disease) undergoing dental procedures involving manipulation of gingival tissue or periapical region.
- Not recommended for genitourinary or gastrointestinal procedures.
- Our practice: Dental clearance preoperatively; prophylaxis for dental extractions.

## Chapter 32: Minimally Invasive Aortic Valve Surgery
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Principles and Benefits
- **Benefits:** Cosmetically superior incision, reduced postoperative pain, faster recovery, improved respiratory function (preserved sternal integrity), comparable mortality to full sternotomy, and facilitated future reoperations.
- **Inviolate Principles:** Ability to safely cross-clamp, fully visualize the valve, achieve adequate myocardial protection, manage root/arch pathology, and convert to full sternotomy if necessary.
- **Essential Requirements:** Experienced anesthesia team, intraoperative TEE, capability for percutaneous cannulation, vacuum-assisted venous drainage, minimal access retractors, and experienced surgeons.

### Upper Hemisternotomy (Preferred Approach)
- **Incision:** 6–8 cm vertical midline incision from sternal notch to 3rd or 4th intercostal space, with a "T" into the right or left interspace (4th space preferred).
- **Cannulation:** Central ascending aorta and direct right atrial appendage cannulation, or percutaneous femoral venous cannulation with vacuum-assisted drainage.
- **Myocardial Protection:** Antegrade/retrograde cold blood cardioplegia; TEE monitors LV distention.
- **Closure:** Horizontal sternal wires plus an oblique wire securing the lower intact segment to the "T" segment.
- **Outcomes:** Equivalent mortality/morbidity to full sternotomy; reduced ventilator time, blood product use, and hospital stay.

### Right Anterior Thoracotomy
- **Incision:** Second right intercostal space, extending to right sternal edge. Avoids sternotomy; useful for patients requiring early crutch ambulation or with sternal damage/radiation.
- **Technique:** Pericardial retraction sutures; central or peripheral cannulation (peripheral preferred); specialized cross-clamps may be needed.
- **Outcomes:** Comparable perioperative morbidity and 5-year survival to full sternotomy; shorter ventilation time and hospital stay.

### Right Parasternal and Transverse Sternotomy
- **Right Parasternal:** Historical approach requiring costal cartilage resection and mammary artery ligation; abandoned due to lung herniation.
- **Transverse Sternotomy:** Incision across manubrio-sternal angle with bilateral cartilage resection; not popular due to sacrifice of both mammary arteries and reported high morbidity/mortality.

### Reoperative Minimally Invasive AVR
- **Rationale:** Patients with prior CABG often develop progressive AS; reoperative risk with full sternotomy is 8–12% in STS database.
- **Key Concepts:** Limit dissection to aorta and root; avoid injuring patent grafts; clipping LIMA is not mandatory.
- **Preoperative Planning:** CT angiography to map graft location relative to sternum; percutaneous coronary/graft intervention if needed.
- **Operative Strategy:** Upper hemisternotomy with right pleural opening; peripheral cannulation (axillary or femoral); posterior sternal division on CPB; systemic hyperkalemia to arrest LIMA-perfused territory; retrograde cardioplegia; modified aortotomy (lazy S or lateral vertical) to avoid grafts.
- **Outcomes:** Facilitates safe reoperation with reduced dissection and graft injury.

## Chapter 33: Percutaneous Treatment of Aortic Valve Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Historical Context
- **Pioneers:** Andersen (1980s animal experiments), Cribier (first human TAVR 2002), Mack/Dewey/Svensson (transapical development), Webb (retrograde transfemoral approach).
- **Valves:** Edwards SAPIEN (balloon-expandable, bovine pericardium) and Medtronic CoreValve (self-expanding, porcine pericardium) are FDA-approved for extreme/high-risk patients.

### Pivotal Trials
- **PARTNER IB (Inoperable):** SAPIEN TAVR vs. medical management. TAVR improved 1-year (30.7% vs 50.7%) and 2-year mortality (43.4% vs 68%), but increased stroke rate (6.7% vs 1.7% at 30 days).
- **PARTNER IA (High Risk):** SAPIEN TAVR vs. surgery. Similar mortality at 30 days, 1 year, and 2 years. TAVR had more strokes, vascular complications; surgery had more bleeding and atrial fibrillation.
- **CoreValve Extreme Risk (Inoperable):** Single-arm study; 1-year death/stroke rate 26% vs. prespecified medical therapy estimate of 43%.
- **CoreValve High Risk:** TAVR vs. surgery. Similar 30-day mortality; TAVR had lower 1-year mortality (14.2% vs 19.1%). No difference in stroke; TAVR had more pacemakers and vascular complications.

### Comparison of Valves (CHOICE Trial)
- **Device Success:** Balloon-expandable SAPIEN XT superior to self-expanding CoreValve due to less residual moderate/severe aortic regurgitation (4.1% vs 18.3%) and fewer multiple valve implants.
- **Pacemaker:** Less frequent with balloon-expandable (17.3% vs 37.6%). Mortality and stroke similar at 30 days.

### Access Routes
- **Transfemoral (TF):** Preferred first choice; percutaneous, often without intubation; requires adequate iliofemoral vessel size (≥6 mm for 23 mm valve, ≥6.5 mm for 26 mm, ≥7 mm for 29 mm).
- **Transapical (TA):** Useful for patients with poor peripheral access; outcomes similar to TF after propensity matching; requires left minithoracotomy, rib resection, and apical purse-string sutures.
- **Alternative Routes:** Transaortic (TAo), transcarotid, transaxillary/subclavian, and caval-aortic puncture.

### Procedural Key Points
- **Multidisciplinary Team:** Cardiac surgery, interventional cardiology, anesthesia, echocardiography, perfusion.
- **Positioning:** Valve placed 60–70% aortic, 30–40% ventricular at hingepoint; rapid pacing (180 bpm) during deployment.
- **Perivalvular Regurgitation:** Most common at noncoronary-left commissure; assessed by TEE and aortography.
- **Vascular Closure:** Percutaneous closure devices for TF; purse-string sutures for TA.

### Current Status and Future Directions
- **Intermediate Risk:** PARTNER 2A showed TAVR noninferior to surgery; FDA approved TAVR for intermediate risk in August 2016.
- **Low Risk:** PARTNER-3 trial currently underway; randomized controlled trials needed before routine use.

## Chapter 34: Pathophysiology of Mitral Valve Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Normal Mitral Valve Anatomy and Function
- **Anatomy:** The mitral valve has two leaflets: anterior (aortic, larger) and posterior (mural, with three scallops: P1, P2, P3). The annulus is saddle-shaped (hyperbolic paraboloid), with the highest point anteriorly (saddle horn) and low points at the commissures.
- **Annular Dynamics:** Annular area varies 20–40% during the cardiac cycle, maximal in late diastole, minimal in early-mid systole. Annular motion contributes up to 20% of LV filling and ejection.
- **Leaflet Structure:** Three layers: fibrosa (collagen, continuous with chordae), spongiosa (proteoglycans), and fibroelastic covering. Leaflets contain contractile cells (myocardium, smooth muscle) under neural control.
- **Chordae and Papillary Muscles:** Two papillary muscles (anterolateral, posteromedial). First-order chordae prevent prolapse; second-order (strut) chordae anchor leaflets and contribute to LV systolic function. Chordal preservation during MVR improves outcomes.

### Mitral Stenosis (MS)
- **Etiology:** Primarily rheumatic heart disease (pancarditis). Commissural fusion, leaflet fibrosis, and chordal shortening cause progressive obstruction.
- **Hemodynamics:** Progressive increase in transvalvular gradient and LA pressure. Atrial contraction is critical for LV filling; loss of sinus rhythm reduces cardiac output by ≥20%.
- **Ventricular Adaptation:** LV end-diastolic volume is normal or reduced; cardiac output is limited by inflow obstruction rather than pump failure. Pulmonary hypertension develops from passive transmission, vasoconstriction, and eventually obliterative changes.
- **Treatment:** Percutaneous balloon valvuloplasty for favorable anatomy; surgical commissurotomy or MVR for advanced disease.

### Mitral Regurgitation (MR)
- **Primary MR (Leaflet Pathology):** Myxomatous degeneration (fibroelastic deficiency in older patients; Barlow's in younger) is the most common cause. Chordal rupture or elongation leads to leaflet prolapse (Type II).
- **Secondary MR (Functional/Ischemic):** Caused by LV dysfunction and dilatation (Type IIIb). IMR occurs in up to 40% of patients with inferior MI. Pathophysiology includes annular dilatation (septal-lateral), leaflet tethering (apical displacement of papillary muscles), and altered LV geometry.
- **Hemodynamics:** Reduced LV impedance allows increased fiber shortening, but chronic volume overload leads to LV dilatation, more spherical remodeling, and eventual contractile dysfunction. Ejection fraction can remain normal despite depressed contractility.
- **Key Indices:** LV end-systolic volume (LVESV) is a better predictor of outcome than EF. Effective regurgitant orifice (ERO) and regurgitant volume quantify severity. Severe primary MR: ERO ≥0.4 cm², regurgitant volume ≥60 mL. Severe secondary MR: ERO ≥0.2 cm², regurgitant volume ≥30 mL.
- **Surgical Indications (Primary MR):** Symptoms, LVEF ≤60%, or LVESD ≥40 mm (Class I). Early repair (Class IIa) if LVEF >60%, LVESD <40 mm, high repair likelihood (≥90%), and low surgical risk (<1%).
- **Surgical Indications (Secondary MR):** Ongoing symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, coronary revascularization, or CRT. Operative risk is higher; outcomes are driven by LV function and comorbidities. Complete chordal-sparing MVR may be preferred over annuloplasty in severe tethering or high-risk patients.

## Chapter 35: Mitral Valve Repair
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Historical Perspective
- **First Repair:** Elliot Carr Cutler performed the first successful mitral valve repair (transventricular commissurotomy) in 1923. Henry Souttar performed a transatrial finger commissurotomy in 1925.
- **Pioneers:** Alain Carpentier (functional classification, ring annuloplasty), Dwight McGoon, Carlos Duran established mitral valve reconstruction techniques in the 1970s–1980s.
- **Paradigm Shift:** Early concerns that repair might worsen LV function by eliminating the "pop-off" mechanism have been disproven; chordal preservation improves LV function.

### Anatomy of the Mitral Valve
- **Five Components:** Annulus, anterior leaflet (AML), posterior leaflet (PML), chordae tendineae, papillary muscles, and left ventricle.
- **Critical Structures:** Circumflex coronary artery (lateral), coronary sinus (medial), aortic root (superior), atrioventricular node (superomedial). Right and left fibrous trigones anchor annuloplasty rings.
- **Scallop Nomenclature:** AML: A1 (lateral), A2 (middle), A3 (medial). PML: P1 (lateral), P2 (middle), P3 (medial).

### Myxomatous Mitral Valve Disease (Primary MR)
- **Pathology:** Fibroelastic connective tissue defect causing leaflet/chordal elongation, annular dilatation, and chordal rupture. Most common cause of MR in the US.
- **Indications for Surgery (2014 AHA/ACC):**
    - **Class I:** Symptomatic patients (stage D); asymptomatic with LVEF ≤60% or LVESD ≥40 mm (stage C2).
    - **Class IIa:** Asymptomatic with preserved LV function (LVEF >60%, LVESD <40 mm) at a Heart Valve Center of Excellence with >95% repair likelihood and <1% mortality (stage C1).
- **Key Principle:** Repair is superior to replacement (better survival, preserved LV function, lower thromboembolism).

### Operative Technique
- **Exposure:** Sondergaard's groove dissection (separating right atrium from left atrium) provides excellent exposure. Transseptal approach is an alternative.
- **Myocardial Protection:** Cold blood cardioplegia (antegrade and retrograde for concomitant CABG). Carbon dioxide insufflation minimizes air emboli.
- **Repair Principles:**
    1. Reduce posterior leaflet height to ≤1.5 cm to prevent systolic anterior motion (SAM).
    2. Perform posterior leaflet resection (quadrangular or fold-over advancement).
    3. Implant remodeling annuloplasty ring (sized by anterior leaflet height, not intertrigonal distance). Ring is mandatory for durability.
    4. Address anterior leaflet prolapse with artificial ePTFE chordae, chordal transfer, or edge-to-edge technique.
- **Ring Sizing:** Oversize slightly in myxomatous disease. Anterior leaflet height is the critical dimension.

### Specific Repair Techniques
- **Posterior Leaflet Quadrangular Resection:** Classic technique for P2 prolapse; sliding valvuloplasty advances remaining segments. Fold-over advancement simplifies resection.
- **Commissuroplasty:** Simple horizontal mattress sutures for commissural prolapse.
- **Artificial Chordae (ePTFE):** Preferred for AML prolapse. Suture passed through papillary muscle and leaflet edge; length adjusted by tenting posterior leaflet to correct height.
- **Edge-to-Edge (Alfieri Stitch):** Figure-of-eight suture at A2-P2 apposition creates double orifice; useful for bileaflet prolapse or to prevent SAM.
- **Remodeling Ring Annuloplasty:** Mattress sutures placed orthogonal to annulus, avoiding radial bites that may impinge circumflex artery. Ring secures repair durability.

### Special Considerations
- **Systolic Anterior Motion (SAM):** Caused by excessive posterior leaflet height or undersized ring. Prevention: reduce PML height, oversize ring, consider edge-to-edge stitch. Treatment: volume loading, beta-blockers; rarely requires revision.
- **Calcified Annulus:** Partial debridement to allow stitch placement; radical debridement may be necessary but increases risk.
- **Rheumatic Disease:** More challenging; repair durability is lower than myxomatous.

### Results
- **Durability:** Myxomatous repair: >90% freedom from reoperation at 10 years.
- **Thromboembolism:** Exceedingly low; no long-term anticoagulation required unless atrial fibrillation present.
- **Etiology as Determinant:** Myxomatous repair has excellent long-term outcomes; rheumatic disease has higher late reoperation rates.

## Chapter 36: Mitral Valve Repair: Rheumatic
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Pathophysiology
- **Global Burden:** RHD affects up to 19.6 million people globally, with 282,000 new cases per year. Peak incidence in individuals aged 25–35 years, with female predominance in developing countries.
- **Valve Pathology:** Mitral valve is involved in 50% of cases. Regurgitant valves show edema, fibrotic thickening, annular dilatation, and anterior leaflet pseudoprolapse. Stenotic valves have stiff, restricted leaflets with commissural and subvalvular fusion, often with calcification.
- **Hemodynamic Consequences:** Mitral stenosis increases diastolic gradients, leading to left atrial enlargement, atrial fibrillation, pulmonary hypertension, and eventual right ventricular dysfunction.

### Surgical Considerations
- **Repair vs. Replacement:** Mitral valve repair is preferred over replacement due to lower operative mortality, better preservation of LV function, lower thromboembolism and endocarditis risk, and greater long-term durability—especially critical in young patients.
- **Repair Rates:** In RMVD, repair is feasible in 50–75% of patients (vs. >95% for degenerative prolapse), depending on age, leaflet dysfunction, and calcification.
- **Progressive Nature:** The ongoing rheumatic process can lead to late failure even after a successful repair, particularly in patients from developing countries where the disease is active.

### Repair Techniques
- **Commissurotomy:** Treats commissural fusion (Grades I–III). Incision starts 5 mm from the annulus and extends to the papillary muscle head, preserving chordae.
- **Subvalvular Techniques:** Chordal resection and fenestration mobilize restricted leaflets. In severe fusion, neochordoplasty with PTFE replaces diseased chords.
- **Leaflet Augmentation:** Glutaraldehyde-fixed autologous pericardium patches increase leaflet surface area in severe retraction. The patch is tailored to the defect and sutured with continuous polypropylene.
- **Decalcification:** Leaflet shaving, peeling, or thinning improves pliability. These techniques often precede patch augmentation.
- **Ring Annuloplasty:** A prosthetic ring or band is mandatory in all repairs to address annular dilatation and ensure durability.

### Outcomes
- **Repair Rate Trends:** Recent series show repair rates increasing from 42% to 69% in the same surgical group over the last decade.
- **Mortality:** Operative mortality for rheumatic mitral valve repair ranges from 0.7% to 4.0% in large, experienced centers.
- **Durability:** Failure rate is 2–5% per year (vs. 1–2% for degenerative disease). Reoperation rates are higher due to progressive fibrosis and calcification of the subvalvular apparatus.
- **Key Advantage:** Meta-analyses confirm lower operative mortality for repair compared to replacement (OR 2.98, CI 95% 1.45–6.15).

## Chapter 37: Surgery for Functional Mitral Regurgitation
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Definition and Impact
- **FMR:** Nonorganic MR caused by LV remodeling, dilation, and dysfunction, not intrinsic valve disease. Leads to papillary muscle displacement and annular dilation.
- **Clinical Impact:** FMR is an independent predictor of mortality in CHF. Even small amounts of FMR are harmful. Studies show 5-year survival: 83% (no/1+ MR), 64% (2+), 58% (3+), 46% (4+). Moderate-severe FMR in advanced CHF carries a 5-year survival as low as 27%.
- **Vicious Cycle:** LV dilatation → FMR → further LV volume overload → more LV dilatation → worsening FMR.

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology
- **MV Apparatus:** Annulus, leaflets, chordae, papillary muscles, and LV form a "closure cylinder." Chordal continuity is essential for ventricular twist mechanics and systolic power.
- **Ischemic FMR:** Primary lesion is leaflet tethering from posterior-lateral/apical papillary muscle displacement. P3 leaflet segment is often restricted (posterior infarction). Annular dilatation is less prominent than in degenerative MR.
- **Nonischemic FMR:** More symmetric distortion with greater annular dilation than ischemic FMR.

### Surgical Outcomes and Controversies
- **Safety:** MV repair for FMR is feasible with low operative mortality (1–5% in experienced centers). Acorn trial: 98% 30-day survival, 85% 24-month survival.
- **Achilles Heel:** Recurrent MR after repair is common (30–80% in some series). Recurrence may obscure any survival benefit.
- **Predictors of Recurrence:** LVEDD >65 mm, coaptation depth >10 mm, posterior leaflet angle >45°, anterior leaflet angle >25–40°, and residual large AP diameter.
- **CTSnet Severe FMR Trial:** Repair vs. replacement. No difference in LVESVI or 1-year mortality (14.3% vs. 17.6%). Repair had significantly higher recurrence of moderate/severe MR (32.6% vs. 2.3% at 1 year).

### Indications and Guidelines
- **Guidelines (AHA/ACC 2014):** Treat ischemia first (CABG/PCI); GDMT; CRT if QRS >150 ms. For symptomatic severe MR (Stage D), MV surgery is a Class IIb indication.
- **Moderate FMR:** RCTs show CABG + MV repair improves MR grade, NYHA class, and LV remodeling vs. CABG alone, but no mortality difference. RIME trial showed better LVESVI, BNP, and exercise performance with repair.
- **Severe FMR:** Choice between repair and replacement remains controversial. "Good" repair (no recurrence) results in better LV remodeling than replacement; "bad" repair is worse.

### Surgical Techniques
- **Undersized Annuloplasty:** Restrictive ring (smaller than measured) to increase leaflet coaptation. Standard approach: 26 or 28 mm rigid, complete ring. Use numerous annular sutures to distribute force.
- **Ring Type:** Rigid, complete rings with disproportionate AP reduction are preferred. Partial or flexible bands have higher failure rates.
- **Valve-Sparing Replacement:** Total chordal preservation (anterior leaflet "flip-over" technique) is mandatory. Nonvalve-sparing replacement should be abandoned.

### Percutaneous Options
- **MitraClip:** Emerging option for high-risk FMR patients. COAPT trial is evaluating safety/effectiveness vs. GDMT. Early reports show improved functional class but variable recurrence rates and mortality (12–18% at 1 year in high-risk cohorts).

### Key Principles
- FMR is a ventricular disease, not a valvular disease.
- Patients with LVEDD >65 mm, severe RV dysfunction, or high pulmonary pressures have poor outcomes and may not benefit from surgery.
- If repair is chosen, durable repair (no recurrence) is critical; patients at high risk for recurrence should undergo valve-sparing replacement.

## Chapter 38: Surgical Treatment of Mitral Valve Endocarditis
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Pathology
*   **Incidence:** Mitral valve infective endocarditis (IE) is less common than aortic valve IE. Overall incidence has increased over the past 3 decades due to predisposing factors like degenerative valve disease, prosthetic valves, hemodialysis, and IV drug abuse.
*   **Pathophysiology:** Native valve endocarditis (NVE) begins with endocardial injury, leading to fibrin and platelet deposition and bacterial attachment. Invasive mitral disease often involves the base of the atrial aspect of the leaflets, with annular or subannular invasion potentially causing atrioventricular (AV) separation.
*   **Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (PVE):** Risk is 1% for early PVE and 0.5–1% per year for late PVE. Early PVE usually affects the sewing ring and is often a result of intraoperative infection.

### Microbiology and Diagnosis
*   **Causative Organisms:** *Staphylococcus aureus* is the most common pathogen in NVE (27.6%) and is highly associated with invasive disease. Coagulase-negative *Staphylococcus* is most common in PVE (24.1%).
*   **Diagnostic Modalities:** Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is the gold standard, with 90–100% sensitivity. The Duke criteria are used for classification but do not replace clinical judgment.
*   **Neurologic Evaluation:** 20-40% of patients have neurologic events. Preoperative head CT or MRI is mandatory. Surgery is typically delayed 1-2 weeks after nonhemorrhagic stroke and 3-4 weeks after hemorrhagic stroke.

### Indications and Timing for Surgery
*   **Key Indications:** Severe mitral regurgitation, uncontrolled sepsis, annular abscess, large mobile vegetations (>1 cm), fungal endocarditis, and *S. aureus* infection.
*   **Timing:** Surgery should not be delayed once indications are present. Early surgery is crucial for PVE to prevent progression to heart failure or abscess. The presence of cerebral complications typically warrants a delay.

### Operative Techniques and Outcomes
*   **Native Valve:** Repair is the preferred approach when feasible, achieving success in up to 80% of patients. Techniques include pericardial patch repair for anterior leaflet defects and quadrangular resection with sliding repair for the posterior leaflet. Valve repair results in lower hospital mortality and better long-term survival compared to replacement.
*   **Prosthetic Valve:** Surgery involves reoperation via sternotomy. The David technique, using an autologous pericardial patch, is key for reconstructing the mitral annulus after abscess debridement. For extensive infections involving the fibrous trigones, combined aortic and mitral valve replacement with reconstruction is required.
*   **Postoperative Care:** All patients with active infection receive 6 weeks of postoperative antibiotics. Fungal infections require lifelong oral antifungal suppression.

## Chapter 39: Mitral Valve Repair for Congenital Mitral Valve Disease in the Adult
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Clinical Context
*   **Rarity in Adults:** Isolated, non-reoperative congenital mitral valve abnormalities are rare in adults. More commonly, mitral pathology is concurrent with other anomalies, such as an ostium primum atrial septal defect, where almost all cases have an associated mitral valve cleft.
*   **Reoperative Burden:** A growing adult population requires reoperation for formerly corrected congenital mitral pathology. The prevalence of atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) is 5.3 per 10,000 live births, and mitral regurgitation is the principal reason for reoperation in these patients, with 15-year freedom from reoperation being 81–83%.

### Pathophysiology of Congenital Lesions
*   **Normal Anatomy:** The normal mitral annulus is saddle-shaped and dynamic, contracting 23–40% in systole. The anterior leaflet occupies one-third of the annulus, and the posterior leaflet two-thirds. Chordae are classified as primary (free edge), secondary (mid-leaflet), and tertiary (basal).
*   **Congenital Mitral Stenosis (MS):** Ruckman and Van Praagh's classification includes:
    *   **Typical:** Normal annulus, obstruction from thickened leaflets, shortened chordae, and closely spaced papillary muscles.
    *   **Hypoplastic:** Small annulus associated with other left heart hypoplasias, rarely seen in adults.
    *   **Supravalvar:** Includes Cor Triatriatum (membrane subdividing LA), supramitral ring (atrial side of annulus), and intramitral ring (adherent to leaflets).
    *   **Parachute MV:** Single papillary muscle from which all chordae radiate, often part of Shone's complex.
*   **Combined Stenosis and Insufficiency:**
    *   **Mitral Arcade:** Failure of chordal elongation; papillary muscles may insert directly onto leaflet edges.
    *   **Hammock Valve:** Posterior leaflet tethered by extraneous papillary muscles and secondary chordae.
*   **Congenital Mitral Insufficiency (MR):**
    *   **Cleft Leaflet:** Inverted "V" separation in anterior leaflet, often with AVSD.
    *   **Double Orifice MV:** Single annulus with two orifices, predominantly causing MR.
    *   **Ebstein's Malformation:** Apical displacement of a flattened posterior leaflet.

### Indications and Operative Techniques
*   **Indications for MS:** Surgical intervention for adults with congenital MS follows AHA/ACC guidelines for acquired disease. It is indicated for symptomatic patients with severe MS (≤1.5 cm²) and for patients with moderate to severe MS undergoing concomitant cardiac surgery.
*   **Repair for Stenosis:** A systematic approach aims to create mobile leaflets:
    *   **Supravalvar:** Complete resection of the obstructive membrane (Cor Triatriatum, ring).
    *   **Annular:** Annular enlargement is limited by the aortic valve (anteriorly), His bundle (rightward), and circumflex artery (posteriorly).
    *   **Subvalvar:** Commissurotomies, chordal fenestration, and splitting of fused or single papillary muscles. For Hammock valves, the leaflet is detached to divide muscular attachments.
*   **Indications for MR:** Surgery is recommended for symptomatic patients with severe MR and LVEF >30%, or asymptomatic patients with LV dysfunction. Repair is considered for asymptomatic patients with preserved function if durable success is likely.
*   **Repair for Insufficiency:**
    *   **Cleft Repair:** Simple closure is effective if adequate leaflet tissue is present. For deficiencies, patch augmentation with or without artificial chordae is used. Complex cases may require leaflet detachment and patch augmentation.
    *   **Posterior Leaflet:** Quadrangular resection and reduction annuloplasty are adjuncts to improve coaptation.

## Chapter 40: Minimally Invasive and Robotic Mitral and Tricuspid Valve Surgery
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Evolution and Definition
*   **Definition:** Minimally invasive mitral valve surgery (MIS) encompasses techniques and technologies designed to lessen surgical trauma. It includes direct-vision, video-assisted (endoscopic), and robot-assisted approaches via a right minithoracotomy.
*   **Historical Context:** The concept evolved from the "Heartport Era" with balloon occlusion and port-access. Carpentier performed the first videoscopic repair (1996), and our group performed the first camera-directed replacement. Carpentier and Mohr accomplished the first robotic repairs in 1998. The daVinci system was FDA-approved for intracardiac surgery in 2002.
*   **Benefits:** Compared to sternotomy, MIS is associated with decreased blood loss, fewer transfusions, less pain, shorter hospital stays, faster recovery, and lower healthcare costs.

### Patient Selection and Preoperative Planning
*   **Selection:** Candidates should meet standard AHA/ACC or ESC/EACTS guidelines. Patients with significant comorbidities, multivessel coronary disease, or a dilated ascending aorta are better served by sternotomy.
*   **Screening:** Preoperative screening includes CT angiogram, coronary angiogram, and pulmonary function tests. A detailed 3D transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) is used to create a "blueprint" for repair, including measurements of leaflet segments, annular diameter, and the aortomitral angle to predict and prevent systolic anterior motion (SAM).
*   **Contraindications:** Absolute contraindications include previous right thoracotomy, severe pulmonary dysfunction, and significant aortic stenosis or insufficiency. Relative contraindications include previous sternotomy, moderate pulmonary dysfunction, and poor LV function (EF < 50%).

### Operative Management: Perfusion and Exposure
*   **Cannulation:** Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is typically established via femoral arterial and venous cannulation, often augmented by an internal jugular venous cannula for a dry field. Vacuum-assisted venous drainage is used. CO<sub>2</sub> insufflation is critical for air removal.
*   **Aortic Occlusion:** Options include a transthoracic aortic clamp (our preference) or an endo-aortic balloon (IntraClude). The transthoracic clamp is passed through the transverse sinus under vision.
*   **Myocardial Protection:** Custodiol (HTK) solution is preferred for its prolonged protection, requiring only single or half-dose infusions, which simplifies the procedure.

### MIS and Robotic Repair Techniques
*   **Posterior Leaflet Repair:** The "Technique Toolbox" includes triangular/trapezoidal resection for focal prolapse, folding-plasty for elongated segments, the "haircut" resection for large P<sub>2</sub> with diminutive adjacent scallops, and PTFE neochord replacement. For globally redundant Barlow's valves, a quadrangular resection with sliding-plasty is used.
*   **Anterior Leaflet Repair:** Techniques include triangular resection, PTFE neochord placement, "flip over" chord transfer from the posterior leaflet, chordal advancement, and papillary muscle folding (shortening).
*   **Annuloplasty:** A trigone-to-trigone annuloplasty band (e.g., Cosgrove) is used in all repairs. The Cor-Knot titanium clip system provides secure, rapid, and reproducible band fixation.
*   **Robotic System:** The daVinci SI or XI systems provide 3D visualization, tremor filtration, and seven degrees of freedom. Trocars are placed in the 3rd (left arm), 5th (right arm), and 5th (retractor) intercostal spaces.

### Outcomes and Future Directions
*   **Outcomes:** Large series show MIS mitral surgery has low operative mortality (~0.75%) and stroke rates (~1.2%). Our single-surgeon experience with over 900 robotic repairs shows a 0.15% mortality for isolated repairs, with 97.1% of patients having no or trivial MR post-repair.
*   **Complications:** Specific risks include phrenic nerve injury, unilateral pulmonary edema, and retrograde perfusion-related complications (stroke, dissection). Careful screening and technique minimize these risks.
*   **Future:** The future lies in even less invasive approaches, including transcatheter therapies (MitraClip, neochord, annuloplasty) and continued refinement of robotic techniques.

## Chapter 41: Percutaneous Catheter-based Mitral Valve Repair
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Introduction and Rationale
*   **Background:** Mitral valve repair (MVR) is the standard of care for severe mitral regurgitation (MR). While transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has advanced rapidly, percutaneous correction of MR has progressed more slowly due to complex valve anatomy, diverse etiologies, and challenges in delivery and anchoring.
*   **Unmet Need:** Percutaneous techniques are being developed primarily for high-risk patients who are poor candidates for conventional open-heart surgery.

### Pathophysiology of MR
*   **Primary MR (Degenerative):** Intrinsic leaflet disease (Carpentier Type II), ranging from fibroelastic deficiency to Barlow's disease. Annular dilatation is often a secondary phenomenon.
*   **Secondary MR (Functional):** Anatomically normal leaflets with regurgitation due to ventricular dilation (Carpentier Type IIIB). Ventricular dilation leads to papillary muscle tethering, preventing leaflet coaptation. Surgical repair focuses on undersized annuloplasty to correct the septal-lateral annular diameter.

### Current Transcatheter Approaches
*   **Approved Devices:** Only three devices have CE Mark approval: MitraClip (edge-to-edge), NeoChord DS1000 (artificial chordae), and Carillon Mitral Contour System (coronary sinus annuloplasty). Only MitraClip has FDA approval, for primary MR in high-risk patients.
*   **MitraClip (Edge-to-Edge):** Mimics the Alfieri stitch, creating a double-orifice valve. Performed via transfemoral venous access and transseptal puncture. The EVEREST II trial showed it is safer (fewer transfusions) but less effective at reducing MR than surgery. Large European registries show high procedural success (~95%) in high-risk patients with both primary and secondary MR.
*   **NeoChord DS1000 (Artificial Chords):** Implants PTFE neochordae via a left minithoracotomy and transapical access. Early results show a steep learning curve, with improved outcomes after initial experience.
*   **Coronary Sinus Annuloplasty (Carillon):** A device placed in the coronary sinus to plicate the posterior annulus. Efficacy is limited by anatomical variability and proximity of the circumflex artery.

### Emerging Technologies and Future Directions
*   **Direct Annular Remodeling:** Devices like Mitralign (transventricular suture plication) and Valtech Cardioband (transseptal adjustable band) aim to directly reduce the septal-lateral annular diameter.
*   **Transcatheter Mitral Valve Replacement (TMVR):** An intriguing concept to eliminate MR recurrence, especially in secondary MR. Challenges include anchoring in a non-calcified annulus and avoiding LV outflow tract obstruction. Valve-in-valve (VinV) and valve-in-ring (VinR) procedures are established, and dedicated TMVR devices (CardiAQ, Tiara, Tendyne, Fortis) are in early feasibility trials.
*   **Guidelines:** 2012 ESC/EACTS guidelines give a Class IIb recommendation for MitraClip in symptomatic patients with severe primary or secondary MR who are inoperable or at high risk. 2014 ACC/AHA guidelines restrict it to prohibitive-risk primary MR.

## Chapter 42: Mitral Valve Replacement
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Indications
*   **Mitral Stenosis (MS):** Almost exclusively rheumatic. Severe MS is defined as a valve area ≤1.5 cm². Percutaneous balloon valvuloplasty is first-line for suitable anatomy (Wilkins score ≤8). Mitral valve replacement (MVR) is indicated for severe symptomatic MS when valvuloplasty is not feasible or has failed, or for moderate/severe MS during other cardiac surgery.
*   **Primary Mitral Regurgitation (MR):** Surgery is Class I for symptomatic patients with LVEF >30% or asymptomatic patients with LV dysfunction (LVEF 30-60%, LVESD ≥40 mm). While repair is preferred, MVR is necessary for rheumatic, calcific, or endocarditic valves where repair is not feasible.
*   **Secondary (Ischemic/Functional) MR:** A recent RCT showed MVR resulted in lower MR recurrence (2% vs. 33%) than repair, with no difference in mortality or LV remodeling.

### Choice of Prosthesis
*   **Mechanical Valves:** Offer durability but require lifelong anticoagulation (INR 2.5–3.5). The 2014 AHA/ACC guideline emphasizes shared decision-making based on patient age and preference. Our institutional data show higher reoperation rates with bioprostheses and higher survival with mechanical valves in patients <65 years.
*   **Bioprosthetic Valves:** Avoid anticoagulation but have limited durability. Structural valve degeneration (SVD) averages 30% at 10 years and accelerates thereafter. They are preferred in patients >65 years, those with contraindications to anticoagulation, or those with limited life expectancy due to comorbidities.
*   **Trends:** In the US, there has been a 100% increase in bioprosthesis use, driven by a desire to avoid anticoagulation.

### Operative Technique
*   **Exposure:** Median sternotomy with left atriotomy (via Sondergaard's plane) is standard. Alternatives include transseptal or biatrial incisions.
*   **Chordal Preservation:** Preserving the subvalvular apparatus is critical for maintaining left ventricular function and preventing ventricular rupture. Techniques include partial anterior leaflet excision with "furling" or chordal transfer.
*   **Suturing:** Bioprostheses are implanted with noneverting (subannular) sutures (ventricle-to-atrium). Bileaflet mechanical valves require everting (supraannular) sutures (atrium-to-ventricle) to ensure leaflet mobility.

### Special Surgical Challenges
*   **Mitral Annular Calcification (MAC):** Strategies include limited debridement with intraatrial washer reinforcement, patch reconstruction, or intraatrial valve insertion.
*   **Unclampable Aorta (Porcelain Aorta):** Options include moderate hypothermia with ventricular fibrillation (no AI), hypothermic low-flow bypass, or ascending aortic replacement under deep hypothermic circulatory arrest.

### Outcomes and Complications
*   **Mortality:** Operative mortality for elective primary MVR is 5–9%. Survival at 10 years is approximately 50–60%, similar between valve types.
*   **Morbidity:** Thromboembolism (1.5–2.0%/patient-year) and anticoagulant-related hemorrhage are major risks with mechanical valves. SVD is the main complication for bioprostheses.
*   **Paravalvular Leak:** Occurs in 7–17% of MVRs. Symptomatic leaks can be managed with percutaneous closure (using Amplatzer devices) or reoperative surgical repair.

## Chapter 43: Tricuspid Valve Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology
*   **Tricuspid Valve Anatomy:** The tricuspid valve consists of three leaflets (anterior, posterior, septal), two discrete papillary muscles, chordae tendineae, and a fibrous annulus. The septal leaflet is fixed, making annular sizing based on the base of the septal leaflet.
*   **Annular Dynamics:** The tricuspid annulus is nonplanar and dynamic, with a ~20% reduction in circumference (~30% reduction in area) during atrial systole. Functional TR (fTR) results in a more planar, circular, and dilated annulus, primarily in the septal-lateral direction.
*   **Functional TR:** Secondary TR (75% of cases) results from left heart disease, pulmonary hypertension, and RV dilation. Annular dilation (anterior/posterior) and chordal tethering prevent leaflet coaptation. Annular diameter ≥70 mm is a key indicator for repair regardless of TR grade.

### Clinical Presentation and Diagnosis
*   **Tricuspid Regurgitation (TR):** Presents with fatigue, weakness, jugular venous distention, ascites, hepatomegaly, peripheral edema, and atrial fibrillation. Severe TR is associated with worse survival independent of pulmonary artery pressure.
*   **Tricuspid Stenosis (TS):** Almost always rheumatic and associated with mitral valve disease. Characterized by a diastolic gradient of 2–5 mm Hg, leading to right atrial pressure elevation and low cardiac output symptoms.
*   **Echocardiography:** Color Doppler, continuous wave Doppler, and hepatic vein flow reversal assess TR severity. TAPSE (tricuspid annular peak systolic excursion) is a sensitive measure of RV function. 3D echocardiography reveals the nonplanar annular shape.

### Surgical Indications (2014 ACC/AHA Guidelines)
*   **Class I:** Tricuspid valve repair is beneficial for severe TR in patients undergoing mitral valve surgery.
*   **Class IIa:** Tricuspid valve replacement or annuloplasty is reasonable for severe primary TR when symptomatic; tricuspid valve replacement is reasonable for severe secondary TR not amenable to annuloplasty.
*   **Class IIb:** Tricuspid annuloplasty may be considered for less-than-severe TR in patients undergoing mitral surgery with pulmonary hypertension or tricuspid annular dilatation.
*   **Class III:** Surgery is not indicated for asymptomatic patients with TR and normal MV if PASP <60 mm Hg, nor for mild primary TR.

### Surgical Techniques
*   **Annuloplasty:** The mainstay for functional TR. Rigid rings (Carpentier-Edwards, MC³) provide the most durable reduction. Flexible bands (Cosgrove, Tri-Ad) are effective. DeVega and bicuspidization suture techniques have higher rates of recurrent TR long-term and should be reserved for mild annular dilatation.
*   **Replacement:** Indicated when repair is not feasible. Mechanical valves (St. Jude) and bioprostheses have similar long-term survival. Bioprostheses in the tricuspid position show longer durability than in the mitral position but are associated with pannus formation on the ventricular side over time (up to 35% at 5 years).
*   **Special Considerations:** Pacemaker leads worsen TR; consider epicardial lead placement at time of surgery. The transseptal approach allows exposure of both mitral and tricuspid valves. Perform tricuspid surgery on the beating heart to avoid conduction injury.

### Outcomes
*   **TR Progression:** Without annuloplasty, TR increases by more than two grades in 48% of patients with annular diameter ≥70 mm. Aggressive annuloplasty reduces late TR and improves functional status.
*   **Mortality:** Operative mortality for combined mitral and tricuspid surgery is 5–10%. Survival at 10 years is approximately 55%.
*   **Conduction Abnormalities:** Complete heart block is a risk; performing tricuspid procedure on the beating heart minimizes this. Late heart block has a 25% actuarial incidence at 10 years for patients with mitral and tricuspid prostheses.

## Chapter 44: Multiple Valve Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Pathophysiology
*   **Incidence:** Multiple valve procedures account for ~15% of all valve surgeries, with 80% involving the aortic and mitral positions.
*   **Secondary Regurgitation:** Left-sided valve disease can cause secondary regurgitation of atrioventricular valves. Secondary mitral regurgitation (MR) often improves after aortic valve replacement (AVR) if the mitral valve is structurally normal. Secondary tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is commonly associated with rheumatic mitral disease and is driven by annular dilatation (≥70 mm) and right ventricular dysfunction, not solely by pulmonary hypertension.

### Surgical Indications and Management
*   **Secondary TR:** Correction of moderate or greater TR at the time of rheumatic mitral surgery is indicated to prevent symptom progression. For degenerative mitral disease, less-than-moderate functional TR is unlikely to progress after successful repair.
*   **Valve Selection:** For left-sided double-valve replacement, valves should be chosen from the same class (both mechanical or both bioprosthetic) to simplify anticoagulation and avoid a higher reoperation rate. For tricuspid replacement, bioprostheses may be preferred due to lower thrombosis risk, and hemodynamic performance is rarely an issue.

### Operative Techniques
*   **Aortic and Mitral Replacement:** Aortic valve is exposed first but usually replaced after the mitral valve to avoid hindering exposure. The mitral valve is accessed via a left atriotomy. Chordal preservation is crucial. Aortic annulus can be enlarged with a pericardial patch if small.
*   **Mitral and Tricuspid Surgery:** Bicaval cannulation and a transseptal incision provide excellent exposure for both valves. Tricuspid repair (DeVega or ring annuloplasty) is preferred over replacement when possible. The tricuspid procedure is often performed on the beating heart to avoid conduction injury.
*   **Carcinoid Heart Disease:** Typically affects the tricuspid and pulmonary valves. Tricuspid replacement is always required; pulmonary valve replacement is preferred over simple excision to preserve right ventricular function. Preoperative octreotide is essential to manage carcinoid syndrome.

### Outcomes
*   **Mortality:** Operative mortality for multiple valve surgery is 6–17%. Ten-year survival is 43–81%.
*   **Morbidity:** Thromboembolism rates are 1–7% per patient-year. Anticoagulant-related hemorrhage and prosthetic valve endocarditis are significant risks.
*   **Bioprosthetic Durability:** Structural valve deterioration is more rapid in the mitral position than the aortic position, influencing the choice of prosthesis in double-valve replacement.

## Chapter 45: Valvular and Ischemic Heart Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Clinical Impact
*   **Combined Disease:** Concomitant valvular heart disease and coronary artery disease (CAD) is increasingly common in an aging population, with patients often presenting with complex comorbidities and worse ventricular function.
*   **Pathophysiology:** CAD exacerbates ventricular dysfunction from valve disease, particularly in mitral regurgitation where ischemic remodeling leads to annular dilatation and leaflet tethering.
*   **Preoperative Assessment:** TEE, myocardial viability studies (PET), and coronary angiography (for patients >40 years or with risk factors) are essential for surgical planning.

### Aortic Stenosis (AS) with CAD
*   **Management of Moderate AS:** Controversial. Concomitant AVR is often recommended for younger patients (<70 years) with peak gradient >25–30 mm Hg. In elderly patients (>80 years), isolated CABG may be appropriate unless AS is severe or symptomatic.
*   **Operative Technique:** Antegrade and retrograde cardioplegia are optimal. Myocardial protection is critical in hypertrophied ventricles.
*   **Outcomes:** Operative mortality for AVR+CABG is 2–10%, with 5-year survival of 60–80%. Bioprosthetic valves are increasingly used, even in younger patients, due to improved durability.

### Aortic Regurgitation (AR) with CAD
*   **Pathophysiology:** AR causes left ventricular volume overload and dilation. CAD can worsen ventricular dysfunction.
*   **Operative Management:** Retrograde cardioplegia is essential due to aortic incompetence. Afterload reduction with inotropes (milrinone, dobutamine) is important during weaning.
*   **Outcomes:** Hospital mortality <10%. Recovery of ventricular function is less predictable than with AS, emphasizing the need for timely intervention before irreversible dilation occurs.

### Mitral Regurgitation (MR) with CAD
*   **Pathophysiology:** Ischemic MR is primarily functional, resulting from ventricular dilation and papillary muscle displacement. Correction of MR increases afterload, which can be poorly tolerated in a failing ventricle.
*   **Operative Decision-Making:** Moderate MR may improve with revascularization alone. Persistent significant MR requires annuloplasty. Intraoperative TEE is critical for assessment.
*   **Outcomes:** Operative mortality ranges from 3–60%, with higher risk in acute ischemic MR and poor ventricular function. Mitral repair is superior to replacement when feasible.

### Mitral Stenosis (MS) with CAD
*   **Pathophysiology:** MS is usually the dominant lesion. Right ventricular dysfunction from pulmonary hypertension is a key concern.
*   **Operative Management:** Valve replacement is often required due to calcific changes. Bioprosthetic valves are commonly used. Meticulous right ventricular protection with cardioplegia is essential.
*   **Outcomes:** Operative mortality ~8%, with 7-year survival ~50%. Bioprosthetic valves confer a survival advantage in older patients with CAD.

### Complex Combinations (AS+MR+CAD; AR+MR+CAD)
*   **AS+MR+CAD:** Functional MR often improves after AVR alone. If intrinsic mitral disease is present, repair is performed after AVR. Operative mortality 12–16%.
*   **AR+MR+CAD:** Preoperative assessment of myocardial viability is critical. Mitral regurgitation is often secondary to ventricular dilation and may require only annuloplasty. Operative mortality is high in this group.

## Chapter 46: Reoperative Valve Surgery
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Risk Factors
*   **Increasing Incidence:** The number of reoperative valve surgeries is rising due to an aging population and increased use of bioprostheses with finite durability.
*   **Key Risk Factors:** Advanced age, poor NYHA class, urgent/emergency status, concomitant CABG, prosthetic valve endocarditis, renal dysfunction, and pulmonary hypertension are major predictors of mortality.
*   **Mortality Trends:** Operative mortality for first reoperation is ~5-10%, rising to ~14% for third reoperation. Mortality is higher in mitral position (19.6%) vs. aortic (5.9%) in some series.

### Preoperative Planning
*   **Imaging:** ECG-gated multidetector CT (MDCT) is the modality of choice to assess proximity of RV, aorta, and bypass grafts to the sternum, modifying surgical approach in up to 20% of cases.
*   **Reentry Strategy:** Peripheral cannulation (femoral, axillary) before sternotomy is recommended in high-risk cases. Early institution of CPB prior to sternal reentry prevents catastrophic injury.

### Reoperative Aortic Valve Surgery
*   **Patent LITA Graft:** Management is controversial. Options include: (1) dissection and clamping of LITA (risk of injury), or (2) "no-dissection" technique with deep hypothermic cardioplegia (avoids graft injury but risks cardioplegia washout).
*   **Minimally Invasive Approach:** Partial upper resternotomy limits dissection, reduces bleeding, and is feasible for isolated reoperative AVR.
*   **Homograft Reoperation:** Simplified approach with mechanical valve implantation is preferred; redo root operations are reserved for specific indications (endocarditis, root pathology).
*   **Aortic Valve Bypass (AVB):** Apical-aortic conduit is an alternative for "inoperable" patients with severe AS, porcelain aorta, or vulnerable grafts, avoiding sternotomy and CPB.
*   **Transcatheter Valve-in-Valve (VinV):** Promising alternative for high-risk patients with failed bioprostheses; 30-day mortality ~7.6% with 1-year survival ~83%.

### Reoperative Mitral Valve Surgery
*   **Right Thoracotomy:** Preferred approach to avoid resternotomy risks, especially with patent bypass grafts. Performed with femorofemoral bypass and fibrillatory arrest (if no AI). Provides excellent exposure with minimal dissection.
*   **Transseptal Approach:** Minimizes dissection and is useful for combined mitral and tricuspid procedures.
*   **Periprosthetic Leak:** If valve is competent, direct patch repair (bovine pericardium) is preferred. For large defects, valve replacement with a pericardial skirt may be necessary.
*   **Subvalvular Preservation:** Preserving chordal apparatus in redo MVR reduces operative mortality (3.6% vs. 13.3%).

### Reoperative Tricuspid Valve Surgery
*   **High Risk:** TVR is associated with high operative mortality (~22%). Functional etiology and pulmonary hypertension are major risk factors.
*   **Endocarditis:** Tricuspid valvectomy without replacement is tolerated initially but leads to late right-sided failure. Delayed replacement (3-9 months) is an option.

## Chapter 47: Thoracic Aortic Dissection
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

## Classification & Timing
*   **Stanford Type A:** Involves ascending aorta (60-75% of cases).
*   **Stanford Type B:** Does not involve ascending aorta (25-40% of cases).
*   **Timing:**
    *   Acute: < 2 weeks.
    *   Subacute: 2 weeks - 2 months.
    *   Chronic: > 2 months.

## Key Epidemiology & Risk Factors
*   **Incidence:** 0.2–2.95 per 100,000/year. Often underdiagnosed (only 15% diagnosed ante-mortem in one series).
*   **Hypertension:** Present in >75% of cases.
*   **Age & Sex:**
    *   Type A: Mean age 50-56, M:F 1.7-2.6:1.
    *   Type B: Mean age 60-70, M:F 2.3-3:1.
*   **Connective Tissue Disorders:** Marfan’s (1:5000), Ehlers-Danlos IV (1:5000), bicuspid aortic valve.

## Clinical Presentation
*   **Mortality:** 40% die immediately; untreated Type A has **~1% mortality per hour** (50% at 48h).
*   **Pain:**
    *   Type A: Retrosternal (anterior chest).
    *   Type B: Interscapular (back).
    *   Often described as "tearing" or "ripping."
*   **Malperfusion Syndromes (Frequency):**
    *   Renal: 23-75%
    *   Extremities: 25-60%
    *   Mesenteric: 10-20%
    *   Coronary: 5-11%
    *   Cerebral: 3-13%
    *   Spinal: 2-9%
*   **Other Signs:** Syncope (up to 40% in Type A), aortic regurgitation (up to 75% in Type A), pulse deficits.

## Diagnostic Imaging (Sensitivity/Specificity)
*   **CT Angiography (CTA):** 90-100% / 90-100% (Modality of choice for stable patients).
*   **Transesophageal Echo (TEE):** 90-99% / 85-98% (Modality of choice for unstable patients in OR).
*   **MRI/MRA:** 98-100% / 98-100% (Best for surveillance, not acute).
*   **Aortography:** 80-90% / 88-93% (Historical gold standard, now second-line).

## Initial Medical Management (Anti-Impulse Therapy)
*   **Goal:** SBP **90-110 mmHg**; HR **< 60 bpm**.
*   **Protocol:**
    1.  **Narcotics** (e.g., Morphine) for pain control (reduces catecholamines).
    2.  **IV Beta-Blocker FIRST** (e.g., Esmolol) to reduce dP/dt.
    3.  **Vasodilator SECOND** (e.g., Nitroprusside or Nicardipine) if SBP still high. *Warning: Never give vasodilator without beta-blocker (reflex tachycardia increases shear stress).*

## Surgical Indications
*   **Acute Type A:** **Presence of dissection = Indication.** (All but highest-risk patients).
*   **Acute Type B (Complicated):**
    *   Failure of medical management (pain, uncontrolled HTN).
    *   Expanding diameter / progression.
    *   Impending rupture (periaortic hematoma).
    *   Malperfusion.
*   **Chronic Dissection (Any Type):**
    *   Aneurysm size: **Type A > 5.5 cm**; **Type B > 6.5 cm**.
    *   Growth rate: **> 1 cm/year**.
    *   Symptoms (CHF, pain, malperfusion).

## Surgical Techniques (Type A)
*   **Cannulation:** Right axillary artery (preferred), femoral, or direct ascending aorta.
*   **Cerebral Protection:**
    *   Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA). Goal temp **18-21°C** or EEG silence.
    *   Cooling time: min **45 min** if no EEG monitoring.
    *   Adjuncts: Antegrade/Retrograde cerebral perfusion for arrest times >20 min.
*   **Aortic Valve:** Preserved in ~85% of acute cases (commissural resuspension). Root replacement (composite graft) if valve unsalvageable.

## Type B Dissection: Outcomes & Management
*   **Uncomplicated (70-80%):**
    *   **Treatment:** Medical management (anti-impulse).
    *   **Survival:** In-hospital ~90%; 1-year ~85%; 5-year ~71%.
    *   **INSTEAD Trial:** 2-year survival similar for medical vs. elective TEVAR; however, at 5 years, TEVAR reduced aorta-specific mortality (6.9% vs 19.3%).
*   **Complicated (20-30%):**
    *   **Treatment:** Endovascular (TEVAR, fenestration/stenting) preferred.
    *   **IRAD Data (Mortality):** Surgical 34% vs. Endovascular 11% (similar to medical management of uncomplicated cases).
    *   **Meta-Analysis (Endovascular):** Procedural success 98%; peri-op mortality 5%; paraplegia ~0-3.4%.

## Long-Term Follow-Up
*   **False Lumen:** Remains patent in >90% after Type A repair (leading cause of late death).
*   **Surveillance Imaging:**
    *   Modality: CTA or MRA.
    *   Schedule: Baseline at discharge; 6, 12 months; then **yearly** if stable.
    *   If expansion >0.5cm/6mo, shorten interval to 3 months.
*   **Blood Pressure:** Strict control (SBP <120-130 mmHg) with beta-blockers as first line.
*   **Re-intervention:** ~20-40% of chronic patients require surgery for aneurysm progression.

## Chapter 48: Ascending and Arch Aortic Aneurysms
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

## Key Etiologies & Genetics
*   **Marfan Syndrome (MFS):** Autosomal dominant (FBN1 gene); incidence 1:3,000-10,000. Results in aortoannular ectasia. Losartan (ARB) may slow aortic growth (TGF-β antagonism).
*   **Loeys-Dietz Syndrome (LDS):** Autosomal dominant (TGFBR1/2). More aggressive than MFS; rupture at smaller sizes.
*   **Ehlers-Danlos (Type IV):** Defective Type III collagen; extremely friable tissue.
*   **Familial TAAD (FTAAD):** Accounts for ~20% of thoracic aneurysms. Mutations in ACTA2, SMAD3, etc. Risk of rupture/dissection at diameters <5 cm.
*   **Bicuspid Aortic Valve (BAV):** Male predominance (3:1). 9% prevalence in first-degree relatives. Associated with **4 distinct aortopathy patterns** (Fazel): Root only (13%), Ascending only (10%), Ascending + Proximal arch (28%), Diffuse (45%).
*   **Infectious/Inflammatory:** Syphilis (tree-bark appearance), Takayasu’s (young females), Giant Cell Arteritis (elderly).

## Natural History & Size Criteria
*   **Untreated Prognosis:** 5-year survival only **13%** (vs. 75% controls) due to rupture/dissection (Bickerstaff).
*   **Growth Rates:** <4 cm: ~0.1 cm/yr; >8 cm: ~0.4 cm/yr.
*   **Rupture Risk (Coady):** 6.0-6.9 cm aneurysm has **4.3x** higher risk of rupture/dissection vs. 4.0-4.9 cm.
*   **IRAD Caveat:** 59% of acute Type A dissections occur in aortas <5.5 cm; 40% occur in aortas <5.0 cm.

## Indications for Surgery (Elective)
*   **Degenerative Aneurysm:** **5.5 cm** absolute diameter, or growth rate **>1 cm/year**.
*   **Connective Tissue Disorder (MFS):** **4.5 cm** (or aortic ratio 1.3-1.4).
*   **Loeys-Dietz Syndrome:** **4.2 cm**.
*   **Bicuspid Aortic Valve (BAV):**
    *   STS Guidelines: **5.0 cm** (4.5 cm with family history of dissection).
    *   ACC/AHA Guidelines: **5.5 cm** (5.0 cm with family history or rapid growth >5 mm/yr).
*   **Chronic Dissection:** **4.5 cm** (weak aortic wall).
*   **Concomitant Cardiac Surgery:** Replace ascending aorta if > **5.0 cm** or ratio >1.5.
*   **Pseudoaneurysm:** Repair upon diagnosis (high rupture risk).

## Medical Management
*   **Anti-impulse Therapy:** Beta-blockers are first line (decrease dP/dT).
    *   *Landmark Study (Shores):* In MFS, beta-blockers reduced aortic dilatation and composite endpoints over 10 years.
*   **Exercise Restriction:** Avoid isometric exercise (weight lifting > 1/3-1/2 body weight).

## Preoperative Preparation
*   **Carotid Duplex:** For patients >65 years (stroke risk).
*   **Coronary Angiography:** Routine; bypass lesions >50-60% stenosis.
*   **Imaging:** CT/MRI of entire aorta (10-20% have concomitant AAA).
*   **Pulmonary Function:** Spirometry and ABG if COPD suspected.

## Cerebral Protection (DHCA)
*   **Goal:** EEG silence. Cooling time minimum **50 min** if no EEG monitoring (Cheung: only 60% achieve silence at 30 min/18°C).
*   **Temperature:** Core 18-21°C. Rewarming gradient **<10°C** (perfusate to patient).
*   **Safe Arrest Times:** ~30 min at 15°C; ~40 min at 10°C. TND risk increases with age and arrest time >25 min.
*   **Antegrade Cerebral Perfusion (ACP):** Used for arrest times >35-45 min. Flow rate 10 cc/kg/min; pressure 40-70 mmHg.
*   **Retrograde Cerebral Perfusion (RCP):** Flow 200-300 cc/min; maintain SVC pressure at 25 mmHg.

## Operative Techniques
*   **Cannulation:** Right axillary (preferred for ACP via 8-10 mm graft), ascending aorta, or femoral (avoid if atheroma present).
*   **Myocardial Protection:** Cold blood cardioplegia (6-8°C septal temp). Give every 20 min.
*   **Root Replacement (Bentall):** For aortoannular ectasia or abnormal leaflets.
    *   Coronary buttons mobilized 1-2 cm. Cabrol or interposition vein grafts if buttons too friable.
*   **Valve-Sparing Root Replacement (David/Reimplantation):** For normal leaflets with dilated annulus/sinuses.
*   **Modified Wheat:** Separate AVR + supracoronary tube graft (sinus segment normal, elderly patients).
*   **Hemiarch Replacement:** Resect aorta to innominate (greater curve) and to subclavian (lesser curve). Preferred for proximal arch involvement.
*   **Total Arch Replacement:**
    *   **Kazui Technique:** Branched arch graft with selective ACP.
    *   **Elephant Trunk (ET):** For two-stage repair of extensive arch + descending disease.
    *   **Arch First (Kouchoukos):** Bilateral thoracotomy approach.

## Hybrid and Endovascular Approaches
*   **Landing Zones (0-4):** Zone 0 (ascending to innominate) is proximal landing zone for hybrid arch repair.
*   **Hybrid Type I-III (Desai):**
    *   Type I: Debranch + TEVAR (suitable Zone 0).
    *   Type II: Ascending replacement + debranch + TEVAR (needs Zone 0 reconstruction).
    *   Type III: Mega-aorta requiring extensive reconstruction.
*   **Outcomes (Hybrid Pooled Analysis):** Mortality 10.8%, Stroke 7%, Spinal cord ischemia 7%, Endoleak up to 15%.

## Postoperative Complications & Outcomes
*   **Bleeding:** Re-exploration rate 2.4-11.1%. Use of antifibrinolytics (aminocaproic acid).
*   **Stroke:** Risk increases with DHCA >40 min.
*   **Mortality:**
    *   Elective root replacement (Gott, Marfan): 1.5% (elective) vs. 12% (emergent).
    *   General series: 1.7-17.1% (depends on acuity and arch involvement).
*   **Late Survival:** 73-92% at 5 years; 60-73% at 10 years.
*   **Reoperation:** Freedom 86-90% at 10 years. Higher in MFS, inclusion technique, or inadequate initial resection.
*   **Graft Infection:** 0.9-6%; mortality 25-75%. Treatment: Graft explant, homograft, omental flap.

## Chapter 49: Descending and Thoracoabdominal Aortic Aneurysms
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Definitions and Classification
*   **Descending Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm (DTAA):** Aneurysm limited to the chest, distal to the left subclavian artery.
*   **Thoracoabdominal Aortic Aneurysm (TAAA):** Aneurysm that traverses the diaphragm, extending into both the chest and abdomen. The **Crawford classification** (extents I–IV) standardizes reporting based on the extent of aortic involvement, crucial for risk stratification and surgical planning.

### Pathogenesis and Natural History
*   **Etiology:** Historically tertiary syphilis; today, the most common types are **atherosclerotic aneurysms**, though the primary mechanism is **age-related medial degeneration** (elastin fragmentation, fibrosis, medial degeneration). Other causes include aortic dissection, connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, Ehlers-Danlos), aortitis, infection (mycotic saccular aneurysms), and trauma.
*   **Risk of Rupture:** The risk of rupture increases sharply at diameters > 6 cm, with a 14% combined risk of rupture, dissection, or death. For aneurysms ≥ 6 cm, the 5-year rupture risk is 31%.
*   **Expansion Rate:** The descending thoracic aorta expands at an average rate of 1–4 mm/year, accelerating as diameter increases. Chronic dissection increases the rate of dilation.

### Diagnosis and Indications for Repair
*   **Asymptomatic Presentation:** Approximately 43% of degenerative TAAAs are asymptomatic. Most are discovered incidentally on imaging.
*   **Symptomatic Presentation:** The most frequent symptom is back pain between the scapulae. Other symptoms (hoarseness, dysphagia, hemoptysis) result from compression or erosion of adjacent structures.
*   **Indications for Elective Repair (Asymptomatic):**
    *   Degenerative aneurysm: diameter > 6 cm.
    *   Chronic dissection: diameter > 5.5 cm.
    *   Rapid expansion: > 0.5 cm/year.
    *   Connective tissue disorders: lower diameter thresholds.
*   **Indications for Urgent Repair (Symptomatic):** New onset pain (heralding expansion, leakage, or rupture), malperfusion, or acute dissection superimposed on a degenerative aneurysm.

### Open Surgical Repair and Organ Protection
*   **Multimodal Organ Protection:** A multimodal approach is essential, including moderate heparinization (1 mg/kg), mild passive hypothermia (32–34°C), left heart bypass (LHB), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, cold renal perfusion, and selective visceral perfusion.
*   **Spinal Cord Protection:** Paraplegia risk is highest for extent II repairs. Key strategies include CSF drainage (maintaining pressure 8–10 mm Hg), LHB for distal perfusion, aggressive reattachment of segmental arteries (T8 to L1), and sequential aortic clamping. Modern centers report paraplegia rates of 1–5%.
*   **Renal Protection:** Cold (4°C) crystalloid perfusion via balloon catheters is independently protective against acute renal dysfunction.
*   **Visceral Protection:** Selective perfusion of the celiac and superior mesenteric arteries via balloon catheters from the LHB circuit reduces hepatic and bowel ischemia.
*   **Operative Mortality:** In a contemporary series (2009–2015), 30-day mortality was 5% overall, varying by extent: 4% for DTAA, 3% for extent I, 7% for extent II, 3% for extent III, and 3% for extent IV.

### Endovascular and Hybrid Approaches
*   **TEVAR:** Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) is now the dominant approach for DTAA. It is associated with lower early mortality and morbidity than open repair, though 5-year survival is similar, with a higher reintervention rate.
*   **TAAA Endovascular:** Fenestrated or branched endovascular repair of TAAA remains experimental in the US.
*   **Hybrid Approaches:** Combine open and endovascular techniques (e.g., elephant trunk, frozen elephant trunk, open visceral bypass with stent-graft coverage) to treat complex anatomy, though they have not yet substantially decreased morbidity and mortality compared to open repair.

### Postoperative Management and Surveillance
*   **Blood Pressure Control:** Critical to balance hypertension (risk of bleeding) and hypotension (risk of spinal cord ischemia). Target mean arterial pressure (MAP) is 80–90 mm Hg.
*   **Lifelong Surveillance:** Annual CT or MR imaging is recommended to detect new aneurysms in other aortic segments or pseudoaneurysms, especially in patients with connective tissue disorders or residual distal aortic dissection.

## Chapter 50: Endovascular Therapy for the Treatment of Thoracic Aortic Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Historical Development
*   **Pioneering Era:** Endovascular stent-graft technology was first introduced by Parodi for abdominal aortic aneurysms. At Stanford, a collaborative effort produced the first homemade thoracic stent graft using Gianturco Z stents covered with woven Dacron, implanted in 1992.
*   **First-Generation Results:** In a high-risk trial of 103 patients (60% deemed unfit for open repair), complete aneurysm thrombosis was achieved in 83%, with 30-day mortality of 9%. Actuarial survival was 82% at 1 year, 49% at 5 years, and 27% at 8 years.
*   **Commercial Devices:** In 2005–2006, commercially produced stent grafts became available (Gore Excluder TAG, Medtronic Talent, Cook Zenith), featuring smaller delivery systems, nitinol exoskeletons, and improved flexibility.

### Endoleak Classification
*   **Type I:** Endoleak at proximal (A) or distal (B) attachment sites—failure of hemostatic seal.
*   **Type II:** Communication between a branch vessel (e.g., intercostal arteries) and the excluded aneurysm sac.
*   **Type III:** Endoleak at graft-to-graft junctions.
*   **Type IV:** Increase in aneurysm sac size without identifiable endoleak ("endotension").

### Early Results (TAG Trial)
*   **Outcomes:** Compared to open repair, stent-graft patients had significantly lower operative blood loss, renal failure, paraplegia, and mortality. ICU stay, hospital stay, and time to return to normal activity were reduced by approximately 50%.
*   **Mortality:** Aneurysm-related mortality at 2 years was 3% (stent-graft) vs. 10% (open repair); all-cause mortality was similar between groups.

### Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm
*   **Natural History:** Thoracic aortic aneurysms have a 5-year rupture risk of 30%. The Yale Group reported mean rupture/dissection rates of 2%/year for aneurysms <5 cm, 3% for 5.0–5.9 cm, and 6.9% for ≥6 cm.
*   **Open Repair Outcomes:** Mortality 5–10%, paraplegia 3–8% from experienced centers.
*   **Endovascular Considerations:** Coverage of the left subclavian artery is frequently employed; preoperative transposition is reserved for select cases. Spinal drains are recommended for high-risk patients.

### Thoracic Aortic Dissection
*   **Complicated Acute Type B (rupture, malperfusion, rapid expansion):** Endovascular repair covers the primary entry tear, redirecting flow into the true lumen and eliminating dynamic malperfusion. Uncovered stents can address static malperfusion in branch vessels.
*   **Uncomplicated Acute Type B:** Increasing enthusiasm for prophylactic stent grafting to promote aortic remodeling and prevent late aneurysmal degeneration.
*   **Chronic Dissection:** More challenging due to multiple fenestrations and extension into the abdomen. Complete false lumen exclusion remains difficult.

### Penetrating Atherosclerotic Ulcers (PAU) and Intramural Hematomas (IMH)
*   **Relationship:** IMH is often associated with or precipitated by PAUs. Covering the PAU with a stent graft may limit progression of IMH and allow healing.
*   **Outcomes:** In a Stanford series of 26 patients (average 51 months follow-up), primary success was 92%, with actuarial survival of 85%, 76%, and 70% at 1, 3, and 5 years. Perioperative mortality was 12%. Increasing aortic diameter and female gender were predictors of treatment failure.

### Thoracic Aortic Trauma
*   **SVS Classification:** Class I (intimal laceration only), Class II (intimal laceration + mild IMH), Class III (pseudoaneurysm), Class IV (rupture/impending rupture).
*   **Management:** Classes I and II—observation with repeat imaging. Classes III and IV—stent-graft coverage is procedure of choice when anatomically feasible. Delayed repair may be appropriate for stable patients to allow recovery from polytrauma.
*   **Paraplegia Risk:** Endografts longer than 15 cm may be associated with increased risk.

### Future Directions
*   Unibranch grafts for proximal repairs into the aortic arch; multibranch and fenestrated grafts for thoracoabdominal aneurysms.
*   Lifelong surveillance is mandatory, as even completely thrombosed sacs can elongate with late Type I endoleaks.

## Chapter 51: Trauma to the Great Vessels
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Outcomes
*   **Incidence:** Blunt thoracic aortic injury (BTAI) occurs in 7500–8000 cases/year in the US, with 75–90% dying at the scene. Mortality is 46% for nonoperative management, 19% for open repair, and 9% for endovascular repair.
*   **Common Injury Sites:** Aortic isthmus (most common), followed by innominate artery base, left subclavian artery base, and left carotid artery base.

### Zonal Anatomy and Classification
*   **Landing Zone Map:** Standardized anatomical classification of the thoracic aorta (Fig. 51-1) for endograft deployment and surgical planning.
*   **BTAI Grading (Fig. 51-3):** Grade I (intimal tear/flap), Grade II (intramural hematoma), Grade III (pseudoaneurysm), Grade IV (rupture). SVS guidelines recommend TEVAR for Grades II–IV; Grade I typically heals spontaneously.

### Diagnosis
*   **Chest X-Ray:** Mediastinal widening (>8 cm or >25% chest width), aortic knob obliteration, apical cap, first rib fracture. Up to 40% of aortic ruptures have normal chest x-ray.
*   **Helical CT:** Sensitivity nearing 100%; identifies intimal flaps, periaortic hematoma, pseudoaneurysm.
*   **TEE:** Sensitivity 93%, specificity 100%; useful in unstable patients.
*   **Aortography:** Gold standard; now used primarily for endovascular intervention rather than diagnosis.

### Management Principles
*   **Anti-impulse Therapy:** Goal SBP < 120 mm Hg or MAP < 80 mm Hg with beta-blockade to reduce aortic wall stress.
*   **Delayed Repair:** Safe in selected patients with severe associated injuries (intracranial, intra-abdominal hemorrhage). In-hospital rupture rates reduced with aggressive beta-blockade.
*   **TEVAR:** Preferred treatment for BTAI; associated with decreased mortality, paraplegia, operating time, and hospital stay compared to open repair.

### Ascending Aorta
*   **Mechanism:** Torsion, water-hammer effect from aortic occlusion at diaphragm.
*   **Repair:** Open via sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass; deep hypothermic circulatory arrest may be required. TEVAR for ascending aorta remains investigational.

### Aortic Arch Branch Vessels
*   **Innominate Artery:** Second most common site. Repair via aorto-innominate bypass graft; endovascular stent-graft repair feasible in stable patients.
*   **Carotid Artery:** Anticoagulation for dissection; operative repair for pseudoaneurysm via sternotomy with interposition grafting.
*   **Subclavian Artery:** Mortality 5–30%. Endovascular repair increasingly used; stent fracture risk from compression between clavicle and first rib requires long-term surveillance.

### Descending Thoracic Aorta
*   **Mechanism:** Whiplash theory (differential deceleration) or osseous pinch (compression between sternum and spine). Isthmus involved in 84–97% of surgical cases.
*   **Open Repair:** Paraplegia risk reduced with lower body perfusion (centrifugal pump, partial left heart bypass). Cross-clamp time < 30 minutes critical with clamp-and-sew technique.
*   **TEVAR:** Requires proximal landing zone ≥ 1.5 cm; left subclavian coverage safe in most patients. Oversizing 10–20%. Long-term surveillance with CTA at 1, 3, and 12 months, then every 3–5 years.

## Chapter 52: Pulmonary Embolism and Pulmonary Thromboendarterectomy
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology
*   **Incidence:** PE occurs in >630,000 symptomatic cases/year in the US (third leading cause of death). DVT affects ~2.5 million Americans annually.
*   **Mortality:** 12–21% of hospitalized PE patients die in hospital; 24–39% die within 12 months. 75% of autopsy-proved PE undiagnosed antemortem.

### Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)
*   **Virchow's Triad:** Venous stasis, vein wall injury, hypercoagulopathy.
*   **Risk Factors:** Previous thromboembolism, age >45, immobilization >7 days, major surgery, cancer, multiple trauma.
*   **Diagnosis:** Duplex scanning (sensitivity 89–100%), venography (gold standard), MRI.
*   **Prophylaxis:** Compression stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression, low-dose heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin.

### Acute Pulmonary Embolism
*   **Classification:** Minor (<30% occlusion, stable), major (30–50% occlusion, mild hypotension), massive (>50% occlusion, hemodynamic instability).
*   **Diagnosis:** V/Q scan (high sensitivity), CT angiography, TTE/TEE (right ventricular dysfunction).
*   **Management:** Heparin, thrombolytics (higher bleeding risk), catheter embolectomy, emergency surgical embolectomy (40–92% mortality), ECLS for unstable patients.

### Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension (CTEPH)
*   **Incidence:** Estimated 2500–25,000 new cases/year in US; often no history of acute PE.
*   **Pathology:** Organized thrombus with bands, webs, or complete occlusion; classification Type I (central clot) to Type IV (inoperable small-vessel disease).
*   **Diagnosis:** V/Q scan (essential), pulmonary angiography (gold standard). PVR often >800 dynes/sec/cm<sup>-5</sup>.

### Pulmonary Thromboendarterectomy (PTE)
*   **Indications:** NYHA class III–IV, PVR >300 dynes/sec/cm<sup>-5</sup>, surgically accessible thrombus.
*   **Technique:** Median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass, deep hypothermia (20°C), circulatory arrest (≤20 min per side). Bilateral endarterectomy with dissection to subsegmental "tails."
*   **Outcomes:** Mortality <5% in recent series (>3000 patients since 1990), <2% in last 5 years. 95% of patients in NYHA class I–II at 1 year. Survival 75% at ≥6 years.
*   **Complications:** Reperfusion injury (10%), persistent pulmonary hypertension (Type IV disease), delirium (if circulatory arrest >55 min).

## Chapter 53: Interventional Therapy for Atrial and Ventricular Arrhythmias
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Ablation Technologies
*   **Surgical Ablation:** First successful surgical elimination of accessory pathway reported in 1968; limited by thoracotomy morbidity.
*   **Catheter Ablation:** Scheinman (1981) first closed-chest ablation using DC shock; RF energy introduced in 1987 as primary energy source.
*   **RF Biophysics:** Resistive heating to 50–58°C causes coagulation necrosis; lesion size proportional to temperature, electrode size, contact force, duration.
*   **Cryoablation:** Allows reversible injury ("Cryomapping") before permanent lesion; preferred near AV node and in young patients.
*   **Risks:** PV stenosis, atrioesophageal fistula (0.04%), phrenic nerve paralysis, cardiac tamponade, stroke.

### Electrophysiology Study
*   **Catheter Positioning:** Femoral vein access; catheters in RA, RV, His bundle, CS (Fig. 53-1); left heart access via transseptal or retrograde aortic.
*   **Anticoagulation:** Heparin for left-sided ablation (ACT 300–400 sec); uninterrupted warfarin now standard; reversal with protamine/PCC.
*   **Complications:** Overall 3.1% for ablative procedures; access complications (bleeding, hematoma, AV fistula), cardiac perforation/tamponade, heart block, stroke.

### Mapping Techniques
*   **Activation Mapping:** Earliest activation site identifies focal tachycardia origin.
*   **Entrainment Mapping:** Post-pacing interval = tachycardia cycle length identifies re-entrant circuit sites.
*   **Pace Mapping:** Paced QRS compared to tachycardia QRS; match indicates exit site.
*   **Advanced Systems:** CARTO (magnetic field, 3D activation/voltage maps), EnSite (electrical field, real-time catheter visualization), ICE (intracardiac ultrasound, Fig. 53-2).

### Clinical Applications

**AVNRT (most common SVT)**
- Re-entry using slow/fast AV nodal pathways; slow pathway ablation success >96%, complete heart block <1%.

**AVRT (WPW syndrome)**
- Re-entry using accessory pathway; orthodromic (narrow QRS, retrograde P after QRS), antidromic (wide QRS). Success 94%, mortality <1%.

**Atrial Tachycardia**
- Focal (10% of SVTs); often from crista terminalis, PV ostia, valve annuli. Success 86%, recurrence 8%.

**Atrial Flutter**
- Typical: cavotricuspid isthmus-dependent; ablation success 87–95%. Atypical: post-surgical or post-ablation macro-reentry.

**Atrial Fibrillation**
- Triggers from pulmonary veins; PVI success: 70–75% (paroxysmal), 63–65% (persistent). Major complications 4.5%, mortality 0.15%. Alternative: Cryoballoon, laser balloon.

**Ventricular Tachycardia**
- Scar-related (ischemic > nonischemic); substrate mapping identifies low-voltage regions. Success: 53% VT-free at 6 months. Epicardial approach often required for nonischemic cardiomyopathy.

### Outcomes and Cost-effectiveness
*   AF ablation superior to antiarrhythmic drugs (89% vs 23% AF-free at 1 year).
*   Cost-effective with incremental cost-effectiveness ratio ~USD50,000/QALY.
*   Future directions: force-sensing catheters, remote navigation, left atrial appendage occlusion devices.

## Chapter 54: Surgery for Atrial Fibrillation
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Historical Evolution
*   **Left Atrial Isolation (1980):** First procedure specifically for AF; confined AF to left atrium but did not eliminate thromboembolic risk.
*   **AV Node Ablation (1982):** Controlled ventricular rate but required pacemaker; did not address hemodynamics or stroke risk.
*   **Corridor Procedure (1985):** Isolated septal strip; abandoned due to persistent AF in atria and no hemodynamic benefit.
*   **Atrial Transection (1985):** First attempt to terminate AF; laid foundation for Maze procedure.

### The Cox-Maze Procedure
*   **Maze III ("Cut-and-Sew"):** Gold standard for decades. Long-term freedom from symptomatic AF: **97%** at mean 5.4 years.
*   **Maze IV (Ablation-Assisted):** Uses bipolar RF and cryoablation to replace incisions. Freedom from AF: **91%** at 6 months, **90%** at 24 months.
*   **Stroke Reduction:** Post-Maze stroke rate **0.2%/year**; most patients discontinue anticoagulation.

### Ablation Technologies
*   **Cryoablation:** Freezes tissue (‑89.5°C with N₂O, ‑185.7°C with Ar); preserves collagen; long ablation time (1–3 min); risk of coronary stenosis.
*   **RF Energy:** Unipolar RF unreliable on beating heart (only 10% transmural). **Bipolar RF clamps** achieve transmural lesions on beating heart; superior safety profile.

### Surgical Techniques
*   **Cox-Maze IV:** Biatrial lesion set; performed on cardiopulmonary bypass via sternotomy or right minithoracotomy. Bipolar RF for most lesions; cryoablation for mitral/tricuspid annulus.
*   **Left Atrial Procedures:** PVI + mitral isthmus line; avoids right atrial lesions. Success rates lower than biatrial approach (73% vs 87%).
*   **PVI:** Stand-alone minimally invasive; isolates pulmonary veins. Success: 66–88% for paroxysmal AF; only 35% for persistent AF at 6 months.

### Indications
*   Symptomatic AF undergoing other cardiac surgery (Class I)
*   Stand-alone ablation for symptomatic patients preferring surgery, failed catheter ablation, or not catheter candidates
*   Relative: contraindication to anticoagulation with CHADS₂ ≥2

### Future Directions
*   **ECGI (Electrocardiographic Imaging):** Noninvasive mapping of AF mechanisms; allows patient-specific lesion design.
*   **Hybrid Procedures:** Combine epicardial surgical and endocardial catheter ablation; promising early results (74% freedom at 24 months).

## Chapter 55: Surgical Implantation of Pacemakers and Automatic Defibrillators
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Pacemaker Fundamentals
*   **Components:** Generator (battery, telemetry, integrated circuits) + leads (endocardial or epicardial).
*   **Pacemaker Code (NASPE/BPEG):** Three-letter code: I = chamber paced, II = chamber sensed, III = response (I = inhibited, T = triggered, D = dual). R suffix indicates rate responsiveness.
*   **Indications (Class I):** Complete heart block, symptomatic second-degree AV block, symptomatic sinus node dysfunction, advanced heart block after MI.
*   **Dual-Chamber (DDD) Pacing:** Maintains AV synchrony, preserves atrial contribution to stroke volume (5–15% increase vs VVI). Preferred except in chronic atrial fibrillation.

### Implantation Techniques
*   **Venous Access:** Cephalic cutdown preferred (lower pneumothorax risk, 0% in author's series vs 1.8% with subclavian puncture). Subclavian puncture alternative but carries risk of pneumothorax and "subclavian crush."
*   **Lead Types:** Positive fixation (screw-in) leads preferred for thin-walled chambers. Bipolar leads reduce oversensing vs unipolar.
*   **Pacing Thresholds:** Ventricular < 0.7 V, R-wave > 5 mV; atrial < 2.0 V, P-wave > 2.0 mV; impedance 400–1000 ohms.
*   **Complications:** Early 6.7%, reoperation 4.9%. Lead displacement 2%, pneumothorax 1.8%, infection 0.8%, death rare.

### Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy (CRT)
*   **Indications:** LVEF ≤35%, QRS >120 ms, NYHA class III–IV. Biventricular pacing (RV apex + coronary sinus) improves LV function.
*   **CS Lead Insertion:** Technical failure rate 5–10%. Epicardial LV lead via thoracotomy or minimal access for failed endocardial attempts.

### Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillators (ICDs)
*   **Indications:** Secondary prevention (survivors of VT/VF), primary prevention (ischemic cardiomyopathy LVEF ≤30–35%, nonischemic cardiomyopathy LVEF ≤35%).
*   **Efficacy:** Sudden death mortality 1–2%/year, lowest of any therapy. ICD + CRT (CRT-D) for heart failure with prolonged QRS.
*   **DFT Testing:** Confirms 10-J safety margin; increasingly controversial for primary prevention implants.
*   **Complications:** Lead failure ~50% by 10 years; inappropriate shocks up to 30% (sinus tachycardia, SVT). Infection requires complete hardware removal.

### Special Populations and Considerations
*   **Pediatric:** Intracardiac loops allow growth; subpectoral generators reduce infection in children <6 years.
*   **Congenital Heart Disease:** Persistent left SVC, Fontan physiology, Mustard procedure require specialized lead placement.
*   **EMI Management:** Unipolar cautery can inhibit pacemaker; reprogram to VOO/DOO during surgery. MRI-compatible systems available.

## Chapter 56: Surgery for Adult Congenital Heart Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Philosophy and Systems
*   **Cumulative Trauma Reduction:** Multidisciplinary team approach selecting least traumatic effective therapy first (e.g., device closure before surgical repair for ASD).
*   **Information Systems:** Web-based EMR with intraoperative images improves reoperative planning; personal health records (PHRs) enhance patient education.

### Reoperative Sternotomy
*   **Risk Factors:** Retrosternal conduits (especially calcified/stented), pulmonary hypertension, RV enlargement, prior sternal infections, outflow tract aneurysms.
*   **Technique:** Cautery dissection from inferior sternal edge; oscillating saw; if injury occurs, immediate neck incision for innominate artery cannulation. Carbon dioxide field flooding reduces air embolism risk.

### Common Procedures

**Atrial Septal Defects**
*   Device closure preferred for secundum defects. Sinus venosus, primum, and defects with deficient rims require surgery.
*   Sinus venosus: Bicaval cannulation, lateral atrial incision, pericardial patch to baffle anomalous veins to left atrium.
*   Primum defects: Cleft mitral valve routinely repaired; results excellent even in advanced age.

**Ebstein's Anomaly**
*   Cone technique: Dissect anterior/posterior leaflets, rotate anterior leaflet clockwise, suture to septal leaflet border, creating cone-shaped valve. Results: low mortality, reduced TR, improved functional class.

**Fontan Revision**
*   Convert atriopulmonary connections to extracardiac cavopulmonary conduit. Combined with arrhythmia surgery and atrioventricular valve repair/replacement. Routine placement of dual-chamber antitachycardia pacemaker recommended.

**Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Reconstruction**
*   Indications: Pulmonary insufficiency/stenosis after tetralogy of Fallot, truncus, pulmonary atresia. Percutaneous pulmonary valve replacement (PPVR) increasingly available (32% stent fractures, benign).
*   Conduit options: Homografts, bovine jugular vein (Contegra), bioprosthetic valves. Mechanical valves no longer used in pulmonary position.

**Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Reconstruction**
*   Ross, Ross-Konno, Konno for complex LVOTO. 4% require conduit replacement at 10 years; 19–39% reoperation rate for autograft at 5–10 years. Reoperations challenging; antiadhesion barriers recommended.

**Arrhythmia Surgery**
*   Right-sided Maze: 93% freedom from arrhythmia. Lead extraction in ACHD patients often requires hybrid surgical/electrophysiology approach.

**Hybrid Procedures**
*   Combined surgical and interventional approaches in single operation. Allows central vascular access, stent deployment under direct vision, and emergent cardiopulmonary bypass support.

**Coarctation of the Aorta**
*   Increasingly managed with balloon dilation and stents. Surgical repair via left thoracotomy with interposition graft; 75% normotensive without medication at follow-up.

### Interventional Device Management
*   Stents in pulmonary arteries and aorta require careful planning for reoperation. After 3 months, stents incorporated into vessel wall—incision through stent with division of all links, onlay patch reconstruction.

### Outcomes
*   Pediatric congenital heart surgeons have lower mortality for ACHD surgery (1.87%) vs adult cardiac surgeons (4.84%, p < .0001). Arrhythmia leading cause of death in cyanotic lesions; myocardial infarction now leading cause in noncyanotic ACHD.

## Chapter 57: Pericardial Disease
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Anatomy and Function
*   **Structure:** Fibroserous sac with two layers (serous visceral/parietal and fibrous). Normal fluid volume 10–20 mL.
*   **Function:** Maintains heart position, prevents acute distention, equalizes ventricular compliance, contributes to diastolic ventricular interaction.

### Congenital Abnormalities
*   **Pericardial Cysts:** Most common congenital disorder; 75% asymptomatic, 70% right costophrenic angle. Observation or resection for large/symptomatic cysts.

### Pathophysiology

**Tamponade**
*   Acute fluid accumulation > pericardial reserve → rapid rise in intrapericardial pressure → impaired diastolic filling, decreased CO, increased CVP. Beck's triad: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds. Pulsus paradoxus from exaggerated ventricular interdependence.
*   As little as 100 mL can cause tamponade acutely; chronic effusions may exceed 1 L.

**Constrictive Pericarditis (CP)**
*   Scarring limits late diastolic filling → 70–80% of filling occurs in first 25–30% of diastole → "square-root sign," prominent *y* descent, septal bounce, Kussmaul's sign.
*   Differentiation from restrictive cardiomyopathy (Table 57-1): CP has normal pericardial thickness in 20% of cases, respiratory variation in flows, preserved ventricular hypertrophy absent.

### Acquired Pericarditis

**Infectious**
*   Viral most common; self-limited. Bacterial: life-threatening, requires drainage. Tuberculous: 30–60% develop CP despite treatment. Fungal: immunocompromised hosts.

**Metabolic**
*   Uremic: treat with dialysis; drainage for tamponade. Drug-induced: discontinue culprit. Rheumatoid arthritis: 50% have effusions; pericardiectomy for constriction. Hypothyroidism: large effusions, tamponade rare.

**Radiation Pericarditis**
*   Now most common CP etiology in US; 10–15 years after mantle radiation. Often combined with RCM, valvular disease, ostial coronary lesions.

**Neoplastic**
*   >95% secondary (lung, breast, lymphoma). Life expectancy <4 months. Pericardial window has lower recurrence than pericardiocentesis.

**Traumatic**
*   Penetrating: tamponade provides hemostasis. Blunt: may cause rupture with cardiac herniation.

### Post-Cardiac Surgery
*   Postpericardiotomy syndrome (5–30%): treat with NSAIDs + colchicine. Postoperative tamponade: localized clot can cause compression; late tamponade (0.5–6%) presents ~3 weeks postop, often with anticoagulation.

### Operations
*   **Pericardiocentesis:** ECG-guided, subxiphoid approach; recurrence rate higher than surgical drainage.
*   **Pericardial Window:** Partial resection via thoracoscopy, thoracotomy, or subxiphoid; lower recurrence.
*   **Pericardiectomy:** Median sternotomy with CPB standby; release ventricles from pericardial shell. Operative mortality 5–6%.
*   **Waffle Procedure:** Epicardial incisions for visceral pericardial thickening when standard pericardiectomy inadequate.

### Key Outcomes
*   Pericardiectomy survival depends on etiology; worst with radiation-induced CP (concurrent RCM).

## Chapter 58: Cardiac Neoplasms
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology
*   **Incidence:** Primary cardiac tumors 0.17–0.19% at autopsy. 75% benign, 25% malignant. Benign: 50% myxomas. Malignant: 75% sarcomas.
*   **Metastatic:** 10–20% of cancer patients have cardiac metastases; 20–40× more common than primary tumors.

### Benign Tumors

**Myxoma**
*   Most common benign tumor (50% of benign). 75% left atrium, 10–20% right atrium. Sporadic (95%) vs familial (5%, Carney complex).
*   Clinical triad: obstruction (CHF 67%), embolization (29%), constitutional symptoms (fever 19%, weight loss 17%).
*   Surgery: complete resection with full-thickness margin. Operative mortality <5%. Recurrence: 1–4% sporadic, 12–40% with abnormal DNA, 22% familial.
*   Minimally invasive approaches (MICS) feasible for selected tumors.

**Other Benign Tumors**
*   **Papillary fibroelastoma:** Valve tumors; embolic risk → resect when diagnosed.
*   **Rhabdomyoma:** Most common in children; associated with tuberous sclerosis.
*   **Fibroma:** Second most common in children; may cause sudden death.
*   **Lipoma, hemangioma, paraganglioma, Castleman tumor:** Rare; resection for symptoms.

### Malignant Tumors (Sarcomas)
*   **Classification by location** (prognosis more dependent on location than histology):
    *   **Right heart sarcomas:** Bulky, infiltrative, early metastasis. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy + radical resection.
    *   **Left heart sarcomas:** Solid, less infiltrative, later metastasis. Often misdiagnosed as myxoma.
    *   **PA sarcomas:** Grow distally within vessel; often confused with pulmonary embolism.

**Angiosarcoma**
*   Most common primary cardiac malignancy (33%). Predilection for right atrium. 90% mortality within 9–12 months without resection.

**Cardiac Autotransplantation**
*   Technique: explant heart, ex vivo tumor resection, reconstruction, reimplantation.
*   Outcomes: 30-day survival 85%, 1-year 59%, 2-year 44% for primary sarcomas. Autotransplant + pneumonectomy: 43% operative mortality (contraindicated).

### Secondary Metastatic Tumors
*   Most common primaries: lung, breast, lymphoma, melanoma, leukemia.
*   Presentation: pericardial effusion (most common), tamponade, arrhythmias.
*   Management: subxiphoid pericardial window for symptomatic effusions.

### Special Considerations
*   **Renal cell carcinoma with IVC extension:** 10% invade IVC, 40% reach RA. Surgical resection with 75% 5-year survival.
*   **Multidisciplinary approach:** Essential for sarcomas; diagnostic algorithm (Fig. 58-37).

## Chapter 59: Immunobiology of Heart and Lung Transplantation
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC/HLA)
*   **Structure:** Class I (HLA-A, B, C) on all nucleated cells; Class II (HLA-DR, DQ, DP) on APCs. HLA genes on chromosome 6; >5000 alleles.
*   **Function:** Present peptide antigens to T cells. Direct allorecognition: recipient T cells recognize donor HLA. Indirect: recipient T cells recognize donor peptides on self-APCs.
*   **Hyperacute Rejection:** Caused by preformed anti-HLA or ABO antibodies; prevented by crossmatching.

### T-Cell Activation (Three-Signal Model)
*   **Signal 1 (TCR):** Antigen presentation via MHC. Direct pathway (donor APCs) activates CD8<sup>+</sup> T cells; indirect pathway (recipient APCs) activates CD4<sup>+</sup> T cells.
*   **Signal 2 (Co-stimulation):** CD28-B7 (activation), CTLA-4-B7 (inhibition), CD40-CD154 (licensing). Co-stimulation blockade (belatacept) limits T-cell response.
*   **Signal 3 (Cytokines):** IL-2 drives proliferation; targeted by calcineurin inhibitors and basiliximab.

### Rejection Mechanisms
*   **Cell-Mediated Rejection (CMR):** CD8<sup>+</sup> CTLs kill target cells via perforin/granzyme. CD4<sup>+</sup> Th1 cells produce IFNγ, driving inflammation.
*   **Antibody-Mediated Rejection (AMR):** DSA (preformed or de novo) activates complement (C4d, C3d deposition) and ADCC. Incidence 10–15% of heart recipients. Treatment: plasmapheresis, IVIG, rituximab, bortezomib.
*   **Chronic Rejection:** Coronary allograft vasculopathy (CAV) in heart; chronic lung allograft dysfunction (CLAD)/obliterative bronchiolitis in lung.

### HLA Antibody Analysis
*   **Solid-Phase Assays (SPA):** Single-antigen beads detect DSA with MFI >1000 positive; >4000 predictive of positive flow crossmatch.
*   **Virtual Crossmatch (VXM):** Compares donor HLA genotype to recipient antibodies; 80% positive predictive value.
*   **Non-HLA Antibodies:** Anti-MICA, anti-vimentin, anti-cardiac myosin; associated with chronic rejection.

### Immunosuppressive Agents
| Class | Drugs | Mechanism |
|-------|-------|-----------|
| Glucocorticoids | Prednisone | NF-κB inhibition, anti-inflammatory |
| Purine analogues | Azathioprine, MMF | Inhibit purine synthesis, lymphocyte proliferation |
| Calcineurin inhibitors | Cyclosporine, Tacrolimus | Block NFAT, inhibit IL-2 production |
| mTOR inhibitors | Sirolimus, Everolimus | Block proliferation signals; anti-CAV effects |
| Anti-IL-2R | Basiliximab | Block IL-2 signaling |
| Anti-CD20 | Rituximab | Deplete B cells |
| Anti-CD52 | Alemtuzumab | Deplete T/B cells, monocytes |
| CTLA4-Ig | Belatacept | Block CD28-B7 co-stimulation |
| ATG | Thymoglobulin | Polyclonal T-cell depletion |

### Immune Monitoring
*   **Allomap:** Gene expression profiling; may reduce need for surveillance biopsies in low-risk patients.
*   **ImmuKnow:** Measures ATP in activated T cells; <200 ng/mL associated with infection risk.

## Chapter 60: Heart Transplantation
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Historical Milestones
*   **1905:** Alexis Carrel and Charles Guthrie performed the first heterotopic canine heart transplant. Frank Mann (Mayo Clinic, 1930s) proposed the concept of allograft rejection as leukocytic infiltration.
*   **1960:** Norman Shumway and Richard Lower at Stanford established orthotopic heart transplantation using hypothermia, CPB, and atrial cuff anastomosis in a canine model.
*   **1967:** Christiaan Barnard performed the first human-to-human heart transplant. Poor results led to a moratorium; Shumway's group persisted.
*   **1973:** Philip Caves introduced transvenous endomyocardial biopsy — the first reliable tool for rejection monitoring.
*   **1981:** Cyclosporine introduction marked the modern era of successful cardiac transplantation.

### Recipient Selection
*   **Indications:** Systolic HF (EF <35%), intractable angina/arrhythmia, hypertrophic or restrictive cardiomyopathy, selected congenital heart disease. ~90% of cases are ischemic HF or nonischemic cardiomyopathy.
*   **Prognosis threshold:** 1-year survival without transplant <50%. Strongest predictors: EF <20%, $\dot{V}O_{2,max}$ <14 mL/kg/min.
*   **Other poor prognostic indicators:** PCWP >25 mm Hg, norepinephrine >600 pg/mL, Na<sup>+</sup> <130 mEq/dL, NT-proBNP >5000 pg/mL.
*   **Absolute contraindications:** Fixed PVR >5–6 Wood units, TPG >15 mm Hg (right heart will fail against fixed PH); irreversible renal/hepatic dysfunction; active malignancy (most centers require ≥5 years disease-free).

### Pulmonary Hypertension Assessment
*   **Formulas:** $PVR = \frac{MPAP - PCWP}{CO}$; $TPG = PAP - PCWP$.
*   **Reversibility testing:** Sodium nitroprusside starting at 0.5 μg/kg/min; target PVR reduction to ≤2.5 Wood units or ≥50% decrease. Alternatives: adenosine, PGE<sub>1</sub>, milrinone, inhaled NO, aerosolized iloprost. LVAD support may durably reduce PVR.

### Recipient Prioritization (UNOS Status)
*   **Status 1A:** Requires MCS (TAH, VAD ≤30 days, IABP, ECMO), mechanical ventilation, high-dose IV inotropes with hemodynamic monitoring, or life expectancy <7 days.
*   **Status 1B:** VAD >30 days or continuous IV inotropes.
*   **Status 2:** All other candidates.

### Donor Selection and Management
*   **Standard donor criteria:** Age <50–60, no prolonged arrest/hypotension, no preexisting cardiac disease, hemodynamically stable on low-dose dopamine (<20 μg/kg/min).
*   **Brain death physiology:** "Autonomic and cytokine storm" → subendocardial ischemia, myocardial depression, vasodilatation. Management targets: MAP ≥60 mm Hg, CVP 4–12 mm Hg, PCWP 8–12 mm Hg, CI >2.4 L/min/m².
*   **Hormonal resuscitation package:** T3 (4 μg bolus + 3 μg/h infusion), arginine vasopressin (1 unit bolus + 0.5–4 units/h), methylprednisolone 15 mg/kg, insulin titrated to glucose 120–180 mg/dL.
*   **Preservation:** Single cardioplegia flush + static hypothermic storage at 4–10°C; safe ischemic time 4–6 hours. Organ Care System (OCS) can extend preservation in warm beating state.
*   **Donor-recipient matching:** ABO compatibility mandatory; weight within 30% of recipient; PRA >10% → prospective T-cell cross-match required.

### Operative Techniques
*   **Standard biatrial technique (Shumway-Lower):** LA cuff → RA cuff → PA → aorta anastomoses.
*   **Bicaval technique (preferred at most centers):** Individual IVC and SVC end-to-end anastomoses; associated with fewer arrhythmias, less TR, lower pacemaker need, shorter hospital stay.
*   **Reperfusion:** Warm substrate-enhanced (aspartate/glutamate) reperfusion before cross-clamp removal. Half of patients require defibrillation.

### Postoperative Management
*   **Denervated heart physiology:** Resting HR 90–110 bpm (intrinsic SA node rate); no response to atropine, carotid massage, Valsalva; chronotropic response to exercise delayed until circulating catecholamines act. Orthostatic hypotension common.
*   **Hemodynamic support:** Epinephrine, norepinephrine, dobutamine, milrinone infusions for 5–7 days. Vasoplegia → vasopressin/norepinephrine. RV dysfunction → inhaled NO.
*   **Early allograft failure:** Accounts for up to 20% of perioperative deaths. Causes: donor instability, PH, preservation injury, acute rejection. Support: IABP, VAD, ECMO.
*   **Arrhythmias:** Bradycardia in up to 50% (amiodarone use, ischemia, biatrial anastomosis are risk factors); theophylline reduces pacemaker need. SVT in 5–30%.
*   **Systemic hypertension:** Afflicts 50–90% of recipients; cyclosporine-related; treat with nitroprusside/nitroglycerin acutely, then oral agents.

### Rejection
*   **Acute cellular rejection (ACR):** Mediated by T-lymphocytes/macrophages/cytokines. ~85% reversed with corticosteroids alone. Grade 0 R (none) → Grade 1 R (mild) → Grade 2 R (moderate) → Grade 3 R (severe, diffuse infiltrate ± myocyte damage ± edema ± vasculitis).
*   **Hyperacute rejection:** Preformed donor-specific antibodies; catastrophic onset within minutes to hours; rare with ABO/PRA screening.
*   **Antibody-mediated rejection (AMR):** Humoral limb; more hemodynamic instability; diagnosed by endothelial swelling on LM + immunofluorescence. Graded pAMR 0–3 (2011 ISHLT scheme). Treatment: plasmapheresis, high-dose steroids, heparin, IgG, cyclophosphamide.
*   **Gold standard diagnosis:** Right ventricular endomyocardial biopsy (minimum 4 adequate fragments). AlloMap gene expression profiling now FDA-cleared for non-invasive rejection surveillance.
*   **Treatment of ACR:** IV methylprednisolone 1000 mg/day × 3 days (severe/early); oral prednisone taper (other episodes). Refractory: tacrolimus switch, OKT3, ECMO.
*   **Biopsy schedule:** Every 7–10 days early; taper to every 3–6 months after first year.

### Infectious Complications
*   **Highest risk:** First 3 months post-transplant and after increased immunosuppression.
*   **Bacteria:** Gram-negative bacilli (E. coli → UTI; Pseudomonas → pneumonia); Staphylococcus spp.
*   **CMV:** Most important single infectious cause of morbidity/mortality; linked to ACR, CAV progression, PTLD. Treatment: IV ganciclovir 5 mg/kg BID × 2–3 weeks. Prophylaxis: valganciclovir (Valcyte).
*   **Fungi:** Candida (mucocutaneous → fluconazole); Aspergillus (highest attributable mortality, 5–10% pneumonia in first 3 months; CNS dissemination near-uniformly fatal → voriconazole/amphotericin B).
*   **Protozoa:** Pneumocystis carinii 1–10% (prophylaxis: TMP-SMX); Toxoplasma (reactivation from seropositive donor heart → pyrimethamine/sulfadiazine).

### Chronic Complications
*   **Cardiac Allograft Vasculopathy (CAV):** Leading cause of death after first post-transplant year. Detectable angiographically in 40–50% by 5 years. Concentric intimal proliferation (vs eccentric in atherosclerosis), diffuse involvement, no calcification. Clinically silent (denervated heart). Surveillance: annual coronary angiography; IVUS is most sensitive. Prevention: statins, ACE inhibitors, CCBs, everolimus/sirolimus. Definitive treatment: retransplantation.
*   **Renal dysfunction:** Calcineurin inhibitor nephrotoxicity (cyclosporine/tacrolimus); significant improvement in recent cohorts. C2 monitoring of cyclosporine microemulsion (Neoral) improves dosing precision.
*   **Hypertension:** 50–90% of recipients; cyclosporine-mediated vasoconstriction + fluid retention. Tacrolimus associated with lower incidence. No single agent uniformly effective.
*   **Malignancy:** 4–18% incidence (10–100× general population). Lymphoproliferative disorders (EBV-driven) and skin cancer most common. Statins may be protective.
*   **Other:** Hyperlipidemia (majority), osteoporosis, obesity/cachexia, cholelithiasis.

### Cardiac Retransplantation
*   **Indications:** Early graft failure, CAV, refractory acute rejection. Accounts for <3% of cardiac transplants.
*   **Outcomes:** 1-/2-/3-year survival 65/59/55% (1987–1998 ISHLT data). Intertransplant interval <6 months → dismal 50% 1-year survival. Interval >2 years → approaches primary transplant outcomes.

### Results
*   **Operative (30-day) mortality:** 5–10%. **1-year survival:** up to 86%. Survival then declines ~3.5%/year.
*   **Early causes of death (<30 days):** Primary graft failure, multisystem organ failure, infection.
*   **Late causes (>1 year):** CAV and malignancy.
*   **Favorable predictors of long-term survival:** Recipient age <55 years, shorter ischemic time, younger donor age, annual center volume >9 (centers >40/year: 30-day mortality <5%).

## Chapter 61: Lung Transplantation and Heart-lung Transplantation
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### History and Epidemiology
*   **First human lung transplant:** 1963 (Hardy, University of Mississippi) — 18-day survival. First successful series: 1986 (Toronto Lung Transplant Group).
*   **Sequential bilateral lung transplant (BLT):** Introduced by Pasque 1990; living lobar transplant by Starnes 1992.
*   **Volume:** >3800 procedures/year reported to ISHLT (2013); 153 centers; bilateral lung transplant volume growing while SLT is stable.
*   **First successful heart-lung transplant in humans:** March 9, 1982 (Reitz, Stanford) — 45-year-old woman with end-stage primary pulmonary hypertension.

### Lung Allocation Score (LAS)
*   **Pre-2005:** Allocation by waiting time only → increasing waitlist with rising mortality.
*   **Post-2005 LAS:** Prioritizes net transplant benefit = posttransplant survival measure minus waitlist urgency measure. Scale 0–100. Reduced average wait time from 432 to 262 days; decreased waitlist mortality.
*   **LAS consequences:** Sicker, older patients with more comorbidities prioritized; more IPF, less COPD; more patients bridged with ECMO pretransplant; post-LAS era shows DLT associated with improved outcomes in most all-comers.

### Indications for Lung Transplantation
*   **COPD/Emphysema** (36% of transplants): BODE index 7–10; consider LVRS alternative if apical emphysema and FEV₁/DLco ≥20%.
*   **IPF** (20%): Refer as soon as diagnosed; DLco <39%, ≥10% FVC decline in 6 months, SpO₂ <88% on 6MWT, or honeycombing on CT.
*   **Cystic Fibrosis** (16%): Bilateral sepsis → mandatory bilateral transplant; FEV₁ <30% as trigger for referral; requires pre-listing ENT evaluation and sinus management.
*   **IPAH** (3.3%): NYHA III/IV despite ≥3 months therapy; cardiac index <2 L/min/m², RA pressure >15 mm Hg.
*   **Sarcoidosis** (2.6%): NYHA III/IV with hypoxemia, pulmonary hypertension, or RA pressure >15 mm Hg.
*   **Retransplantation** (<2%): Survival poor overall; ambulatory, non-ventilated patients retransplanted after 1991 have outcomes comparable to first-time transplants.

### Recipient Selection and Contraindications
*   **General:** <50% 2–3-year predicted survival; disabling symptoms (dyspnea, cyanosis, syncope, hemoptysis); capacity for full rehabilitation.
*   **LAS testing:** Two-phase evaluation (Phase I screening, Phase II listing); follow-up every 3–6 months.
*   **Absolute contraindications:** Age ≥65 years, active malignancy, HIV, HBsAg+ or HCV with biopsy-proven liver disease, pan-resistant respiratory flora, active smoking.
*   **Size matching:** Donor pTLC within 75–125% of recipient pTLC (ISHLT guidelines); oversized allografts associated with improved 1-year survival; donor lung dimensions ≤4 cm over recipient by CT.
*   **HLA:** Not preoperatively matched (ischemic time constraints) but HLA-B and HLA-DR matching correlates with improved long-term graft function and reduced OB. PRA >25% requires prospective crossmatch.

### Donor Selection and Organ Procurement
*   **Standard criteria:** Age <50 (lung), <40 (heart-lung); smoking <20 pack-years; PaO₂ >140 mm Hg on FiO₂ 40% or >300 mm Hg on FiO₂ 100%; clear chest x-ray; absent purulent secretions on bronchoscopy.
*   **Liberalized criteria trend:** Age 50–64 acceptable; modified smoking history if no COPD; positive Gram stain (no fungus) does not predict posttransplant pneumonia.
*   **DCD lungs:** Non-heart-beating donors accepted since Steen et al. 2001 report; exposed to warm ischemic time.
*   **EVLP (Ex Vivo Lung Perfusion):** Allows assessment and rehabilitation of rejected donor organs; up to 40% of rejected lungs have only minimal deficiencies — significant pool expansion potential.
*   **Preservation:** Hypothermia (<10°C) primary method; Perfadex (low-potassium dextran, extracellular solution) preferred over Euro-Collins; PGE₁ infusion (vasodilator) before flush; methylprednisolone to donor for lymphocyte inactivation; inflated organ + 100% O₂ for transport.

### Recipient Operation
*   **SLT:** Posterolateral thoracotomy; replace poorer functioning lung (by V/Q scan); bronchial → pulmonary venous → pulmonary arterial anastomosis sequence; bronchial anastomosis with 4-0 polypropylene.
*   **BLT:** Sequential SLT via median sternotomy (or clamshell); preferred on CPB for improved exposure, shorter ischemic time, controlled reperfusion, and contamination prevention in septic disease.
*   **Heart-lung transplant recipient:** Median sternotomy; bilateral pneumonectomy on CPB (28–30°C); tracheal anastomosis (3-0 polypropylene); bicaval anastomosis; aortic anastomosis; isoproterenol on reperfusion (0.005–0.01 μg/kg/min).

### Postoperative Management
*   **Graft physiology:** Lung denervation → diminished cough reflex, impaired mucociliary clearance → aggressive pulmonary toilet required.
*   **Ventilatory goals (ICU):** PaO₂ >75 mm Hg on FiO₂ 40%; PaCO₂ 30–40 mm Hg; pH 7.35–7.45; peak airway pressure <40 cm H₂O; extubation within 24 hours.
*   **Reimplantation response:** Diffuse interstitial infiltrate from ischemia-reperfusion or inadequate preservation; managed with fluid restriction and loop diuretics.
*   **Immunosuppression induction:** RATG 1.5 mg/kg (POD 1, 2, 3, 5, 7) or daclizumab 1 mg/kg; highly sensitized → intraoperative plasmapheresis + IVIG; methylprednisolone 500 mg IV intraoperatively.
*   **Maintenance:** Prednisone + Prograf (target 12–15 ng/mL) + CellCept; Rituximab (375 mg/m²) at POD 7 and 14.
*   **Surveillance:** Bronchoscopy with transbronchial biopsy and BAL at 2, 4–6, 12 weeks, 6 months, then annually.

### Postoperative Complications
*   **Hyperacute rejection:** Preformed antibodies → thrombosis of graft vessels; near-universally fatal; prevented by ABO matching and prospective crossmatch for PRA >25%.
*   **Primary graft failure:** 10–15% incidence; linked to ischemia-reperfusion; ECMO and inhaled NO for treatment; mortality >60%.
*   **Acute rejection:** 36% rate in first year; diagnosed by transbronchial biopsy (gold standard); histology = lymphocytic perivascular infiltrates; treated with steroid pulse (methylprednisolone 500–1000 mg/day × 3 days) for moderate/severe, oral prednisone taper for mild.
*   **Chronic rejection / OB / BOS:** 50% by year 5; BOS classified by FEV₁ decline (grade 0–3); most sensitive early markers: FEF₂₅₋₇₅ and FEF₅₀/FVC. Risk factors: acute rejection episodes, CMV, GERD, HLA mismatch, reduced immunosuppression. Management: augment immunosuppression; retransplantation for terminal OB.
*   **Airway complications:** Up to 27% (stenosis, necrosis, dehiscence); treated by dilatation/stenting or reoperation.
*   **Infection:** Leading cause of mortality in first year; bacterial (Gram-negatives) dominate early; CMV at 1–3 months (prophylaxis: ganciclovir ± hyperimmune globulin); fungal infections 10 days–2 months (prophylaxis: inhaled amphotericin B); *Pneumocystis* (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole lifelong).
*   **PTLD:** 6% incidence in lung recipients; 350× greater than general population; EBV-associated; treat with reduced immunosuppression + acyclovir/ganciclovir.

### Long-Term Outcomes
*   **Lung transplant survival (ISHLT 1994–2012):** 88% at 3 months, 80% at 1 year, 65% at 3 years, 53% at 5 years, 32% at 10 years. Median survival: BLT 7.1 years (conditional 9.7 years); SLT 4.6 years (conditional 6.5 years). CF best prognosis (median 8.3 years); IPF worst (4.7 years).
*   **Heart-lung transplant survival (ISHLT 1982–2012):** Median 3.3 years; conditional median 10.0 years; 72% at 3 months, 64% at 1 year.
*   **Cardiac complications of heart-lung transplant:** Sinus node dysfunction in 10–20%; isoproterenol/pacing for HR 90–110 bpm. Graft CAD: diffuse concentric intimal proliferation; 89% of heart-lung recipients free at 5 years vs 73% of cardiac transplant recipients alone.

## Chapter 62: Long-term Mechanical Circulatory Support and the Total Artificial Heart
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Clinical Burden of Heart Failure
*   **Prevalence:** Approximately 5.1 million Americans live with HF; lifetime risk for Americans over 40 is ~20%. Annual US healthcare costs exceed $30 billion.
*   **Treatment Tiers:** (1) Medical therapy (antihypertensives, diuretics, inotropes); (2) Surgical intervention (revascularization, valve repair, ventricular restoration); (3) Cardiac support/replacement (MCS, transplantation).
*   **MCS Role:** For patients with end-stage HF refractory to guideline-directed therapy, MCS offers documented survival benefit over medical therapy alone.

### History of MCS Development
*   **1953:** First successful CPB (Gibbon); slow weaning from CPB demonstrated the concept of temporary cardiac assistance.
*   **1963/1966:** DeBakey performs first LVAD implantations; 1966 patient survived 10 days of support and returned to work.
*   **1969:** Cooley implants the world's first TAH (Liotta device) as BTT; 64 hours of support.
*   **1970s–1980s:** HeartMate Implantable Pneumatic pump → first FDA-approved BTT device. Development of HeartMate XVE (first outpatient-managed pump, 1991). Hemopump (1988) demonstrated feasibility of high-speed continuous-flow (CF) support.
*   **Jarvik 2000:** First CF LVAD with blood-washed bearings placed directly in the left ventricle.
*   **HeartMate II:** Became the most widely used LVAD worldwide; FDA-approved for BTT (2007) and DT (2009).
*   **REMATCH Trial (2001):** Landmark study showing ~50% reduction in mortality with LVAD vs optimal medical therapy—first statistical proof of MCS survival benefit.
*   **HeartWare HVAD:** Magnetically levitated centrifugal-flow design; intrapericardial placement; BTT approval after ADVANCE trial (2010).

### Pulsatile vs. Continuous-Flow Technology
*   **Pulsatile devices:** Mimic native physiology but suffer from mechanical wear, large size, and limited durability (HeartMate XVE failure rate high between years 1–2).
*   **Continuous-flow (CF) devices:** Smaller, more durable, fewer moving parts. HeartMate II DT trial definitively showed CF superiority over pulsatile XVE in adverse events, survival, and QoL.
*   **Physiologic consequences of CF:** Association with GI bleeding (reduced pulsatility → AVM formation); aortic wall smooth muscle degeneration; carotid bulb and aortic valve stasis/thrombus; acquired von Willebrand syndrome.

### Current MCS Device Options
*   **ECMO:** VV (respiratory failure) or VA (full cardiopulmonary support); short-term days. Central or peripheral cannulation.
*   **Paracorporeal/Percutaneous:** CentriMag (up to 10 L/min, magnetically levitated, days–weeks); TandemHeart (left atriofemoral bypass, up to 4 L/min); Impella (microaxial, 2.5–5 L/min; Impella RP for RV support—first FDA-approved percutaneous RV device).
*   **Implantable CF LVADs:** HeartMate II (axial, 6000–15,000 rpm, >10 L/min) and HeartWare HVAD (centrifugal, 1800–4000 rpm, 145 g; intrapericardial).
*   **TAH:** SynCardia (only FDA-approved for BTT biventricular failure; stroke volume 70 mL; CO up to 15 L/min; Freedom Driver portable since 2010).

### Goals of Therapy
*   **BTT (Bridge to Transplantation):** Most common (~65% of MCS implants are transplant candidates); standard for transplant-eligible patients.
*   **DT (Destination Therapy):** For transplant-ineligible patients; increased from 14.7 to 41.6% of implants over 7 years; CF support >9 years reported.
*   **Bridge to Decision:** Short-term MCS for hemodynamically unstable patients pending stabilization and reassessment of long-term candidacy.
*   **Myocardial Reconditioning ("Bridge to Recovery"):** Histologic and clinical recovery in select patients; aggressive weaning strategies are appropriate for all device patients.
*   **Fluidity:** ~26% of BTT patients no longer listed at 1 year; ~15% of DT patients deemed transplant-eligible by 12 months. Frequent reassessment is mandatory.

### Indications for Device Support
*   **Hemodynamic thresholds (not absolute):** BP <80 mm Hg, MAP <65 mm Hg, cardiac index <2 L/min/m², PCWP >20 mm Hg.
*   **ACCF/AHA 2013:** Durable MCS supported for Stage D HF with reduced LVEF for survival benefit and BTT.
*   **CMS DT criteria:** NYHA IV, ineligible for transplant; LVEF <25%; peak VO₂ <14 mL/kg/min; failure of 45-day optimal medical therapy (or IABP/inotrope dependence).
*   **INTERMACS profiles:** 7 clinical profiles (1="Crash and burn," 7=mild limitation); >60% of pre-2011 implants were INTERMACS 1 or 2; trend toward operating at level 3 (improved outcomes with less acute patients).

### Preoperative Evaluation
*   **Risk scores:** DTRS (9-variable, derived from pulsatile era—modest CF utility); MELD/MELD-XI (creatinine, bilirubin, INR—correlates with survival); HMRS (5-variable CF-specific: age, albumin, creatinine, INR, center volume; predicts 90-day mortality—low risk 96% survival, high risk 71%).
*   **Concomitant procedures:** Aortic insufficiency ≥moderate → intervene; mechanical prosthesis → surgical LV outflow exclusion; tricuspid regurgitation ≥moderate → repair/replace; MR → usually resolves with unloading; PFO/ASD → close at implant.
*   **End-organ function:** Preoperative dialysis generally precludes MCS; deranged liver function (bilirubin, aminotransferases, INR) warrants evaluation; preoperative ventilation → increased risk.
*   **RV function predictors of post-LVAD RHF:** Preoperative ventilator support, elevated CVP and CVP/PCWP ratio, decreased RV stroke work index, elevated WBC and BUN, reduced free-wall strain, TR severity, RV EF.

### Surgical Technique (HeartMate II / HVAD)
*   Median sternotomy with 6-cm subxiphoid extension; bilateral pleural entry.
*   Inlet placement along the **diaphragmatic surface** of the LV (parallel to interventricular septum, lateral to posterior descending artery) — institutional preference over apical positioning.
*   HeartMate II: 12 horizontal mattress sutures through pledgeted ring; transdiaphragmatic pump positioning.
*   HVAD: Intrapericardial placement; no abdominal access required.
*   Outflow graft anastomosed end-to-side to the ascending aorta with 5-0 polypropylene; driveline exits 2 fingerbreadths below right subcostal margin; velour-silicone interface preserved beneath skin to reduce infection risk.
*   Pump initiated at minimum speed (HeartMate II 6000 rpm; HVAD 1800 rpm); titrated under TEE guidance; protamine administered; outflow graft wrapped in ringed Gore-Tex.

### Postoperative Management
*   **Hemodynamics:** Target MAP 60–80 mm Hg via vasoactive medications (not pump speed); Doppler measurements gold standard (cuff unreliable with CF). CVP control critical.
*   **Device monitoring:** Pump speed (rpm) and power (watts) directly measured; flow estimated. PI (HeartMate II–specific) reflects LV filling; HVAD logs 30-day retrievable data. "Speed-change echo" for optimization.
*   **RHF management:** Signs: rising CVP, low SvO₂, hemodynamic instability. Criteria (Potapov): MAP <55, CVP >16, SvO₂ <55%, flow index <2 L/min/m². Treatment: inotropes (milrinone, dobutamine, epi), diuresis, sinus rhythm, inhaled NO; mechanical RV support if refractory.
*   **Anticoagulation:** Not standardized. ISHLT: heparin POD1, warfarin + aspirin by POD3 (INR 2–3). Institutional variation common. Hemorrhagic complications far exceed thrombotic events in registry data.
*   **BiVAD management:** Right pump speed titrated to prevent pulmonary edema; left speed titrated to physiologic output. HVAD preferred for biventricular use due to intrapericardial profile.

### Postoperative Outcomes
*   **Survival milestones:** REMATCH: 52% 1-year survival (vs 25% medical therapy). HeartMate II BTT: 68% (73% in continued access). HVAD ADVANCE: 86% at 1 year. INTERMACS: 95% at 1 month, 80% at 12 months, 47% at 48 months (n=9372).
*   **Mortality risk factors (INTERMACS):** INTERMACS levels 1–2, renal dysfunction, age >70, RV dysfunction, surgical complexity.
*   **LVAD therapy ≈ transplantation** in 1- and 2-year mortality; pre-transplant MCS does not adversely affect post-transplant outcomes.

### Adverse Events and Device Complications
*   **Bleeding (perioperative):** Most common INTERMACS adverse event. 50–80% of HeartMate II patients required blood product transfusion; 30% reoperated for bleeding. HVAD associated with fewer blood products than HeartMate II in single-center analyses.
*   **RHF:** Incidence 15–30% post-LVAD; 8–10% require mechanical RV support.
*   **Infection:** Second most common INTERMACS event. Driveline infections: 9.8% overall; 19% by 1 year; 25% by 2 years. Infection increases 1-year mortality up to 6-fold.
*   **Renal failure:** 14–30% incidence; 10–20% require renal replacement therapy; ~50% recover independent function within 1 month.
*   **Stroke:** 11% at 1 year, 17% at 2 years. Hemorrhagic ≈ ischemic in frequency. BP control reduces neurologic events. Stroke → 25% 30-day and ~50% 1-year mortality.
*   **GI Bleeding (nonsurgical):** 18–30% incidence; 70% GI source; mechanisms include anticoagulation, acquired von Willebrand syndrome, and Heyde-syndrome-like reduced pulse pressure → intestinal AVM.
*   **Pump Thrombosis:** Freedom from device malfunction 95% at 6 months, 93% at 1 year. Diagnosed by sfHg (>40 mg/dL) or LDH (>600 IU/L — more sensitive). Treatment: heparin, bivalirudin, thrombolytics (risk of ICH), or surgical exchange/transplantation.

### Total Artificial Heart
*   **SynCardia:** FDA/CMS-approved for biventricular BTT. Pneumatically driven; stroke volume 70 mL; CO up to 15 L/min. Requires BSA >1.8 m². >1100 implants; survival-to-transplant 72%; median support 554 days. Freedom Driver (13 lb) enables outpatient use.
*   **AbioCor:** Self-contained electrohydraulic TAH with transcutaneous energy transfer. Stroke volume 65 mL; CO up to 12 L/min. 14 patients enrolled; longest survival 512 days. FDA HDE 2006; no implants since feasibility study due to economic barriers.
*   **CF TAH (BiVACOR):** Single magnetically levitated rotor; no bearings or valves; impellers on both sides of rotor. Dynamic speed profiles allow pulsatility. >70 calf implants; 2 animals survived >90 days. Human trials anticipated.

## Chapter 63: Tissue Engineering for Cardiac Valve Surgery
</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Clinical Need and Rationale for Tissue Engineering
*   **Scope of Problem:** Approximately 60,000 cardiac surgical procedures per year in the United States address diseases of heart valves and large conduit arteries.
*   **Limitations of Current Devices:** Mechanical valves require lifelong anticoagulation; biologic valves (allograft and heterograft) undergo structural deterioration; neither type has growth potential, making repeated reoperation necessary in pediatric patients.
*   **TEHV Goals:** A tissue-engineered heart valve (TEHV) aims to combine durability, growth capacity, thromboresistance, and infection resistance by using living cells capable of ECM synthesis, remodeling, and self-repair.

### Normal Heart Valve Biology
*   **Mechanical Demands:** Valves open and close ~40 million times per year under hemodynamic forces of blood pressure and shear stress. Semilunar valves must allow forward systolic flow without gradient and prevent regurgitation against diastolic pressure loads.
*   **ECM Layered Architecture:** Semilunar valve cusps have a trilaminar ECM: the *fibrosa* (dense collagen, diastolic pressure resistance), the *spongiosa* (glycosaminoglycans, compressive load absorption), and the *ventricularis* (elastin, systolic stretch). This structure is anisotropic.
*   **Cell–ECM Interactions ("Tensegrity"):** Valve interstitial cells (VICs) reside within and reciprocally remodel the ECM; surface endothelium prevents thrombosis and transduces mechanical force to underlying VICs.
*   **Embryologic Development:** Valvulogenesis begins with endothelial-to-mesenchymal transformation (EMT) in cardiac jelly; valve stratification into a trilaminar structure occurs during late fetal and early postnatal life, driven by changing hemodynamic loading conditions.

### Engineered Valve Inputs: Cell Sources
*   **Differentiated Vascular Cells:** The first TEHV experiments used smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells from systemic vessels. Problems included tissue contraction, excessive ECM, senescence, and the morbidity of vessel harvest.
*   **Stem and Progenitor Cells:** Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) are attractive alternatives; they are less invasively harvested, have multilineage differentiation potential, and can be programmed for lineage specification.
*   **Cell Plasticity ("Geography Is Destiny"):** Cell phenotype is strongly influenced by the local microenvironment (mechanical, biochemical, and ECM signals). EPCs can transdifferentiate toward a mesenchymal phenotype in response to TGF-β1, a phenomenon related to embryologic EMT.

### Engineered Valve Inputs: Scaffold Strategies
*   **Bioresorbable Polymer Scaffolds:** Most widely used approach. Scaffold degrades (ideally replaced by cell-secreted ECM) via hydrolysis. Key materials: polyglycolic acid (PGA; degrades 2–4 weeks), poly-L-lactic acid (PLLA; 4–6 weeks), PGA/P4HB composites, PGA/PLLA composites, and elastomeric polyglycerol sebaceate.
*   **Limitations of Polymer Scaffolds:** Significantly stiffer than native leaflets; thick scaffolds create nutrient gradients limiting cell viability in deeper layers.
*   **Decellularized Tissue Scaffolds:** Preserve native three-dimensional ECM complexity; disadvantages include homograft shortage, xenograft immunogenicity, ECM density preventing cell penetration, and risk of thrombosis/calcification.
*   **Hydrogel-Based Scaffolds:** Collagen, fibrin, alginate, and others allow homogeneous cell distribution in thin layers; subject to tissue contraction.
*   **Three-Dimensional Fabrication:** Stereolithography and electrospinning are emerging approaches; anisotropic scaffolds (mimicking native leaflet mechanics) are an active area of development.

### Engineered Valve Inputs: Biochemical and Mechanical Signals
*   **Biochemical Signals:** Valvulogenesis depends on VEGF, bone morphogenetic proteins (BMP-2 and BMP-4), and hyaluronic acid as key regulators of EMT and ECM remodeling. Microarray technologies promise to identify additional critical gene regulatory events.
*   **Mechanical Signals (Bioreactor Conditioning):** Hemodynamic forces are epigenetic regulators of valve tissue development. Bioreactors apply pulsatile flow and cyclic strain to precondition engineered tissues; demonstrated to increase collagen production and allow pre-implantation functional evaluation. MSCs and porcine valve tissues subjected to cyclic flexure produce more collagen and are stiffer than static controls.
*   **Ross Procedure Evidence:** Transplantation of a pulmonary valve to the systemic (aortic) position demonstrates in vivo phenotypic adaptation of VICs under altered hemodynamic loading, including increased matrix metalloproteinase activity.

### In Vivo Experimental Outcomes
*   **Four Major TEHV Implantation Studies** (de novo approach) have been completed, primarily in the ovine pulmonary circulation.
*   **Initial Proof-of-Concept:** Single PGA-seeded leaflet implants persisted and produced ECM for up to 11 weeks.
*   **Trileaflet Valved Conduits (PGA-P4HB, autologous cells):** Acceptable hemodynamics up to 20 weeks; histologic layering with collagen, glycosaminoglycans, and elastin resembling native tissue; no thrombosis or stenosis; central regurgitation appeared at 16 weeks.
*   **MSC-Seeded PGA/PLLA Conduits:** Functioned without significant gradient change over 4 months; histologic layering developed; in vivo endothelialization confirmed (von Willebrand factor expression on surface cells). Worsening pulmonary regurgitation observed after 6 weeks in larger studies (n=19).
*   **Fibrin-Based Valves:** Gross appearance intact at 3 months; leaflet contraction led to regurgitation.
*   **Electrospun Anisotropic PCUU Leaflets (Unseeded):** Good early function and cell repopulation; leaflet excursion decreased over time.
*   **Key Finding:** No group has demonstrated in vivo leaflet growth; however, microscopic remodeling from homogeneous to layered structure consistently occurs, suggesting implantation of an immature construct may be acceptable.

### Future Directions
*   Optimal cell type, scaffold material, and in vitro preconditioning conditions for TEHV remain undefined.
*   Stem and progenitor cells offer growth potential through self-renewal and multilineage differentiation.
*   Advanced imaging (MRI, echocardiography) enables real-time monitoring of post-implantation remodeling.
*   Bioreactor preconditioning can reduce in vitro culture times and allow pre-implantation functional evaluation.
*   Ultimate goal: a clinically translatable valve replacement with lifelong durability and intrinsic growth capacity.
