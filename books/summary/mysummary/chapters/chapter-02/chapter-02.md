## Chapter 1: Synopsis of Adult Cardiac Surgical Disease</summary>

### Coronary Artery Disease
*   **SIHD Management:** Optimal medical therapy first; PCI for symptom relief; CABG improves survival in left main disease, three-vessel disease, and diabetes. SYNTAX score (0–22 low risk, 23–32 intermediate, >32 high) guides revascularization choice.
*   **ACS Management:** Non-STEMI: early invasive strategy for intermediate–high risk. STEMI: primary PCI with door-to-balloon <90 minutes. CABG for failed PCI, cardiogenic shock, mechanical complications.
*   **Preoperative:** Continue aspirin (81 mg); stop P2Y12 inhibitors 5 days (clopidogrel/ticagrelor) or 7 days (prasugrel) before surgery. Platelet aggregation testing guides timing. IV cangrelor as bridge.

### Valvular Heart Disease

**Aortic Stenosis**
*   Severe AS: AVA <1.0 cm², Vmax >4 m/s, mean gradient >40 mm Hg. Indications: symptomatic (D1), asymptomatic with LVEF <50% (C2), or very severe (Vmax ≥5 m/s).
*   TAVR approved for low-risk patients; superior hemodynamics, faster recovery. SAVR remains standard for young patients, bicuspid valves, concomitant surgery.

**Aortic Regurgitation**
*   Surgery: symptomatic (Stage D) or asymptomatic with LVEF <50% or LVESD >50 mm (Stage C2). Valve-sparing root replacement for selected patients.

**Mitral Stenosis**
*   Percutaneous mitral balloon valvuloplasty (PMBV) for favorable anatomy (echo score <8). Surgery for failed PMBV, left atrial thrombus, or concomitant procedures.

**Mitral Regurgitation**
*   Primary MR: repair preferred if feasible (95% likelihood). Surgery for symptomatic (Stage D) or asymptomatic with LVEF ≤60% or LVESD ≥40 mm (Stage C2).
*   Secondary MR: repair at time of CABG for severe MR; MitraClip for high-risk patients with LVEF 20–50%, LVESD <70 mm.

**Tricuspid Regurgitation**
*   Repair at time of left-sided surgery if annular dilatation (>40 mm) or moderate–severe TR. Ring annuloplasty preferred.

### Infective Endocarditis
*   Urgent surgery for heart failure, uncontrolled infection (abscess, persistent bacteremia), fungal/S. aureus, large vegetations (>10 mm) with emboli.

### Aortic Dissection
*   Type A: emergency surgery. Type B: medical therapy for uncomplicated; TEVAR for complicated (rupture, malperfusion, expansion).

### Thoracic Aortic Aneurysms
*   Ascending: surgery at >5.5 cm (or >5.0 cm with bicuspid valve, family history, rapid growth). Aortic height index (AHI >2.43 cm/m) refines risk.
*   Descending: surgery at >5.5 cm; TEVAR preferred.

### Atrial Fibrillation
*   Maze procedure at time of cardiac surgery for persistent AF. Left atrial appendage excision reduces stroke risk.

### Advanced Heart Failure
*   CABG for ischemic cardiomyopathy with viable myocardium. CRT for EF ≤35%, QRS >150 ms. ICD for EF ≤35%. LVAD or transplant for Stage D.

## Chapter 2: Diagnostic Techniques in Cardiac Surgery</summary>

### Chest Radiography
- **PA and lateral chest x-ray** is mandatory before cardiac surgery. It confirms the clinical diagnosis (LV enlargement in volume overload, LVH in AS/hypertension, enlarged cardiac silhouette in pericardial effusion, large left atrium in MS, wide mediastinum in aortic dissection/aneurysm) and identifies additional pathology (pulmonary disease, pleural effusions, prior surgical changes).
- **Surgical implications:** Ascending aortic calcification increases stroke risk and may necessitate alternative cannulation sites (femoral/axillary), avoidance of aortic cross-clamping, or transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Mitral annular calcification complicates valve repair/replacement. In reoperations, lateral film shows proximity of RV, aorta, or ITA grafts to the sternum, guiding cannulation strategy.

### Electrocardiography
- **Sinus tachycardia** indicates inadequate β-blockade, increasing risk of preoperative ischemia and postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF).
- **Sinus bradycardia** predicts need for temporary pacing; patients may not tolerate β-blockers or amiodarone.
- **Atrial fibrillation** with rapid ventricular response must be controlled; longstanding persistent AF requires postoperative anticoagulation; Maze procedure may be added.
- **Left bundle branch block (LBBB)** increases risk of asystole during Swan-Ganz insertion → defer until after sternotomy.
- **Right bundle branch block (RBBB) + first-degree AV block** increases risk of complete heart block after TAVR.
- **New conduction abnormalities in aortic endocarditis** suggest annular extension → urgent surgery.
- **Permanent pacemakers:** electrocautery may disrupt function; magnet must be available. ICDs must be inactivated preoperatively.

### Stress Testing and Myocardial Perfusion Imaging
- **Exercise ECG (ETT)** is most useful in patients with intermediate pretest probability of CAD. Findings predicting adverse prognosis at low workload (<6 METs): anginal symptoms, >2 mm ST depression in multiple leads persisting >5 min into recovery, ST elevation, hypotensive response, sustained VT.
- **Radionuclide MPI (SPECT/PET)** is more sensitive/specific than ETT, especially with baseline ECG abnormalities. Pharmacologic stress with regadenoson (adenosine A₂A agonist) is preferred when exercise is not possible. Rest/stress protocols assess viability and ischemia.
- **Stress MRI** has excellent resolution; dobutamine cine CMR has >80% sensitivity/specificity for significant coronary stenosis.
- **Stress echocardiography** identifies stress-induced regional wall motion abnormalities; comparable to nuclear imaging.
- **Viability studies** (SPECT with Tc-99m or thallium, FDG-PET, dobutamine stress echo, delayed enhancement MRI) distinguish hibernating myocardium from necrosis. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) >15% correlates with sudden death risk in HCM; MRI-defined diastolic wall thickness >5.5 mm predicts contractile recovery after CABG. STICH trial: viability associated with improved LV function but not survival.

### Stress Echocardiography in Valvular Heart Disease
- **Abnormal findings:** symptoms at low workload, exercise capacity <85% predicted, blood pressure fall or <20 mm Hg rise, RVSP >60 mm Hg, worsening regurgitation, EF fall, regional wall motion abnormalities, sustained VT.
- **Aortic stenosis:** Symptom-limited ESE in asymptomatic severe AS identifies 8× higher MACE risk. DSE distinguishes true from pseudo-severe AS in low-flow, low-gradient AS (stage D2). Projected AVA based on normal flow rate may help.
- **Aortic regurgitation:** ESE elicits symptoms, assesses contractile reserve; exercise-induced EF fall prompts early surgery.
- **Mitral stenosis:** Nearly 50% of asymptomatic moderate-severe MS develop symptoms with exercise. High-risk: mean gradient >18 mm Hg (DSE) or >15 mm Hg (exercise), RVSP >60 mm Hg.
- **Mitral regurgitation:** Primary MR: RVSP >60 mm Hg, exercise capacity <85% predicted, latent LV dysfunction, AF, abnormal heart rate recovery. Secondary MR: pulmonary edema, ischemic wall motion abnormalities, EROA increase >13 mm².

### Cardiac Catheterization
- **Gold standard** for most cardiac diagnoses, except acute type A dissection (surgical emergency) and aortic endocarditis with vegetations (embolization risk).
- **Right-heart catheterization:** Swan-Ganz for pressures, oxygen saturations (shunt detection), thermodilution cardiac output.
- **Left-heart catheterization:** LVEDP measurement, ventriculogram (EF calculation, MR detection), aortic gradient. Crossing stenotic aortic valve in severe AS may increase stroke risk.
- **Fluoroscopy** identifies ascending aortic calcification (noncontrast CT for confirmation), coronary calcification, mitral annular calcification, prosthetic valve rocking (endocarditis), or restricted leaflet motion (thrombosis/pannus).

### Coronary Angiography
- **Gold standard for CAD.** Determines dominance, location/extent/degree of stenoses. SYNTAX scores guide PCI vs. CABG decisions.
- **Limitations:** Diffuse disease underestimates stenosis severity. **IVUS** measures minimal luminal area (MLA): left main MLA <7 mm² (symptomatic) or <5.5–6 mm² (asymptomatic) is significant; proximal arteries MLA <4 mm² significant. **OCT** has 10× higher resolution for stent assessment.
- **Fractional flow reserve (FFR)** ≤0.8 indicates functionally significant lesion. **iFR** ≤0.89 correlates with FFR ≤0.8. FFR-guided revascularization reduces grafting, improves patency, reduces MI/mortality.
- **Indications:** Suspected CAD requiring intervention, age >40 for open-heart surgery, annual transplant follow-up.
- **Contrast-induced AKI prevention:** Hemodynamically guided hydration (POSEIDON protocol) based on LVEDP: pre-procedure 3.5 mL/kg/h ×4 h; intra-procedure 5/3/1.5 mL/kg/h based on LVEDP <13/13–18/>18 mm Hg; post-procedure 1.5 mL/kg/h ×1 h.

### Echocardiography
- **Transthoracic (TTE):** initial assessment of LV/RV size/function, valve function, gradients, regurgitation.
- **Transesophageal (TEE):** superior imaging for mitral valve pathology (prolapse, torn chordae, papillary muscle rupture, annular dilatation), endocarditis (vegetations, periannular abscesses), aortic dissection (intimal flap, AR, pericardial effusion), cardiac masses, pericardial effusion/tamponade.
- **3D imaging** critical for MitraClip planning and precise pathology localization.
- **Preoperative echo** (Table 2.3) and **postoperative echo** (Table 2.4) provide comprehensive information across all clinical scenarios.

### Noninvasive Coronary Angiography
- **Coronary CT angiography (CCTA):** 64-slice or higher, heart rate <70 bpm. Sensitivity/specificity ~80–90% for >50% stenosis. Limitations: irregular rhythm, severe calcification, stents, small vessels (<1.5 mm). FFR-CT derived from CCTA superior to standard CTA, SPECT, PET.
- **Cardiac MRI (CMR):** Avoids radiation, less interference from calcification. Indications: coronary anomalies (young patients), coronary aneurysms, graft stenosis. Stress CMR with dobutamine or vasodilators identifies ischemia. Newer pacemakers/ICDs are MRI-conditional; legacy devices safe at ≤1.5 tesla.

### CT and MR for Aortic and Cardiac Disease
- **CT scanning:** Primary for aortic disease. Indications: aortic dissection (type A/B), aortic size assessment (centerline analysis for true diameter), ascending aortic calcification (noncontrast), reoperative planning (RV/aorta/graft proximity to sternum), iliofemoral evaluation pre-TAVR (size, calcification, tortuosity), retroperitoneal hematoma, pericardial/pleural effusions, intracardiac masses.
- **CMR:** Most sensitive/specific for aortic dissection. **Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE)** patterns differentiate ischemic (subendocardial/transmural) from nonischemic cardiomyopathies (midwall, epicardial). **MRA** with gadolinium defines aortic arch aneurysms. **Cine CMR** differentiates blood from tissue for ventricular function, valvular pathology, masses. Distinguishes restrictive from constrictive pericarditis.

## Chapter 3: General Preoperative Considerations and Preparation of the Patient for Surgery</summary>

### Preoperative Evaluation Overview
- **Comprehensive assessment** of medical condition and comorbidities is essential before cardiac surgery. The **STS risk calculator** (www.sts.org) provides predicted operative mortality (PROM) and risks of major complications (re-exploration, stroke, prolonged ventilation, renal failure). **EuroSCORE II** is used in other countries.
- **Patient education** through information booklets and clinical care pathway discussions reduces anxiety and promotes early recovery.

### History: Key Comorbidities and Management
- **COPD:** Defined by FEV₁ (mild: 60–75% predicted; moderate: 50–59%; severe: <50%). Severe COPD increases risks of pneumonia, prolonged ventilation, AF, sternal infection, and mortality. **BNP** <100 pg/mL suggests pulmonary etiology; >500 pg/mL suggests heart failure. Smoking cessation ≥4 weeks preoperatively reduces pulmonary complications. **Amiodarone** toxicity may cause ARDS postoperatively.
- **Liver disease:** **Child-Turcotte-Pugh (CTP) Class A** (5–6 points) generally tolerates CPB; Class B/C have mortality 38–52%. **MELD score** ≥15 predicts 50% mortality. Off-pump surgery preferred in advanced liver disease.
- **Diabetes mellitus:** HbA1c >7% predicts worse outcomes (sternal infection, stroke, renal failure, mortality). **Bilateral ITAs** improve long-term results but increase sternal infection risk in obese diabetic women with skeletonized harvesting. Maintain blood glucose 120–180 mg/dL perioperatively.
- **Neurologic symptoms:** Carotid screening indicated for age >65, bruits, TIA/stroke, hypertension, PVD. **Asymptomatic carotid stenosis** >90% with acute coronary syndrome → combined CEA–CABG; stable angina → staged approach.
- **Conduit assessment:** Vein mapping, radial artery evaluation (ulnar dominance confirmed), avoidance of venipuncture in harvest arm.

### Physical Examination
- **Carotid bruits:** warrant noninvasive studies; bilateral disease (>75% bilaterally) carries 10–15% stroke risk with isolated CABG.
- **Differential arm BP** (>10 mm Hg) suggests subclavian stenosis → contraindication to pedicled ITA.
- **PVD:** aortoiliac disease may preclude femoral cannulation; CT assessment required for TAVR planning.
- **Varicose veins/DVT:** may compromise saphenous vein conduits; consider BITA or radial artery.

### Medication Adjustment Prior to Surgery

| Drug Class | Discontinuation Timing | Special Considerations |
|------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| **Aspirin** | Continue 81 mg for CABG; stop 3–5 days for non-CABG | Higher doses increase bleeding |
| **Clopidogrel** | 5 days | Irreversible; platelet function testing (PRU <30%) may allow earlier surgery |
| **Ticagrelor** | 5 days (72h may suffice) | Reversible; 80% recovery by 72h |
| **Prasugrel** | 7 days | Irreversible; not for age >75 or h/o TIA/stroke |
| **Warfarin** | 5 days | Bridge with LMWH if high risk (mechanical mitral valve, AF with CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥5, VTE within 3 months) |
| **NOACs** | 48 hours (longer if renal dysfunction) | Reversal agents: idarucizumab (dabigatran), andexanet alfa (apixaban, rivaroxaban) |
| **UFH** | 4 hours | Continue in critical CAD |
| **LMWH** | 24 hours | Only 60% neutralized by protamine |
| **IIb/IIIa inhibitors** | 4 hours (eptifibatide, tirofiban); 24h (abciximab) | Platelet transfusion effective for abciximab |
| **Omega-3 fatty acids** | Not routinely stopped per OPERA trial | No increased bleeding when antiplatelets/anticoagulants stopped |

### Laboratory Assessment
- **Anemia** (Hb <10 g/dL) increases mortality; restrictive transfusion (Hb <7.5 g/dL) comparable outcomes to liberal strategy. Consider preoperative erythropoietin for high-risk patients.
- **Renal dysfunction:** Stage III–IV CKD increases mortality; delay on-pump surgery ≥24h after catheterization if creatinine elevated.
- **Hyponatremia** and **hyperkalemia** require correction preoperatively.
- **BNP** >385 pg/mL predicts postoperative LV dysfunction and mortality.
- **ECG:** LBBB increases asystole risk during Swan-Ganz insertion; RBBB + first-degree AV block increases post-TAVR heart block risk.

### Preoperative Orders and Checklist
- **Blood setup:** Type and screen for minimally invasive CABG; 1–2 units based on weight/hematocrit; 3 units for reoperations/aortic surgery; 6 units for descending aortic surgery.
- **Antibiotics:** Cefazolin 1–2 g within 1 hour of incision (redose on pump). Vancomycin 20 mg/kg for penicillin allergy (infuse over ≥30 min).
- **Skin preparation:** Chlorhexidine shower night before and morning of surgery; clippers (not razor) for hair removal.

### Risk Assessment and Informed Consent
- **STS risk calculator** provides predicted mortality and morbidity. **EuroSCORE II** comparable accuracy.
- **Major predictors of mortality:** emergency surgery, renal dysfunction, reoperations, age >75–80, EF <30%, female gender, left main disease.
- **Postoperative complications and mortality (STS data):**
  - Multisystem failure: 74% mortality
  - Cardiac arrest: 64%
  - Renal failure (dialysis): 48%
  - Ventilated >5 days: 21%
  - Permanent stroke: 28%
  - Re-exploration for bleeding: 13%
  - Deep sternal infection: 11%

## Chapter 4: Cardiac Anesthesia</summary>

### Preoperative Visit and Medication Management
- **Preoperative evaluation** by anesthesiologist includes history (cardiac symptoms, comorbidities, prior anesthesia), examination (Mallampati score, thyromental distance, neck mobility), and medication review.
- **Medication adjustments:** Continue antianginal/antihypertensive medications except ACE inhibitors/ARBs (withhold morning of surgery to reduce vasoplegia). Withhold long-acting insulin night before and oral hypoglycemics morning of surgery. **Anticoagulants:** Warfarin stop 4 days preop; aspirin continue 81 mg for CABG; P2Y₁₂ inhibitors stop 5 days (clopidogrel/ticagrelor) or 7 days (prasugrel); NOACs stop 48 hours; LMWH stop 24 hours.

### Intraoperative Monitoring
- **Standard monitoring:** 5-lead ECG, radial arterial line, CVP or Swan-Ganz, pulse oximetry, end-tidal CO₂, cerebral oximetry, temperature, urine output.
- **Swan-Ganz catheter:** Provides CVP, PAP, PCWP, cardiac output. Caution in LBBB (risk of complete heart block). **Complications:** arrhythmias, PA perforation (management: PEEP, bronchial blocker, possible ECMO).
- **Cerebral oximetry (rSO₂):** Monitors cerebral oxygenation; >20% drop associated with adverse outcomes. Helps identify cerebral autoregulation limits.

### Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE)
- **Routine for most cardiac surgery** — detects ischemia, guides valve repair, assesses ventricular function, identifies air.
- **Key views:** Mid-esophageal (ME) 4-chamber, 2-chamber, commissural, LAX, SAX; transgastric (TG) SAX, 2-chamber, LAX; deep TG 5-chamber.
- **Specific uses:** Mitral valve pathology (scallop localization, SAM risk), aortic valve (gradients, annular size), off-pump surgery (RWM abnormalities), minimally invasive surgery (catheter positioning, deairing).

### Anesthetic Considerations by Procedure Type

| Procedure | Key Anesthetic Concerns |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **CABG** | Prevent tachycardia/hypertension; TEE for ischemia; off-pump requires continuous CO monitoring, α-agents, normothermia |
| **Aortic Stenosis** | Maintain preload, avoid vasodilation/tachycardia; treat hypotension with α-agents; preserve atrial kick |
| **Aortic Regurgitation** | Avoid bradycardia (increases regurgitation); patients vasodilated post-bypass |
| **Mitral Stenosis** | Slow heart rate (<80), avoid increased PVR, treat RV dysfunction with milrinone, anticipate coagulopathy |
| **Mitral Regurgitation** | Maintain preload, avoid hypertension; TEE essential for repair assessment; SAM risk markers (EDD <45 mm, posterior leaflet >15 mm) |
| **Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy** | Avoid hypovolemia/vasodilation (increase gradient); β-blockers; avoid inotropes |
| **Aortic Dissection** | Avoid hypertension; rapid-sequence induction; TEE for flap, AR, tamponade; DHCA for type A |
| **Thoracoabdominal Aneurysm** | CSF drainage; one-lung anesthesia; maintain proximal pressure >130 mm Hg; distal perfusion |
| **Tricuspid Disease** | Maintain elevated CVP for stenosis; faster HR for TR; anticipate coagulopathy from hepatic congestion |
| **Endocarditis** | TEE for abscess/vegetation; vasopressin for refractory hypotension; transvenous pacing if heart block |

### Induction and Maintenance of Anesthesia
- **Induction agents:** Propofol (1–2 mg/kg), etomidate (0.2–0.4 mg/kg), fentanyl (2.5–5 μg/kg), vecuronium (0.1 mg/kg).
- **Maintenance:** Inhalational agents (sevoflurane, desflurane) provide cardioprotection, allow early extubation. **Dexmedetomidine** (α₂-agonist) provides sedation, analgesia, sympatholysis; reduces delirium, shortens ventilation.
- **Muscle relaxants:** Vecuronium (renal-safe), rocuronium (rapid onset), cisatracurium.

### Prebypass Considerations
- **Antifibrinolytics:** ε-aminocaproic acid (Amicar) 5 g load + 1 g/h; tranexamic acid (TXA) 10–30 mg/kg load + infusion; reduce bleeding in on- and off-pump surgery.
- **Anticoagulation:** Heparin 3 mg/kg for CPB, maintain ACT >480 seconds. **Heparin resistance** (AT deficiency) treat with FFP or AT concentrate (Thrombate III). **HIT:** Use bivalirudin (1.25 mg/kg load, 2.5 mg/kg/h infusion) for CPB.
- **Steroids:** High-dose methylprednisolone (30 mg/kg) or dexamethasone (1 mg/kg) may reduce inflammatory response but associated with hyperglycemia and pulmonary dysfunction.

### Cardiopulmonary Bypass Management
- **Hypothermia:** Cool to 28–32°C for routine cases; DHCA (18–20°C) for arch surgery; avoid overwarming >37°C.
- **Blood pressure:** Target MAP ~65 mm Hg; higher (80 mm Hg) in diabetic/hypertensive patients. Use α-agents or vasodilators based on flow.
- **Glucose:** Maintain <180–200 mg/dL; avoid <100 mg/dL (increased stroke risk).
- **Renal protection:** Maintain MAP above autoregulatory threshold, avoid profound anemia (Hct <20%).

### Termination of Bypass
- **Weaning:** Gradual reduction of venous return, volume infusion. **TEE** for air, ventricular function, valve competence.
- **Inotropes:** Epinephrine (1–2 μg/min), dobutamine (5–10 μg/kg/min), milrinone (vasodilator + inotrope).
- **Vasoplegia:** Norepinephrine, phenylephrine, vasopressin (1 unit bolus), methylene blue (1.5–2 mg/kg) if refractory.
- **Protamine:** 0.5–1:1 ratio to heparin. **Reactions:** Type I (hypotension from rapid infusion), Type II (anaphylaxis), Type III (pulmonary vasoconstriction). Treatment: α-agents, calcium, heparin readministration, methylene blue.

### Off-Pump Coronary Surgery (OPCAB)
- **Monitoring:** Continuous CO, SvO₂, TEE for RWM abnormalities.
- **Heparin:** ACT >250 seconds.
- **Hemodynamics:** Trendelenburg position, α-agents, maintain BP 120–140 mm Hg; avoid tachycardia; have pacing available.
- **Temperature:** Maintain normothermia with warming devices.

### Minimally Invasive and Transcatheter Procedures
- **Minimally invasive CABG/mitral:** One-lung anesthesia, TEE for catheter positioning, cerebral oximetry for retrograde perfusion, femoral cannulation.
- **TAVR:** Sedation preferred (dexmedetomidine); monitor via femoral access; maintain BP >100 mm Hg pre-deployment; rapid ventricular pacing for balloon valves; TTE post-deployment for paravalvular leak.
- **MitraClip:** General anesthesia with TEE; transseptal puncture guided by bicaval and SAX views; clip positioned with commissural and LVOT views; assess residual MR and mean gradient (<5 mm Hg for second clip).

### Coagulopathy Management
- **Point-of-care testing:** Thromboelastography (TEG) or rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) guide targeted therapy.
- **Transfusion triggers:** Platelets for antiplatelet drugs; FFP for warfarin/hepatic dysfunction; cryoprecipitate for low fibrinogen; prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) for rapid reversal.

## Chapter 5: Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary>

### General Concepts
- **CPB** provides systemic perfusion of oxygenated blood when the heart and lungs are not functional. It involves normovolemic hemodilution and nonpulsatile flow.
- **Systemic inflammatory response** results from blood contact with the extracorporeal circuit, activating kallikrein, coagulation, and complement cascades. This causes thrombin generation, cytokine release, platelet/leukocyte activation, and capillary leak.
- **Mitigating strategies:** membrane oxygenators, biocompatible circuits, centrifugal pumps, restrictive transfusion, avoidance of cardiotomy suction, leukocyte filters, and steroids.

### CPB Circuit Components
- **Heparin-coated circuits** improve biocompatibility, reduce complement/platelet activation, but do not decrease thrombin generation.
- **Priming volume:** 1000–1500 mL of balanced electrolyte solution. **Miniaturized circuits** (500–800 mL) reduce hemodilution and inflammatory response.
- **Venous drainage:** gravity or vacuum-assisted (up to –60 mm Hg) into reservoir. Vacuum assist increases risk of air emboli.
- **Pumps:** Centrifugal pumps (afterload-sensitive) preferred over roller pumps (pressure-insensitive, can cause high pressures).
- **Suction lines:** cardiotomy suction returns shed blood but introduces proinflammatory mediators. **Cell-savers** wash blood, removing mediators but also coagulation factors.
- **Oxygenators:** integral heater-cooler; require maintenance to prevent *Mycoplasma chimaera* contamination.
- **Arterial line filter** (40 μm) removes microemboli.

### Anticoagulation and Cannulation
- **Heparin:** 3–4 mg/kg, target ACT >480 seconds (non-coated circuits) or >400 seconds (coated circuits).
- **Heparin resistance:** due to antithrombin (AT) deficiency. Treat with FFP or AT concentrate (Thrombate III).
- **HIT:** Use bivalirudin (1.25 mg/kg load, 2.5 mg/kg/h infusion) for CPB.
- **Antifibrinolytics:** ε-aminocaproic acid (10 g load) or tranexamic acid (10 mg/kg + infusion) reduce bleeding.
- **Arterial cannulation:** ascending aorta preferred. **Alternative sites:** femoral (risk of retrograde dissection, stroke), axillary (allows antegrade cerebral perfusion during DHCA).
- **Venous cannulation:** double/triple-stage cavoatrial cannula for most cases; bicaval cannulation for right heart surgery; femoral venous for minimally invasive procedures.

### Conduct of CPB (Table 5.2)

| Parameter | Target |
|-----------|--------|
| ACT | >480 sec (uncoated), >400 sec (coated) |
| Flow rate | 2–2.5 L/min/m² at 37°C; 1.7–2.0 L/min/m² at 30°C |
| MAP | 50–70 mm Hg |
| SvO₂ | >65% |
| Hematocrit | >18% (prefer >21% for renal protection) |
| Glucose | 100–180 mg/dL |

- **Cerebral oximetry (rSO₂):** monitors cerebral oxygenation; >20% drop from baseline or <40% warrants intervention (increase BP, flow, PCO₂, or HCT).
- **pH management:** α-stat (pH 7.40 at 37°C) preferred for DHCA; pH-stat (temperature-corrected) increases cerebral blood flow but may increase microemboli risk.

### Terminating Bypass
- **Rewarm** to ≥36°C; avoid cerebral hyperthermia (outlet temperature <37°C).
- **Deairing:** active root venting, Trendelenburg position, CO₂ flooding, TEE monitoring.
- **Protamine:** 0.5–1:1 ratio to heparin; heparin-protamine titration systems reduce protamine dose and bleeding.
- **Protamine reactions:** Type I (hypotension from rapid infusion), Type II (anaphylaxis), Type III (pulmonary vasoconstriction). Treatment: α-agents, calcium, heparin readministration, methylene blue.

### Potential Problems on CPB (Table 5.3)
- **Inadequate ACT:** AT deficiency → FFP or AT concentrate.
- **Inadequate venous drainage:** airlock, kinking, malposition → readjust cannula.
- **Distended heart:** poor drainage, AI, collateral flow → adjust lines or place vent.
- **Vasoplegia:** post-pump hypotension with normal CO → norepinephrine, vasopressin (0.01–0.1 units/min), methylene blue (1.5 mg/kg).
- **High arterial line pressure:** small cannula, malposition, dissection → check position, consider cannula exchange.
- **Air embolism:** deairing maneuvers; for massive air: stop bypass, vent aorta, Trendelenburg, retrograde SVC perfusion, consider DHCA.
- **Cold agglutinins:** avoid hypothermia; use warm cardioplegia or off-pump surgery.

### Modifications of Extracorporeal Circulation
- **Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA):** cool to 18°C for 45 min safe arrest. **Cerebral protection:** antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP) via axillary artery (6–10 mL/kg at 40–60 mm Hg) or retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP) via SVC (up to 500 mL/min at <20 mm Hg).
- **Left-heart bypass:** for descending aortic surgery; drains left atrium/inferior pulmonary vein, returns to femoral/distal aorta; minimal heparin (ACT >250 sec).
- **Perfusion-assisted CABG (PADCAB):** active perfusion of target vessel during off-pump anastomosis.

### Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO)
- **VA ECMO:** cardiac + respiratory support; femoral vein → oxygenator → femoral/axillary artery.
- **VV ECMO:** respiratory support only; femoral vein → oxygenator → central vein (or dual-lumen jugular cannula).
- **Indications:** refractory cardiogenic shock, postcardiotomy failure, cardiac arrest, ARDS.
- **Complications:** bleeding (maintain ACT >180 sec), renal failure (50%), LV distention (consider venting or Impella), Harlequin syndrome (differential oxygenation on VA ECMO).
- **Prognosis:** 30-day survival 50–70% for ARDS, 20–35% for postcardiotomy shock/cardiac arrest.

## Chapter 6: Myocardial Protection</summary>

### Principles of Myocardial Protection
- **Ischemic arrest** without protection → irreversible damage after 15–20 minutes due to anaerobic metabolism, ATP depletion, lactate accumulation, and intracellular calcium overload.
- **Cardioplegic arrest** induces diastolic arrest, preserves energy stores, minimizes calcium entry, allowing several hours of safe aortic cross-clamping.

### Techniques for Myocardial Protection (Table 6.1)

| Technique | Key Features |
|-----------|--------------|
| **Off-pump CABG** | Beating heart; only territory of occluded vessel at risk; shunts (intracoronary, aortocoronary, perfusion-assisted) for ischemia |
| **On-pump beating heart** | No aortic clamp; for calcified aorta, reoperations with patent ITA, LV aneurysm repair; may use shunts; tolerated if no AI |
| **Hypothermic fibrillatory arrest** | Cold, vented, fibrillating heart; rarely used; suboptimal protection in hypertrophied hearts |
| **Intermittent ischemic arrest** | Multiple short cross-clamp periods; tolerated but violates principle of continuous protection |
| **Ischemic preconditioning** | Brief ischemia before prolonged occlusion may reduce injury; applicable in OPCAB |
| **Ischemic postconditioning** | Medications at reperfusion; adenosine 1.5 mg/kg reduces troponin leak |

### Cardioplegia Principles (Table 6.2)

**Diastolic arrest:** Potassium chloride (20–25 mEq/L) induces depolarization arrest.

**Types of Cardioplegia:**
- **Crystalloid cardioplegia (CCP):**
  - *St. Thomas/Plegisol:* extracellular, high K⁺, requires redosing q20 min; procaine may cause seizures
  - *Custodiol (Bretschneider):* intracellular, histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate; single dose provides 2–3 hours protection; comparable to blood cardioplegia except in low EF patients
- **Blood cardioplegia (BCP):** Provides oxygen, buffers, antioxidants, free-radical scavengers
  - *Buckberg:* 4:1 or 8:1 blood:crystalloid; requires redosing q20–30 min; K⁺ 20–25 mEq/L induction, 12–15 mEq/L maintenance
  - *del Nido (DN):* 1:4 blood:crystalloid with lidocaine, magnesium, bicarbonate, mannitol; single dose for cross-clamp <90 min; reduces cross-clamp time; heart often sluggish post-unclamping

**Maintenance of arrest:** For Buckberg: redose q15–20 min; for DN: redose after 45–90 min or if activity returns.

**Temperature:**
- **Cold (<15°C):** reduces oxygen consumption; systemic hypothermia + topical cold saline
- **Tepid (20–32°C):** allows better oxygen/glucose utilization; may improve recovery
- **Warm (normothermic):** continuous or brief interruptions; requires continuous delivery to prevent activity; "miniplegia" systems minimize hemodilution
  - **Warm induction:** 500 mL after cross-clamp; beneficial in ischemic hearts
  - **Hot shot:** terminal warm BCP before unclamping; improves metabolism; benefit unclear in some studies

**Route of Delivery:**
- **Antegrade (ACP):** aortic root; compromised by severe CAD or AI; cumbersome during valve surgery
- **Retrograde (RCP):** coronary sinus catheter; easy to administer; monitor pressure (20–50 mm Hg) to avoid rupture; protects RV poorly
- **Combined ACP/RCP:** optimal protection; especially for high-risk patients; simultaneous delivery down completed grafts + RCP is safe

**Modified Reperfusion:** Controlled reperfusion with low K⁺ (8–10 mEq/L), low pressure (<50 mm Hg), substrate enrichment (aspartate/glutamate) — part of "integrated cardioplegia"; benefit mainly in high-risk cases.

**Additives:** Aspartate/glutamate (Krebs cycle intermediates) benefit energy-depleted hearts; esmolol, GIK may modestly reduce troponin.

### Cardioplegia Strategy

- **Low-risk cases:** multidose cold crystalloid, cold/tepid/warm BCP (antegrade ± retrograde) all acceptable
- **High-risk patients:** combined ACP/RCP with cold, tepid, or warm BCP recommended
- **Proposed protocol (except DN):**
  1. Warm induction only for severely ischemic hearts
  2. Induce with 1 L (750 mL antegrade + 250 mL retrograde)
  3. Maintain with low-K⁺ RCP q20 min; for coronary cases: perform RCA first, administer cardioplegia down grafts + RCP
  4. Hot shot (500 mL) retrograde before unclamping
  5. Redo AVR with patent ITA: continuous cold retrograde blood/BCP + systemic hypothermia ≥28°C
  6. For valve cases after initial antegrade: continuous warm RCP or intermittent cold RCP
- **Del Nido:** single dose for clamp <90 min; redose 10 mL/kg after 45–90 min if longer clamp anticipated

## Chapter 7: Admission to the ICU and Monitoring Techniques</summary>

### Admission to the ICU
- **Safe transfer** from OR to ICU requires coordinated handoff, battery-powered infusion pumps, transport monitor displaying ECG, arterial pressure, and SpO₂.
- **Critical initial assessment:** airway patency, breath sounds, ECG rhythm, blood pressure. The two most common problems are low blood pressure and indecipherable ECG.
- **Low BP protocol:** palpate pulse, obtain cuff pressure, check IV lines (nitroglycerin/nitroprusside), examine chest tubes for bleeding, assess filling pressures, treat with volume and calcium chloride. If no response, prepare for emergency sternotomy.
- **Indecipherable ECG:** palpate pulse; if absent, treat as cardiac arrest. Check pacemaker connections if present; obtain 12-lead ECG.

### ICU Monitoring Techniques

| Monitor | Key Points |
|---------|------------|
| **ECG** | Continuous display, ST segment analysis; 12-lead for ischemia/arrhythmias |
| **Arterial line** | Radial or femoral; dampening from air bubbles, underdampening from long tubing; mean pressure most reliable; remove when no longer needed for vasoactive drugs |
| **Pulse oximetry** | Continuous SpO₂ monitoring; ear lobe if vasoconstricted; does not replace ABG for pH/PCO₂ |
| **CVP** | Adequate for preserved LV function; low = hypovolemia; high = RV dysfunction, TR, tamponade |
| **Swan-Ganz catheter** | Provides CVP, PAP, PCWP, cardiac output, SvO₂; useful in high-risk patients, valve surgery; continuous CO catheters available |
| **PA catheter complications** | Arrhythmias, heart block (LBBB risk), PA rupture (withdraw catheter, PEEP, bronchial blocker if bleeding), infection |
| **Vigileo/FloTrac** | Continuous CO from arterial waveform; useful when PA catheter not placed or unreliable (TR); inaccurate in arrhythmias |
| **Left atrial line** | Most accurate left-sided filling pressure; risk of air embolism; remove with chest tubes in place |
| **Chest tubes** | Record drainage q15 min; −20 cm H₂O suction; gentle milking; remove when <100 mL/8h; always remove off suction |
| **Urinary catheter** | Hourly output; remove POD#2 to reduce UTI risk; may leave if vigorous diuresis |
| **Pacing wires** | Two atrial + two ventricular; understand settings; unused wires in insulating caps |

### Removal Guidelines
- **Swan-Ganz:** when inotropes/vasodilators no longer needed
- **Central line:** when no longer necessary; change over guidewire if fever
- **Arterial line:** after stable ABGs on room air; hold pressure for femoral removal
- **LA line:** remove with chest tubes in place
- **Chest tubes:** when drainage <100 mL/8h; remove off suction
- **Foley:** POD#2; earlier if no longer needed

## Chapter 8: Early Postoperative Care</summary>

### Basic Features of Early Postoperative Period
- **Early extubation** (within 6 hours) is feasible in most patients using short-acting anesthetics (remifentanil, sevoflurane) and sedation (propofol, dexmedetomidine). Criteria: hemodynamic stability, normothermia, minimal bleeding, adequate gas exchange.
- **Hypothermia** (<36°C) causes vasoconstriction, hypertension, arrhythmias, coagulopathy, and delayed extubation. Treat with forced-air warming (Bair Hugger), meperidine for shivering.
- **Mediastinal bleeding** >200 mL/h requires evaluation. Thromboelastography helps identify coagulopathy. Early re-exploration for surgical bleeding or tamponade.
- **Analgesia:** multimodal approach — ketorolac, acetaminophen, gabapentin; avoid excessive opioids. Dexmedetomidine provides sedation + analgesia.
- **Hemodynamic support:** target CI >2.2 L/min/m², MAP 70–80 mm Hg. Fluid resuscitation (crystalloid, then colloid), inotropes (epinephrine, dobutamine, milrinone), vasopressors (phenylephrine, norepinephrine, vasopressin for vasoplegia).
- **Fluid management:** patients are total body fluid overloaded post-CPB but often require volume for preload. Avoid excessive fluids (>2 L/6h) to prevent pulmonary edema.
- **Electrolytes:** maintain K⁺ >4.5 mEq/L, Mg²⁺ replacement to reduce arrhythmias. Metabolic acidosis (lactate >4 mmol/L) indicates inadequate perfusion.
- **Glucose:** maintain <180 mg/dL with insulin protocol.

### Common Postoperative Scenarios

| Scenario | Management |
|----------|------------|
| **Vasoconstriction + hypertension** | Warm patient, vasodilators (clevidipine, nitroprusside), volume to maintain preload (PAD 15–20 mm Hg), inotropes if CI low |
| **Vasodilation + hypotension** | Volume resuscitation (crystalloid then colloid); if CI >2.2 → phenylephrine/vasopressin; if CI low → inotropes then norepinephrine |
| **Copious urine output** | Replace volume, correct hyperglycemia, consider α-agonist to reduce fluid requirement |
| **Low CO with LV dysfunction** | Optimize preload, inotropes (epinephrine, milrinone), IABP if refractory |
| **Low CO with diastolic dysfunction** | Avoid inotropes with chronotropic effects; use lusitropic drugs (milrinone, dobutamine); volume to higher filling pressures (PAD 20–25 mm Hg) |
| **Low CO with RV dysfunction** | Optimize preload (avoid CVP >20 mm Hg), milrinone, pulmonary vasodilators (inhaled nitric oxide, epoprostenol, iloprost) |

### Procedure-Specific Postoperative Care

| Procedure | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **On-pump CABG** | Low-dose inotropes; atrial pacing for bradycardia; β-blocker for AF prophylaxis; aspirin within 6–24h; statins; DAPT for ACS patients |
| **OPCAB** | Maintain normothermia; evaluate ECG for ischemia; low threshold for angiography if graft concerns; DAPT for one year per AHA |
| **MIDCAB** | Epidural analgesia; no pacing wires; monitor for bleeding in pleural space |
| **SAVR (AS)** | Maintain sinus rhythm (atrial pacing); higher filling pressures (PCWP >20 mm Hg); treat hypertension with β-blockers; ARBs for diastolic function |
| **TAVR** | Monitor for heart block (RBBB at highest risk); temporary pacing wire overnight; monitor femoral access for bleeding; DAPT for 6 months; NOAC for AF |
| **Mitral stenosis** | RV dysfunction common; pulmonary vasodilators; aggressive diuresis; consider Maze for AF |
| **Mitral regurgitation** | Anticipate LV dysfunction post-repair; inotropes + afterload reduction; monitor for RV failure; SAM risk with excess posterior leaflet height |
| **Minimally invasive** | Pain control (intercostal blocks); monitor for undrained blood; assess distal perfusion after femoral cannulation; compartment syndrome risk |
| **Aortic dissection (Type A)** | Aggressive BP control (β-blockers); coagulopathy from DHCA; neurologic assessment |
| **Aortic arch aneurysms** | DHCA → coagulopathy, temperature afterdrop; delayed awakening; strict BP control |
| **Descending thoracic aneurysms** | CSF drainage (pressure <10 mm Hg), MAP >90 mm Hg; delayed paraplegia risk; TEVAR preferred |

### Anticoagulation Guidelines

| Valve Type | Anticoagulation |
|------------|-----------------|
| **Tissue aortic** | Aspirin indefinitely; VKA for 3–6 months optional (ACC IIa) |
| **Mechanical aortic** | Warfarin INR 2.5 (2.0 for On-X); aspirin; heparin bridge until INR therapeutic |
| **Tissue mitral** | VKA for 3–6 months; then aspirin if sinus rhythm |
| **Mechanical mitral** | Warfarin INR 3.0 + aspirin; heparin bridge |
| **Mitral repair ring** | VKA for 3 months per guidelines; aspirin may be sufficient |

## Chapter 9: Mediastinal Bleeding</summary>

### Overview
- **CPB** causes significant coagulation disruption: hemodilution, platelet activation, coagulation cascade activation, fibrinolysis. Heparin alone induces platelet dysfunction and fibrinolysis.
- **OPCAB** avoids hemodilution and minimizes platelet activation; coagulopathy is unusual but may occur with cell-saver use.
- **Chest tubes** (28–32 Fr or Blake drains) placed to −20 cm H₂O suction; gentle milking maintains patency. Undrained blood in pleural spaces can cause deceptive bleeding patterns.
- **Bleeding >200 mL/h** for several hours requires evaluation. Re-exploration (1–3% of patients) carries 2–4× increased mortality.
- **Transfusions** are not benign: associated with graft occlusion, infection, renal failure, stroke, TRALI, TACO, and increased mortality.

### Etiology of Mediastinal Bleeding (Table 9.1)
- **Surgical:** anastomotic sites, side branches, sternal edges, raw surfaces
- **Residual anticoagulants:** heparin, LMWH, NOACs, bivalirudin, fondaparinux
- **Platelet dysfunction:** antiplatelet drugs, CPB circuit, uremia, inadequate heparinization
- **Thrombocytopenia:** preoperative (HIT, drugs), CPB hemodilution, protamine
- **Heparin/protamine:** residual heparin, rebound, excessive protamine (procoagulant)
- **Coagulation factor depletion:** warfarin, hepatic dysfunction, hemodilution, cell-saver loss
- **Fibrinolysis:** thrombolytics, CPB, heparin

### Risk Factors for Bleeding (Table 9.2)
- **Patient-related:** older age, female, anemia, advanced cardiac disease, comorbidities
- **Medications:** high-dose aspirin, P2Y₁₂ inhibitors, LMWH within 18h, NOACs within 48h
- **Procedure-related:** complex operations, urgent/emergent, reoperations, bilateral ITA

### Prevention of Blood Loss (Table 9.3)
- **Preoperative:** stop anticoagulants/antiplatelets (except low-dose aspirin for CABG), correct anemia (erythropoietin, iron)
- **Medication cessation:**
  - Warfarin: stop 4–5 days; reverse with vitamin K, PCC, or FFP for urgent surgery
  - P2Y₁₂ inhibitors: clopidogrel 5d, ticagrelor 5d, prasugrel 7d; PRU testing may allow earlier surgery
  - NOACs: stop 48h (longer if renal dysfunction); reversal: idarucizumab (dabigatran), andexanet alfa (apixaban/rivaroxaban), PCC
  - Aspirin: continue 81 mg for CABG; stop 3–5 days for non-CABG
- **Antifibrinolytics:** ε-aminocaproic acid or tranexamic acid for all on- and off-pump cases
- **Heparin/protamine:** heparin-protamine titration systems optimize dosing, reduce bleeding
- **Perfusion:** autologous blood withdrawal, biocompatible circuits, miniaturized circuits, retrograde autologous priming, cell salvage (washed)
- **Surgical technique:** meticulous hemostasis, normothermia before bypass termination

### Assessment of Bleeding in ICU (Table 9.4)
- **Quantify drainage:** >200 mL/h concerning; >300 mL/h for 2–3h prompts re-exploration
- **Hemodynamics:** filling pressures, cardiac output; rising pressures with falling CO suggests tamponade
- **Coagulation studies:** PT/INR, PTT, platelets, fibrinogen; TEG/ROTEM guides therapy
- **Chest x-ray:** widened mediastinum suggests clot accumulation; compare with postoperative film
- **Echocardiography:** TEE for suspected tamponade when TTE inadequate

### Management of Bleeding (Table 9.5)
- **General:** warm patient, control hypertension/agitation/shivering, ensure chest tube patency
- **Blood components (empiric):**
  - Platelets: antiplatelet drugs, uremia, CPB, or platelet count <100,000/μL
  - FFP: warfarin, hepatic dysfunction, prolonged CPB, multiple PRBCs
  - Cryoprecipitate: fibrinogen <100 mg/dL
  - PCC: warfarin reversal, NOAC reversal, refractory bleeding
- **Protamine:** 25–50 mg if elevated PTT (heparin rebound)
- **Desmopressin (DDAVP):** von Willebrand disease, uremia, aortic stenosis-associated bleeding
- **Calcium:** if multiple CPD blood transfusions or ionized calcium <1.1 mmol/L
- **rFVIIa:** 90 μg/kg for intractable coagulopathy after all other measures (5–10% thrombotic risk)

### Blood Transfusions: Red Cells
- **Indications:** maintain HCT >24% in bleeding patient; restrictive strategy (HCT 21%) for stable patients
- **Risks:** infection, TRALI, TACO, immunomodulation, graft occlusion, increased mortality
- **Cell-saver blood:** washed, no clotting factors/platelets; safe
- **Autotransfusion of unwashed mediastinal blood:** controversial; may cause coagulopathy, infection

### Blood Components and Factor Concentrates
| Product | Indication | Dose |
|---------|------------|------|
| **Platelets** | Count <100,000/μL or dysfunction | 1–2 six-packs |
| **FFP** | Elevated INR, factor deficiency | 2–4 units |
| **PCC** | Warfarin/NOAC reversal, refractory bleeding | 25–50 units/kg |
| **Cryoprecipitate** | Fibrinogen <100 mg/dL | 1 unit/7–10 kg |
| **Fibrinogen concentrate** | Hypofibrinogenemia | 25–75 mg/kg |
| **rFVIIa** | Intractable coagulopathy | 90 μg/kg |
| **Albumin 5%** | Volume expansion (safe) | PRN |
| **HES** | Avoid in bleeding patient | — |

### Re-exploration for Bleeding or Tamponade
- **Indications:**
  - >400 mL/h × 1h
  - >300 mL/h × 2–3h
  - >200 mL/h × 4h
  - Sudden cessation of bleeding with hemodynamic compromise (tamponade)
- **Tamponade signs:** elevated filling pressures, low CO, narrowed pulse pressure, respiratory variation, widened mediastinum
- **Early exploration (<12h)** reduces mortality, transfusions, organ failure
- **TEE** for diagnosis when unclear

### Emergency Resternotomy Technique
- **Equipment:** emergency resternotomy pack in all cardiac ICUs
- **Steps:** remove dressing, antiseptic prep, cut sternal wires, place retractor, control bleeding, internal massage if needed
- **Internal massage:** compress between both hands; avoid avulsing LITA to LAD
- **Stabilize in ICU before OR transfer if possible**

## Chapter 10: Respiratory Management</summary>

### General Principles
- **Early extubation** (within 4–6 hours) is desirable and safe in most patients, reducing pulmonary complications, ICU stay, and costs. OR extubation is feasible in low-risk patients.
- **Median sternotomy** and **ITA harvesting** impair pulmonary function (30–50% reduction in FVC, FEV₁, FRC). CPB causes systemic inflammatory response with capillary leak and pulmonary dysfunction, though on- vs. off-pump differences are minimal.
- **Postoperative respiratory impairment** is multifactorial: atelectasis, V/Q mismatch, phrenic nerve injury (ice slush, diabetic patients), pleural entry, fluid overload, transfusions (TRALI, TACO).

### Routine Ventilator Management
- **Initial settings:** Volume-cycled, SIMV or A/C mode; tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg; rate 10–12/min; FiO₂ 1.0; PEEP 5 cm H₂O. Reduce FiO₂ to 0.4–0.5 once PaO₂ >80 torr confirmed.
- **Sedation:** Propofol (25–75 μg/kg/min) for short-term; dexmedetomidine (1 μg/kg load, 0.2–1.5 μg/kg/h) for early extubation—less respiratory depression, lower delirium risk. Avoid benzodiazepines (delirium).
- **Analgesia:** Multimodal—ketorolac, IV acetaminophen, low-dose narcotics, epidural, ON-Q bupivacaine. Avoid excessive opioids delaying extubation.

### Oxygenation and Ventilation Concepts
- **Oxygen content** primarily determined by hemoglobin (1.39 mL O₂/g Hb) and SaO₂; PaO₂ contributes minimally. SaO₂ >90% corresponds to PaO₂ >60 torr (shoulder of dissociation curve).
- **Methemoglobinemia:** from high-dose IV nitroglycerin (>10 μg/kg/min for days); falsely low SpO₂ with normal PaO₂.
- **PaO₂/FiO₂ ratio:** <300 indicates acute lung injury; <200 indicates ARDS; <150 postoperatively concerning.
- **Hypocarbia** (PCO₂ 30–35 torr) acceptable initially; profound hypocarbia causes hypokalemia, left shift, cerebral vasoconstriction.
- **Hypercarbia:** inadequate minute ventilation; may indicate mechanical problem (ETT malposition, pneumothorax) or inadequate respiratory drive.

### Early Extubation: Criteria and Risk Factors
- **Weaning criteria (Table 10.3):** awake, hemodynamically stable (CI >2.2, BP stable, HR <120), core temp >35.5°C, chest tube drainage <50 mL/h, PaO₂/FiO₂ >150, PCO₂ <50 torr.
- **Exclusion criteria (Table 10.2):** preoperative pulmonary edema, intubation, cardiogenic shock, sepsis; intraoperative DHCA, coagulopathy, long pump run (>4–6h); postoperative bleeding, hemodynamic instability, stroke.
- **STS risk calculator** (www.sts.org) predicts prolonged ventilation risk.

### Postextubation Respiratory Care
- **Oxygen delivery:** facemask, high-flow nasal cannula (up to 60 L/min, heated/humidified), non-rebreather mask.
- **Noninvasive ventilation (NIV):** BPAP (inspiratory 8–12 cm H₂O, expiratory 3–5 cm H₂O) superior to CPAP for hypercarbia; CPAP (5–10 cm H₂O) for pulmonary edema.
- **Incentive spirometry:** deep breaths q1–2h; cough pillow.
- **Diuresis:** once hemodynamically stable, aggressive furosemide to eliminate extravascular lung water.
- **VTE prophylaxis:** compression stockings, pneumatic compression, early mobilization; pharmacologic prophylaxis (heparin/LMWH) controversial—risk of delayed tamponade.

### Acute Respiratory Insufficiency
- **Prolonged ventilation (>48h)** occurs in 5–10% of patients; mortality 20–25%. Risk factors: age, COPD, renal dysfunction, long CPB, emergency surgery, blood transfusions.
- **Etiology:** low cardiac output, pulmonary edema (cardiogenic/noncardiogenic), pneumonia, atelectasis, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, bronchospasm, TRALI, TACO.
- **Management (Table 10.7):** increase FiO₂ to 1.0, hand-ventilate, assess ETT position, chest x-ray, optimize hemodynamics, add PEEP (2.5–5 cm H₂O increments), treat underlying cause.
- **PEEP:** improves oxygenation but may reduce venous return; monitor cardiac output. High PEEP can cause barotrauma, auto-PEEP in COPD.

### Chronic Respiratory Failure/Ventilator Dependence
- **Hypoxemic failure:** persistent low PaO₂ from hemodynamic instability, pulmonary edema, pneumonia, ARDS.
- **Hypercapnic failure:** imbalance between ventilatory capacity and demand—increased CO₂ production (fever, sepsis, overfeeding), decreased respiratory drive (sedation, stroke), decreased muscle function (malnutrition, phrenic nerve injury, myopathy).
- **ARDS:** noncardiogenic pulmonary edema with PaO₂/FiO₂ <200; risk factors: long CPB, reoperation, emergency surgery, transfusions; mortality 18–80%. Manage with low tidal volume ventilation (6 mL/kg), plateau pressure <30 cm H₂O, prone positioning, inhaled nitric oxide/epoprostenol, ECMO.
- **Tracheostomy:** consider if ventilation >2 weeks; early tracheostomy (<10 days) may reduce mortality; percutaneous bedside approach safe; no increased mediastinitis risk when performed >10 days post-sternotomy.

### Ventilatory Modes

| Mode | Use |
|------|-----|
| **SIMV** | Initial full support; weaning—gradual rate reduction |
| **A/C** | Full support only; not for weaning |
| **CMV** | Full support; leads to muscle deconditioning |
| **PSV** | Patient-triggered, pressure-limited; best for weaning, reduces work of breathing, comfortable |
| **NIV (BPAP/CPAP)** | Avoid intubation; support post-extubation |

### Weaning Techniques
- **Short-term ventilation:** T-piece or CPAP 5 cm H₂O for 30–60 min SBT; extubate if criteria met.
- **Chronic ventilation:** PSV weaning (reduce support by 2 cm H₂O daily) or SIMV weaning (reduce rate). Rapid shallow breathing index (RSBI = RR/tidal volume in L) <100 predicts successful weaning.
- **Cuff leak test:** >110 mL or >10% leak to avoid postextubation stridor. Steroids (methylprednisolone 20–40 mg q4–6h) if high risk.

### Other Respiratory Complications
- **Pneumothorax:** suspect with sudden peak pressure increase, hemodynamic instability, ABG deterioration. Manage with chest tube; water seal may resolve small persistent leaks.
- **Pleural effusions:** 60% of patients; usually self-limited. Large effusions causing dyspnea require thoracentesis. Hemothorax may cause tamponade.
- **Chylothorax:** rare; milky fluid with triglycerides >110 mg/dL. Conservative treatment: fat-free diet, octreotide; refractory cases require thoracic duct ligation.
- **Bronchospasm:** treat with albuterol ± ipratroprium; steroids for refractory cases. β-blockers generally safe in COPD patients.

## Chapter 11: Cardiovascular Management</summary>

### Hemodynamic Principles
- **Cardiac Output (CO)** is determined by stroke volume and heart rate. Stroke volume depends on three major factors: **preload** (LV end-diastolic volume, approximated by filling pressures), **afterload** (systemic vascular resistance, SVR), and **contractility** (intrinsic strength of contraction).
- **Tissue Oxygenation** is primarily determined by CO, hemoglobin level, and arterial oxygen saturation. The mixed venous oxygen saturation (\(\mathrm{SvO}_2\)) reflects the balance between oxygen delivery and consumption; a value <60% suggests inadequate oxygen delivery.
- **Myocardial Oxygen Supply:Demand Ratio** is optimized by maintaining a heart rate of 80–90 bpm, MAP >70 mm Hg, minimizing afterload and ventricular distention, and maintaining an adequate hematocrit.

### Low Cardiac Output Syndrome
- Defined as a cardiac index <2.0 L/min/m², often with elevated filling pressures (>20 mm Hg) and high SVR (>1500 dyn·s/cm⁵).
- **Management strategy** follows a stepwise approach: optimize ventilation, treat ischemia/spasm, optimize preload (PAD/PCW 18–20 mm Hg), stabilize heart rate/rhythm (AV synchrony, ~90 bpm), and then add inotropes (epinephrine, dobutamine) or vasodilators based on hemodynamics.
- **Right ventricular (RV) failure** is a distinct entity often associated with pulmonary hypertension. Management focuses on optimizing RV preload (CVP 18–20 mm Hg), ensuring AV conduction, maintaining systemic perfusion, reducing RV afterload with pulmonary vasodilators (iNO, epoprostenol, milrinone), and using inotropes.
- **Diastolic dysfunction** presents with low CO but high filling pressures. Treatment involves lusitropic drugs (CCBs, milrinone), bradycardic agents (β-blockers), and diuresis to reduce myocardial edema.

### Inotropic and Vasoactive Drugs
- **Epinephrine** is a potent β₁-inotrope; low doses have β₂-vasodilator effects, high doses cause α-vasoconstriction. It is a first-line drug for borderline CO.
- **Dobutamine** is a strong β₁-inotrope with mild β₂-vasodilator effects, useful when SVR is elevated. It can cause significant tachycardia.
- **Milrinone** is a phosphodiesterase (PDE) III inhibitor with both inotropic and vasodilatory ("inodilator") effects. It is particularly useful for RV failure and diastolic dysfunction but often requires an α-agent for blood pressure support.
- **Norepinephrine** is a potent α-agonist with β₁ effects, used for hypotension with low SVR and marginal CO.
- **Phenylephrine** is a pure α-agonist, indicated only when CO is satisfactory.
- **Vasopressin** is used for vasoplegic shock unresponsive to catecholamines.
- **Levosimendan** is a calcium-sensitizing "inodilator" with cardioprotective effects, used to facilitate weaning from bypass.

### Intra-aortic Balloon Counterpulsation (IABP)
- **Mechanism:** Reduces afterload (rapid deflation before systole) and increases coronary perfusion (rapid inflation after aortic valve closure).
- **Indications:** Refractory ischemia, high-risk surgery (prophylactic), postcardiotomy low CO, bridge to transplantation.
- **Complications:** Vascular (distal ischemia, thrombosis, embolism), thrombocytopenia, sepsis. Distal pulses must be monitored frequently.

### Mechanical Circulatory Support (MCS)
- **LVADs/RVADs/BiVADs** provide short- or long-term support for postcardiotomy failure or as a bridge to transplantation/decision. They are preload- and afterload-dependent.
- **ECMO** provides combined cardiac and respiratory support, useful for biventricular failure with hypoxemia.
- **Short-term devices** include percutaneous systems (Impella, TandemHeart) for high-risk PCI or cardiogenic shock, and surgically placed devices (CentriMag) for postcardiotomy support.
- **Long-term devices** (HeartMate II/3, HeartWare) are continuous-flow pumps used for destination therapy or as a bridge to transplantation.

### Hypertension and Vasodilators
- Postoperative hypertension is common and usually due to elevated SVR. Treatment is indicated to keep SBP <130 mm Hg to prevent myocardial ischemia and bleeding.
- **Sodium nitroprusside** is a potent arterial and venous dilator; risk of cyanide toxicity.
- **Clevidipine** is a very short-acting CCB, ideal for rapid, titratable control of blood pressure.
- **Nicardipine** is an arterial vasodilator with a longer duration, also useful for radial artery spasm.
- **β-blockers** (esmolol, labetalol) are used for hyperdynamic hypertension with tachycardia.

### Perioperative Myocardial Infarction (PMI)
- **Definition (Type 5 MI):** cTn >10× URL within 48 hours of surgery, plus new Q waves, graft occlusion, or imaging evidence of new myocardial loss.
- **Presentation:** Can be intraoperative (new wall motion on TEE), postcardiotomy low CO with ECG changes, or a "good CO but low SVR" syndrome.
- **Management:** Immediate attention to graft flow; IABP is helpful. Persistent ischemia may require coronary angiography with PCI or graft revision.

### Coronary Vasospasm
- Affects native vessels, arterial grafts, or vein grafts. Presentation is ST elevation and hemodynamic compromise.
- **Treatment:** IV nitroglycerin, CCBs (diltiazem, verapamil), and milrinone. Hypomagnesemia should be corrected. If refractory, emergency catheterization is indicated.

### Cardiac Arrest
- **Key principle for ICU patients:** Delay external compressions for up to one minute to attempt defibrillation (VT/VF) or pacing (asystole). If unsuccessful, perform emergency sternotomy within five minutes to allow for internal massage and control of bleeding.
- **Common causes:** VF, tamponade, and massive bleeding.
- **Management:** Ventilation, defibrillation/pacing, epinephrine/vasopressin, amiodarone. Emergency sternotomy is a life-saving procedure.

### Pacing Wires and Pacemakers
- Atrial pacing improves CO by 20–30% compared to ventricular pacing. AV pacing is used for heart block.
- **Diagnostic uses:** Atrial electrograms (AEGs) help differentiate arrhythmias.
- **Complications:** Bleeding and tamponade can occur with wire removal, especially ventricular wires.
- **Indications for permanent pacemaker (PPM):** Complete heart block, symptomatic sinus node dysfunction, slow ventricular response to AF (<50 bpm) after stopping causative drugs.

### Cardiac Arrhythmias
- **Atrial Fibrillation (AF):** Occurs in 25–30% of patients, peak on POD #2–3. Prophylaxis includes β-blockers, amiodarone, and magnesium. Treatment involves rate control (β-blockers, diltiazem, amiodarone) or rhythm control (cardioversion, amiodarone, ibutilide). Anticoagulation is considered after 48 hours of persistent AF.
- **Ventricular Tachycardia/Fibrillation (VT/VF):** Less common but serious. Nonsustained VT may be benign in normal LV; sustained VT or VF requires immediate defibrillation and evaluation for ischemia.

### Antiarrhythmic Medications
- **Class III agents (Amiodarone, Sotalol, Ibutilide)** are commonly used postoperatively. Amiodarone has multiple mechanisms and a long half-life; it requires monitoring of thyroid, liver, and pulmonary function.
- **Class IC agents (Propafenone, Flecainide)** are effective for AF conversion but are contraindicated in structural heart disease.
- **Class IV agents (Diltiazem, Verapamil)** are primarily for rate control.
- **Digoxin** is used less frequently; toxicity manifests as various arrhythmias (PVCs, AV block, PAT with block).

## Chapter 12: Fluid Management, Renal, Metabolic, and Endocrine Problems</summary>

### Body Water Distribution and Fluid Management
- Body water constitutes 60% of body weight (50% in women), with two‑thirds intracellular and one‑third extracellular. The extracellular space is divided into interstitial (two‑thirds) and intravascular (one‑third) compartments.
- **Starling's law** governs fluid shifts: elevated hydrostatic pressure or low colloid oncotic pressure shifts fluid into the interstitium. CPB causes capillary leak, requiring continued fluid administration for 6–12 hours to maintain preload despite total body fluid overload.
- **Fluid selection:** Crystalloids (e.g., lactated Ringer's) provide transient volume expansion; colloids (5% albumin, HES) provide more sustained expansion. Saline‑based solutions carry a high chloride load associated with AKI risk. Chloride‑restricted solutions (lactated Ringer's, Plasma‑Lyte) are preferred.
- **Diuresis:** Loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide) are used after capillary leak resolves. Continuous infusion may improve response. Thiazides can be added for synergy. "Renal‑dose" dopamine is not renoprotective and should not be used.

### Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) — Risk, Prevention, and Management
- **Definition (RIFLE/AKIN/KDIGO):** Increase in SCr ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours, or ≥50% within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours. Even mild postoperative SCr elevation increases mortality.
- **Risk factors:** Preoperative CKD (GFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m²), advanced age, diabetes, hypertension, vascular disease, low EF, prolonged CPB, low cardiac output, transfusions, contrast exposure.
- **Risk models:** STS risk calculator, AKICS score, Cleveland Clinic model.
- **Prevention:**
  - *Preoperative:* Hydration before catheterization, delay surgery 24–48 hours after contrast, withhold ACE inhibitors/ARBs/NSAIDs, treat anemia (erythropoietin, iron, transfusions), optimize hemodynamics.
  - *Intraoperative:* OPCAB may reduce AKI in selected patients; heparin‑coated circuits, higher perfusion pressure (75–80 mm Hg), avoid extreme hemodilution (HCT >20%), control hyperglycemia, use del Nido cardioplegia.
  - *Post‑bypass:* Optimize hemodynamics, dexmedetomidine for sedation, early dialysis catheter placement if high risk.
- **Diagnosis:** SCr lags; biomarkers (NGAL, cystatin C, KIM‑1) detect AKI earlier. Nonoliguric AKI has better prognosis than oliguric.
- **Management of oliguria:**
  - Optimize preload (avoid chloride‑liberal solutions)
  - Optimize heart rate and rhythm (pacing, cardioversion)
  - Improve contractility with inotropes
  - Reduce afterload with vasodilators; maintain higher BP (130–140 mm Hg) in hypertensive CKD patients
  - Diuretics: furosemide up to 200 mg IV or continuous infusion (10–20 mg/h); add thiazide if resistant
  - Avoid mannitol postoperatively (risk of pulmonary edema)
  - Early initiation of RRT if persistent oliguria with fluid overload

### Renal Replacement Therapy (RRT)
- **Indications:** Fluid overload, hyperkalemia, metabolic acidosis, uremia
- **Intermittent hemodialysis (HD):** Efficient solute removal, risk of hypotension; use in stable patients
- **Continuous veno‑venous therapies (CVVH, CVVHD, SCUF):** Better hemodynamic tolerance, preferred in unstable patients. CVVH removes fluid and solute by convection; CVVHD adds diffusion; SCUF removes fluid only.
- **Access:** 12 Fr double‑lumen catheter in internal jugular vein (avoid subclavian to preserve fistula sites)

### Electrolyte and Acid‑Base Disorders
- **Hyperkalemia:** ECG changes (peaked T waves, QRS widening) require emergency treatment: calcium gluconate (membrane stabilization), insulin/glucose (shift into cells), albuterol, furosemide, kayexalate, or hemodialysis.
- **Hypokalemia:** Causes: diuresis, insulin, alkalosis, hypomagnesemia. Treat with KCl (10–20 mEq/h central line). Correct hypomagnesemia first.
- **Hypocalcemia:** Ionized calcium <1.0 mmol/L. Treat with calcium gluconate; calcium chloride reserved for emergencies. Correct hypomagnesemia first.
- **Hypomagnesemia:** Common after CPB; administer MgSO₄ 2 g to reduce arrhythmias.
- **Metabolic acidosis:** Usually lactic acidosis from low cardiac output. Type A (tissue hypoxia) vs. Type B (epinephrine, metformin, liver failure). Treatment: correct underlying cause; sodium bicarbonate considered if pH <7.20 and base deficit >8–10 mmol/L.
- **Metabolic alkalosis:** Usually from diuretics, NG suction. Treatment: correct volume and chloride depletion (0.9% NaCl, KCl); acetazolamide if fluid overloaded.

### Hyperglycemia and Endocrine Disorders
- **Hyperglycemia:** Stress response increases morbidity (infection, AF, AKI). Maintain blood glucose <180 mg/dL with IV insulin. Transition to subcutaneous insulin using basal‑bolus regimen.
- **Hypothyroidism:** Low T₃ after CPB; severe cases may contribute to low CO. T₃ 10–20 μg can be given as salvage therapy.
- **Adrenal insufficiency:** Suspect in vasopressor‑dependent hypotension. Diagnosis: low cortisol, failure to respond to cosyntropin. Treatment: hydrocortisone 100 mg q8h.
- **Pituitary abnormalities:** Pituitary apoplexy (headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia) after CPB; diabetes insipidus (polyuria, hypernatremia) treated with desmopressin.

## Chapter 13: Post-ICU Care and Other Complications</summary>

### Postoperative Recovery Framework
*   **Fast-track protocols** standardize care but do not replace careful patient evaluation. Most patients transfer to the floor on POD#1.
*   **Timeline:** Extubation within 6–8 hours; off inotropes by POD#1; chest tubes removed POD#1–2; discharge POD#4–5 in uncomplicated cases.
*   **Transfer orders** include telemetry, incentive spirometry, early mobilization, and individualized medication regimens.

### Differential Diagnosis of Common Symptoms
*   **Chest pain:** most commonly musculoskeletal, but must rule out myocardial ischemia, pericarditis, pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism, sternal infection.
*   **Shortness of breath:** evaluate for atelectasis, pneumonia, pleural effusion, pulmonary embolism, tamponade, heart failure, arrhythmias.
*   **Fever:** common in first 48–72 hours (inflammatory response, atelectasis). After 72 hours — investigate UTI, wound infection, *C. difficile*, catheter sepsis, endocarditis, postpericardiotomy syndrome.

### Respiratory Care
*   **Standard orders:** supplemental oxygen, incentive spirometry q1h, progressive mobilization, adequate analgesia.
*   **Diaphragmatic dysfunction** (10–20%): primary cause is cold injury from iced slush. Most unilateral cases asymptomatic; bilateral cases cause weaning failure. Treatment supportive; plication for marked dyspnea.
*   **Venous thromboembolism (VTE):** symptomatic in 1–2% despite 15–20% screening incidence. Risk factors: older age, obesity, heart failure, prolonged immobilization. Prevention: early mobilization, compression stockings, consider pharmacologic prophylaxis after mediastinal bleeding subsides.

### Cardiac Complications
*   **Atrial arrhythmias:** peak POD#2–3. Management: rate control, conversion to sinus rhythm, anticoagulation if persistent.
*   **Conduction abnormalities:** new bundle branch blocks in up to 45% after CABG (resolve in >50%). Higher risk after AVR (5–9% require PPM).
*   **Pacemaker wire removal:** risk of tamponade. Remove when INR <2; if persistently elevated — cut wires. Monitor for hypotension after removal.
*   **Hypertension:** oral antihypertensives substituted for IV agents. β-blockers for all patients (AF prophylaxis). Choose agent based on LV function, heart rate, graft type.
*   **Hypotension:** common causes: hypovolemia, anemia, β-blockers, rapid AF. Exclude tamponade if refractory with poor perfusion.
*   **Pericardial effusions and delayed tamponade:** occurs in 1–5% of patients. **Critical diagnosis** — symptoms often attributed to slow recovery. TTE may miss posterior effusions; TEE or CT if suspicion high. Treatment: pericardiocentesis (50% effective), subxiphoid exploration, or sternal reopening.
*   **Postpericardiotomy syndrome (PPS):** 10–20% incidence; autoimmune inflammatory response. Prevention: colchicine 0.5 mg bid × 1 month. Treatment: aspirin + colchicine ± NSAIDs ± steroids.
*   **Constrictive pericarditis:** rare late complication; presents with right heart failure. Pericardiectomy indicated if no response to diuretics/steroids.

### Renal and Metabolic Management
*   **Fluid overload:** compare daily weights to preoperative weight. Diuretics to achieve dry weight.
*   **AKI:** rising creatinine with oliguria — **always suspect delayed tamponade**. Avoid ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs; use diuretics cautiously.
*   **Hyperglycemia:** IV insulin protocol reduces wound infection and mortality. Target blood glucose <180 mg/dL.

### Hematologic Complications and Anticoagulation
*   **Anemia:** transfusion threshold Hb <7 g/dL (restrictive) or <9 g/dL (liberal) — comparable outcomes. Iron therapy if HCT <30% at discharge.
*   **Thrombocytopenia:** platelet count nadir POD#1–2, recovers by POD#4. Falling count after recovery → suspect HIT.
*   **Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT):** occurs 5–14 days postop. Suspect if platelet count falls >50% or thrombosis develops. **Stop all heparin**; use argatroban or bivalirudin. Do not start warfarin until platelets >150,000.
*   **Anticoagulation after CABG:** aspirin 81–325 mg started 6–24 hours postop, continue indefinitely. DAPT (aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor) for ACS patients or after OPCAB.
*   **Prosthetic valves — tissue:** AVR — aspirin alone or VKA for 3–6 months. MVR — VKA 3–6 months, then aspirin. NOACs possible after 3 months for AF.
*   **Prosthetic valves — mechanical:** VKA indefinitely. AVR target INR 2.5; MVR target INR 3.0. Add aspirin only if vascular disease or recurrent embolism (ESC) or all patients (ACC/AHA). NOACs contraindicated.
*   **Warfarin dosing:** start 5 mg (2.5 mg in elderly, hepatic dysfunction, on amiodarone). Overanticoagulation: hold warfarin; give vitamin K (oral for INR 5–9, IV for bleeding); consider 4F-PCC for life-threatening bleeding.

### Wound Care and Infectious Complications
*   **Nosocomial infections:** 5–10% incidence. Prevention: mupirocin, chlorhexidine, hyperglycemia control, early catheter removal.
*   **Sternal wound infections:** ~1% incidence, 20% mortality. Risk factors: obesity (BMI >40), diabetes, bilateral ITA use, prolonged CPB. Prevention: perioperative antibiotics, hair clipping, blood glucose <180 mg/dL.
*   **Treatment of deep SWI:** debridement, foreign body removal, antibiotic ×6 weeks. Closure options: primary closure, muscle flaps, NPWT as bridge.

### Neurologic Complications
*   **Ischemic stroke:** 1–2% incidence. Mechanisms: thromboembolic or hypoperfusion. Prevention: minimize aortic manipulation, maintain MAP >80 mmHg on CPB, HCT >20%.
*   **Delirium:** 20–30% in elderly. Management: early mobilization, avoid benzodiazepines, dexmedetomidine for sedation, haloperidol or atypical antipsychotics for agitation.
*   **Neurocognitive dysfunction:** up to 63% early, improves at 6 months, may recur at 5 years. Related to baseline cognitive function.
*   **Brachial plexus injury:** stretch injury from sternal retraction. Usually ulnar distribution; resolves in >95% within months.
*   **Compartment syndrome:** rare after groin cannulation (>4–6 hours) or endoscopic vein harvesting. Pressure >35 mm Hg → fasciotomy.

### Gastrointestinal Complications
*   **Incidence:** ~4%; associated with 3× mortality. Strongest predictors: prolonged ventilation, AKI, sepsis.
*   **Paralytic ileus:** differentiate from Ogilvie's syndrome (cecal diameter >12 cm → risk of perforation). Treatment: NPO, NG suction, neostigmine for pseudo-obstruction.
*   **Upper GI bleeding:** 0.5–1%. Prophylaxis: PPI or H2 blocker for patients on ventilation, with coagulopathy, or ulcer history. Treatment: stop anticoagulants, PPI infusion, endoscopy.
*   **Mesenteric ischemia:** 0.2–0.4%, mortality 37–65%. Suspect in patient with persistent ileus, lactic acidosis, leukocytosis. Early CT angiography, urgent exploration if necrosis suspected.
*   **Hepatic dysfunction:** transient hyperbilirubinemia in 25%; severe liver failure rare. Treat underlying cause.

### Nutrition
*   **Enteral nutrition:** initiate within 48 hours if unable to eat. Start at 8–10 kcal/kg/day, advance to 25 kcal/kg/day within 7 days.
*   **Parenteral nutrition:** delay 3–7 days if enteral not tolerated; use if caloric intake <60% of goal.

### Valve-associated Problems
*   **Thromboembolism risk:** AVR 1–2%/year; MVR 2–4%/year.
*   **Pregnancy with mechanical valve:** first trimester — warfarin if dose <5 mg/day or LMWH/UFH; second/third trimesters — warfarin ± aspirin; peripartum — UFH/LMWH.
*   **Prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE):** 1–2%/year. Early PVE (<60 days) — staphylococci, fungi, high mortality. Late PVE — coagulase-negative staph, *S. viridans*.

### Discharge Planning
*   **Medications:** aspirin (all CABG), P2Y12 inhibitors (ACS, stents, TAVR), warfarin (AF, mechanical valves), statins (all CAD patients), β-blockers, amiodarone (AF prophylaxis), ACE inhibitors (EF <40%, diabetes, CKD).
*   **Activity:** avoid lifting >10–15 lbs for 6 weeks; no driving for 6 weeks.
*   **Lifestyle modification:** weight loss, smoking cessation, glycemic control, blood pressure control.
*   **Follow-up:** arrange INR monitoring for warfarin patients. Contact patient at 30 days for outcomes data.
