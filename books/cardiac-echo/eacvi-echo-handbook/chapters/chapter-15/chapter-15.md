# Chapter 15. Diseases of the Aorta

<details class="med-details" open><summary>
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Acute Aortic Syndromes (AAS)**: Encompass classical aortic dissection (AD), intramural haematoma (IMH), and penetrating aortic ulcer (PAU). Classification systems include DeBakey (Type I: ascending + arch/descending, 60%; Type II: ascending only, 10%; Type III: descending only) and Stanford (Type A: involving ascending aorta, surgical emergency; Type B: descending aorta only, medically managed unless complicated). Time course: Acute ($\le 14$ days), Subacute (15–90 days), Chronic (> 90 days).
- **Echocardiographic Modalities**: Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the first-line bedside tool with high specificity for proximal ascending aorta, pericardial tamponade, LV function, and acute aortic regurgitation (AR). Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) has near-100% sensitivity and specificity, visualizing intimal tears, differential true vs. false lumen flow, coronary ostial involvement, and IMH/PAU, but has an acoustic "blind spot" in the distal ascending aorta / anterior arch due to tracheal air interposition.
- **Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm (AA)**: Normal upper limits in adults: aortic annulus $\le 31$ mm (16 mm/m²), sinus of Valsalva $\le 40$ mm (21 mm/m²), sinotubular junction $\le 36$ mm (19 mm/m²), tubular ascending aorta $\le 36$ mm (19 mm/m²), arch and descending aorta $< 36$ mm. Surgical thresholds: $\ge 55$ mm generally; $\ge 50$ mm for Marfan syndrome and BAV with risk factors; $\ge 45$ mm for Marfan with additional risk factors or rapid expansion ($\ge 5$ mm/year); $\ge 40$ mm for Loeys-Dietz syndrome with family history.
- **Aortic Atherosclerosis & Plaques**: Graded I to V by TOE: Grade I (normal), Grade II (mild intimal thickening 1–3.9 mm), Grade III (moderate atheroma $< 4$ mm), Grade IV (severe $\ge 4$ mm), Grade V (complex/severe with mobile components or ulceration, carrying high embolic stroke risk).
- **Sinus of Valsalva Aneurysm (SVA)**: Predominantly congenital, affecting right coronary sinus (65–85%), non-coronary sinus (20–30%), or left sinus (1–5%). Rupture presents as acute shunt with "windsock" appearance into RVOT/RV (from RCC) or RA (from NCC).

## 15.0 Introduction & Anatomy of the Thoracic Aorta</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Evaluation of the aorta is an integral, mandatory component of every comprehensive transthoracic (TTE) and transoesophageal (TOE) echocardiographic examination. The thoracic aorta is anatomically and functionally divided into distinct segments:

1. **Aortic Annulus (Segment I)**: Virtual ring formed by the basal attachments of the aortic valve leaflets within the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT).
2. **Aortic Root (Segment IIa)**: Extends from the basal ring to the sinotubular junction (STJ), encompassing the three sinuses of Valsalva and coronary ostia.
3. **Tubular Ascending Aorta (Segment IIb)**: Extends from the STJ to the origin of the brachiocephalic (innominate) artery.
4. **Aortic Arch (Segment III)**: Curves posteriorly and to the left over the pulmonary bifurcation, giving rise to the brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid artery, and left subclavian artery.
5. **Aortic Isthmus**: Critical transition zone between the arch and descending aorta just distal to the left subclavian artery, anchored by the ligamentum arteriosum (common site of deceleration tear).
6. **Descending Thoracic Aorta (Segments IVa & IVb)**: Courses through the posterior mediastinum down to the diaphragmatic hiatus (crura of the diaphragm).
7. **Abdominal Aorta (Segment Va)**: Infra-diaphragmatic segment extending to the iliac bifurcation.

### Standard Echocardiographic Views for Aorta
- **TTE Projections**:
  - Parasternal long-axis (PLAX): Standard view for annulus, sinuses of Valsalva, STJ, and proximal tubular ascending aorta (first 3–4 cm). High-intercostal right parasternal view allows visualization of mid-to-distal ascending aorta.
  - Parasternal short-axis (PSAX) at aortic valve level: Evaluates symmetry of sinuses of Valsalva and origins of coronary arteries.
  - Apical 5-chamber (A5C) and Apical long-axis (A3C): Demonstrates aortic root, LVOT alignment, and Doppler flow across the aortic valve.
  - Suprasternal notch view: Visualizes aortic arch, origin of great vessels, and proximal descending thoracic aorta.
  - Subcostal view: Visualizes abdominal aorta, coeliac trunk, and superior mesenteric artery.
- **TOE Projections**:
  - Mid-oesophageal long-axis view ($120^\circ$–$140^\circ$): Optimal alignment for precise measurement of annulus, sinuses of Valsalva, STJ, and proximal ascending aorta.
  - Mid-oesophageal short-axis view ($30^\circ$–$60^\circ$): Cross-section of root, aortic leaflets, and coronary ostia.
  - Upper oesophageal view ($0^\circ$ and $90^\circ$): Visualizes distal ascending aorta and aortic arch.
  - Descending aortic views ($0^\circ$ cross-section and $90^\circ$ longitudinal): Consecutive sweep from diaphragmatic hiatus up to the aortic isthmus.

## 15.1 Acute Aortic Syndromes (AAS): Pathophysiology & Classification</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Acute Aortic Syndromes (AAS) represent a spectrum of interrelated, life-threatening emergency medical conditions sharing acute aortic wall disruption and common clinical presentations:

1. **Classical Aortic Dissection (AD)**: Separation of the aortic media by blood under arterial pressure, initiated by a primary intimal tear. The dissection flap divides the lumen into a true lumen (lined by intima/endothelium) and a false lumen (lined by exposed media).
2. **Intramural Haematoma (IMH)**: Aortic wall haemorrhage within the media without an identifiable primary intimal entry tear or macroscopic two-lumen flow. Often arises from rupture of aortic wall *vasa vasorum*.
3. **Penetrating Aortic Ulcer (PAU)**: Ulceration of an atherosclerotic plaque that burrows through the internal elastic lamina into the aortic media, potentially triggering local intramural haematoma, pseudoaneurysm, or transmural rupture.

  <img src="images/fig-15-01.jpg" alt="AAS entities: AD, IMH, PAU by TOE" class="med-image" loading="lazy" /
  <figcaption class="med-caption">Fig. 15.1.1: Transoesophageal echocardiographic features of the three main AAS entities: Classical Aortic Dissection (AD), Intramural Haematoma (IMH), and Penetrating Aortic Ulcer (PAU).</figcaption>
</figure>

### Classification Systems (Stanford & DeBakey)

| Classification | Involving Ascending Aorta (AA) | Confined to Descending Aorta (DA) | Clinical Management Strategy |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stanford Type A** | Yes (involves ascending aorta regardless of tear site) | No | **Immediate Emergency Surgery** (high hourly mortality if untreated) |
| **Stanford Type B** | No (originates and confined distal to left subclavian artery) | Yes | **Medical Therapy / TEVAR** (surgery/TEVAR reserved for complicated cases: malperfusion, rupture, uncontrolled pain/hypertension) |
| **DeBakey Type I** | Originates in ascending aorta and propagates distally to arch and descending aorta ($60\%$) | No | Emergency surgical replacement of ascending aorta $\pm$ arch |
| **DeBakey Type II** | Originates in and remains strictly confined to ascending aorta ($10\%$) | No | Emergency surgical replacement of ascending aorta |
| **DeBakey Type III** | No ($30\%$) | Type IIIa (confined above diaphragm), Type IIIb (extends below diaphragm) | Medical management (beta-blockers, BP control) or TEVAR |

<figure class="med-figure" id="fig-15-02">
  <img src="images/fig-15-02.jpg" alt="Aortic dissection classification systems" class="med-image" loading="lazy" /
  <figcaption class="med-caption">Fig. 15.1.2: Stanford (Types A and B) and DeBakey (Types I, II, and III) classification systems of thoracic aortic dissection.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Time Course from Onset of Symptoms
- **Acute Phase**: $\le 14$ days (highest risk of fatal rupture, cardiac tamponade, and malperfusion).
- **Subacute Phase**: 15 to 90 days (healing, remodeling, or gradual aneurysm formation).
- **Chronic Phase**: $> 90$ days (endothelialization of false lumen, fibrotic thick intimal flap, chronic aneurysmal dilation).

### High-Risk Predisposing Clinical Features
- **Aortic Wall Vulnerability**: Systemic arterial hypertension ($75\%$), Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV), Coarctation of the aorta, Turner syndrome.
- **Heritable Thoracic Aortic Disease**: Marfan syndrome (*FBN1*), Loeys-Dietz syndrome (*TGFBR1/2*), Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (Type IV, *COL3A1*).
- **Inflammatory / Infectious**: Giant cell arteritis, Takayasu arteritis, Syphilis.
- **Iatrogenic & Traumatic**: Cardiac surgery (cannulation/cross-clamp sites), cardiac catheterization / TAVI, deceleration trauma.

## 15.2 Echocardiographic Diagnosis of Aortic Dissection: True vs. False Lumen</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Definitive diagnosis of aortic dissection by echocardiography requires visualization of an **intimal flap** that undulates independently of the aortic wall and divides the aorta into two flow lumina.

<figure class="med-figure" id="fig-15-04">
  <img src="images/fig-15-04.jpg" alt="TTE visualization of intimal flap" class="med-image" loading="lazy" /
  <figcaption class="med-caption">Fig. 15.1.4: TTE visualization of intimal flap. A: Parasternal long-axis view showing the intimal flap (arrow) undulating in the ascending aorta. B: Suprasternal notch view demonstrating flap propagation across the aortic arch.</figcaption>
</figure>

<figure class="med-figure" id="fig-15-05">
  <img src="images/fig-15-05.jpg" alt="TOE illustration of dissection flap" class="med-image" loading="lazy" /
  <figcaption class="med-caption">Fig. 15.1.5: Transoesophageal echocardiographic (TOE) high-resolution demonstration of the dissection flap (arrow) dividing the aortic root and tubular ascending aorta into true and false lumina.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Differentiation Between True and False Lumina

| Echocardiographic Parameter | True Lumen (TL) | False Lumen (FL) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cross-sectional Size** | Usually smaller ($TL < FL$) due to compression by pressurized false lumen | Usually larger ($FL > TL$), often eccentric |
| **Systolic Pulsatility** | Systolic expansion (systolic expansion with forward ejection) | Systolic compression (compressed during peak ventricular ejection) |
| **Colour Doppler Flow Direction** | Fast, pulsatile systolic anterograde laminar flow | Sluggish anterograde, retrograde, delayed, or absent flow |
| **Spontaneous Echo Contrast / Thrombus** | Absent | Frequently present; partial or complete thrombosis common |
| **Intimal Tear Jet Direction** | Entry site: flow exits from TL into FL ($TL \to FL$) | Re-entry site: flow re-enters from FL into TL ($FL \to TL$) |
| **Myocardial Contrast Echo** | Rapid, intense, early opacification | Delayed, faint, or absent contrast appearance |
| **Anatomical Boundary** | Inner surface lined by endothelial intima | Outer wall formed by adventitia/media; "cobweb" strands common |

### Communication Types Between Lumina
1. **Primary Entry Tear**: Primary site of intimal disruption where high-velocity forward jet enters from the true lumen into the false lumen ($TL \to FL$).
2. **Re-entry Tears**: Secondary fenestrations, typically located more distally, allowing decompression of the false lumen back into the true lumen ($FL \to TL$).
3. **Secondary Communications**: Multiple small fenestrations showing bidirectional, pendular flow during the cardiac cycle (systolic $TL \to FL$, diastolic $FL \to TL$).

## 15.3 Life-Threatening Complications of Type A Aortic Dissection</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Echocardiography must immediately detect secondary complications that determine immediate surgical urgency and mortality risk:

<figure class="med-figure" id="fig-15-06">
  <img src="images/fig-15-06.jpg" alt="AD complications diagnosed by TTE" class="med-image" loading="lazy" /
  <figcaption class="med-caption">Fig. 15.1.6: Major complications of Acute Type A Aortic Dissection diagnosed by TTE: A: Apical 5-chamber view with colour Doppler demonstrating severe acute aortic regurgitation. B: Parasternal long-axis view showing hemopericardium and cardiac tamponade. C: Right ventricular / inferior wall motion abnormalities due to right coronary artery (RCA) dissection.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Mechanisms of Acute Aortic Regurgitation (AR) in Type A AD
Acute AR occurs in up to $50\%$ of Type A dissections via one of three distinct mechanisms:
1. **Aortic Annular / STJ Dilation**: Dissection extends into the root, dilating the sinotubular junction and annulus, preventing central leaflet coaptation.
2. **Asymmetric Leaflet Prolapse**: The dissection flap extends beneath the commissural attachment of one aortic cusp (most frequently the non-coronary or right coronary cusp), causing leaflet flail or severe eccentric prolapse.
3. **Flap Intussusception / Prolapse**: The mobile, torn intimal flap itself prolapses through the aortic orifice into the LVOT during diastole, mechanically propping the leaflets open.

### Other Critical Complications
- **Pericardial Tamponade & Hemopericardium**: Transudation or rupture through the thin aortic adventitia into the pericardial reflection (which extends up the ascending aorta to the level of the innominate artery). Manifests as right ventricular diastolic collapse, inferior vena cava plethora, and severe pulsus paradoxus.
- **Coronary Malperfusion & Myocardial Infarction**: The dissection flap directly involves or shears off the coronary ostium (most commonly the Right Coronary Artery, $RCA > LCA$), or the false lumen expands and compresses the coronary artery from without. Manifests as new acute inferior/RV wall motion abnormalities.
- **Pleural Effusion & Mediastinal Rupture**: Free blood extravasation into the left pleural space (hemothorax) or mediastinum, signaling impending catastrophic demise.
- **Arch and Visceral Branch Malperfusion**: Static or dynamic obstruction of the brachiocephalic trunk, carotid arteries, coeliac trunk, superior mesenteric, or renal arteries.

## 15.4 Diagnostic Work-up Algorithm for Suspected AAS</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Selection of the primary imaging modality depends on patient hemodynamic stability, local equipment availability, and immediate surgical logistics:

<figure class="med-figure" id="fig-15-07">
  <img src="images/fig-15-07.jpg" alt="Diagnostic algorithm for acute aortic syndrome" class="med-image" loading="lazy" /
  <figcaption class="med-caption">Fig. 15.1.7: Proposed algorithm for the diagnostic work-up of suspected Acute Aortic Syndrome (AAS) in hemodynamically stable vs. unstable patients.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Clinical Workflow Principles
1. **Hemodynamically Unstable Patient**:
   - Immediate Bedside **TTE**: Identifies proximal flap (Type A), cardiac tamponade, acute severe AR, and RV failure.
   - If TTE is unequivocally positive for Type A AD in an unstable patient, the patient is transferred directly to the operating room; **intra-operative TOE** is performed on the operating table after induction of general anesthesia.
   - If TTE is negative or inconclusive in an unstable patient, bedside **TOE** must be performed immediately in the resuscitation area.
2. **Hemodynamically Stable Patient**:
   - Contrast-enhanced **Computed Tomography (CT) Angiography** is the preferred first-line modality, providing complete volumetric imaging of the entire aorta, supra-aortic branches, iliofemoral access, and visceral perfusion.
   - **TOE** is complementary: clarifies dynamic valve leaflet motion, coronary ostial flow, intimal entry tears, and differential velocities.

## 15.5 Intramural Haematoma (IMH) & Penetrating Aortic Ulcer (PAU)</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Intramural Haematoma (IMH)
- **Pathology**: Haemorrhage into the media without an identifiable primary entry tear. Accounting for $10–25\%$ of all AAS.
- **Echo Diagnostic Criteria**:
  - Circular or crescentic thickening of the aortic wall $> 5$ mm.
  - Absence of an undulating intimal flap.
  - Absence of internal colour Doppler flow within the thickened wall.
  - Smooth, curvilinear luminal contour (distinguishing it from jagged atherosclerotic plaque).
  - Internal displacement of calcified intima toward the center of the lumen.
- **Clinical Evolution**:
  - Progression to overt classical dissection ($25–40\%$).
  - Aneurysmal dilatation or rupture ($15–20\%$).
  - Spontaneous complete resolution ($10–30\%$).
- **Management**: Type A IMH requires urgent surgical intervention; Type B IMH is initially managed medically with intensive blood pressure lowering, with TEVAR indicated for high-risk signs (maximum aortic diameter $> 50$ mm, hematoma thickness $> 10$ mm, focal intimal disruption).

<figure class="med-figure" id="fig-15-08">
  <img src="images/fig-15-08.jpg" alt="TOE illustration of intramural haematoma" class="med-image" loading="lazy" /
  <figcaption class="med-caption">Fig. 15.1.8: Transoesophageal echocardiogram showing characteristic crescentic aortic wall thickening (> 5 mm) of an acute Intramural Haematoma (arrow) without internal flow or intimal flap.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Penetrating Aortic Ulcer (PAU)
- **Pathology**: Deep atherosclerotic ulceration eroding through the internal elastic membrane into the aortic media. Most common in elderly patients with extensive systemic atherosclerosis and severe smoking history. Typically located in the middle-to-distal descending thoracic aorta ($> 90\%$).
- **Echo Criteria**:
  - Focal crater-like out-pouching of the aortic lumen extending through a thickened, calcified atherosclerotic plaque into the media.
  - Often surrounded by localized intramural hematoma.
- **Risk of Rupture**: Substantially higher risk of transmural perforation and pseudoaneurysm formation ($30–40\%$) compared to uncomplicated Type B dissection. TEVAR is the primary intervention of choice.

### Postoperative Follow-up & Pseudoaneurysm Formation
Lifelong imaging surveillance is mandatory following surgical or endovascular repair of AAS to detect:
- Persisting patent false lumen in the downstream native aorta.
- Suture-line dehiscence and **anastomotic pseudoaneurysm formation**.
- Graft infection or endoleak (Types I to IV after TEVAR).

## 15.6 Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm (TAA): Dimensions, Measurement & Surgical Thresholds</summary>
<div class="details-content">

An aortic aneurysm is defined as a permanent localized dilatation of the artery having at least a $50\%$ increase in diameter compared to the expected normal dimension for that anatomical site and body habitus.

### Echocardiographic Measurement Rules
- **Methodology**: Measurements should be made perpendicular to the long axis of the vessel.
- **Timing**: Measurements are strictly taken at **end-diastole** (synchronous with the peak of the R-wave or onset of the QRS complex).
- **Edge Conventions**:
  - **TTE**: Conventionally measured from **leading edge to leading edge** (L-E to L-E) in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) and EACVI chamber quantification guidelines.
  - **TOE, CT, and MRI**: Measured from **inner edge to inner edge** (I-E to I-E). Inner-to-inner measurements yield values $2–4$ mm smaller than leading-to-leading measurements.

### Upper Limits of Normal Aortic Dimensions (Adults 20–74 Years)

| Aortic Level | Men: Absolute (mm) | Men: Indexed (mm/m²) | Women: Absolute (mm) | Women: Indexed (mm/m²) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Aortic Annulus** | $\le 31$ mm | $\le 16$ mm/m² | $\le 26$ mm | $\le 16$ mm/m² |
| **Sinuses of Valsalva** | $\le 40$ mm | $\le 21$ mm/m² | $\le 36$ mm | $\le 21$ mm/m² |
| **Sinotubular Junction (STJ)** | $\le 36$ mm | $\le 19$ mm/m² | $\le 32$ mm | $\le 19$ mm/m² |
| **Tubular Ascending Aorta** | $\le 36$ mm | $\le 19$ mm/m² | $\le 32$ mm | $\le 19$ mm/m² |
| **Aortic Arch** | $< 36$ mm | — | $< 36$ mm | — |
| **Descending Thoracic Aorta** | $< 36$ mm | — | $< 36$ mm | — |

### Surgical Timing Thresholds for Thoracic Aortic Aneurysms

| Aortic Diameter Threshold | Clinical Indications & Patient Subsets |
| :--- | :--- |
| **$> 60$ mm** | General surgical indication for descending thoracic aorta and aortic arch |
| **$\ge 55$ mm** | **General indication** for ascending aorta aneurysm in patients without genetic connective tissue disorders or bicuspid valve risk factors. Also threshold for abdominal aortic aneurysms (TEVAR / open repair). |
| **$\ge 50$ mm** | **Marfan syndrome** and other heritable connective tissue diseases.<br>• **Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV)** with additional risk factors (family history of dissection, coarctation, systemic hypertension, expansion rate $\ge 5$ mm/year).<br>• Tricuspid aortic valve with severe aortic regurgitation.<br>• Rapid aortic expansion rate ($\ge 5$ mm/year). |
| **$\ge 45$ mm** | • **Marfan syndrome with high risk factors**: family history of dissection, severe AR/MR, desire for pregnancy, or growth $> 3$ mm/year.<br>• **Concomitant aortic valve surgery**: patient undergoing surgical AVR for bicuspid or severe tricuspid valve disease with ascending aorta $\ge 45$ mm. |
| **$\ge 40$ mm** | • **Loeys-Dietz syndrome** with documented family history of early dissection or marked craniofacial features (lower threshold justified by aggressive natural history). |

## 15.7 Traumatic Aortic Injury & Aortic Atherosclerosis</summary>
<div class="details-content">

### Traumatic Injury of the Thoracic Aorta
- **Mechanism**: High-energy blunt deceleration trauma (motor vehicle collision, high-altitude fall).
- **Anatomical Site**: In $> 90\%$ of survivors, the injury occurs at the **aortic isthmus**, where the mobile aortic arch transitions into the fixed descending aorta tethered by the ligamentum arteriosum and intercostal arteries.
- **Echocardiographic Spectrum**:
  - Intimal flap and small subintimal hematoma.
  - Circumferential wall disruption / pseudoaneurysm.
  - Complete transmural transection with mediastinal hematoma.
- **Role of TOE**: Highly sensitive, rapid bedside assessment in the trauma resuscitation room or ICU when transport to CT is precluded by critical instability.

### Aortic Atherosclerosis & Embolic Risk Grading
Atherosclerotic disease of the thoracic aorta is an established, independent risk factor for systemic embolic events, including ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), and visceral or lower-extremity embolization. Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) provides gold-standard resolution for plaque morphology and mobility.

| Grade | Severity | Detailed Morphological Description |
| :---: | :--- | :--- |
| **Grade I** | Normal / Minimal | Normal intimal surface or minimal intimal thickening ($< 1$ mm). |
| **Grade II** | Mild | Diffuse intimal thickening between $1.0$ and $3.9$ mm without distinct atheromatous protrusion into the lumen. |
| **Grade III** | Moderate | Discrete, sessile atheromatous plaque protruding $< 4.0$ mm into the aortic lumen. |
| **Grade IV** | Severe (Large) | Protruding atheromatous plaque $\ge 4.0$ mm in thickness, without mobile components. |
| **Grade V** | Complex (Mobile / Ulcerated) | Any atheroma containing mobile, pedunculated components (thrombus/fibrin strand oscillating in bloodstream) or ulcerated plaque regardless of absolute thickness. Carries the highest embolic stroke hazard. |

<summary>
## 15.8 Sinus of Valsalva Aneurysms (SVA)</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Aneurysms of the sinus of Valsalva (SVA) are rare anatomical abnormalities characterized by congenital or acquired weakness in the aortic media between the aortic valve annulus and the sinotubular junction.

<figure class="med-figure" id="fig-15-13">
  <img src="images/fig-15-13.jpg" alt="Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm and rupture" class="med-image" loading="lazy" /
  <figcaption class="med-caption">Fig. 15.5.1: Echocardiographic evaluation of Sinus of Valsalva Aneurysm (SVA): A: Short-axis view demonstrating saccular aneurysmal dilation of the right coronary sinus. B: Colour Doppler demonstrating perforation and high-velocity left-to-right shunt rupture into the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT).</figcaption>
</figure>

### Etiology & Distribution
- **Congenital ($> 90\%$)**: Separation of the aortic media from the fibrous annulus fibrosis of the aortic valve.
- **Acquired**: Atherosclerosis, infectious endocarditis, tertiary syphilis, Behçet disease, systemic trauma, or previous aortic valve surgery.
- **Anatomical Distribution**:
  - **Right Coronary Sinus**: $65–85\%$ (most frequent; ruptures predominantly into RV or RVOT).
  - **Non-Coronary Sinus**: $20–30\%$ (ruptures predominantly into Right Atrium).
  - **Left Coronary Sinus**: $1–5\%$ (rare; ruptures into Left Atrium, LVOT, or pericardium).

### Clinical & Echocardiographic Presentation
1. **Unruptured SVA**:
   - Usually asymptomatic; discovered incidentally.
   - May cause mass effect: RVOT dynamic obstruction, coronary compression (angina/infarction), or conduction disturbances (AV block due to compression of the bundle of His in non-coronary SVA).
   - TTE/TOE demonstrates thin-walled, sac-like expansion protruding outward from the sinus into adjacent cardiac chambers.
2. **Ruptured SVA**:
   - Acute clinical catastrophe: sudden onset of severe dyspnoea, chest pain, and continuous machinery murmur with progressive right heart failure.
   - **"Windsock" Deformity**: Elongated, tubular aneurysmal sac with a terminal perforation protruding into the recipient low-pressure right heart chamber.
   - **Continuous High-Velocity Jet**: Continuous systolic and diastolic left-to-right turbulent shunt demonstrated by colour and continuous-wave Doppler ($TL \to RV/RA$).
   - Associated Anomalies: Ventricular septal defect (supracristal VSD in $40–50\%$), bicuspid aortic valve ($10–15\%$), and progressive aortic regurgitation.
   - Immediate surgical correction is indicated upon diagnosis of rupture.
