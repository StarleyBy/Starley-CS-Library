## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Rationale and Principles of Native Aortic Valve Preservation
* **Preservation over Replacement:** Aortic valve repair (AVr) and valve-sparing aortic root replacement (VSARR) aim to restore normal aortic root anatomy and valve competence. Sparing native valve tissue minimizes prosthesis-related complications (anticoagulation-related bleeding, thromboembolism, prosthetic endocarditis, and premature structural valve degeneration), significantly improving long-term survival and quality of life (QoL).
* **Optimal Target Populations:** Benefits extend across pediatric and young adults, patients with bicuspid aortic valves (BAV), and individuals with heritable thoracic aortic diseases (HTADs, e.g., Marfan syndrome). AVr avoids or substantially delays the need for prosthetic replacement.

### Functional Classification of Aortic Regurgitation (El Khoury / Boodhwani)
* **Functional Aortic Annulus (FAA):** The aortic root is a dynamic functional entity bounded proximally by the virtual basal ring (VBR, the plane through cusp nadirs) and distally by the sinotubular junction (STJ). The ventriculo-aortic junction (VAJ) is the anatomical myocardial-arterial junction.
* **Type I (Normal cusp motion):** Due to FAA dilatation or perforation. Type Ia: STJ enlargement; Type Ib: Sinus of Valsalva and STJ dilatation with commissural displacement and cusp tethering; Type Ic: VBR/annular dilatation; Type Id: Cusp perforation without FAA lesion.
* **Type II (Excessive cusp motion / Prolapse):** Cusp free margin elongates or drops below the annular plane, resulting in an eccentric regurgitant jet. In severe prolapse, a transverse fold crosses the cusp belly; flail represents complete cusp eversion into the LVOT.
* **Type III (Cusp restriction / Retraction):** Caused by fibrosis, calcification, or thickening (rheumatic, degenerative). Generally deemed non-repairable due to high recurrence rates.

### Bicuspid Aortic Valve (BAV) Phenotypes and Repairability
* **2021 International Consensus Classification:**
 1. *Fused BAV (90%–95%):* 3 sinuses, 2 functional cusps, congenital fusion (most often with a raphe). Subtypes: Right-Left (70%–80%), Right-Noncoronary (20%–30%), Left-Noncoronary (3%–6%).
 2. *Two-sinus BAV (5%–7%):* 2 sinuses, 2 symmetrical cusps without raphe (laterolateral or anteroposterior).
 3. *Partial-fusion BAV (forme fruste):* Small commissural fusion (< 50%), "tricuspid-like" with 3 functional commissures.
* **Anatomical Determinants of Repairability:**
 - *Commissural Orientation (CO):* Type A (Symmetric: 160°–180°) is the optimal substrate for durable repair. Type B (Asymmetric: 140°–159°) has intermediate durability. Type C (Very asymmetric: 120°–139°) carries high failure rates and often necessitates complex strategies or replacement.
 - *Cusp Geometry:* Geometric height (gH) must be $\ge 20\text{ mm}$ for the non-fused cusp in BAV (and $\ge 17\text{ mm}$ in TAV). Effective height (eH) target is $9\text{--}10\text{ mm}$. Coaptation length (cL) must be $\ge 4\text{ mm}$ above the VBR plane.
 - *Pathology:* Non-full-thickness raphe fibrosis/calcification can be shaved or resected; diffuse calcification, cusp retraction (gH $\le 16\text{ mm}$ in TAV, $\le 19\text{ mm}$ in BAV), multiple large fenestrations ($> 10\text{ mm}$), and large defects ($> 1\text{ cm}^2$) strongly contraindicate repair.

### BAV Aortopathy Phenotypes and Replacement Thresholds
* **Aortopathy Patterns:** Ascending phenotype (~ 70%, tubular ascending dilatation beyond STJ); Root phenotype (~ 20%, sinus of Valsalva dilatation, male predominance, higher dissection risk); Extended phenotype (root + ascending ± arch).
* **Surgical Cutoffs:** Elective ascending/root replacement indicated at $\ge 55\text{ mm}$; $> 52\text{ mm}$ in low-risk patients; $\ge 50\text{ mm}$ with risk factors (root phenotype, rapid growth, family history); and $\ge 45\text{ mm}$ when concomitant AV surgery is planned. In dedicated repair centers, root replacement at lower diameters ($40\text{--}45\text{ mm}$) is performed to restore root geometry and valve symmetry.

### Preoperative Multimodal Imaging Evaluation
* **Transthoracic & 3D Transesophageal Echocardiography:** Systematic assessment of valve phenotype, cusp mobility, calcification, gH, eH, cL, annular/root dimensions, and jet direction. Severe AR indications: symptoms, or asymptomatic with indexed LVESD $> 22\text{ mm/m}^2$ or indexed LVESV $> 45\text{ ml/m}^2$.
* **Electrocardiogram-Gated Cardiac CT:** Gold standard for 3D root geometry, annular sizing, exact commissural orientation, calcium localization, sinus symmetry, and coronary artery anomalies (e.g., anomalous origin of RCA from left sinus or Cx from right sinus; short left main stem).

### Surgical Techniques: Annuloplasty and Root Replacement
* **Aortic Annuloplasty Principles:** Annular dilatation ($> 25\text{--}26\text{ mm}$) is present in most chronic AR cases and must be corrected. Subcommissural plication stitches are obsolete due to redilatation and late failure. Annuloplasty must be circumferential and subvalvular.
* **Annuloplasty Modalities:** External ring annuloplasty (Dacron/Teflon strip or dedicated expansible Lansac ring placed subannularly 2–3 mm below nadirs) or internal rigid geometric ring (HAART ring; 2:3 elliptical for TAV, circular 180° for BAV). STJ stabilization is required to restore normal annulus-to-STJ ratio (1:1.2).
* **VSARR Techniques:** Reimplantation (David technique) and Remodelling with circumferential annuloplasty (Yacoub + ring) demonstrate equivalent mid-to-long-term survival and freedom from reoperation when post-repair AR is $\le 1$.
* **Cusp Repair:** Central free-margin plication is the primary reliable technique for cusp prolapse. Autologous pericardial patches are associated with higher long-term failure rates (especially in BAV) and should be reserved for minor fenestration closure or limited defect repair.

### Postoperative Quality Control and Operating Room Echocardiography
* **On-Table Post-CPB Quality Criteria:** Physiological loading conditions; residual AR none/trivial or central grade 1; transvalvular mean gradient $\le 15\text{ mmHg}$ and peak gradient $\le 30\text{ mmHg}$; coaptation length $\ge 4\text{ mm}$ located entirely above the VBR plane; effective height $9\text{--}10\text{ mm}$.
* **Criteria for Immediate Second CPB Run (Back on Pump):** Residual AR $>$ grade 1; any eccentric jet $>$ trivial; cL $< 4\text{ mm}$; eH $< 9\text{ mm}$; mean transvalvular gradient $> 15\text{ mmHg}$; peak gradient $> 30\text{ mmHg}$; significant systolic restriction (SRCM) or residual cusp prolapse; annular dimension $\ge 26\text{ mm}$.

### Postoperative Medical Therapy and Secondary Prevention
* **Strict Blood Pressure Control:** Target systolic BP $< 120\text{--}130\text{ mmHg}$ using ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or beta-blockers. Beta-blockers remain standard in Marfan syndrome to reduce aortic root wall shear stress and expansion rate.
* **Antithrombotic Therapy:** Low-dose acetylsalicylic acid ($75\text{--}100\text{ mg/day}$) for 3 months postoperatively in patients without indication for oral anticoagulation.
* **Endocarditis Prophylaxis:** Recommended for high-risk procedures in patients with prior AVr involving prosthetic rings/materials or history of infective endocarditis.

### Center Standards and Learning Curve
* **Aortic Valve Centers of Excellence:** Dedicated multidisciplinary team (cardiac surgeons, adult/pediatric cardiologists, advanced imaging echocardiographers, cardiovascular radiologists, geneticists).
* **Institutional Volume:** Minimum center volume of $\ge 30$ root procedures/year; institutional learning curve of 40–60 cases per surgical team to achieve stability, safety, and durability.

## 1. INTRODUCTION</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic valve (AV) reparative surgery covers a wide field where health care professionals encounter diverse causes and mechanisms of aortic regurgitation (AR) across different patient ages. Several surgical techniques are currently available. Presently, most AVs that could be spared are often replaced. The challenge and goal are to identify the common principles behind various reparative techniques and apply them appropriately to the specific mechanisms causing AR. This consensus document brings together experts in aortic valve repair (AVr) to address this challenge by reviewing available data and offering insights into several unresolved questions.

The consensus is designed to benefit clinicians (both paediatric and adult), echocardiography sonographers, cardiovascular advanced imaging specialists, and cardiovascular surgeons. In the absence of strong evidence, most patient care suggestions are based on the collective experience of the authors.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 1.1 Definition, aetiology, epidemiology, and rationale of AVr</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic valve repair includes all surgical procedures that restore or preserve the function of the native AV. The AVr operations can be categorized, based on root involvement, into valve-sparing aortic root replacement (VSARR) and primary AVr (without root replacement). According to recent evidence, the patient groups who benefit from AVr have gradually broadened to include a wider age range and specific conditions, such as heritable thoracic aortic diseases (HTADs), and bicuspid aortic valves (BAVs). 

Patients referred for AVr can be broadly divided into 3 groups: those with root/ascending aorta disease; patients with isolated severe AR; and those with a combination of both. The aetiologies of AR and root/ascending aorta disease are diverse and vary with age at presentation. Aortic regurgitation caused by rheumatic disease is generally not suitable for repair and is thus excluded from the consensus document. In early life (infants and young adults), AR is linked mainly to congenital valve malformations such as unicuspid (UAV) and BAV, whereas root/ascending aneurysms are primarily associated with HTADs and BAV-related aortopathy. Later in life (adults and older individuals), AR and root/ascending aorta diseases are mainly acquired and related to conditions like hypertension, atherosclerosis, or idiopathic medial degeneration. 

According to population-based studies, the prevalence of moderate to severe AR increases with age, and it has been reported as high as 2% in individuals aged $\ge 75$ years. Reports from a large population-based cohort of newborns documented a prevalence of any grade of AR up to 1.3%. Bicuspid aortic valve is the most common congenital cardiac anomaly and is generally considered to affect between 0.5% and 1.4% of the population. Although BAVs more commonly degenerate towards stenosis, at first presentation, 30% of patients with a BAV have moderate or severe AR. 

Among patients referred for clinically significant AR, those with BAV are younger compared to patients with tricuspid aortic valves (TAV) (mean age of $46 \pm 14$ and $67 \pm 14$ years, respectively). The patients with a UAV represent the youngest group at the time of the operation and are most often referred for stenosis or mixed lesions. 

The epidemiology of AR and BAV varies between the sexes: AR is more common in males (13% vs 8.5%), and BAV occurs 3 times more frequently in men than in women. Recent evidence indicates that the pathophysiological mechanisms leading to AR and their clinical impacts also differ between the sexes, which opens the door to personalized surgical indications. 

The epidemiology of thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAAs) is challenging to determine because the disease often presents without symptoms. The overall incidence of TAA ranges from 5 to 10 per 100,000 person-years, with a probable increase due to more widespread use of computed tomography (CT) scans and the ageing population. About 60% of TAAs involve the aortic root and/or ascending aorta. 

The AV function relies on the correct geometric relationships among the cusps and root components. The repair procedures aim to restore the normal diameters of the functional unit consisting of the cusps and the root. However, the high-pressure system and the quality of valve tissue make AVr challenging. The living nature of the cusps makes creating a durable tissue substitute particularly difficult. Therefore, the amount and quality of the cusp tissue are critical factors in determining the feasibility and long-term success of the repair.

Repaired or spared native valve tissue reduces prosthesis-related complications and ultimately enhances both the quality and quantity of life for patients. Native cusp tissue is less susceptible to developing endocarditis and its complications compared to prosthetic material, resulting in an overall survival benefit, although no specific systematic comparative study between repair and replacement has been conducted so far.

Understanding AVr principles and the standardization of AVr procedures enables cardiac surgeons to extend AVr to a broader range of patients. In particular, young patients require lifelong management for AV and root pathology. It is clear that AVr is the optimal choice to either avoid or delay the need for replacing the AV with a prosthesis.

## 2. METHODOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

An expert task force thoroughly evaluated the evidence and assessed the safety and effectiveness of AVr procedures to determine their role as a treatment option for various forms of AV disease across different patient groups. The aim was to critically evaluate the available evidence and produce consensus statements to guide health care providers, policy-makers, and researchers involved in managing AV disease. This task force, composed of leading specialists in the field, was appointed by the European Association of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS) Committee for Practice Guidelines following the procedures outlined in the EACTS Methodology Manual for Clinical Practice Documents. The final document acts as the official stance of EACTS on this subject, seeking to promote standardized, evidence-based clinical decision-making.

The composition of the task force emphasized diversity, transparency, and expertise, ensuring a balanced representation of different perspectives. All members were required to declare any industrial or external affiliations to uphold integrity. Conflict of interest declarations were submitted at the start of the project and updated as needed throughout the drafting process. Once the scope and content were defined, members without relevant conflicts of interest were assigned to draft specific sections based on their expertise. A systematic literature review was carried out following the PICOT framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Time), with searches conducted across MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Library (see search strings in the supplementary material). The review was limited to English-language studies focusing on adult cardiac surgery.

Based on the literature review, consensus statements and accompanying textual explanations were developed following the methods outlined in the EACTS Methodology Manual. Each section was collaboratively drafted, with expert discussions shaping key statements during task force meetings. Given the variability in economic factors and the absence of cost-effectiveness data, this document focuses solely on best clinical practices rather than on economic assessments. To ensure rigour and transparency, an anonymous voting system was used. Task force members reviewed the drafted statements, figures, and algorithms from prior meetings, providing their votes along with comments. A minimum of 75% agreement was required for statement approval, with at least 80% participation necessary for the voting process to be considered valid.

The final draft underwent internal validation by the writing committee and was subsequently subject to external peer review by anonymous reviewers chosen by the Editorial Board of the *European Journal of Cardio-Thoracic Surgery* and the EACTS Committee for Practice Guidelines. The document was then formally endorsed by the EACTS governing bodies.

## 3. AETIOLOGY OF AORTIC REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 3.1 The functional classification of valve dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

The functional classification of AR identifies 3 different mechanisms based on the cusp function and considers the aortic root as a functional entity—the functional aortic annulus (FAA) ([Fig. 1](#fig-1)). The classification system includes all AR types and guides the repair techniques employed. This system revolves around the concept that the AV, like the mitral valve, consists of the annulus and the valve cusps.

<span id="fig-1"></span>![](fig1_functional_classification_ar.jpg)

**Figure 1.** Functional Classification of Aortic Regurgitation with an Explanation of Disease Mechanisms. *Abbreviations:* AR, aortic regurgitation; FAA, functional aortic annulus.

The aortic root has 2 boundaries: The virtual basal ring (VBR), proximally, and the sinotubular junction (STJ), distally. The VBR, or echocardiographic "annulus", is a virtual planar surface passing through the nadir of the aortic cusps. From a surgical perspective, it is the location where the annuloplasty sutures are placed. The VBR is a distinct entity and should not be confused with the ventriculo-aortic junction (VAJ), which is more precisely defined as the myocardial-arterial junction. This point is the anatomical site where the ventricular myocardium merges with the wall of the aortic sinuses, typically found only in the right and left sinuses and their interleaflet triangles ([Fig. 2](#fig-2)). Both the VAJ and the VBR can be mutually considered the lower ring of the FAA and are often referred to as the "aortic annulus". The STJ connects the sinuses to the ascending aorta. The crown-shaped, semilunar attachments of the aortic cusps serve as a connection between the VBR and the STJ.

<span id="fig-2"></span>![](fig2_functional_aortic_annulus.jpg)

**Figure 2.** Internal View of the Functional Aortic Annulus and External Dissection Boundaries of the Subvalvular Plane. The aortic root has been opened with the cusps removed. The black dotted line indicates the sinotubular junction. The white dotted line indicates the virtual basal ring, which is a distinct structure compared to the myocardial-arterial junction (also known as ventriculo-aortic junction), marked by the shorter black dotted line. The circles denote the myocardium at the base of the coronary sinuses. The triangles represent the interleaflet triangles.

Three major AR mechanisms can be identified: 

1. **Type I: Aortic regurgitation associated with normal cusp motion.** Type I AR results mainly from lesions of the FAA, usually causing a central jet, with:
 - *Type Ia:* stemming from STJ enlargement;
 - *Type Ib:* stems from the sinus of Valsalva and STJ dilatation (extended phenotype), both of which lead to cusp tethering by displaced commissures;
 - *Type Ic:* caused by VBR dilatation;
 - *Type Id:* occurs from cusp perforation without a primary FAA lesion.
2. **Type II: Aortic regurgitation resulting from excessive cusp motion**, leading to an eccentric jet. In cases of severe prolapse, the cusp edge may cross the annular plane. It can resemble a transverse fold of the cusp, causing a partial distal prolapse ([Fig. 3](#fig-3)) but may also involve the entire cusp. A flail is characterized by the aortic side of the cusp passing into the left ventricular outflow tract, indicating complete eversion of the cusp.
3. **Type III: Aortic regurgitation resulting from cusp restriction**, producing either a central or an eccentric jet, which may occur in degenerative or rheumatic valvular disease due to calcification, thickening, and fibrosis of the cusps. Type III AR is generally considered non-repairable because of the risk of AR recurrence after repair.

### 3.2 Aetiology of AR in TAV</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic regurgitation in TAV can result from primary valve abnormalities (intrinsic changes of the AV) or from alterations in the aortic root dimensions. In this context, it is easier to classify the AR based on its intrinsic mechanism, whether cusp or root-related (see [Fig. 1](#fig-1)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 3.2.1 Cusp mechanisms</summary><div class="details-content">

A tricuspid aortic valve with a normal aortic root develops AR due to primary cusp abnormalities. The most common mechanism of AR in TAVs is cusp prolapse, mainly from the right cusp, secondary to degeneration. Cusp retraction and perforation can also explain AR in TAVs, as well as mixed defects with prolapse, retraction, and/or perforation. In a recent study, the prevalence of AV prolapse in patients with AR was 45.9%, followed by 24.2% for cusp retraction, 17.8% for cusp perforation, and 11.4% for mixed defects. 

Aetiologies of primary cusp abnormalities leading to AR in TAVs include the following: 
* **Age-related degenerative changes:** Cusp calcification and prolapse.
* **Infective endocarditis:** Perforation of the cusp and formation of vegetation.
* **Rheumatic heart disease:** Cusp fibrosis, thickening, and retraction, leading to chronic AR.
* **Other less common causes:**
 - Autoimmune diseases (Takayasu's arteritis, ankylosing spondylitis, systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis);
 - Trauma;
 - Iatrogenic;
 - Post-balloon dilatation;
 - Mediastinal radiation;
 - Drug-induced valvular heart disease (for migraine treatment).

Successful surgical repair of AR in TAVs requires a comprehensive preoperative echocardiographic assessment to identify the specific mechanism of AR in each case. The particular type of AR mechanism and the geometric and effective valve heights are key determinants of the feasibility of surgical repair and long-term outcomes. Understanding the exact mechanism of AR is vital for selecting the correct surgical intervention and improving patient outcomes.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 3.2.2 Aortic and root mechanisms</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic dilatation is also linked with AR in TAVs. Dilatation of the ascending aorta at the STJ causes outward displacement of the AV commissures, resulting in inadequate valve coaptation that leads to AR. In aortic root dilatation, an increase in valve cusp size as a compensatory response is not enough to prevent regurgitation; therefore, AR occurs. Additionally, changes in aortic root structure can also raise stress on the valve cusps, resulting in AR. Annular dilatation may also be present and is a significant cause of AR.

Different pathophysiological mechanisms have been described to explain root/ascending aorta dilatation in patients with TAVs: 
* Tunica media smooth muscle cell apoptosis and inflammatory cascade abnormalities (in contrast to BAVs, where tissue repair mechanisms are compromised);
* Disruptions to connective tissue integrity.

Causes of aortic dilatation that lead to AR in TAVs include:
* **Genetic conditions:**
 - Connective tissue abnormalities: cystic medial necrosis and matrix disarrangement, isolated or associated with Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, Turner syndrome; 
 - Angiotensin-converting enzyme insertion/deletion polymorphism: impacts aortic dimensions and is a risk factor for TAA; 
 - GATA5 gene expression and eNOS gene expression: alterations in AV morphology and size; 
 - Pathogenic variants in ACTA2, PRKG1, MYH11, MYLK and LOX genes: association with non-syndromic HTAD. 
* **Infectious conditions:** syphilitic aortitis. 
* **Autoimmune diseases:** ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, ulcerative colitis, giant cell arteritis, Behçet's syndrome, Takayasu arteritis.
* **Trauma, aortic dissection.** 
* **Hypertension.** 
* **Acute presentations** are typically caused by infective endocarditis or aortic dissection extending into the aortic root.
* **Autograft dilatation** following Ross procedure or root dilatation after a switch operation.

Aortic regurgitation associated with dilatation of the aortic root or ascending aorta in TAVs results from structural and histopathological abnormalities.

### 3.3 Aetiology of AR in the BAV</summary><div class="details-content">

The BAV is the most common congenital heart defect. The AR mechanisms in patients with BAV involve combinations of cusp prolapse and aortic annulus and/or STJ dilatation. Patients with BAV develop AR approximately a decade earlier than aortic stenosis, and when compared with patients with TAV with AR, they are almost 2 decades younger. 

Most BAVs occur sporadically (about 90%), whereas 8% are familial and 2% are syndromic. Regardless of the underlying genetic cause, BAV is highly heritable, mainly as an autosomal dominant trait with reduced penetrance and variable expressivity. The prevalence of BAV among screened family members of parents with BAV is estimated at 7.3%, which is 10 times higher than in the general population. Therefore, echocardiographic screening for first-degree relatives is strongly advised.

Bicuspid aortic valve is more common in males than in females, with a ratio of 2–3:1. It can be syndromic, as seen in Turner syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, and Shone's complex and may exhibit other anatomical abnormalities such as patent ductus arteriosus, coronary anomalies, ventricular septal defect and coarctation of the aorta. Given the high prevalence of aortic coarctation and its strong association with BAV, patients with coarctation of the aorta require careful, lifelong follow-up.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 3.3.1 Cusp mechanisms</summary><div class="details-content">

Different BAV morphologies may arise from various embryological mechanisms: formation of three instead of four endocardial cushions during preseptation, leading to laterolateral two-sinus BAV (and possibly right-non-coronary cusp fusion); abnormal outflow tract septation, resulting in antero-posterior two-sinus BAV (and possibly right-left cusp fusion); and defective cushion remodelling during excavation, which can produce all fused BAV anatomical types. 

The AR almost invariably begins with inadequate cusp coaptation, driven by the anatomical variations inherent in different bicuspid valve phenotypes. Variations in cusp fusion patterns and symmetry can significantly influence the progression of AR from mild to severe. Right-noncoronary cusp fusion is associated with more rapid progression of AR. Annular dilatation is commonly present and often underlies the progression of AR, contributing to altered valve geometry and promoting cusp prolapse. Most frequently, it is the conjoined cusp that is prolapsing, yet both cusps may prolapse, especially in long-standing AR.

</div></details>

#### 3.3.2 Aortic and root mechanisms—BAV aortopathy</summary><div class="details-content">

Several factors contribute to the development and progression of BAV aortopathy ([Fig. 4](#fig-4)). It is difficult to predict the phenotypic characteristics of patients who will develop aortic aneurysms and the exact rate of aneurysm progression.

<span id="fig-4"></span>![](fig4_bav_aneurysm_factors.jpg)

**Figure 4.** Factors Contributing to the Development and Progression of a BAV-related Aneurysm. *Abbreviations:* BAV, bicuspid aortic valve; MMP, matrix metalloproteinase; TIMP, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases.

* **Genetic causes.** Many genes have been implicated in the aetiology of TAA BAV aortopathy. FBN1 and transforming growth factor beta receptor 1/2 have been involved in syndromic TAA, and ACTA2, MYH11, FLNA, and SMAD3 have been involved in non-syndromic TAA, but none have been conclusively proven to cause BAV aortopathy. Similarly, linkage studies have demonstrated novel associations between BAV and non-syndromic familial TAA with chromosomal regions 5q, 13q, and 18q. More recent studies have shown an association between patients with BAV with GATA5 and NOTCH1 mutations. However, only a small proportion of patients with BAV with TAA carry these mutations. Therefore, it is apparent that BAV-associated aortopathy is a polygenic disease.
* **Altered aortic haemodynamics.** Aortic dilatation in BAV occurs primarily at the ascending and aortic root levels, less frequently at the arch and even less so in the descending or abdominal aorta. Doming, wrinkling, and folding of the BAV during the cardiac cycle can cause an abnormal blood jet compared to the TAV. Additionally, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have demonstrated turbulent flow through the BAV and ascending aorta, even when the valve appears functionally normal. Several researchers have shown increased wall shear stress on BAV aortas, especially with right-to-left valve orientation, using four-dimensional MRI. 
* **Aortic wall intrinsic properties.** A BAV is linked to an intrinsic disease of the aortic wall, characterized by early cystic medial degeneration in about half of BAV aortas and decreased fibrillin-1 content, regardless of valve function. It has also been demonstrated that ascending aortas continue to enlarge despite AV replacement. Furthermore, patients with BAV tend to be significantly younger and have substantially larger aortas than those with TAV. The severity of the stenosis and the regurgitation is not always proportional to the extent of the aortic dilatation. Therefore, TAA seems to be independent of the presence of valvular disease, although it may be worsened by it.
* **Extracellular matrix remodeling:** The extracellular matrix (ECM) plays a crucial role in regulating cellular events, binding and storing secreted proteins, and maintaining the structural integrity of the vascular wall. The degradation of the ECM is tightly controlled by the matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and their specific tissue inhibitors (TIMPs), which are secreted by vascular smooth muscle cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells. The balance between MMPs and the tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases regulates ECM degradation in both normal and pathological conditions and can lead to aneurysm formation when MMP activity surpasses inhibitory control. Various studies have shown a disturbance in the ECM of a TAA in the BAV, with increased MMPs activity.

## 4. BAV PHENOTYPES AND REPARABILITY (PATIENT SELECTION)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 4.1 BAV phenotypes</summary><div class="details-content">

Several attempts have been made to categorize the anatomical heterogeneity of the BAV. Various diverging classifications with different criteria for defining BAV phenotypes have ultimately caused confusion in clinical practice and research. To promote the use of a common language, facilitate analyses of clinical outcomes, and guide AVr and VSARR procedures, a new international expert classification has been proposed. This new nomenclature defines 3 phenotypes of BAV:

1. **The fused BAV (90%–95% of all BAV cases)** is characterized by the presence of 3 sinuses and 2 functional cusps due to congenital fusion, most often indicated by a raphe (though fused BAVs can exist without a raphe) between either the 2 coronary cusps (right/left BAV, 70%–80% of fused types) or 1 coronary cusp and the non-coronary (right/N BAV, 20%–30% of fused types, or left/N BAV, 3%–6%). Notably, the 2 fused cusps have reduced surface area, and the area covered by the non-fused cusp is larger than expected.
2. **The two-sinus BAV (5%–7% of cases)** features only 2 sinuses and 2 cusps without macroscopic signs of fusion and no significant difference in the surface area of the 2 cusps. It may present in 2 different phenotypic orientations, namely, the antero-posterior and the latero-lateral (defined by the AV short-axis tomographic view).
3. **The partial-fusion BAV (or forme fruste BAV, unknown prevalence)** is characterized by the presence of a small commissural fusion, with a surface area of the partially conjoined cusp roughly twice that of the non-fused one: It is thought to represent a milder form of bicuspid malformation of the AV ("tricuspid-like" valve with 3 functional commissures), often detected only during the operation, whereas echocardiographic imaging can often mistake it for a TAV. 

The partial-fusion and the two-sinus BAVs are at the extremes of a phenotypic continuum ([Fig. 5](#fig-5)); the fused types are intermediate and, in repair-oriented terminology, need to be further characterized in terms of commissural orientation (CO) ([Fig. 6](#fig-6) and [Fig. 7](#fig-7)).

### 4.2 Phenotypic features of bicuspid aortic valves determining reparability</summary><div class="details-content">

Valve reparability reflects not only the technical aspects of the repair procedure but also the prognosis regarding the risk of postoperative valve dysfunction and the recurrence of AR. Three interconnected anatomical features of the BAV have been focused on for their association with the degree of reparability: CO (or valve symmetry); length of fusion; and height of the rudimentary "non-functional" commissure (or under-the-raphe triangle). The above-mentioned interconnection lies in the fact that the more asymmetric the valve is, in terms of CO, the shorter is the fusion and the higher is the height of the non-functional commissural insertion in the root ([Fig. 6](#fig-6)).

Commissural orientation ([Fig. 7](#fig-7)) is categorized into 3 conventional groups:
* **Symmetric BAV (type A: CO 160°–180°)**;
* **Asymmetric BAV (type B: CO 140°–159°)**;
* **Very asymmetric BAV (type C: CO 120°–139°)**.

Both the two-sinus BAV types and the fused symmetric BAVs fall within the "symmetrical" type A category. For valve repair, the symmetrical BAV is the most suitable substrate for achieving a durable outcome, whereas the very asymmetric BAV necessitates a more complex surgical approach or valve replacement. 

In addition to the 3 anatomical features noted above, the anatomical-pathological characteristics of the cusps must also be considered, including cusp retraction, fibrosis and calcification, and the presence of fenestrations.

If fibrosis and/or calcification involves almost exclusively the raphe and is not full-thickness, it does not necessarily jeopardize the chances of repair: Shaving of the raphe, contained resection (so that the resulting gap can be closed by direct suture rather than requiring patch placement), and even its partial detachment from its insertion at the aortic wall and cusp base can be performed to ensure good, fused cusp mobility. Conversely, extensive and multifocal fibrosis and calcification (especially when this latter involves a functional commissure) are features that reduce reparability. 

Non-endocarditic fenestrations can occur at various sites, such as in the paracommissural position or parallel to the free margin. Their development and significance are debated. Paracommissural fenestrations, particularly if small and involving only 1 cusp, can be left untreated; they do not represent an absolute contraindication to BAV repair, though it is important to note that they may be less well tolerated than in TAV regarding immediate and long-term outcomes. Multiple fenestrations, especially if distant from the commissure (free margin or belly of the cusp), are generally a strong deterrent in the BAV setting.

### 4.3 The BAV aortopathy phenotypes</summary><div class="details-content">

There are 3 major forms of aortic dilatation phenotypes: 
1. **The ascending phenotype** (dilatation located mainly at the tubular ascending tract beyond the STJ, accounting for approximately 70% of BAV aortopathy cases);
2. **The root phenotype** (dilatation located mainly at the root [sinuses of Valsalva], possibly involving the annulus, accounting for roughly 20% of BAV aortopathy cases); and
3. **The extended phenotypes** ([Fig. 8](#fig-8)).

<span id="fig-8"></span>![](fig8_bav_aortopathy_phenotypes.jpg)

**Figure 8.** Schematic of the Bicuspid Aortic Valve Aortopathy Phenotypes. Left: The most common phenotype (around 70%) is preferential dilatation of the tubular ascending aorta; this is termed the ascending phenotype. Middle: The root phenotype involves preferential root dilatation, observed in approximately 20% of patients with BAV and aortopathy. The most common extended phenotypes are root plus ascending aorta and ascending aorta plus arch. *Abbreviations:* BAV, bicuspid aortic valve.

The BAV aortic root phenotype features dilatation primarily at the root (sinuses of Valsalva), AR, and male sex. (Figure 9) The root phenotype is associated with faster growth of the tubular ascending aorta and may sometimes appear as an extended root phenotype. ([Fig. 8](#fig-8)). Due to the complex three-dimensional (3D) and often asymmetric nature of BAV root dilatation, long-axis echocardiography views can underestimate the maximum sinus-to-sinus diameter, requiring the use of electrocardiographic-gated CT or MRI for precise assessment. 

The root phenotypes are linked to a higher risk of dissection independent of AV surgery, a greater prevalence of aortopathy among first-degree relatives, and a higher occurrence of rare genetic variants. Consequently, the BAV root phenotype is regarded as an indication for elective surgical aortic repair once the diameter reaches $50\text{ mm}$ or more. 

If AR is accompanied by significant dilation of the aortic root and the valve is repairable, VSARR should be considered. Reimplantation or remodelling combined with annuloplasty may be suitable. If the valve is not repairable and root replacement is necessary, a composite valved conduit (mechanical or bioprosthetic) can be implanted.

Observational, propensity-matched studies have reported a lower rate of valve-related complications and subsequent fewer cardiac deaths associated with VSARR compared to composite valved conduits. Another "native valve preserving" option for younger patients (typically under 50 years of age) with non-repairable AR is to replace the aortic valve and root with the patient's own pulmonic valve and root (pulmonic autograft or Ross procedure), with a homograft inserted in the pulmonic valve position.

#### 5.1.1 Two-dimensional and three-dimensional echocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

A precise assessment of the severity and mechanism of AR is essential to determine the surgical indication and feasibility of repair. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), primarily two-dimensional, is the first-line imaging modality and the gold standard for evaluating the severity of AR on a four-grade scale (1 to 4) according to the International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement in heart valve disease and the haemodynamic consequences of AR on the left ventricle. The severity of AR and its haemodynamic effects require a comprehensive assessment of qualitative, semi-quantitative, and quantitative factors, alongside a systematic multiparametric evaluation of left ventricular function to determine prognosis and operative need. 

Goal-directed pre-repair transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE), including two-dimensional and 3D imaging, is performed intraoperatively, immediately before cardiopulmonary bypass, where the AV within its root complex is assessed in a blood-pressured, dynamic state. Qualitative features and quantitative parameters must be evaluated and discussed with the surgeon ([Table 2](#table-2)).

* No retraction or perforation of the cusps;
* Symmetrical or mildly asymmetric BAV.

**Specific measurements of cusp geometry.** Specific functional measurements related to cusp geometry are essential when planning AVr. These include the CO in cases of BAV, the coaptation length (cL), the effective height (eH), the geometric height (gH), and the free margin length ([Fig. 10](#fig-10)).
* **Commissural orientation (CO):** defined as the angle formed by the lines connecting the commissures to the central axis of the valve. The CO influences the surgical approach and the long-term durability of the repair; the aim of the operation is to "symmetrize" the valve to achieve a CO of 180°. A symmetrical CO is linked to better valve durability. A type A is easier to repair than a type C and is more frequently associated with isolated root dilatation, whereas types B and C more often present with severe AR. 
* **Geometric height (gH):** defined as the distance between the cusp nadir and the midpoint of the free margin. The cusp is considered retracted when the gH measures $16\text{ mm}$ or less in TAV and $19\text{ mm}$ or less in the BAV non-fused cusp. This parameter is vital when planning an AVr.
* **Coaptation length (cL):** (average $4\text{--}5\text{ mm}$) is the length of cusp apposition in diastole.
* **Effective height (eH):** (average $8\text{--}10\text{ mm}$) is the perpendicular distance from the annulus to the centre of the free margin of the cusp. Systematic measurement of eH allows the sonographer to detect cusp prolapse.
* **Free margin length:** measures the free edge of the cusp between the 2 commissures. When this margin elongates—often seen with a dilated aortic root—the cusp may prolapse.

**Valve phenotype: from uni- to quadricuspid.** It is essential to identify the specific valve phenotype among the numerous types, because the phenotype influences surgical planning ([Fig. 11](#fig-11)). The most common is the classical TAV. The BAV is the second most frequently encountered. Despite its relatively low prevalence (1%), the latter is often seen in cardiac patients due to its typical association with aortic stenosis, AR and aortopathy. The UAV and quadricuspid aortic valves (QAV) are even rarer but are also associated with valvular stenosis or regurgitation. Sometimes, identifying the valve type can be difficult. This is especially true when the sonographer needs to distinguish between a very asymmetric BAV, a UAV, or a classical TAV in the presence of commissural and/or raphe calcifications.

The key principle is that the number of commissures defines the phenotype:
* A **TAV** normally has 3 functional commissures at the same height.
* A **BAV** has 2 functional commissures (which may be asymmetrically positioned) and a non-functional commissure with a hypoplastic interleaflet triangle from which the raphe arises.
* A **UAV** has 2 non-functional commissures and a single functional commissure or can be acommissural.
* A **QAV** has 4 commissures, 1 of which is usually underdeveloped, resulting in 3 functional commissures ([Fig. 11](#fig-11)). Commissural height is measured using multiplanar reconstruction (MPR) based on a 3D volume of the FAA.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 5.2 Preoperative CT</summary><div class="details-content">

A CT scan should be performed on all patients undergoing AVr. It should be done with contrast medium and synchronized with the heartbeat. It provides precise information regarding the annular dimension (which can be compared with TOE 3D evaluation), root dimension, and CO for BAV. It is also a valuable tool for measuring the eH, cL, and cusp gH. All these measurements are important for the surgeon to be able to assess the valve's repairability. Compared to echocardiography, CT scans offer invaluable information about the presence, location, and extent of calcification that could affect the repairability of the valve. Sinus symmetry/asymmetry and muscular inclusion are easily assessed by CT scan.

Additionally, a detailed anatomy of the coronary system is provided by a CT scan. This information is crucial because potential anomalies in the origin or course of the coronary artery might influence the surgical approach. The most common variants are the aberrant origin of the right coronary artery from the left sinus and the aberrant origin of the circumflex coronary artery from the right sinus. In both cases, their abnormal path increases the risk of injury or may require additional procedures during primary AVr or VSARR operations. A short left mainstem must also be identified to prevent issues with selective cardioplegia perfusion via the ostial cannula and to avoid damaging or kinking the circumflex artery from external ring placement or suture annuloplasty. Coronary ostia close to the commissure can affect VSARR and the re-attachment of coronary ostia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 5.3 Indications for aortic regurgitation intervention</summary><div class="details-content">

Indications for intervention in patients with severe AR are based mainly on symptoms or, in asymptomatic patients, on the impact of the regurgitant flow on left ventricular function. An indexed left ventricular end-systolic diameter greater than $22\text{ mm/m}^2$ or an indexed left ventricular end-systolic volume greater than $45\text{ ml/m}^2$ requires treatment. 

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 5.4 Indications for aortic root and ascending aorta replacement</summary><div class="details-content">

The absolute and indexed aortic diameters remain the primary factors for determining aortic replacement. In patients with TAV and BAV, proximal ascending aorta replacement is recommended for:
* A diameter of $\ge 55\text{ mm}$;
* $> 52\text{ mm}$ in patients with low surgical risk;
* $\ge 50\text{ mm}$ in the presence of risk factors for acute events (including root phenotype, short stature, age, ascending aortic length, and others);
* $\ge 45\text{ mm}$ if the AV triggers the need for an operation. 

These recommendations, however, do not differentiate between valve repair and valve replacement. Indeed, if the valve is repaired, especially BAVs, root replacement at a lower diameter ($40\text{--}45\text{ mm}$) might help restore valve symmetry to improve its durability. When deciding on root replacement, the surgeon should consider the increased complexity of the procedure and weigh it against the improved durability of repair after root replacement. In expert-dedicated repair centres, whenever root dimensions are enlarged (even mildly), the ascending aorta and root are replaced to stabilize a valve repair.

</div></details>

## 6. SURGICAL PLANNING AND TECHNIQUES</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 6.1 Intraoperative valve and root assessment</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic valve repair operations are performed through a median full sternotomy, or a partial upper sternotomy into the third or fourth intercostal space may be used in selected patients. 

The operation is performed with cardiopulmonary bypass and arterial cannulation in the distal ascending aorta or the proximal aortic arch. Other arterial cannulation sites (i.e., axillary or femoral artery) may be chosen in the presence of concomitant arch surgery. Venous drainage is classically performed via the right atrium or superior vena cava or via the femoral vein in the case of a partial sternotomy. A left heart drainage cannula is inserted into the right superior pulmonary vein.

After aortic cross-clamping, the heart is arrested with root, ostial, or retrograde cardioplegia, depending on the surgeon's preferences and the severity of the AR. A complete transverse aortotomy is usually performed. Stay sutures, essential for valve exposure and analysis, are placed 4–5 mm above each commissure ([Fig. 12](#fig-12)). In fused BAVs, a stay suture is placed above the raphe at the level of the STJ. For reproducible analysis, equal traction must be applied to all 3 stay sutures along the axis of the aortic root and in the outward direction. The orientation of the stay sutures must respect the orientation of the native commissures (i.e., 120° in TAV). Asymmetrical orientation or traction may be misleading in valve analysis.

<span id="fig-12"></span>![](fig12_commissural_stay_sutures.jpg)

**Figure 12.** Aortic Valve Exposure with Commissural Stay Sutures. (a) In a tricuspid aortic valve with right coronary cusp prolapse, note the traction on the stay sutures at 120°. (b) In a tricuspid valve after sinus of Valsalva resection for valve-sparing aortic root replacement, note the traction at 120° on the stay suture. (c) In a symmetrical right/non-bicuspid aortic valve, note the traction on the stay suture that follows commissural orientation. The third suture is placed just at the level of the sinotubular junction for a better view of the raphe.

The direct visual analysis of the AV involves confirming the analysis of the preoperative images ([Table 4](#table-4)). The valve phenotypes (TAV, BAV, UAV, QAV or a variant) and subtypes (BAV type A, symmetrical; type B, asymmetrical; or type C, very asymmetrical) are determined. Some forms of fruste BAV (very asymmetrical) can be missed by preoperative imaging and may only be detected by intraoperative analysis. After determination of valve phenotype, the cusp size, configuration, and quality must be assessed to evaluate reparability and to plan the repair approach.

Cusp size is best assessed intraoperatively by measuring the gH, also known as cusp height ([Fig. 13a](#fig-13)). The gH is the curvilinear distance along the cusp tissue from the central free margin to the nadir of the cusp insertion. It is measured by grasping the nodulus of Arantius with forceps and applying gentle traction to the free margin to align the cusp with a ruler ([Fig. 13b](#fig-13)). A normal gH is $19\text{--}20\text{ mm}$ in TAV and $23\text{--}24\text{ mm}$ for the non-fused cusp in BAV. A gH $< 16\text{ mm}$ in TAV and $< 19\text{ mm}$ in BAV would discourage performing a primary AVr or VSARR ([Table 4](#table-4)). 

Cusp configuration is assessed by measuring its eH. The eH is the orthogonal distance from the centre of the free margin to the basal plane ([Figs. 13a and 13d](#fig-13)). The eH is measured intraoperatively using a dedicated calliper (Schäfers Calliper, Fehling Surgical Instruments, Karlstein, Germany) with traction on the stay sutures ([Fig. 13c](#fig-13)). Normal cusp configuration is indicated by an effective height of $9\text{--}10\text{ mm}$ in adults. An eH less than $9\text{ mm}$ suggests cusp prolapse, and an eH below $0\text{ mm}$ indicates complete prolapse. Associated features can confirm the presence of cusp prolapse, such as:
* Unequal free margin level;
* Elongation of a free margin compared with the other cusp(s); 
* A ruptured paracommissural fenestration;
* A transverse fold crossing the cusp belly in TAV.

The presence of normal or abnormal cusp tissue determines cusp quality. The analysis generally distinguishes 3 locations: the insertion line, the belly, and the free margin. Thin, pliable valvular tissue indicates optimal cusp quality. Fibrotic thickening, calcification, fenestrations, and destruction/perforation are pathological conditions that limit cusp quality, thereby affecting repair quality and durability. Their size and location are essential determinants of valve reparability. Valve repair is generally considered contraindicated in the following situations:
* Diffuse calcification (multiple spots on more than 1 cusp);
* Fibrous thickening, particularly when associated with cusp retraction (gH $\le 16\text{ mm}$);
* Multiple and large fenestrations ($> 10\text{ mm}$) ([Fig. 14](#fig-14));
* Large cusp defect ($> 1\text{ cm}^2$).

Any cusp pathology requiring patch repair increases the risk of repair failure compared with repair without a patch. Isolated small paracommissural fenestrations can be left untreated because they do not cause AR.

<span id="fig-14"></span>![](fig14_commissural_fenestrations.jpg)

**Figure 14.** Example of Commissural Fenestrations. (a and b) Fenestrations of different sizes. Small fenestrations do not need to be closed, whereas large fenestrations should be closed with a pericardial patch; (c) multiple and large fenestrations contraindicate AV repair.

In BAV, specific morphologic characteristics enable classification into subtypes (types A, B, and C) ([Fig. 5](#fig-5)), which guide the repair technique and affect the outcome. Among these characteristics, CO is generally measured on preoperative imaging, whereas the length of the cusp fusion and the commissural height at the raphe are measured intraoperatively with a simple ruler. In type A (symmetrical, CO: 160°–180°), the commissural height at the raphe is low ($< 10\text{ mm}$), and fusion is almost complete. In the rare, perfectly symmetrical 2-sinus BAV (CO 180°), the 2 cusps are similar; there is no raphe, fusion is complete, and the gH of the fused cusp equals that of the non-fused cusp. In type B (asymmetrical, CO: 140°–159°), the raphe reaches approximately mid-height of the root (average $14\text{ mm}$), and fusion is incomplete (average $14\text{ mm}$). In type C (very asymmetrical, CO: 120°–139°), the commissural height at the raphe is close to the STJ, and the length of fusion is short ($< 10\text{ mm}$).

Aortic annular dilatation is present in most patients with chronic AR and is associated with progression of AR, generally being more pronounced in those with a BAV. Stabilizing and/or reducing the aortic annulus at the time of the operation has been shown to significantly improve the durability of the repair. A diameter greater than $25\text{--}26\text{ mm}$ is generally considered indicative of aortic annular dilatation, with adjustments made for patient body size. Intraoperatively, the annulus is measured using a Hegar dilator. Care must be taken to avoid damaging the cusps with large dilators. In some BAVs, the valve opening can be smaller than the annulus itself. Because the STJ contributes to valve function, like the VBR, its normal diameter needs to be re-established. Some authors also advocate its stabilization to prevent long-term dilatation. 

Besides aortic dimensions, several other pathological lesions or anatomical variants require attention, including an intimal tear, a thin or fragile aortic wall (especially in HTADs), a wide muscular inclusion at the nadir of the right coronary sinus, and an abnormal origin of the coronary artery. An intimal tear or a fragile aortic wall should prompt extensive aortic root replacement, particularly in cases of moderate aortic dilatation or HTADs. A large muscular inclusion of the right coronary sinus, particularly in a BAV ("sinking sinus"), can make deep external root dissection more challenging for placing the external ring in the primary AVr or for lowering the Dacron graft in VSARR.

The position and aberrant course of the coronary ostia must be carefully assessed on preoperative imaging and intraoperatively to avoid damage or kinking during AV and root surgery. Coronary ostia may be displaced cranially in an aortic root aneurysm and can be damaged during external root dissection or aortotomy. They may also be located close to a valve commissure, making the creation of coronary buttons more challenging.

### 6.2 Techniques for aortic annuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic regurgitation, with or without an aneurysm, is almost always associated with an enlarged annulus and/or STJ (annulus $> 25\text{ mm}$, STJ $\ge 30\text{ mm}$). Managing the aortic annulus as part of treating AR is not new. Taylor and colleagues first performed an aortic annuloplasty in 1958, termed aortic circumclusion, by placing silk sutures as a circumferential annuloplasty beneath the coronary arteries on a beating heart. Later, Cabrol et al. described the first internal annuloplasty technique in 1966 using sub- and supra-commissural plication sutures. This technique offered the potential to address the valve at subvalvular (annulus) and supravalvular (STJ) levels to restore proper valve function. Despite initial good outcomes, mid- and long-term results showed that subcommissural stitches were associated with unfavourable postoperative haemodynamics and were predictors of recurrent AR and reoperation due to re-dilatation of the aortic annulus. As a result, any type of subvalvular annuloplasty should be circumferential, aiming to achieve a symmetrical valve configuration to increase cusp mobility and minimize postoperative gradients. Aortic annuloplasty can be performed either externally or internally ([Fig. 15](#fig-15)). 

<span id="fig-15"></span>![](fig15_annuloplasty_techniques.jpg)

**Figure 15.** Different Annuloplasty Techniques. Left: Coroneo, Inc. Extra-Aortic Ring (Lansac 2003). Middle: Suture Annuloplasty (Schäfers 2009). Right: Hemispherical Aortic Annuloplasty Ring Technology (HAART) Ring (Rankin 2011).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 6.2.1 External annuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

The principle of addressing both functional diameters of the FAA to restore proper valve function is now well established as a key parameter to ensure the long-term durability of TAV and BAV repair, with or without an aneurysm. 

**Annuloplasty in AR without a root aneurysm.** External annuloplasty can be performed using a Dacron or Teflon strip, depending on the surgeon's preference. For proper placement, a complete external dissection of the root is required. Next, 6 to 7 (large annulus) subannular sutures are passed below both coronary ostia and then fixed to the annuloplasty. In primary AVr, adding an additional annuloplasty at the STJ level has been proposed to protect the repair and increase the coaptation surface. The absence of STJ stabilization has been identified as an independent risk factor for reoperation in both isolated AR and BAV repair. Overall, it is advised to re-establish the physiological ratio between the annulus and the STJ diameters (i.e., 1:1.2) as part of the repair process ([Fig. 15](#fig-15)). Once the annular dimensions have been re-established (usually between 21 and 23 mm), the correct STJ dimension ($26\text{--}28\text{ mm}$) is obtained by using a second external ring or by replacing the ascending aorta with a Dacron graft.

As an alternative to external annuloplasty, which requires root dissection and sub-annular placement of sutures, the simple placement of a polytetrafluoroethylene Gore-Tex 0 suture to encircle the annulus from outside has been proposed. In all cases, annular stabilization has been shown to be important for the long-term stability of the results. 

**Annuloplasty with root aneurysm.** When the root is replaced in the context of a VSARR, FAA stability is guaranteed by the procedure. When adopting the reimplantation type of VSARR, annuloplasty and STJ stabilization are ensured by the Dacron prosthesis and the procedure's well-known intrinsic characteristics. Similarly, when adopting the remodelling procedure, annuloplasty is achieved by adding a complete annuloplasty ring at the end of the procedure. A dedicated, expansible ring that mimics annular dynamics has been proposed as a critical adjunct to the remodelling procedure, as an improvement over homemade Dacron strips ([Fig. 16](#fig-16)). 

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 6.2.2 Internal annuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

Internal annuloplasty is performed with a dedicated ring. Schomburg and Rankin described a rigid internal ring, Hemispherical Aortic Annuloplasty Ring Technology (HAART), in 2011. It was developed from mathematical analyses of human CT angiograms. For TAV, it has a 2:3 elliptical base geometry and 3 equally spaced subcommissural posts, each deflected outward by 10 degrees. For BAV, the shape is circular, with the 2 posts at 180 degrees, ensuring symmetrical orientation. The internal geometric ring provides 3D annular stability while minimizing dissection of the aortic root, pulmonary artery, right ventricle, and coronary arteries. A specially designed ball sizer is used to select the appropriate ring diameter. The ring is positioned below the valve and fixed with looping sutures on the aortic side or externally, thereby preventing interference with cusp movements ([Fig. 15](#fig-15)). The internal ring can also be applied both in primary AVr and in association with the remodelling procedure.

### 6.3 Technique for VSARR</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 6.3.1 Remodelling and reimplantation</summary><div class="details-content">

In 1979, Magdi Yacoub introduced a VSARR, which he later described as a remodelling technique in which a tube graft was tailored to create 3 neo-sinuses. In 1992, Tirone David described the AV reimplantation technique, in which the valve was contained within a Dacron tube graft. Over the years, there has been considerable debate regarding the pros and cons of each technique. The remodelling technique can preserve aortic root dynamics and physiological cusp motion. This is achieved through anatomical root reconstruction with neo-sinuses of Valsalva. Several reports subsequently demonstrated that a dilated annulus ($> 25\text{--}28\text{ mm}$) was a risk factor for recurrent AR and reoperation, and several meta-analyses identified it as suboptimal compared with the reimplantation technique. On the other hand, the reimplantation technique addresses the annulus; however, valve dynamics within a stiff, rigid tube are altered, increasing the risk of valve dysfunction and early degeneration. 

<span id="fig-16"></span>![](fig16_reimplantation_vs_remodelling.jpg)

**Figure 16.** Aortic Valve Reimplantation (Middle) and Aortic Valve Remodelling Plus External Ring Annuloplasty (Right). From the axial view (left), it is possible to appreciate the bulging of the sinuses of Valsalva.

The consequences of these different findings led physicians to consider the respective weaknesses of both techniques. The reimplantation technique benefited from a conduit with sinuses, whereas the remodelling technique was supplemented with a circumferential annuloplasty ([Fig. 16](#fig-16)). In both cases, blood flow, cusp movement, and durability were improved. Overall, VSARR is now a safe and durable procedure that restores normal life expectancy with a low risk of reintervention, especially when immediate surgical results are satisfactory (residual AR is $\le 1$ and central). 

External dissection down to the subvalvular plane is a key step for both the reimplantation and the remodelling plus ring annuloplasty procedure. Subannular sutures need to be placed in a horizontal manner, 2–3 mm below the nadir of each cusp ([Fig. 2](#fig-2)). A muscle inclusion at the right coronary cusp might require more dissection or an oblique direction of the sub-annular sutures. Sometimes when the heart is rotated, the muscle inclusion may also concern the left coronary cusp.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 6.3.2 Choice of the conduit size</summary><div class="details-content">

Many formulas have been described to determine the appropriate graft size for both remodelling and reimplantation techniques, using either the annulus size, the height of the interleaflet triangle, or the desired neo STJ size. With years of experience, this approach has proved more of an academic discussion than a practical problem. The most frequently used sizes in adults are $30\text{ mm}$ for reimplantation and $28\text{ mm}$ for remodelling, with a few exceptions based on the patient's size or the specific technique used. Straight grafts may require a larger diameter to avoid contact between the cusps and the Dacron conduit. Patients with a BAV may require larger grafts than patients with a TAV to reduce the risk of induced cusp prolapse or the need for extensive plication. It is important to re-establish the correct ratio (1.2–1.3) between the new annulus and the new STJ. Knowing the size of the new STJ (graft size minus the 4–5 mm of tissue thickness), it is easy to adjust the annulus to the correct diameter.

When replacing a dilated root with a Dacron graft of normal anatomical size, there is a good chance of inducing a cusp prolapse that needs to be addressed to avoid residual AR.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 6.3.3 Suturing the graft</summary><div class="details-content">

Suturing the graft to the remnants of the aortic wall requires special attention, because these sutures are haemostatic for reimplantation and remodelling. In the remodelling technique, where all sutures are exteriorized, the aortic tissue should be sutured close to the fibrous attachment of the cusps, taking care to avoid suture gaps.

### 6.4 Techniques for cusp repair</summary><div class="details-content">

There are 2 basic principles to abolish AR and improve cusp coaptation: cusp enlargement to increase the coaptation area or narrowing the root and the annulus to bring the cusp closer together. Although sub- (VBR) and supra-valvular (STJ) annuloplasty restore the functional diameter of the root, cusp restoration is achieved by suturing (plicating) or adding autologous or xenologous pericardial tissue. Both repair principles restore the proper valve configuration and enhance the coaptation surface for durable results. A true cusp prolapse is attributed to free-margin elongation or rupture of the fenestration. It can be corrected by shortening (e.g., by plication) or free-margin reinforcement (mostly using polytetrafluoroethylene suture). 

Cusp restriction needs to be treated by cusp enlargement. However, to date, better long-term results have been found in AVr for patients with prolapse corrected with central plication rather than for those with retraction corrected with cusp augmentation with a patch, due to poorer repair durability. Cusp fenestrations located within the coaptation area do not induce AR in a normally functioning valve; therefore, their presence does not contraindicate AVr and does not necessitate any specific management with no impact on long-term results. However, in some instances, a fenestration can be involved in the cusp prolapse mechanism (i.e., by elongation or rupture of the free margin in relation to the fenestration). In those cases, the fenestration area should be closed and reinforced using a small pericardial patch, or, if very small, with a direct running suture (taking care not to shorten the free-margin length).

Aortic valve reconstruction using small pericardial patches allows repair of many cusp defects that cannot be approached with cusp suturing alone. Valve repair could be achieved through a central patch, a commissural patch, a strip along the entire free margin, closure of the defect, even replacement of the entire cusp or commissural reconstruction. Although closure of the cusp perforation with autologous pericardium has been demonstrated as the most durable repair among all AVr techniques, it has some limitations. Harvesting the pericardium and its intraoperative preparation prolong the operation time. Furthermore, individual differences in tissue quality and intraoperative treatment (clearing, fixation) can impact long-term durability. Unfortunately, the long-term results after AVr using autologous pericardium vary significantly and are frequently unfavourable, especially with BAVs. Yet it is unclear whether the pericardium itself or the extent of cusp pathology impacts the outcome over time, because no randomized studies are available. There is limited evidence that the reason for repair failure is progression of the underlying valve disease rather than any degeneration of the patch. 

Consequently, the choice of the repair technique should be based on many factors. A repair technique without patch material (cusp plication/suturing with additional root/annulus narrowing) can be considered in cases with sufficient viable cusp tissue. However, caution should be taken not to narrow the annulus too extensively, because this could lead to a dramatic reduction in the valve orifice area, potentially preventing the implantation of a sufficiently large prosthesis in the future. The use of current xenogenous material, although promising, needs to be evaluated in the medium and long term. 

After cusp repair, a functional assessment, such as the water test and/or suction test and graft pressurization with cardioplegia, may provide useful intraoperative guidance during AVr. Nevertheless, the only definitive method to confirm AV competence is declamping and post-bypass evaluation using TOE.

## 7. POSTOPERATIVE EVALUATION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 7.1 Echocardiography: Role of the sonographer in the operating room</summary><div class="details-content">

The timely presence of the echocardiographer in the operating room to assess the AVr under normal physiological loading conditions (patient completely off bypass, appropriate left ventricular volume, and physiological blood pressure and heart rate) ([Table 5](#table-5)) is extremely important. The repaired valve should exhibit ample systolic cusp excursion, and the residual mean systolic gradient should be measured in the transgastric TOE view with optimal alignment with the aortic flow and should not exceed $15\text{ mmHg}$. The annular size should be measured, and size reduction confirmed if dilated pre-repair ([Fig. 17](#fig-17)). The cusp cL should be more than $4\text{ mm}$ and clearly above the aortic annulus. 

Occasionally, the belly of the cusps may billow 2–3 mm below the annulus without affecting valve function or durability. The residual AR should be none/trivial or grade I; if suspected to be more than grade I, the severity must be quantified. The presence of eccentric residual jets, even if mild (grade I), suggests residual prolapse, which likely needs to be corrected before leaving the operating room. Effective communication of these features to the surgeon is critical. Failure of 1 or more of these features requires a second bypass run for further repair, unless the condition of the heart, the length of the operation, and the technical difficulty would indicate otherwise.

### 7.2 Factors predisposing to recurrent operations</summary><div class="details-content">

The AVr operations aim to achieve cusp coaptation by fully restoring the integrity of the entire FAA. Thus, the FAA components must be assessed using postoperative echocardiography. A possible cause of cusp prolapse is STJ over-reduction by the vascular graft, thereby reducing the intercommissural distance. In all AVr operations, the level of cusp coaptation should be above the VBR. This means that the lower level of coaptation should be higher than the annulus, and the highest level should approach the mid-height of the sinuses of Valsalva ([Fig. 18](#fig-18)). If cusp coaptation occurs below the aortic annulus, the risk of recurrent AR is high ($> 70\%$). Another possible cause of cusp prolapse is failure to address a dilated annulus. A dilated preoperative annulus diameter ($\ge 26\text{ mm}$) was found to be associated with an increased risk of early and long-term repair failure. 

Geometric measurements are also important for assessing the quality of the AVr. The cL and eH are the most representative and should be compared with the pre-repair measurements. The targeted eH must be 9 to 10 mm. A cL of less than 4 to 5 mm or an eH $< 9\text{ mm}$ is associated with an increased risk of recurrent AR ($> 30\%\text{--}40\%$). The presence and direction of any residual AR in the immediate postoperative period must be evaluated because of its prognostic implications. If there is no residual AR and coaptation is above the aortic annulus, recurrent AR is very unlikely. One should not accept more than a grade I (mild) post-repair AR, especially if the AR is eccentric.

### 7.3 Factors predisposing to AV stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Systolic cusp motion and transvalvular flow must be evaluated postoperatively in the operating room. Incomplete opening and early valve closure may indicate low cardiac output rather than cusp restriction. A slight vibratory movement of the cusp in end-systole is a good criterion for cusp flexibility. The top of each aortic cusp passing behind the virtual line between 2 commissures indicates no systolic restrictive cusp motion and must be systematically assessed. The transvalvular gradient and colour flow through the left ventricular outflow tract must also be assessed. A postoperative mean gradient across the valve of up to $15\text{ mmHg}$ and a peak transvalvular gradient of up to $30\text{ mmHg}$ are deemed acceptable. Higher gradients are associated with an increased risk of developing severe aortic stenosis and of a redo-operation. Postoperative correction of a BAV should preferably have a CO at 180° to decrease the postoperative systolic gradient ([Fig. 19](#fig-19)).

### 7.4 Management of recurrent AR and/or stenosis in the operating room</summary><div class="details-content">

The main goal of postoperative echocardiography, which must be performed in the operating room, is to identify factors associated with recurrent AR, aortic stenosis and, ultimately, poor clinical outcomes. Post-AVr assessment is challenging for the echocardiographer because of poor image quality caused by air bubbles and acoustic shadowing from the aortic graft or a retro-aortic haematoma (which is commonly seen postoperatively). The unstable haemodynamic state must also be considered when interpreting the transvalvular gradient and assessing residual AR. The echocardiographic criteria associated with successful AVr, as well as those deemed unacceptable and requiring a second cardiopulmonary bypass run, are presented in [Table 6](#table-6).

Notably, TOE imaging is crucial for determining whether a patient should undergo a second run of cardiopulmonary bypass. At this stage, dialogue between the sonographer and the surgeon is more crucial than ever to the decision-making process. The key intraoperative echocardiographic parameters to assess include:
1. **Residual AR:** More than grade I residual AR is a significant concern. Eccentric regurgitant jets are particularly worrisome, even at grade I, often indicating an underlying structural issue (residual prolapse or cusp restriction).
2. **Mechanism of residual AR:** residual cusp prolapse may be corrected by repeat cusp resuspension, whereas cusp restriction (e.g., due to overly tight suspension sutures) may require more complex manoeuvres.
3. **Aortic annulus diameter and cusp coaptation:** Insufficient coaptation (cL $< 4\text{ mm}$) indisputably increases the risk of recurrent AR. Excessive annular dilation, if uncorrected, may compromise long-term repair durability.
4. **Transvalvular gradients:** Elevated gradients may indicate postoperative restriction of cusp systolic motion ([Fig. 19](#fig-19)). Asymmetry in cusp motion should be carefully evaluated.

If 1 or more of these criteria are present, returning to cardiopulmonary bypass for further intervention may be warranted to adjust the repair or to proceed with AV replacement in cases of technical failure.

### 7.5 Postoperative evaluation</summary><div class="details-content">

Before discharge, a comprehensive TTE and a CT scan should be recommended for all patients after AVr, especially when the valve repair involved root replacement. These imaging methods will best assess the newly reconstructed anatomy and help identify potential pitfalls and complications.

With high-quality images, it is possible to verify correct anatomical reconstruction. In particular, the surgeon should assess the degree of annular reduction and ensure a favourable ratio between the new annulus and the new STJ. Similarly, eH and cL can be assessed and compared with echocardiographic data. Potential pitfalls, such as small pseudoaneurysms, fluid collections around the graft, or kinking of the coronary artery, may be detected in asymptomatic patients. In the absence of complications, further follow-up is usually performed by TTE.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 7.6 Secondary prevention medication therapy</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients undergoing AVr should be aware of oral health issues. Furthermore, antibiotic prophylaxis should be considered for high-risk procedures in patients with AVr (when prosthetic material was used) and in those with previous episodes of infective endocarditis. In patients who have undergone AVr surgery, particularly those who continue to have hypertension, strict blood pressure control is recommended, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and beta-blockers are useful. Furthermore, guideline-directed medical therapy for coexistent heart failure should be optimized. Finally, beta-blockers remain the mainstay of medical treatment in patients with Marfan syndrome, reducing shear stress and aortic growth rate. They should be considered before and after the operation. Although angiotensin receptor blockers did not show a superior effect compared with beta-blockers, they may be considered an alternative for patients intolerant to beta-blockers. 

Low-dose acetylsalicylic acid ($75\text{--}100\text{ mg/day}$) should be considered for the first 3 months after surgical AVr in patients without an indication for oral anticoagulation.

## 8. AORTIC VALVE REPAIR IN SPECIAL PATIENT POPULATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 8.1 Congenital AV disease in the paediatric and young adult population</summary><div class="details-content">

Congenital AV disease likely has a common embryologic origin, resulting from the fusion of apposed endocardial cushions. The morphologic variants of the AV can be classified as UAV, BAV, or QAV. Here, the focus is on UAVs; QAVs occur more often in the context of truncus arteriosus.

Unicuspid morphology is characterized by 2 hypoplastic commissures, with concomitant cusp fusion adjacent to the affected commissures. The characteristic echocardiographic features are doming of the valve and its eccentric opening, usually in the posterior part. Commissural hypoplasia may be difficult or impossible to define outside the operating room using imaging. A CT angiography scan may be used to successfully diagnose UAV when appropriate 3D reconstructions are used. However, even intraoperatively, subnormal or hypoplastic commissural height may not be recognized. Thus, precise epidemiological data are lacking, and it has been shown in an adult population that the prevalence of unicuspid valves is vastly underestimated. Clinical observations suggest that most AVs requiring treatment in childhood or adolescence are unicuspid. 

The clinical presentation depends on the predominant type of dysfunction, the age at which symptoms appear, and their severity. Symptomatic newborns may present in a critical condition with severe aortic stenosis at the time of ductal closure. In childhood or adolescence, patients may become symptomatic from the underlying AV disease or from the sequelae of the initial treatment; the predominant dysfunction may be stenosis, regurgitation, or a mixed lesion. Aneurysm formation of the ascending aorta may occur in conjunction with unicuspid anatomy, as is known for BAVs; it may require prophylactic surgery in young adults. Surgical correction of the AV is commonly performed at that time. 

The surgical approach to the UAV depends on age, symptoms, and the predominant haemodynamic dysfunction. Aortic regurgitation is an exceptional feature in the first months of life. In adolescents and adults, the lesion most frequently comprises a mix of stenosis and regurgitation. In newborns, treatment is surgical in some centres, but most patients undergo balloon dilatation. The mortality rate for this procedure has decreased significantly over the past decade. Freedom from valve replacement appears to be limited to around 15% before adulthood. Some individual centre data seem more promising. 

The initial surgical treatment aims to relieve stenosis. The valves may show varying degrees of dysplasia, and fibrous nodules may be present on any part of the cusps, including the hinge points. Repair allows for somatic growth. It comprises commissurotomy, extensive debridement of the valve, and the creation of a new interleaflet triangle. The results of an operation are similar to those achieved by balloon dilatation; the potential benefit of this neonatal surgery appears to be that a small proportion of patients may remain free of reoperation for up to 2 decades. 

In childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood, an initially asymptomatic stenosis may become symptomatic. Alternatively, recurrent stenosis may develop after initial surgical or interventional palliation. Symptoms may arise from AR, either of the native valve or as a result of the initial treatment. Relevant aneurysm formation is rare before adulthood.

The valves are less dysplastic but persistently thicker and more dysplastic than normal valves. De novo AR may be due to commissural hypoplasia and/or insufficient cusp tissue (hypoplasia of the right cusp). Aortic regurgitation as a consequence of balloon dilatation usually involves a tear in the cusp tissue, most frequently in the rudimentary right cusp. Recurrent or de novo stenosis may be treated by commissurotomy, whereas AR is commonly treated by valve replacement. Repair concepts have been proposed as alternatives, allowing preservation of regurgitant UAVs. Tricuspidization and bicuspidization repair techniques share the creation of new commissures of normal height and the implanting of pericardial patches to augment cusp tissue. Long-term results have been published for bicuspidization, with excellent survival and a 10-year freedom from reoperation rate of nearly 70%. 

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 8.2 AV disease in other paediatric and adult congenital heart disease populations</summary><div class="details-content">

Many congenital lesions result in semilunar valve disease of the systemic ventricle. A BAV and its association with an aortic aneurysm are well known. It rarely requires an operation in childhood or adolescence.

**Truncus arteriosus** results from the failure of the primitive outflow tract to separate. Consequently, the systemic semilunar valve comprises a structure that would normally form 2 valves. Around 15% of these valves present with more than moderate regurgitation at birth, and a total of 20% ultimately develop regurgitation requiring surgery in the paediatric age group. Around 60% of the valves are tricuspid, 10% are bicuspid, and 30% are quadricuspid. Quadricuspid morphology rarely occurs in the absence of truncus arteriosus. Repair would be ideal in affected neonates and infants. However, a recent Society of Thoracic Surgeons' database study showed that half of these regurgitant valves are replaced, probably related to the technical difficulty of the repair, which is not yet standardized. Various repair techniques have been employed (e.g., bicuspidization, tricuspidization), and it appears that tricuspidization of the quadricuspid valves yields favourable long-term outcomes. There is increasing agreement that adjusting the annular size and the sinuses, both of which are invariably dilated in this condition, contributes to good repair durability.

**Tetralogy of Fallot:** In patients with tetralogy of Fallot, a dilated aortic root is a normal consequence of the anomalous conotruncal septum. The degree of root dilation is proportional to the severity of outflow tract obstruction. The development of AR is rare, as is the risk of aortic dissection or rupture. Consequently, it remains unclear what size criteria should be used for surgical treatment. Because the aortic cusps are generally normal, standard VSARR techniques can be applied should surgery be deemed necessary. The results should be identical to those obtained in degenerative root aneurysm.

**Ventricular septal defect (VSD) with Venturi effect:** Aortic regurgitation may be associated with a subaortic ventricular septal defect (VSD) and is caused by the Venturi effect generated at the level of the VSD. The left-to-right shunt at the level of the VSD increases blood velocity as it passes through the restrictive VSD, pulling down the adjacent aortic cusp, most commonly the right one. Aortic regurgitation is more prevalent in doubly committed VSDs, where the defect is closer to the aortic annulus. In the natural course, this defect may lead to progressive prolapse of 1 or 2 cusps, ultimately requiring cusp repair, i.e., plication of the free edge of the affected cusp. When the patient presents at a much older age, repair may become more challenging, in part because of marked annular dilatation, which can be difficult to address at the time of VSD closure.

**Arterial switch operation:** After the arterial switch operation for transposition of the great arteries, up to 40% of patients will have mild AR immediately postoperatively. The exact mechanism is unclear, but regurgitation may relate to the transfer of the coronary buttons to the neo-aortic root. Over time, dilatation of the neo-aortic root may occur, further contributing to AR. Existing 30-year follow-up studies indicate a growing need for valve procedures in this population. Aortic valve repair and VSARR have been employed for this pathology; long-term studies following repair are currently lacking, but some have reported inferior outcomes compared with other populations. 

**Subaortic stenosis and supra-AV syndrome:** Patients with supra-AV syndrome, Williams syndrome, and subaortic stenosis may show a global fibrotic process of the root, sometimes involving the cusps, particularly in those requiring reoperation. Aortic regurgitation in the presence of fibrous subaortic stenosis may result from cusp restriction and retraction caused by fibrous tissue encroaching on the cusps. These cases are often difficult to repair because of the challenge in developing a dissection plane between the ventricular myocardium, the cusp, and the tissue of the subvalvular stenosis.

**Single-ventricle disease:** The neo-AV root in single-ventricle disease is susceptible to dilatation across various underlying pathologies, which can cause or contribute to regurgitation. In infants and small children, repair is best suited to the patient's needs, including the possibility of somatic growth. Subcommissural sutures, which have not provided sufficient annular stabilization in adults, have been used in these instances. The durability of this approach in these pathologies is not well documented. At a later age, these patients can be repaired with valve-preserving surgery, including the reimplantation procedure, even though this latter technique is challenging to perform in patients who have had a previous Damus or Norwood procedure. The late results of this approach remain to be defined.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 8.3 Heritable thoracic aorta disease</summary><div class="details-content">

Heritable thoracic aorta disease in paediatric patients follows the same principles as in adults. The probability of aortic complications in infancy and childhood is very small; most patients present in adolescence. Patients presenting at a very early age may have small airway disease that may dominate their symptoms. Such symptomatic neonates are at a very high risk of death because of their respiratory compromise.

In most patients, the indications and procedures are the same as those used in adults. A particular scenario concerns Loeys-Dietz syndrome or special variants of ACTA2, which can present at a very early age with severe dilation and may require early surgery, as early as 3 years of age. The main challenge in treating aortic root dilatation in HTAD in paediatric patients is determining the optimal age to offer surgery. The criteria for adults cannot be applied to younger patients or to smaller sizes. It should be noted that rupture and dissection are exceptional in paediatric patients, and it is possible to follow the principle that patients should grow until adult-size prostheses can be considered. Most available grafts have a smaller diameter of $24\text{ mm}$, and therefore an annulus size of $21\text{ mm}$ can be the threshold at which an operation can be offered. Anecdotal use of smaller straight grafts has been reported, particularly in the context of root remodelling.

## 9. AORTIC VALVE CENTRE</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 9.1 Structure of the AV centre</summary><div class="details-content">

The heart valve centre of excellence is defined in both the European and American guidelines. However, there is controversy over what constitutes individual surgical and hospital volumes. The requirements for managing AV disease with transcatheter therapy versus surgery and mitral valve repair are well defined. However, the details of surgical experience and competency for AVr, as well as the management of associated aneurysms, are less well defined. Institutions should aim to meet minimum quality standards, including minimally invasive AVr/replacement. These standards should be applied within the practice of an AV multidisciplinary team (MDT). Specialist valve clinics should offer a forum for referrals from general practices and community centres, as well as from other hospitals that do not have specialist cardiac services.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 9.2 Membership of the valve centre</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinics should provide expertise from non-cardiac specialists, as well as from non-invasive cardiologists, interventional cardiologists and surgeons. The valve centre should offer specialized imaging, including TTE and TOE, 3D echocardiography, stress echocardiography, and perioperative TOE. In addition, the radiology department should be equipped to provide cardiac CT, CT aortography, and MRI, with experts interpreting the results. Specialist MDT meetings should be held with experts in AV and aortic surgery, as well as transcatheter therapy. The outcomes of these MDTs should be documented, and efforts should be made to interpret the results of these investigations and minimize delay. Other specialists involved in the patient's care who should be part of the heart valve centre include anaesthesiologists, heart failure cardiologists and electrophysiologists, geneticists, intensive care specialists, vascular surgeons, specialist radiologists, neurologists, renal physicians, haematologists, and dental surgeons.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 9.3 Data collection and quality assurance</summary><div class="details-content">

Data on patients with AR should be collected regularly, including pre-intervention demographic, risk factor, and risk stratification score data. The data should also include the extent of the valve lesions, the feasibility of repair, and the need for concomitant aortic surgery. A standardized system for primary AVr and VSARR outcomes is essential for consistent reporting and effective comparisons. Such a framework should capture not only survival but also long-term valve function, root stability, and patient quality of life. This system can guide personalized treatment decisions by defining failure mechanisms and aligning with the established International Consortium for Health Outcomes Measurement in heart valve disease, endorsed by all cardiology and surgical societies that edit the guidelines. 

Additionally, data on patients who have been turned down after surgery should be collected. Similarly, data on patients undergoing surveillance for asymptomatic severe disease to determine the optimal timing of surgery, as well as those with infective endocarditis, should be collected. Operative data, including details of cardiopulmonary bypass, type of prosthesis, whether the procedure involved repair or replacement, and any concomitant aortic surgery, including its extent and the type of prosthesis, need to be collected. Perioperative TOE data need to be documented. Postoperative data on early death (in-hospital and at 30 days), complications including reoperation rate (for bleeding or revision of repair/replacement), transient ischaemic attack/stroke, and renal replacement therapy need to be recorded.

Specific data obtained by TTE for assessing haemodynamic performance before discharge should include transvalvular velocity and mean gradient, effective orifice area, the presence and degree of paraprosthetic regurgitation, and residual AR after repair or replacement.

There should be regular in-house follow-up of patients at approximately 6 weeks and 6 months, and then annually, assessing the durability of the repair using TTE and monitoring growth of the aneurysm and of the arch and descending aorta in patients who have undergone ascending aortic and/or root surgery. In the presence of residual AR, follow-up should follow the guidelines recommended for isolated AR. These data should be presented monthly at internal meetings of the heart valve centre and available for inclusion in national databases and external review by national bodies.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 9.4 Learning curves and volume-outcome relationship</summary><div class="details-content">

The relationship between case volume and surgical outcomes, particularly for major complex cases, is well established and has been examined in several series on valve surgery. However, to date, no specific case volume has been defined as optimal for AV and aortic surgery. High hospital volume may reflect high individual surgeon volumes, but it may also indicate excellent facilities and processes. Risk-standardized mortality rates can vary among hospitals with similar volumes, indicating that infrastructure, team composition, resource allocation, and procedural standardization are relevant factors. The effect of volume has been reported for aortic root replacement when case volume is below 30 per year, with an exponential increase in deaths. Similarly, complex AVr is characterized by a steep learning curve. In a retrospective analysis of 582 patients at 1 centre, increased surgeon experience was associated with shorter cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp times, as well as improved reoperation-free survival. 

Data from institutions implementing new AVr programmes indicate that a surgical team may require between 40 and 60 cases to achieve stable outcomes and efficiency.

## 10. CLINICAL OUTCOMES</summary><div class="details-content">

The long-term results of various primary AVr and VSARR techniques, in terms of survival, AR severity, and re-intervention, are difficult to assess. Studies examining long-term outcomes often have small patient numbers and are heterogeneous in terms of pathology, surgical technique, and outcome reporting. Other confounding factors include the surgeon's experience and whether the procedure was performed as an elective or emergency case.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 10.1 Mechanisms of late failure</summary><div class="details-content">

Late failures may result from progressive structural deterioration of the native valve tissue or ongoing remodelling of the aortic root complex. Over time, even apparently well-repaired cusps can undergo degenerative changes, leading to restricted motion and consequent AR or stenosis, particularly in the presence of BAVs. Patients with HTAD may present with late dilatation of the aortic annulus or distal aorta, which can compromise the durability of the repair. In patients with root pathology, an imperfect initial correction of cusp geometry or inadequate intervention at the annular level may lead to recurrence of AR and valve failure. 

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 10.2 Reimplantation versus remodelling</summary><div class="details-content">

A meta-analysis by Toh and colleagues summarized studies comparing reimplantation with the remodelling technique without annuloplasty. A total of 2433 patients were included in the analysis ([Table 7](#table-7)). Reimplantation was used in 1283 patients, and remodelling was performed in 1150 patients. Patients undergoing reimplantation were younger ($48 \pm 16\text{ years}$ vs $56 \pm 15\text{ years}$). No differences were reported in New York Heart Association III–IV status or ischaemic heart disease. A BAV was present in 16% of the reimplantation cohort versus 15% of the remodelling group. Patients with Marfan syndrome accounted for 27% undergoing reimplantation versus 16% undergoing remodelling. Both cohorts had 4% of patients with previous cardiac surgery. Mean aortic root diameter was similar in both groups ($45 \pm 14\text{ mm}$ vs $44 \pm 14\text{ mm}$, $P = 0.59$). There was no statistically significant difference in the severity of AR ($\ge +1$ grade) between the 2 cohorts (53% vs 59%, respectively, $P = 0.31$). The late mortality rate at $\ge 1\text{ year}$ was similar (10% in reimplantation vs 12% in remodelling, $P = 0.42$). The mean follow-up was $58 \pm 29\text{ months}$ in the reimplantation group and $62 \pm 30\text{ months}$ in the remodelling group. The AV reintervention rates were comparable in both the reimplantation and remodelling cohorts at the 5-year follow-up (4% vs 7%, $P = 0.07$). Postoperative AR of $\ge +2$ grade was significantly lower in reimplantation than in remodelling (5% vs 8%, $P = 0.01$).

The reimplantation technique was also favoured in patients with impaired aortic root integrity, including those with Marfan syndrome, acute type A aortic dissection, and additional annular dilatation. Hanke et al. reported that reimplantation was advised for patients with Marfan syndrome and for those with a large preoperative aortic annulus diameter. In contrast, remodelling was preferred in patients with a smaller annulus diameter because of a lower risk of AR.

Overall, Toh and colleagues' meta-analysis reported that both surgical techniques were comparable and yielded good postoperative outcomes in patients presenting with BAV, Marfan syndrome, aortic root aneurysm, and aortic root dissection. Aside from the postoperative AR at 5 years, other postoperative outcomes (stroke rate, reoperation for bleeding, 30-day mortality, late mortality, early and late AV reintervention) were not significantly different. However, the severity of postoperative AR remained higher in the remodelling group, without affecting the reintervention rate.

### 10.3 Remodelling with ring annuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

In recent years, Lansac et al. proposed a modification to the remodelling technique by adding an extra-aortic ring annuloplasty using a dedicated expansible ring to address annular stabilization. Only a handful of studies have assessed the effect of adding the ring to the remodelling technique and compared it with reimplantation. In a cohort of 486 patients, they demonstrated improved outcomes for reoperation and residual AR, even when compared with a standard Dacron ring. Lenoir et al. set out to compare mid-term outcomes in patients undergoing reimplantation (n = 59) versus remodelling with an extra-aortic annuloplasty ring (n = 83). Among their patients, there was a significantly higher rate of cusp repair during remodelling in the annuloplasty group compared with the reimplantation group (49% vs 25%). At 5 years, overall survival was 100% in both groups. The 5-year freedom from AV reoperation was comparable ($97 \pm 2\%$ in both groups, $P = 0.95$). Furthermore, 5-year freedom from AR greater than 2 or reoperation was $84\% \pm 5\%$ in the remodelling with annuloplasty group versus $83 \pm 6\%$ in the reimplantation group ($P = 0.62$). The mean annular diameter was $24.3 \pm 0.5\text{ mm}$ at 5 years versus $23.6 \pm 0.3\text{ mm}$ at discharge in the remodelling plus ring group ($P = 0.28$) and $24.4 \pm 0.6\text{ mm}$ versus $23.2 \pm 0.3\text{ mm}$, respectively, in the reimplantation group ($P = 0.10$). They reported no differences in the rates of recurrent AR or the need for reoperation, suggesting that remodelling with a ring is at least as effective as the reimplantation technique at mid-term follow-up.

On the other hand, a recent propensity-matched comparison of remodelling plus annuloplasty and reimplantation demonstrated a superiority of reimplantation in terms of the need for reintervention over a median follow-up of 6 years. The 2 techniques had similar results only after exclusion of patients with worse than mild AR immediately after repair.

In the long term, external annuloplasty has not been reported to have significant complications. Immediately after implantation, there may be concerns about the potential risk of coronary distortion and/or tissue lesions at the sub-annular level.

Regarding internal annuloplasty, early outcomes confirm the device's safety. Mid-term clinical outcomes are heterogeneous, with some promising results, with and without root replacement, and others have raised concerns about recurrent AR at 4 years. Potential issues highlighted include the interaction of the valve with the internal rigid ring of predetermined geometry, potential left ventricular obstruction by the subvalvular ring, incomplete STJ stabilization and ring dehiscence. To ameliorate these risks, secondary stabilization of the internal ring by externalizing sutures has been proposed. Long-term results are needed to assess outcomes. 

</div></details>

### 10.4 Other factors affecting late outcomes</summary><div class="details-content">

The AR, especially with eccentric jets, often requires cusp repair techniques. However, this approach has a higher rate of late failure. Schäfers and colleagues have been instrumental in emphasizing the importance of correcting cusp prolapse during VSARR. They have developed a calliper to provide a more systematic method of assessing cusp prolapse intraoperatively. They have reported significant improvements in VSARR durability with the systematic use of the calliper.

Patients with BAV have a higher tendency towards late failure. In a study by Esaki et al. of 282 patients undergoing reimplantation, the presence of a BAV was a significant predictor of AR and annular dilatation at follow-up. In addition, postoperative residual AR appears to predict later and progressive AR.

## 11. GAPS IN KNOWLEDGE AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

Currently, there is a lack of large-scale, prospective, randomized trials and registries with long-term follow-up comparing different aortic valve repair techniques. Aortic valve repair usually includes TAV and BAV with normal or dilated root/ascending aorta. Comparing results is generally difficult. In general terms, reporting should follow a precise categorization of AV phenotypes and the different mechanisms of the disease. In this respect, for example, different BAV phenotypes should follow a recent classification to facilitate comparison of similar anatomical conditions. To date, such reports are not yet available. There is also a good amount of available data on both VSARR techniques, whereas much less is available for primary AVr, whether tricuspid or bicuspid. A recent scoring system, if confirmed with further validation, might in the future help in the decision making process of whether to repair or replace a given valve. Another gap is the absence of data reporting on the valves scheduled for repair and finally replaced, whether based on intraoperative contraindications or on failed repair.

Most surgeons refrain from replacing the root if its dimensions are preserved, depending on the patient's age and body surface area. Mid- to long-term observations have demonstrated the stability of the aortic root with this approach, particularly when a circular annuloplasty is used. Recently, more liberal root replacement has been proposed for annular dilatation and/or commissural re-orientation in asymmetrical BAVs. The advantage of this approach over isolated cusp repair and concomitant annuloplasty in improving repair durability remains unknown.

Certainly, the future direction is towards increased diffusion and popularization of primary AVr and VSARR to reduce the burden of valve replacement and Bentall operations performed in patients with AR. In this regard, global monitoring of the rate of repair/sparing compared with replacement is needed. With time and experience, it is also possible to expand the age limit for the application of VSARR or primary AVr. As confidence in the various techniques increases, the decision to replace the valve will be based on the intrinsic characteristics of the valve rather than on the patient's age, in a manner similar to that currently done for mitral valve repair.

</div></details>

## 12. CONCLUSIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

This expert consensus document summarizes and critically reviews current knowledge of the indications, techniques, and management of primary AVr and VSARR across diverse patient populations and valve pathologies. Aortic valve repair encompasses procedures that preserve native valve function, offering improved quality of life and reduced long-term valve-related complications compared with replacement. Proper patient selection, meticulous preoperative imaging, and intraoperative assessment are fundamental to the success of AVr. Images from TTE and TOE, supplemented by CT, provide critical anatomical and functional data to guide surgical decisions. It is essential to recognize that the mechanism of AR differs significantly between TAV and BAV, necessitating tailored repair strategies. Standardized surgical approaches addressing annular dilation and cusp pathology enhance AVr durability. Both VSARR techniques, i.e., reimplantation and remodelling plus annuloplasty, yield comparable mid- to long-term outcomes. Nonetheless, AVr durability is influenced by underlying valve morphology, cusp tissue quality, and the repair technique employed. Effective postoperative management, including intraoperative TOE verification of successful repair and regular long-term surveillance, is crucial for the prompt detection and management of complications or recurrent AR.
