# Mechanical or Biologic Prostheses for Aortic-Valve and Mitral-Valve Replacement (NEJM 2017)

> **Landmark statewide population-based cohort study comparing 15-year long-term mortality, reoperation, stroke, and bleeding risks between inverse-probability-weighted cohorts receiving mechanical versus biologic prostheses for aortic-valve and mitral-valve replacement across different age categories.**

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Primary Mortality Findings Across Valve Positions and Age Strata
* **Aortic-Valve Replacement (AVR):**
  * *Ages 45 to 54 Years:* Implantation of a mechanical prosthesis was associated with a significant long-term survival advantage compared to a biologic prosthesis (15-year mortality: 26.4% mechanical vs. 30.6% biologic; Hazard Ratio [HR]: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.02–1.48; p = 0.03; multivariable HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.03–1.52; p = 0.02; hospital random effect HR: 1.28; 95% CI: 1.08–1.53; p = 0.005).
  * *Restricted Mean Survival Time (RMST) at 15 Years in AVR (45–54 yr):* Biologic prosthesis recipients experienced an average loss of 167.9 days of life (95% CI: -296.5 to -39.3; p = 0.01; ratio of restricted mean time lost: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.05–1.42; p = 0.008).
  * *Ages 55 to 64 Years:* Long-term mortality did not differ significantly between prosthesis types (15-year mortality: 32.1% mechanical vs. 36.1% biologic; HR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.91–1.18; p = 0.60; RMST difference: -5.4 days; 95% CI: -101.8 to 91.0; p = 0.91).
  * *Continuous Age Threshold for AVR:* Spline modeling demonstrated that the mortality benefit of mechanical AVR was concentrated in younger patients and ceased to be statistically significant at ~ 53–54 years of age.
* **Mitral-Valve Replacement (MVR):**
  * *Ages 40 to 49 Years:* Mechanical MVR conferred a marked and highly significant mortality advantage over biologic prostheses (15-year mortality: 27.1% mechanical vs. 44.1% biologic; HR: 1.88; 95% CI: 1.35–2.63; p < 0.001; multivariable HR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.22–2.27; p = 0.001; hospital random effect HR: 1.86; 95% CI: 1.45–2.38; p < 0.001).
  * *RMST Loss in Young MVR (40–49 yr):* Patients receiving a biologic mitral prosthesis lost an average of 619.6 days of life over 15 years (95% CI: -891.5 to -347.6; p < 0.001; ratio of restricted mean time lost: 1.70; 95% CI: 1.39–2.08; p < 0.001).
  * *Ages 50 to 69 Years:* Mechanical MVR maintained a significant long-term survival benefit throughout the entire fifth and sixth decades of life (15-year mortality: 45.3% mechanical vs. 50.0% biologic; HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.04–1.30; p = 0.01; multivariable HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02–1.27; p = 0.02; hospital random effect HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.07–1.28; p < 0.001; RMST difference: -191.3 days; 95% CI: -298.1 to -84.5; p < 0.001).
  * *Continuous Age Threshold for MVR:* In contrast to AVR, the mortality benefit of a mechanical mitral prosthesis persisted up to 70 years of age.
  * *Ages 70 to 79 Years:* Equivalent 15-year mortality between prosthesis types (77.3% mechanical vs. 78.3% biologic; HR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.93–1.08; p = 0.97; RMST difference: 10.4 days; 95% CI: -101.6 to 122.5; p = 0.86).

### Reoperation Hazard and Structural Valve Deterioration
* **Reoperation Risk Disparity:** Cumulative incidence of reoperation was substantially higher with biologic prostheses in both valve positions and across all evaluated age groups.
* **Early Curve Divergence:** Reoperation rates began diverging early, between 6 and 8 years post-implantation, exactly coinciding with the emergence of survival divergence between cohorts.
* **High Reoperative Surgical Mortality:**
  * 30-day mortality for reoperative aortic-valve replacement was 7.1%.
  * 30-day mortality for reoperative mitral-valve replacement reached an alarming 14.0%, providing a definitive mechanistic explanation for the severe excess mortality in younger patients receiving bioprosthetic mitral valves.

### Hemorrhagic and Cerebrovascular Morbidity Trade-Offs
* **Bleeding Hazards:** Mechanical valves in both positions were associated with a significantly higher cumulative incidence of major bleeding across long-term follow-up as a result of mandatory lifelong vitamin K antagonist anticoagulation.
* **Stroke Incidences:** Aortic mechanical valves were associated with higher stroke rates in patients aged 45–54 years, and mitral mechanical valves were associated with higher stroke rates in patients aged 50–69 years.
* **Impact of Morbidity on Late Death:** When analyzed as time-varying covariates, both major bleeding and stroke episodes independently increased the subsequent hazard of death, illustrating the trade-off that penalizes mechanical valves in elderly patients who have elevated baseline vascular fragility.

### Secular Utilization Shift (1996 to 2013)
* **Surge in Bioprosthetic Utilization:** Over the 18-year study window, bioprosthetic valve implantation escalated dramatically:
  * For AVR: Increased from 11.5% in 1996 to 51.6% in 2013 (p < 0.001).
  * For MVR: Increased from 16.8% in 1996 to 53.7% in 2013 (p < 0.001).
* **Disconnect from Clinical Evidence:** This widespread practice change occurred across all age cohorts, including patients under 50 and 60 years of age, directly challenging existing guidelines and preceding robust comparative effectiveness data.

### Guideline Repercussions
* **Decoupling Aortic and Mitral Practice Guidelines:** Contemporary ACC/AHA and ESC/EACTS guidelines historical practice of combining AVR and MVR recommendations under identical age thresholds (mechanical < 50, either 50–70, biologic > 70) is clinically flawed.
* **Position-Specific Decision Making:** The upper age limit favoring mechanical prostheses should be decoupled: ~ 50–55 years for the aortic position, but extended up to 70 years for the mitral position.

## Introduction</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinically significant aortic-valve disease or mitral-valve disease affects 2.5% of the general population in the United States, and surgical valve replacement provides definitive symptomatic relief and substantially prolongs survival in patients with severe valvular dysfunction. When surgical replacement is indicated, surgeons and patients must select between a mechanical prosthesis and a biologic tissue valve. Each prosthesis class is characterized by a distinct balance of lifetime risks and benefits:
* Biologic prosthetic valves carry a substantially higher long-term risk of reoperation because of progressive structural valve deterioration (SVD).
* Mechanical valves possess superior hemodynamic durability but necessitate lifelong systemic anticoagulation, which confers elevated risks of major hemorrhage and thromboembolism.

Practice guidelines have historically failed to distinguish between prosthesis selection in the aortic-valve and mitral-valve positions. Professional society guidelines have recommended mechanical valves in persons younger than 50 years of age, biologic prosthetic valves in persons older than 70 years of age, and either prosthesis type in patients 50 to 70 years of age.

However, these guideline recommendations are rooted primarily in data from randomized controlled trials conducted more than three decades ago with first-generation prosthetic designs that are now obsolete. Contemporary observational registries examining patients aged 50 to 69 years reported equivalent survival between valve types, which catalyzed an accelerating clinical shift toward biologic prostheses in younger patients. Nevertheless, prior studies may have been underpowered to detect moderate but clinically meaningful differences in long-term mortality. The investigators conducted a statewide retrospective cohort study spanning 18 years in California to evaluate long-term mortality and complication rates following mechanical versus biologic valve replacement.

## Methods</summary><div class="details-content">

### Data Sources and Patient Tracking
The investigators utilized comprehensive inpatient administrative data collected by the California Office of Statewide Health Planning and Development (OSHPD). This statewide database captures all patient encounters at nonfederal, acute-care inpatient hospitals throughout California. Long-term all-cause mortality was determined by deterministic linkage of unique patient record linkage numbers to the California Vital Statistics Master File, which represents the authoritative annual state death registry.

Longitudinal clinical follow-up across repeat hospitalizations was established by matching record linkage numbers and birth years across encounters:
* Deterministic matching algorithms tracked readmissions, procedural complications, reoperations, and subsequent interventions across all inpatient institutions in the state.
* The primary study end point was long-term all-cause mortality through up to 15 years of follow-up.
* Secondary end points included 30-day perioperative mortality and cumulative incidences of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, major bleeding events, and surgical reoperation.
* In the absence of an event of interest, patient records were censored at the study termination date on December 31, 2013.

### Study Population and End Points
The study cohort comprised adult patients (aged 18 years or older) who underwent primary aortic-valve replacement or primary mitral-valve replacement in California between 1996 and 2013. Patients were excluded if they had undergone previous valve replacement, had concomitant aortic and mitral valve surgery during the index operation, or had complex congenital cardiac anomalies.

To reflect anatomical and clinical distinctions, the study evaluated two primary procedural cohorts:
1. **Isolated Aortic-Valve Replacement:** Patients undergoing isolated AVR without concomitant major procedures. Patients were stratified into two target age categories based on clinical equipoise: 45 to 54 years and 55 to 64 years.
2. **Mitral-Valve Replacement:** Patients undergoing MVR, analyzed with or without concomitant coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), tricuspid-valve repair, or surgical atrial fibrillation ablation. Patients were stratified into three age categories: 40 to 49 years, 50 to 69 years, and 70 to 79 years.

### Statistical Analysis and Inverse Probability Weighting
The study was powered to achieve at least 85% statistical power at a two-sided alpha level of 0.05 to detect a between-group hazard ratio of 1.15 for mortality at 15 years in patients aged 55 to 64 years undergoing AVR and patients aged 50 to 69 years undergoing MVR.

To minimize confounding by indication inherent to observational treatment selection, the investigators implemented inverse probability weighting (IPW):
* Non-parsimonious multivariable logistic regression models were developed within each age stratum to calculate propensity scores for receiving a biologic versus a mechanical prosthesis.
* Weights were assigned as the inverse of the probability of receiving the prosthesis actually implanted, creating pseudo-populations balanced across baseline demographic, clinical, and operative covariates.
* Balance was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMD), with values < 10% (0.10) indicating excellent covariate balance.
* Weighted Cox proportional-hazards models and restricted mean survival time (RMST) analyses were utilized to estimate marginal hazard ratios and differences in survival days gained or lost through 15 years.
* Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard competing-risk models evaluated cumulative incidences of stroke, bleeding, and reoperation, treating all-cause death as a competing risk.

## Results</summary><div class="details-content">

### Study Population and Secular Trends
Between 1996 and 2013, a total of 9942 patients underwent isolated primary AVR (6097 mechanical, 3845 biologic) and 15,503 patients underwent primary MVR (9982 mechanical, 5521 biologic). At baseline, patients receiving biologic prostheses were older, had higher rates of comorbid medical conditions, and were operated on more frequently in recent calendar years ([Table 1](#table-09-01)).

Following inverse probability weighting, excellent covariate balance was achieved across all age strata (standardized mean differences < 0.10 for virtually all baseline characteristics). Median clinical follow-up for AVR was 5.0 years in biologic recipients and 8.2 years in mechanical recipients; for MVR, median follow-up was 4.6 years in biologic recipients and 7.6 years in mechanical recipients.

Over the 18-year study window, a profound secular evolution occurred in surgical practice:
* AVR with a biologic prosthesis rose from 11.5% of cases in 1996 to 51.6% in 2013 (p < 0.001).
* MVR with a biologic prosthesis rose from 16.8% of cases in 1996 to 53.7% in 2013 (p < 0.001).
* Total annual volume of isolated AVR increased, whereas annual volume of MVR declined as surgical mitral repair expanded concurrently.

### Mortality after Aortic-Valve Replacement
In patients undergoing isolated AVR, perioperative 30-day mortality did not differ significantly between mechanical and biologic valve recipients in either age cohort.

However, long-term 15-year survival demonstrated marked differences based on patient age ([Table 2](#table-09-02) and [Fig. 1](#fig-09-01)):
* **Patients 45 to 54 Years of Age:**
  * 15-year mortality was significantly higher among recipients of biologic prostheses compared with mechanical valves (30.6% vs. 26.4%; HR: 1.23; 95% CI: 1.02–1.48; p = 0.03).
  * Multivariable adjustment (HR: 1.25; 95% CI: 1.03–1.52; p = 0.02) and hospital random-effects modeling (HR: 1.28; 95% CI: 1.08–1.53; p = 0.005) confirmed the robustness of this survival advantage.
  * Restricted mean survival time over 15 years demonstrated a significant deficit of 167.9 days (95% CI: -296.5 to -39.3; p = 0.01) among bioprosthesis recipients.
* **Patients 55 to 64 Years of Age:**
  * Mortality at 15 years was equivalent between biologic and mechanical prostheses (36.1% vs. 32.1%; HR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.91–1.18; p = 0.60).
  * Multivariable-adjusted HR was 1.05 (95% CI: 0.92–1.21; p = 0.47), and RMST difference was only -5.4 days (95% CI: -101.8 to 91.0; p = 0.91).

### Complications after Aortic-Valve Replacement
The cumulative incidence of stroke and bleeding events displayed prominent differences by valve class across long-term follow-up:
* **Stroke:** Recipients of a biologic aortic valve had a significantly lower cumulative incidence of stroke than mechanical valve recipients in the 45-to-54-year age cohort.
* **Bleeding:** Biologic valve recipients experienced significantly lower cumulative bleeding incidences in both the 45–54 and 55–64 age categories compared to mechanical prostheses requiring anticoagulation.
* **Mortality Impact:** When incorporated into multivariable Cox proportional-hazards models as time-varying covariates, both stroke and major bleeding were independent risk factors significantly associated with late death.
* **Reoperation Rates:** The hazard of reoperation was substantially higher among patients receiving a biologic aortic prosthesis, with the risk concentrated heavily in younger patients. Perioperative 30-day mortality following reoperative AVR was 7.1%.

### Age-Dependent Hazard Dynamics
When age was modeled as a continuous spline variable, the relative mortality benefit associated with mechanical AVR demonstrated a progressive attenuation as patient age increased ([Fig. 2](#fig-09-02)).

The hazard ratio for death with a biologic versus mechanical prosthesis crossed the line of clinical equipoise (HR = 1.00) at approximately 53 to 54 years of age in the aortic position:
* Below age 53, mechanical AVR provided superior long-term survival.
* Above age 54, the point estimate remained close to unity with overlapping confidence intervals, confirming equivalent survival.

### Mortality after Mitral-Valve Replacement
Among patients undergoing MVR, perioperative 30-day mortality was equivalent between prosthesis classes in patients aged 50–69 and 70–79 years. However, in patients aged 40 to 49 years, biologic prostheses were associated with higher 30-day perioperative mortality (5.6% vs. 2.2%; Odds Ratio [OR]: 2.62; 95% CI: 1.28–5.38).

Long-term 15-year survival demonstrated a striking survival advantage for mechanical prostheses extending across both younger and middle-aged cohorts ([Table 3](#table-09-03) and [Fig. 3](#fig-09-03)):
* **Patients 40 to 49 Years of Age:**
  * 15-year mortality was catastrophic with a biologic prosthesis (44.1% vs. 27.1%; HR: 1.88; 95% CI: 1.35–2.63; p < 0.001).
  * Multivariable HR was 1.66 (95% CI: 1.22–2.27; p = 0.001), and hospital random-effects HR was 1.86 (95% CI: 1.45–2.38; p < 0.001).
  * RMST demonstrated a massive survival loss of 619.6 days (95% CI: -891.5 to -347.6; p < 0.001) over 15 years with biologic valves.
* **Patients 50 to 69 Years of Age:**
  * 15-year mortality remained significantly higher with a biologic prosthesis (50.0% vs. 45.3%; HR: 1.16; 95% CI: 1.04–1.30; p = 0.01).
  * Robustness was verified by multivariable adjustment (HR: 1.14; 95% CI: 1.02–1.27; p = 0.02) and hospital random-effects modeling (HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 1.07–1.28; p < 0.001).
  * RMST confirmed an average survival deficit of 191.3 days (95% CI: -298.1 to -84.5; p < 0.001) in bioprosthesis recipients.
  * Subdivision into 50–59 and 60–69 decade cohorts revealed consistent RMST survival deficits favoring mechanical valves in both individual decades.
* **Patients 70 to 79 Years of Age:**
  * Mortality at 15 years was equivalent between valve types (78.3% biologic vs. 77.3% mechanical; HR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.93–1.08; p = 0.97; RMST difference: 10.4 days; p = 0.86).

### Complications after Mitral-Valve Replacement
Complication patterns following MVR mirrored those observed in AVR but were amplified by the greater hemodynamic stress of the mitral position:
* **Stroke:** Biologic mitral prostheses were associated with a significantly lower cumulative incidence of stroke during follow-up among patients aged 50 to 69 years compared to mechanical valves, though differences in younger and older groups were not statistically significant.
* **Bleeding:** Bioprosthetic MVR recipients had lower cumulative incidences of major bleeding in both the 50–69 and 70–79 age groups.
* **Reoperation:** Cumulative reoperation was dramatically higher among bioprosthesis recipients. Younger patients experienced structural valve deterioration much sooner than older patients, with a high proportion requiring reoperation within 10 years of initial implantation.
* **Catastrophic Reoperative Mitral Mortality:** Perioperative 30-day mortality following reoperative MVR was 14.0%—twice that of reoperative AVR—constituting a major contributor to the late excess mortality in bioprosthetic mitral cohorts.

## Discussion</summary><div class="details-content">

### Valve-Specific Survival Discrepancies
In this comprehensive statewide population-based cohort study, the long-term survival impact of prosthesis selection diverged fundamentally based on anatomical valve position:
* In the mitral position, mechanical prostheses were associated with a significant survival advantage up to 70 years of age.
* In the aortic position, the survival advantage of mechanical prostheses was restricted to patients younger than 55 years of age.
* In both valve positions, mechanical prostheses achieved a dramatically lower risk of reoperation at the expense of higher cumulative bleeding hazards and, in specific cohorts, higher stroke rates.

Historically, prosthesis selection has been guided by balancing the lifetime risks of anticoagulation-related hemorrhage against the risk of repeat operation for structural valve deterioration. However, this clinical paradigm fundamentally presupposes that long-term mortality between prosthesis classes is equivalent. The findings of Goldstone and colleagues decisively overturn this assumption for patients younger than 70 years receiving mitral replacements and patients younger than 55 years receiving aortic replacements.

### Mechanisms of Divergent Mortality and Reoperation Hazards
The profound disparity in survival between aortic and mitral prostheses is largely explained by structural valve durability and reoperative mortality:
* Accelerated mechanical fatigue and biological calcification affect bioprostheses in the mitral position more severely than in the aortic position due to higher peak systolic closure pressures across the mitral annulus ($dP/dt$).
* Reoperation rates diverged as early as 6 to 8 years postoperatively in both valve positions, coinciding precisely with the divergence of survival curves.
* The 30-day mortality of reoperative mitral replacement was 14.0% (compared with 7.1% for reoperative AVR). A reoperation rate that exceeds 10% to 20% in the presence of a 14% perioperative reoperative mortality introduces an unavoidable survival penalty for bioprosthetic mitral valves in younger patients whose life expectancy exceeds 10 to 15 years.

### Bleeding and Thromboembolic Burden of Mechanical Valves
While mechanical valves prevent structural failure, they demand uninterrupted anticoagulation:
* Mechanical valve recipients faced higher cumulative rates of major hemorrhage and thromboembolism across follow-up.
* In multivariable time-varying models, acute stroke and major bleeding episodes were potent predictors of late death.
* In elderly patients (aged 70 years and older), elevated baseline vascular fragility and competing noncardiac mortality render the hemorrhagic hazard of anticoagulation equal to or greater than the threat of structural valve deterioration, explaining why older patients derive no survival benefit from mechanical prostheses.
* Conversely, in younger patients, the absolute hazard of anticoagulation-related bleeding is low, and its survival impact is completely overshadowed by the lethal consequences of bioprosthetic failure.

### Population Differences and Registry Comparisons
The findings reconcile conflicting results from prior regional registries. A New York state registry by Chiang and colleagues found equivalent survival between bioprosthetic and mechanical AVR in patients aged 50 to 69 years, whereas a Swedish SWEDEHEART registry by Glaser and colleagues demonstrated a marked survival benefit for mechanical AVR in patients aged 50 to 59 years.

These discrepancies reflect baseline population health characteristics:
* Patients in the SWEDEHEART registry had substantially lower baseline burdens of hypertension, diabetes, and heart failure than those in the New York cohort.
* In healthier populations with longer life expectancies, the survival benefits of durable mechanical prostheses manifest prominently, whereas in multimorbid cohorts, competing cardiovascular and noncardiac mortalities diminish the relative advantage of mechanical valves.
* Clinicians must synthesize individual patient life expectancy and comorbidities, rather than chronological age alone, when selecting valve substitutes.

### Study Limitations
Several methodological limitations must be considered when interpreting this study:
* The investigation utilized an administrative inpatient discharge database rather than a dedicated clinical registry, lacking granular hemodynamic parameters such as preoperative ejection fraction, valve effective orifice area, specific prosthesis model, and post-discharge international normalized ratio (INR) stability.
* While inverse probability weighting achieved balance across measured baseline covariates, unmeasured confounding—particularly subtle clinical frailty influencing the choice of a bioprosthesis—cannot be completely excluded.
* Over the 18-year timeframe, secular advancements occurred in bioprosthetic design, surgical techniques, myocardial protection, and catheter-based reintervention options.
* Emerging transcatheter valve-in-valve (ViV) procedures may lower the future procedural risk of reoperation for degenerated bioprostheses, which could alter the risk-benefit equation in coming decades.

### Conclusions and Guideline Repercussions
In conclusion, this population-level comparative study demonstrates that mechanical mitral valves are associated with superior long-term survival compared to biologic valves in patients up to 70 years of age, whereas the survival advantage of mechanical aortic valves is limited to patients younger than 55 years of age.

These results indicate that clinical practice guidelines should decouple recommendations for prosthesis selection in the aortic and mitral positions:
* For aortic-valve replacement, biologic prostheses can be safely recommended starting at age 55 in patients without contraindications to reoperation.
* For mitral-valve replacement, the threshold for biologic prostheses should remain at 70 years of age, and the current clinical drift toward bioprostheses in middle-aged mitral patients should be strongly reconsidered.
