## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Major Paradigm Shifts in the 2026 ESC Heart Failure Guidelines
The 2026 ESC Guidelines introduce profound structural and conceptual updates reflecting landmark clinical trials published since 2021:

1. **Redefined LVEF Phenotypes — Expansion of HFrEF and Abolition of HFmrEF:**
   - **Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF)** is now expanded to encompass all patients with **LVEF $\le 50\%$** (previously $< 40\%$). Post hoc analyses and dedicated trials demonstrate that patients with LVEF $41\text{--}49\%$ derive substantial survival and morbidity benefit from the same foundational neurohormonal therapies as those with LVEF $\le 40\%$.
   - **Heart Failure with Mildly Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFmrEF)** has been **officially abolished** as a distinct entity, streamlining clinical decision-making.
   - **Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF)** is now defined as symptoms/signs of HF with **LVEF $> 50\%$**, accompanied by objective evidence of elevated filling pressures or structural alterations.

2. **Adoption of the Universal Stages of Heart Failure (Stages A to D):**
   - **Stage A (At Risk for HF):** Individuals with risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, ASCVD, obesity, cardiotoxic exposure) but no current or prior symptoms, physical signs, or structural/functional cardiac disease or biomarker elevation.
   - **Stage B (Pre-Heart Failure):** Asymptomatic individuals with objective evidence of structural heart disease (e.g., LV hypertrophy, chamber dilatation, wall motion abnormalities, valvular disease), increased filling pressures, or persistently elevated natriuretic peptides / cardiac troponins.
   - **Stage C (Heart Failure):** Patients with current or previous symptoms and signs caused by structural/functional cardiac abnormalities.
   - **Stage D (Advanced Heart Failure):** Patients with severe, refractory symptoms (NYHA class III–IV) despite optimal therapy, frequent hospitalizations, and persistent intolerance to neurohormonal blockers, requiring specialized interventions (LVAD, heart transplantation, palliative inotropes).

3. **Decompensated Heart Failure (DHF) Replaces "Acute Heart Failure":**
   - The term **Decompensated Heart Failure (DHF)** officially supersedes "acute heart failure", recognizing that almost all acute presentations represent worsening of an underlying, chronic progressive syndrome rather than isolated acute episodes.

4. **New Nomenclature for Heart Failure Therapies:**
   - **Foundational Medical Therapy (FMT):** The four core pharmacological pillars ("quad-therapy") proven to modify disease trajectory and improve survival: ARNI/ACEi, evidence-based beta-blocker, steroidal MRA, and SGLT2 inhibitor.
   - **Additional Medical Therapy (AMT):** Tailored agents based on specific clinical profiles (vericiguat, ivabradine, GLP-1 receptor agonists, non-steroidal MRAs, digoxin, potassium binders).
   - **Guideline-Directed Device and Interventional Therapy (GDIT):** CRT, ICD, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER), catheter ablation, and mechanical circulatory support.

5. **Breakthrough Evidence in Obesity and Cardiometabolic HFpEF:**
   - **GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide)** are given high-priority recommendations for patients with HFpEF and obesity (BMI $\ge 30\text{ kg/m}^2$) based on STEP-HFpEF and SUMMIT trials, demonstrating marked symptom reduction, functional improvement, and reduced HF hospitalization.

6. **Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support (tMCS) in Cardiogenic Shock:**
   - Following the **DanGer Shock** trial, routine use of microaxial flow pump (Impella) in selected patients with STEMI-related cardiogenic shock demonstrates reduced 180-day mortality, whereas routine IABP is Class III (no benefit).

7. **Revolution in Cardiac Amyloidosis (ATTR-CA):**
   - Expanded Class I recommendations for **TTR silencers (vutrisiran)** and **stabilizers (tafamidis, acoramidis)** to reduce cardiovascular hospitalization and all-cause mortality across NYHA classes I–III.

## 1. Preamble</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinical practice guidelines summarize and evaluate all available evidence on a particular issue at the time of the writing process, with the overarching aim of assisting healthcare professionals in selecting the best management strategies for an individual patient suffering from a given condition.

These 2026 Guidelines on the management of Heart Failure were developed by an interdisciplinary Task Force appointed by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), with direct contribution from the Heart Failure Association (HFA) of the ESC, and endorsed by the European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS).

The recommendations are grounded on an extensive synthesis of randomized clinical trials, observational cohorts, systematic reviews, and international registry data published up to 2026. Great care has been taken to avoid biases, manage potential conflicts of interest, and formulate concise, practical guidance. The Guideline provides actionable clinical algorithms structured around the patient pathway, ensuring equitable, evidence-based, and multidisciplinary delivery of cardiovascular healthcare.

## 2. Introduction</summary><div class="details-content">

Heart failure (HF) remains one of the greatest global healthcare challenges of the 21st century. Despite major advances in medical and device therapies, HF is associated with substantial mortality, frequent hospitalizations, and severe impairment of quality of life.

### 2.1 Rationale for the 2026 Revision
Since the release of the 2021 ESC Guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of acute and chronic heart failure and the 2023 Focused Update, transformative evidence has reshaped clinical practice:
1. **Therapeutic parity between mildly reduced and reduced LVEF:** Re-evaluations of the major neurohormonal trials (PARADIGM-HF, DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced, EMPEROR-Preserved, DELIVER) demonstrated consistent benefit across the ejection fraction continuum up to $50\%$.
2. **Metabolic pharmacotherapies in HFpEF and obesity:** Landmark trials (EMPEROR-Preserved, DELIVER, STEP-HFpEF, SUMMIT) established SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists as disease-modifying therapies in HFpEF.
3. **Acute care transitions:** The terminology of "acute heart failure" has been replaced by Decompensated Heart Failure (DHF), emphasizing aggressive early decongestion, inpatient optimization of foundational therapies, and structured pre-discharge planning.
4. **Mechanical circulatory support trials:** The DanGer Shock trial demonstrated that microaxial flow pumps reduce 180-day mortality in STEMI-related cardiogenic shock, redefining the role of temporary MCS.
5. **Genetic and infiltrative cardiomyopathies:** Breakthrough amyloidosis therapies (tafamidis, vutrisiran, acoramidis) and expanding genetics require early systematic screening.

## 3. Definition, Epidemiology, and Classifications</summary><div class="details-content">

### 3.1 Definition of Heart Failure
Heart failure is a complex clinical syndrome comprising cardinal symptoms (breathlessness, fatigue, ankle swelling) and physical signs (elevated jugular venous pressure, pulmonary crackles, peripheral oedema) caused by structural and/or functional abnormalities of the heart, resulting in elevated intracardiac pressures and/or inadequate cardiac output at rest and/or during physical exertion.

To establish a definitive diagnosis of heart failure, clinical symptoms must be corroborated by:
1. **Objective evidence of cardiac dysfunction:** Assessed primarily via transthoracic echocardiography (abnormal ejection fraction, chamber enlargement, diastolic dysfunction, significant valve disease).
2. **Elevated circulating biomarkers:** Natriuretic peptides (B-type natriuretic peptide [BNP] $\ge 35\text{ pg/mL}$ or N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide [NT-proBNP] $\ge 125\text{ pg/mL}$ in outpatients).
3. **Objective evidence of congestion:** Pulmonary or systemic congestion documented by chest radiography, lung ultrasound (B-lines), or invasive hemodynamic assessment.

Asymptomatic individuals with cardiac dysfunction or elevated biomarkers do not have heart failure; they are classified as having **pre-heart failure (stage B)**.

### 3.2 Epidemiology of Heart Failure
- **Prevalence:** Approximately $1\%\text{--}3\%$ of the adult population in high-income nations, rising steeply to $> 10\%$ in individuals aged $\ge 70$ years.
- **Incidence:** Estimated at $1\text{--}5$ cases per 1,000 person-years. While age-adjusted incidence of HFrEF has stabilized or slightly declined due to improved post-MI care, the incidence of HFpEF continues to escalate rapidly due to population aging and the obesity/metabolic syndrome epidemic.
- **Prognosis:** Despite therapeutic strides, 1-year and 5-year mortality following an initial diagnosis remain approximately $15\%\text{--}20\%$ and $50\%$, respectively. Hospitalization for decompensation marks a critical inflection point, carrying a $10\%\text{--}15\%$ in-hospital mortality and a $25\%\text{--}30\%$ 30-day readmission rate.

### 3.3 Classifications of Heart Failure

#### 3.3.1 Classification Based on Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF)
The 2026 Guidelines introduce a modernized, binary ejection fraction framework:

| Phenotype | LVEF Range | Key Diagnostic Criteria | Core Therapeutic Focus |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HFrEF** (Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction) | **$\le 50\%$** | Symptoms $\pm$ signs; LVEF $\le 50\%$ on echocardiography | Universal Foundational Medical Therapy (**FMT quad-therapy**: ARNI/ACEi, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2i), loop diuretics for congestion, device therapy (ICD, CRT). |
| **HFpEF** (Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction) | **$> 50\%$** | Symptoms $\pm$ signs; LVEF $> 50\%$; objective evidence of structural/functional abnormality (LVH, LAE, elevated $E/e'$) and/or elevated natriuretic peptides | **SGLT2 inhibitors** (Class I), GLP-1 RA for obesity, MRA/ARNI, loop diuretics, aggressive comorbidity management. |

> [!NOTE]
> **Abolition of HFmrEF:** The previously designated intermediate category of "heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction" (LVEF $41\text{--}49\%$) has been merged into HFrEF. Clinical trials demonstrated identical response to foundational neurohormonal blockade in this group, eliminating artificial therapeutic barriers.

#### 3.3.2 Classification Based on Heart Failure Stages
The ESC officially adopts the universal four-stage classification (A–D):
- **Stage A (At Risk):** Individuals at high risk for HF (hypertension, diabetes, ASCVD, obesity, cardiotoxin exposure) but without structural heart disease, elevated biomarkers, or symptoms.
- **Stage B (Pre-Heart Failure):** Asymptomatic individuals with structural heart disease (e.g., LVEF $\le 50\%$, LVH, regional wall motion abnormality, moderate/severe valvular disease), increased filling pressures, or elevated natriuretic peptides / troponins.
- **Stage C (Symptomatic Heart Failure):** Current or previous symptoms and signs caused by structural/functional cardiac abnormalities.
- **Stage D (Advanced Heart Failure):** Severe symptoms (NYHA III–IV) refractory to guideline-directed therapies, marked functional limitation, recurrent hospitalizations, and medication intolerance.

![Figure 2: Heart failure stages and classifications](images/fig02_stages_classifications_hf.jpg)

**Figure 2: Heart failure stages and classifications.** Progressive continuum from Stage A (at risk) through Stage B (pre-HF) to Stage C (symptomatic HF) and Stage D (advanced HF), illustrating clinical milestones, diagnostic biomarkers, and tailored therapeutic strategies.

#### 3.3.3 New Nomenclature for Heart Failure Therapies
To foster structured prescribing and prevent clinical inertia, therapies are categorized into three hierarchical tiers:
1. **Foundational Medical Therapy (FMT):** Universal first-line "quad-therapy" proven to reduce mortality and hospitalizations in HFrEF:
   - Renin-Angiotensin System Inhibitor: Angiotensin Receptor–Neprilysin Inhibitor (**ARNI**) preferred, or ACE inhibitor (**ACE-I**), or ARB if intolerant.
   - Beta-Blocker: Evidence-based agents (**bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate**, nebivolol).
   - Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonist (**MRA**): Steroidal (spironolactone, eplerenone).
   - Sodium–Glucose Cotransporter-2 Inhibitor (**SGLT2-I**): **Dapagliflozin or empagliflozin**.
2. **Additional Medical Therapy (AMT):** Tailored add-on pharmacotherapies:
   - **Loop Diuretics:** Titrated to achieve and maintain euvolemia.
   - **GLP-1 Receptor Agonists (semaglutide, tirzepatide):** Indicated for HFpEF with obesity.
   - **Vericiguat:** Soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator for worsening HFrEF despite FMT.
   - **Ivabradine:** For sinus rhythm with resting HR $\ge 70\text{ b.p.m.}$ despite maximally tolerated beta-blockade.
   - **Cardiac Glycosides (digoxin, digitoxin):** In persistent symptoms or AF with rapid ventricular rate.
   - **Potassium Binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate):** Enabling continuation of RAASi and MRA in hyperkalaemia.
3. **Guideline-Directed Device and Interventional Therapy (GDIT):**
   - **ICD:** Primary and secondary prevention of sudden cardiac death.
   - **CRT:** Resynchronization for wide QRS / LBBB and high-degree AV block.
   - **Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair (TEER):** For persistent severe secondary mitral regurgitation.
   - **Catheter Ablation:** For atrial fibrillation in heart failure.
   - **Mechanical Circulatory Support & Transplantation:** For end-stage advanced HF.

## 4. Prevention of Heart Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

Prevention of heart failure requires identifying individuals before overt symptoms emerge, categorizing them into Stage A (at risk) or Stage B (pre-heart failure), and implementing evidence-based lifestyle and pharmacological interventions.

### 4.1 Patients at Risk for Heart Failure (Stage A)
Stage A includes individuals with cardiometabolic risk factors, cardiotoxic exposures, or systemic diseases known to precipitate cardiac dysfunction, but without current or prior HF symptoms, physical signs, structural/functional heart disease, or biomarker elevation.

#### 4.1.1 Cardiometabolic Risk Factors
- **Hypertension:** The single most prevalent attributable risk factor. Long-term observational studies and randomized trials confirm that aggressive blood pressure reduction substantially reduces incident HF. The 2026 Guidelines recommend strict blood pressure control with a **systolic target $< 130\text{ mmHg}$** for patients with hypertension or Stage B HF (**Class I, Level A**).
- **Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM):** SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin) reduce HF hospitalizations and cardiovascular mortality across high-risk diabetes cohorts (**Class I, Level A**). In diabetic kidney disease, the non-steroidal MRA **finerenone** significantly reduces incident HF events (**Class I, Level A**). **GLP-1 receptor agonists** should be considered in T2DM with additional CV risk factors to reduce HF or CV death (**Class IIa, Level A**).
- **Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease (ASCVD):** Statin therapy is recommended for patients with or at high risk of ASCVD to prevent incident HF (**Class IIa, Level A**).
- **Lifestyle & Behavioural Modification:** Structured counselling on smoking cessation, weight management, Mediterranean-style diet, avoidance of heavy alcohol consumption, and physical activity is recommended for all Stage A/B patients (**Class I, Level C**).

Stage B encompasses asymptomatic patients with structural heart disease (asymptomatic LV systolic dysfunction with LVEF $\le 50\%$, LV hypertrophy, chamber dilatation, regional wall motion abnormality, or significant valvular disease), elevated filling pressures, or persistently elevated natriuretic peptides or cardiac troponins.

## 5. Diagnosis of Chronic Heart Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

### 5.1 Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Algorithm
The diagnosis of chronic heart failure requires a comprehensive clinical evaluation integrating history, physical examination, resting 12-lead ECG, circulating natriuretic peptide measurement, and transthoracic echocardiography.

#### Table 7: Symptoms and Signs of Heart Failure
| Category | Typical / More Specific | Less Typical / Less Specific |
|---|---|---|
| **Symptoms** | Breathlessness (dyspnoea on exertion), orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance, fatigue, tiredness, increased time to recover after exercise, ankle swelling | Nocturnal cough, wheezing, weight gain ($> 2\text{ kg/week}$), weight loss (in advanced HF/cachexia), bloating, early satiety, loss of appetite, confusion/dizziness, syncope, bendopnoea |
| **Signs** | Elevated jugular venous pressure (JVP), hepatojugular reflux, third heart sound ($S_3$ gallop), laterally displaced apical impulse | Peripheral oedema (ankle, sacral, scrotal), pulmonary crackles (rales), bilateral pleural effusions, tachycardia, irregular pulse, tachypnoea, hepatomegaly, ascites, cold extremities, oliguria, narrow pulse pressure |

#### Table 8: New York Heart Association (NYHA) Functional Classification
| Class | Degree of Limitation | Clinical Description |
|---|---|---|
| **Class I** | None | Ordinary physical activity does not cause undue breathlessness, fatigue, or palpitations. |
| **Class II** | Slight | Comfortable at rest. Ordinary physical activity results in fatigue, palpitations, or breathlessness. |
| **Class III** | Marked | Comfortable at rest. Less than ordinary physical activity causes fatigue, palpitations, or breathlessness. |
| **Class IV** | Severe | Inability to carry on any physical activity without discomfort. Symptoms of heart failure at rest. |

#### 5.1.1 Natriuretic Peptides in Diagnosis
Circulating natriuretic peptides have high negative predictive value ($> 95\%$) for excluding HF. Recommended diagnostic thresholds in non-acute outpatient settings:
- **NT-proBNP:** $< 125\text{ pg/mL}$ renders HF unlikely.
- **BNP:** $< 35\text{ pg/mL}$ renders HF unlikely.

#### Table 9: Causes for Increase or Decrease in Natriuretic Peptide Levels
| Mechanism | Clinical Causes |
|---|---|
| **Cardiac Causes of Elevation** | Heart failure, acute coronary syndromes, valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathies, atrial fibrillation and flutter, myocarditis, cardiac surgery, cardioversion, cardiac amyloidosis |
| **Non-Cardiac Causes of Elevation** | Advanced age, chronic kidney disease (reduced renal clearance), ischaemic stroke, subarachnoid haemorrhage, pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension, sepsis, severe burns, critical illness, severe anaemia, cirrhosis |
| **Causes of Decreased Levels (False Low)** | **Obesity (BMI $\ge 30\text{ kg/m}^2$):** Circulating BNP and NT-proBNP levels are reduced by $30\%\text{--}50\%$ due to increased clearance receptor expression and blunted adipocyte synthesis; **pericardial constriction, acute "flash" pulmonary oedema** (very early before release). |

## 6. Management of Chronic Heart Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

### 6.1 Pharmacological Management
The treatment of chronic heart failure is structured around disease-modifying **Foundational Medical Therapy (FMT)** for HFrEF, phenotype-specific evidence in HFpEF, and tailored **Additional Medical Therapy (AMT)**.

#### 6.1.1 Foundational Medical Therapy (FMT Quad-Therapy) in HFrEF (LVEF $\le 50\%$)
All four foundational pillars should be initiated rapidly—ideally during hospitalization or within the first 4–6 weeks of outpatient care—and uptitrated every 1–2 weeks to target or maximally tolerated doses:
1. **ARNI / ACE-I:** Sacubitril/valsartan is preferred over ACE inhibitors due to superior reduction in CV death and HF hospitalizations. For patients naïve to ARNI or intolerant, ACE inhibitors (or ARBs if cough/angioedema occurs) are recommended.
2. **Evidence-Based Beta-Blocker:** Bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, or nebivolol reduce all-cause mortality, CV mortality, and sudden cardiac death in stable patients.
3. **Steroidal MRA:** Spironolactone or eplerenone reduces mortality and hospitalizations across the entire HFrEF spectrum.
4. **SGLT2 Inhibitor:** Dapagliflozin ($10\text{ mg}$ o.d.) or empagliflozin ($10\text{ mg}$ o.d.) rapidly improves symptoms, decreases HF hospitalizations, and reduces CV mortality, with minimal blood pressure or renal clearance constraints.

## 7. Decompensated Heart Failure

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>7.1. Definition, Epidemiology, and Clinical Presentations</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Decompensated heart failure (DHF) represents a life-threatening clinical syndrome defined by the rapid onset or worsening of symptoms and signs of heart failure that require urgent medical attention and hospitalization. DHF may present as acute decompensation of pre-existing chronic heart failure (worsening heart failure, accounting for ~75%–80% of presentations) or as *de novo* heart failure (accounting for ~20%–25% of presentations, frequently triggered by acute myocardial infarction, acute myocarditis, or acute valvular disruption).

#### Clinical Categories of Decompensated Heart Failure

Patients with DHF present along a continuum of fluid overload and tissue perfusion. Four major clinical presentations are recognized:
1. **Acute Decompensated Left-Sided Heart Failure:** Characterized predominantly by pulmonary congestion, dyspnoea, orthopnoea, and elevated left ventricular filling pressures, with or without systemic congestion.
2. **Acute Decompensated Right-Sided Heart Failure:** Characterized predominantly by systemic venous congestion, elevated jugular venous pressure, hepatomegaly, ascites, and peripheral pitting oedema, frequently complicated by cardio-hepatic and cardiorenal syndromes.
3. **Acute Pulmonary Oedema:** Severe respiratory distress, tachypnoea, orthopnoea, bilateral diffuse pulmonary rales, and hypoxaemia resulting from rapid hydrostatic fluid shift into lung alveoli.
4. **Cardiogenic Shock:** Severe tissue and end-organ hypoperfusion (hypotension with SBP <90 mmHg or MAP <65 mmHg, cold clammy extremities, oliguria <0.5 mL/kg/h, elevated serum lactate >2.0 mmol/L, altered mental status) resulting from primary cardiac dysfunction.

![Diagnosing decompensated heart failure](images/fig11_diagnosing_decompensated_hf.jpg)

#### Diagnostic Evaluation in the Emergency Setting

All patients presenting with suspected DHF require immediate diagnostic evaluation:
- **12-lead ECG:** To assess rhythm (atrial fibrillation, sustained ventricular tachycardia), ischaemic changes (ST-elevation, pathological Q waves), conduction delay (QRS duration, bundle branch block), or signs of chamber hypertrophy.
- **Continuous Pulse Oximetry and Blood Pressure:** Immediate assessment of respiratory compromise and haemodynamic stability.
- **Laboratory Testing:** Plasma natriuretic peptides (BNP or NT-proBNP) to exclude non-cardiac causes of dyspnoea, cardiac troponin (high-sensitivity), serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, electrolytes (sodium, potassium), liver function tests, complete blood count, and arterial/venous blood gases in distressed or shocked patients.
- **Transthoracic Echocardiography:** Mandatory bedside echocardiography in haemodynamically unstable patients or *de novo* presentation to evaluate LV/RV systolic function, chamber dimensions, regional wall motion abnormalities, valvular structure/regurgitation/stenosis, pericardial effusion/tamponade, and estimated pulmonary artery pressures.
- **Chest Radiography and Lung Ultrasound:** To assess pulmonary venous congestion, alveolar interstitial oedema (B-lines on lung ultrasound), pleural effusions, and exclude non-cardiovascular pulmonary pathology (pneumothorax, pneumonia).

<summary>7.2. In-Hospital Phases and Management Pathways</summary>
<div class="details-content">

In-hospital care of DHF is structured into three continuous phases:
- **Phase 1: Urgent / Initial Management (First 0–6 Hours):** Immediate triage, stabilization of airway, oxygenation, haemodynamic support, relief of congestion, and identification of life-threatening precipitants (*CHAMPIT*: Coronary syndrome, Hypertensive emergency, Arrhythmia, Acute Mechanical cause, Pulmonary embolism, Infection, Tamponade).
- **Phase 2: In-Hospital Stabilization (Days 1–5):** Continued decongestion, daily monitoring of volume status, monitoring renal function and electrolytes, initiation and uptitration of foundational medical therapy (FMT).
- **Phase 3: Pre-Discharge and Early Post-Discharge (Days to Weeks):** Formal assessment of complete decongestion, safety-guided uptitration of GDMT to target doses, patient education, planning early outpatient follow-up within 1–2 weeks, and enrollment in multidisciplinary disease management programmes.

<summary>7.3. Pharmacological and Interventional Treatments in Decompensated Heart Failure</summary>
<div class="details-content">

#### Oxygen and Ventilatory Support
Oxygen therapy is indicated only when hypoxaemia is documented ($SpO_2 <90\%$ or $PaO_2 <60	ext{ mmHg}$). Routine oxygen therapy in non-hypoxaemic patients causes hyperoxia-induced vasoconstriction and reduction in coronary blood flow. Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (CPAP or BiPAP) should be initiated early in patients with acute pulmonary oedema and respiratory distress (respiratory rate >25/min, $SpO_2 <90\%$) to reduce work of breathing, improve lung compliance, and avoid endotracheal intubation.

#### Loop Diuretics and Decongestion Protocols
Intravenous loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide, or torasemide) remain the cornerstone of decongestion therapy:
- For loop diuretic-naïve patients: Initial starting dose is 20–40 mg intravenous furosemide.
- For patients on chronic oral loop diuretics: Initial intravenous dose should be at least 1–2 times the daily oral home dose administered as intravenous boluses or continuous infusion.
- Early assessment of diuretic response at 2 hours (spot urinary sodium content, target $\ge 70	ext{ mEq/L}$) and at 6 hours (hourly urine output, target $\ge 100	ext{ mL/h}$) is recommended to identify diuretic resistance early.
- If diuretic response is inadequate, the intravenous loop diuretic dose should be doubled, and combination nephron blockade (addition of intravenous acetazolamide 500 mg daily or oral hydrochlorothiazide 25–50 mg daily) should be considered.

![Tools for assessment of decongestion](images/fig14_tools_assessment_decongestion.jpg)

![Management of decongestion: diuretic protocols](images/fig15_management_decongestion.jpg)

#### Vasodilators, Inotropes, and Vasopressors
- **Vasodilators (Nitroglycerin, Isosorbide Dinitrate, Nitroprusside):** May be considered in patients with SBP >110 mmHg to reduce cardiac filling pressures, systemic vascular resistance, and pulmonary congestion.
- **Inotropes (Dobutamine, Levosimendan, Milrinone):** Reserved strictly for patients with marked peripheral hypoperfusion, symptomatic hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg), or low cardiac output despite adequate filling pressures. Routine use in non-hypoperfused patients increases myocardial oxygen demand, tachyarrhythmias, and mortality.
- **Vasopressors (Norepinephrine):** Recommended as the vasopressor of first choice in cardiogenic shock to maintain coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure while minimizing tachyarrhythmias compared to dopamine or epinephrine.

<summary>7.4. Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support in Cardiogenic Shock</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Temporary mechanical circulatory support (tMCS) devices are indicated to maintain systemic perfusion, reduce cardiac filling pressures, and unload the failing ventricle in patients with refractory cardiogenic shock. Device options include:
- **Microaxial Flow Pumps (Impella):** Provide transvalvular LV unloading, reduce LV end-diastolic pressure, and increase systemic forward flow up to 3.5–5.5 L/min. The DanGer Shock trial demonstrated that microaxial flow pumps significantly reduce 180-day all-cause mortality in selected patients with STEMI-related cardiogenic shock without anoxic brain injury.
- **Venoarterial Extracorporeal Life Support (VA-ECLS / VA-ECMO):** Provides biventricular circulatory and pulmonary support; however, retrograde aortic inflow increases LV afterload, requiring proactive LV venting (e.g., ECPELLA with concomitant Impella or atrial septostomy).
- **Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP):** Provides counterpulsation with modest afterload reduction and diastolic coronary augmentation, but does not actively pump blood. Multiple randomized trials (IABP-SHOCK II) demonstrated no mortality benefit for routine IABP in post-MI cardiogenic shock (Class III: Not Recommended).

## 8. Advanced Heart Failure

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>8.1. Definition, Triage, and Evaluation of Advanced Heart Failure</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Advanced heart failure (Stage D) is characterized by persistent, severe symptoms (NYHA class III–IV) and recurrent decompensation despite maximum tolerated guideline-directed medical therapy (FMT), cardiac resynchronization therapy, and surgical/interventional procedures. Timely identification and referral to a specialized Advanced Heart Failure Center are vital before the onset of irreversible end-organ damage (severe renal failure, cirrhosis, fixed pulmonary hypertension).

![Triage and timely referral for advanced heart failure](images/fig16_triage_timely_referral_advanced_hf.jpg)

![INTERMACS clinical profiles](images/fig17_intermacs_classification.jpg)

#### Table 14: Criteria for Definition of Advanced Heart Failure (HFA-ESC Criteria)

All the following criteria must be present despite optimal medical and device therapy:
1. **Severe and Persistent Symptoms:** NYHA class III (advanced) or class IV dyspnoea and/or fatigue at rest or on minimal exertion.
2. **Severe Cardiac Dysfunction:** Severe impairment documented by at least one of the following:
   - Severely reduced LVEF ($\le 30\%$).
   - Isolated right ventricular systolic failure (e.g. TAPSE <14 mm, FAC <35%).
   - Inoperable severe valvular disease or severe congenital heart disease.
   - Persistent severe diastolic dysfunction or structural abnormalities with elevated filling pressures.
3. **Severe Episodes of Congestion and/or Hypoperfusion:** At least one of the following within the past 12 months:
   - $\ge 2$ hospitalizations or emergency department visits for decompensated heart failure requiring intravenous therapy.
   - Requirement for continuous or repeated outpatient inotropic support.
   - Need for temporary mechanical circulatory support.
   - Malignant ventricular arrhythmias triggering ICD shocks.
4. **Severe Exercise Impairment:** Inability to perform cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) or objective demonstration of severely reduced peak oxygen consumption:
   - Peak $VO_2 <12	ext{ mL/kg/min}$ (or $<14	ext{ mL/kg/min}$ in patients on beta-blockers), or $<50\%$ predicted.
   - 6-minute walk distance <300 m.

## 9. Cardiovascular Comorbidities

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>9.1. Atrial Fibrillation and Arrhythmias</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia in patients with heart failure, affecting up to 30%–50% of individuals depending on disease severity. AF and HF share common risk factors and promote each other through adverse structural and electrical remodeling ("AF begets HF, and HF begets AF").

#### Anticoagulation in Atrial Fibrillation
Patients with AF and heart failure are at elevated risk of stroke and systemic thromboembolism. In the 2026 ESC Guidelines, thromboembolic risk assessment has been streamlined using the updated **$CHA_2DS_2	ext{-}VA$ score**, which removes the female sex category as an independent risk modifier.

#### Rate vs Rhythm Control Strategies
- **Rate Control:** Recommended first-line approach in stable patients. Beta-blockers are the first choice in HFrEF. Digoxin or digitoxin is indicated when ventricular rates remain elevated despite beta-blockers or when beta-blockers are contraindicated. Lenient resting heart rate target (<110 bpm) is appropriate initially, with tighter control (<80 bpm) if symptoms persist.
- **Rhythm Control:** Urgent electrical cardioversion is mandatory in haemodynamically unstable patients or acute decompensation with rapid ventricular rate. Amiodarone is the only safe antiarrhythmic drug for pharmacological cardioversion and sinus rhythm maintenance in HFrEF. Class IC antiarrhythmics (flecainide, propafenone) and dronedarone increase mortality and are strictly contraindicated in HFrEF.
- **Catheter Ablation:** Catheter ablation for AF should be considered in selected HFrEF patients (high AF burden, paroxysmal or short-duration persistent AF <1 year, and clear tachycardiomyopathy) to restore LV function, improve QoL, and reduce hospitalizations.
- **AV Node Ablation + CRT ("Abate and Pace"):** Should be considered in severely symptomatic permanent AF with refractory rapid ventricular rates despite maximal medical therapy.

<summary>9.2. Chronic Coronary Syndromes and Revascularization</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) is the underlying aetiology in over 50%–60% of patients with HFrEF. The management of CAD in patients with heart failure encompasses optimal medical antianginal therapy integrated with foundational HF medications, alongside selective myocardial revascularization evaluated by a multidisciplinary Heart Team.

![Management of coronary artery disease in heart failure](images/fig19_management_cad_in_hf.jpg)

#### Medical Therapy for Angina in Heart Failure
- **First-Line:** Guideline-directed beta-blockers (bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate) provide dual benefit for prognosis and angina relief.
- **Second-Line:** Ivabradine is indicated for persistent angina in sinus rhythm with resting heart rate $\ge 70	ext{ bpm}$ despite beta-blockers. Oral nitrates, amlodipine, and trimetazidine may be added for symptom control.
- **Contraindicated Drugs:** Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem) have negative inotropic effects and worsen heart failure outcomes in HFrEF.

#### Myocardial Revascularization: CABG vs PCI
Long-term data from the STICH trial (10-year follow-up) demonstrated that in patients with severe LV systolic dysfunction (LVEF $\le 35\%$) and multivessel CAD suitable for surgery, Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG) added to medical therapy produces a significant reduction in all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death compared to medical therapy alone. In the REVIVED-BCIS2 trial, routine PCI did not reduce all-cause mortality or HF hospitalizations in patients with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF $\le 35\%$) and extensive CAD without refractory angina. Therefore, CABG is the preferred revascularization modality when outcome improvement is pursued in multivessel CAD suitable for surgery.

<summary>9.3. Valvular Heart Disease in Heart Failure</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Valvular heart disease can be either the primary cause of heart failure or a secondary consequence of LV/RV remodeling and dilatation.

#### Severe Aortic Stenosis
Severe aortic stenosis imposes chronic pressure overload, inducing LV hypertrophy, fibrosis, and eventual systolic and diastolic failure. Aortic valve intervention is strongly recommended in all patients with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis and heart failure:
- **Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI):** Preferred in older patients ($\ge 75$ years), patients with high surgical risk, or transfemoral anatomical suitability.
- **Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement (SAVR):** Preferred in younger patients (<75 years), low surgical risk, or those requiring concomitant CABG or aortic root surgery.
- **Low-Flow, Low-Gradient Aortic Stenosis:** Low-dose dobutamine stress echocardiography is indicated in patients with reduced LVEF and low transvalvular gradient to distinguish true severe aortic stenosis (valve area remains $\le 1.0	ext{ cm}^2$ with increasing flow) from pseudo-severe stenosis, and to assess contractile flow reserve.

#### Secondary (Functional) Mitral Regurgitation
Secondary mitral regurgitation (SMR) develops due to LV chamber dilatation, papillary muscle displacement, and tethering of structurally normal mitral leaflets. Medical therapy with FMT and CRT must be optimized first:
- **Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair (TEER / MitraClip):** In the COAPT trial, mitral TEER added to maximal GDMT significantly reduced HF hospitalizations and all-cause mortality. Strict selection criteria must be fulfilled to achieve outcome benefit (disproportionate MR).

## 10. Non-Cardiovascular Comorbidities</summary><div class="details-content">

Non-cardiovascular comorbidities are exceptionally prevalent in patients with heart failure across all LVEF phenotypes. They impair functional capacity, deteriorate health-related quality of life, accelerate disease progression, increase non-cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, and frequently confound the assessment of circulatory congestion.

### 10.1 Obesity
Obesity contributes directly to systemic hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, while exerting detrimental mechanical, metabolic, and pro-inflammatory effects on myocardial structure and microvascular endothelium. Obesity is particularly prevalent in HFpEF (accounting for 30%–40% of European cases and up to 60%–70% in North America). Circulating natriuretic peptide concentrations are paradoxically lower in obese patients due to augmented clearance receptor expression and adipose tissue degradation, necessitating lower diagnostic thresholds.

In patients with symptomatic heart failure and obesity, structured lifestyle modification targeting weight reduction is foundational. Following the landmark STEP-HFpEF and STEP-HFpEF DM trials, the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) **semaglutide** (or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist **tirzepatide**) substantially improves physical symptoms, Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) clinical summary scores, 6-minute walk distance, and body weight, while reducing HF hospitalizations.

### Recommendation Table 18 — Recommendations for obesity in patients with heart failure

| Recommendations | Class | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Semaglutide or tirzepatide should be considered for patients with symptomatic HF, LVEF ≥45%, and BMI ≥30 kg/m², regardless of diabetes status, to reduce body weight, and improve exercise capacity and QoL. | **IIa** | **B1** |
| Bariatric surgery may be considered in obese patients with HF and a BMI ≥35 kg/m² to reduce body weight when repetitive and structured lifestyle changes combined with weight-reducing medications do not result in maintained weight loss. | **IIb** | **C** |

### 10.2 Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Type 2 diabetes mellitus is present in 35%–45% of patients with chronic HF. SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) are recommended for all patients with HF and T2DM, irrespective of LVEF, providing simultaneous cardioprotection, renoprotection, and glycaemic control. Metformin remains recommended as a safe foundational agent in patients with eGFR $\ge 30\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2$. Thiazolidinediones (glitazones) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin are contraindicated due to increased risk of fluid retention and HF hospitalization.

### 10.3 Chronic Kidney Disease and Hyperkalaemia
Renal dysfunction and heart failure coexist bidirectionally as cardiorenal syndrome. Moderate-to-severe CKD (eGFR $< 60\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2$) is found in $> 50\%$ of HF cohorts. Foundational neurohormonal blockers (ARNI/ACE-I, MRAs, SGLT2-Is) should not be withheld or discontinued for modest transient declines in eGFR ($< 30\%$) upon initiation, provided volume status and serum potassium remain acceptable. To prevent hyperkalaemia and allow optimal titration of MRAs and ARNI, modern potassium binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) are recommended. In patients with T2DM and CKD, the non-steroidal MRA **finerenone** reduces incident HF hospitalizations and slows renal disease progression.

### 10.4 Iron Deficiency and Anaemia
Iron deficiency (ID) occurs in up to 50% of patients with ambulatory HF and $> 70\%$ with decompensated HF, independent of anaemia. It is biologically defined as **serum ferritin $< 100\text{ \mu g/L}$**, or **ferritin $100\text{--}299\text{ \mu g/L}$ with transferrin saturation (TSAT) $< 20\%$**. Iron deficiency impairs skeletal and cardiac mitochondrial energetics, reduces peak exercise capacity ($pVO_2$), and increases hospitalizations.

Intravenous iron repletion with **ferric carboxymaltose (FCM)** or **ferric derisomaltose (FDI)** is recommended to alleviate symptoms, improve functional capacity, and reduce HF hospitalizations in symptomatic HFrEF/HFmrEF patients with confirmed iron deficiency. Oral iron administration is ineffective and not recommended.

### 10.5 Cancer and Cardio-Oncology
Cancer and heart failure share common epidemiological risk factors and biological pathways. Antineoplastic regimens—particularly anthracyclines (doxorubicin), HER2 inhibitors (trastuzumab), VEGF signaling inhibitors, proteasome inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors—can induce cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD). Multidisciplinary evaluation by a cardio-oncology team is recommended, including baseline risk assessment, serial echocardiography with global longitudinal strain (GLS), and biomarker monitoring (hs-cTn, NT-proBNP). Pre-emptive neurohormonal therapy with ACE-I/ARB and beta-blockers is indicated in high-risk patients.

### 10.6 Lung Disease and Sleep-Disordered Breathing
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma co-occur in 20%–30% of HF patients. Cardioselective beta-1 blockers (bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate, nebivolol) are safe, well tolerated, and not contraindicated in COPD. Both obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) and central sleep apnoea (CSA, including Cheyne-Stokes breathing) are highly prevalent in HFrEF. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is indicated for symptomatic OSA. Conversely, **adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) is strictly contraindicated in patients with HFrEF and predominant central sleep apnoea**, following the SERVE-HF trial which demonstrated increased cardiovascular and all-cause mortality.

### 10.7 Anxiety, Depression, and Cognitive Impairment
Clinically significant depressive symptoms and anxiety affect up to 40% of HF patients, correlating with poor treatment adherence, impaired self-care, frequent emergency visits, and elevated mortality. Routine screening using validated questionnaires (PHQ-9, GAD-7) is recommended. Cognitive impairment (vascular, neurodegenerative, or secondary to cerebral hypoperfusion) mandates simplified medication regimens, blister packaging, and direct family caregiver involvement.

### 10.8 Frailty
Frailty is a multidimensional clinical syndrome characterized by decreased physiological reserve and vulnerability to stressors, affecting 30%–50% of elderly HF patients. Identification using standardized tools (e.g., Clinical Frailty Scale, Fried phenotype) is recommended to tailor therapeutic goals, individualize invasive treatment decisions, and incorporate structured physical rehabilitation and nutritional support.

- **Gout and Hyperuricaemia:** Hyperuricaemia is frequently provoked by loop diuretics and impaired renal clearance. For acute gout flares, low-dose colchicine is preferred; **NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors are strictly contraindicated** as they induce sodium/water retention, blunting of diuretic response, acute kidney injury, and precipitated HF decompensation. - **Thyroid Dysfunction:** Both hyperthyroidism (precipitating high-output failure, tachyarrhythmias, and worsening ischaemia) and hypothyroidism (inducing pericardial effusion, bradycardia, and diastolic dysfunction) should be diagnosed and treated according to endocrine guidelines. Baseline and serial TSH monitoring is mandatory in patients receiving amiodarone.

## 12. Specific Conditions</summary><div class="details-content">

### 12.1 Heart Failure and Pregnancy
Pregnancy imposes profound haemodynamic demands, including a 40%–50% increase in blood volume, elevated cardiac output, and physiological tachycardia. Women with pre-existing heart failure (Stage B or C) face elevated risks of maternal cardiac decompensation and adverse fetal outcomes. Pre-conception counseling, risk stratification using the modified WHO (mWHO) classification, and dedicated multidisciplinary Pregnancy Heart Team management are mandatory.

#### Pharmacotherapy During Pregnancy and Lactation
- **Strictly Contraindicated (Teratogenic / Fetotoxic):** ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs, SGLT2 inhibitors, and ivabradine must be discontinued prior to conception or immediately upon pregnancy confirmation (**Class III, Level C**).
- **Safe and Recommended Agents:** Beta-1 selective blockers (metoprolol succinate; atenolol should be avoided due to fetal growth restriction), hydralazine, oral nitrates, and loop diuretics (at lowest effective doses to prevent placental hypoperfusion). Digoxin may be utilized for rate control.

### 12.2 Cardiomyopathies and Myocarditis
- **Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM):** Diagnosis relies on unexplained LV wall thickness $\ge 15\text{ mm}$ ($\ge 13\text{ mm}$ with positive family history). In symptomatic obstructive HCM, beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers are first-line. The cardiac myosin ATPase inhibitor **mavacamten** is recommended in patients with persistent symptoms and LV outflow tract obstruction despite first-line therapy. Surgical septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation is indicated in drug-refractory severe obstruction.
- **Dilated and Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathies:** Comprehensive multi-gene panel testing and cascade family evaluation are indicated to identify pathogenic variants (e.g., *LMNA*, *FLNC*, *TTN*, *RBM20*, *DSP*) carrying high risks of sudden cardiac death or rapid progression.
- **Acute Myocarditis:** Diagnosis combines clinical presentation, high-sensitivity troponin, ECG, and contrast-enhanced CMR (applying updated Lake Louise Criteria). Endomyocardial biopsy is indicated in fulminant, refractory, or complicated presentations to identify giant-cell, necrotizing eosinophilic, or sarcoid myocarditis requiring immediate aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

Cardiac amyloidosis is an increasingly recognized cause of heart failure, characterized by the extracellular deposition of insoluble amyloid fibrils derived from misfolded transthyretin (**ATTR-CA**, wild-type or hereditary) or immunoglobulin light chains (**AL-CA**). ## 13. Key Messages</summary><div class="details-content">

The 2026 ESC Guidelines for the management of heart failure summarize the fundamental clinical imperatives into **10 Core Key Messages**:

1. **Unified Stages of Heart Failure:** Heart failure is a continuous spectrum spanning Stage A (at risk), Stage B (pre-heart failure with structural/functional changes or elevated biomarkers), Stage C (symptomatic clinical HF), and Stage D (advanced, refractory HF). Proactive intervention at Stages A and B prevents disease onset.
2. **Simplified LVEF Phenotypes:** HFrEF is redefined as LVEF $\le 50\%$ (incorporating former HFmrEF), recognizing that neurohormonal blockade confers equivalent clinical benefits across this entire range. HFpEF is defined as symptomatic HF with LVEF $> 50\%$ and objective evidence of diastolic dysfunction.
3. **Rapid Initiation of FMT Quad-Therapy:** Foundational Medical Therapy for HFrEF consists of four pillars: ARNI/ACE-I, beta-blocker, MRA, and SGLT2 inhibitor. All four drug classes should be initiated promptly—ideally during index hospitalization—and titrated rapidly to target doses within 4–6 weeks.
4. **Evidence-Based Therapy in HFpEF:** SGLT2 inhibitors and MRAs form the backbone of disease-modifying pharmacotherapy in HFpEF to reduce hospitalizations. The GLP-1 RA semaglutide (or tirzepatide) is indicated in obesity-related HFpEF to reduce symptoms and body weight.
5. **Structured In-Hospital Decongestion:** In decompensated heart failure, prompt intravenous loop diuretic administration guided by urine spot sodium ($> 50\text{--}70\text{ mmol/L}$ at 2 hours) and hourly urine output ensures complete decongestion. Inpatient optimization of FMT and early post-discharge visits (within 7–14 days) prevent readmission.
6. **Timely Advanced HF Triage:** Patients with recurring hospitalizations, worsening renal function, persistent intolerance to neurohormonal blockers, or escalating diuretic requirements must be identified early using the **I NEED HELP** mnemonic and referred promptly to an Advanced HF center for LVAD or heart transplantation evaluation.
7. **Concomitant Atrial Fibrillation Management:** Thromboembolic risk must be evaluated with the revised **CHA2DS2-VA score**, prescribing DOACs in preference to VKAs. Catheter ablation is recommended as a first-line rhythm strategy in selected HFrEF patients with tachycardiomyopathy or high arrhythmia burden.
8. **Systematic Comorbidity Screening:** Routine screening and targeted management of non-cardiovascular comorbidities—specifically iron deficiency (treated with IV ferric carboxymaltose or ferric derisomaltose), CKD, diabetes, and sleep-disordered breathing—are mandatory components of holistic HF care.
9. **Non-Invasive Diagnosis of Cardiac Amyloidosis:** Cardiac amyloidosis should be actively sought in patients with unexplained LV hypertrophy, HFpEF, or carpal tunnel syndrome, utilizing bone scintigraphy and light-chain screening without requiring invasive myocardial biopsy. Early initiation of TTR stabilizers (tafamidis, acoramidis) or silencers (vutrisiran) halts disease progression.
10. **Multidisciplinary Team Care and Rehabilitation:** Coordinated management by multidisciplinary heart failure teams, enrollment in exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation, individualized remote telemonitoring, and proactive advance care planning substantially improve clinical outcomes, functional status, and quality of life.

## 14. Gaps in Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Despite monumental therapeutic progress, critical gaps in clinical evidence remain to be addressed by future clinical trials and registries:

### 14.1 Definitions, Epidemiology, and Prevention
- Precise biological consensus regarding normal physiological reference ranges of LVEF across age, sex, and ethnic cohorts.
- Prospective natural history, epidemiological trajectory, and long-term prognosis of patients with heart failure with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF), including the safety of gradual medical de-escalation.
- Comparative long-term efficacy and safety of novel multi-incretin agonists, non-incretin weight-loss molecules, and bariatric/metabolic surgery in reducing incident HF.
- Definitive clinical trials assessing whether SGLT2 inhibitors and non-steroidal MRAs prevent incident HF in non-diabetic individuals with isolated hypertension, obesity, or non-albuminuric CKD.

### 14.2 Diagnostic Phenotyping and Artificial Intelligence
- Deeper biological, proteomic, and metabolomic subphenotyping of HFpEF to identify targeted therapeutic responder subcohorts.
- Clinical validation of artificial intelligence algorithms (deep-learning ECG, automated echocardiographic analysis, generative clinical modeling) in early screening, risk stratification, and trial enrollment.
- Prospectively validated, non-invasive diagnostic criteria replacing invasive exercise right heart catheterization in ambiguous early-stage HFpEF.
- Clarification of BMI-adjusted natriuretic peptide decision cut-offs across morbidly obese and cachectic patient populations.

### 14.3 Pharmacological and Device Therapies
- Head-to-head randomized trials comparing steroidal (spironolactone, eplerenone) versus non-steroidal MRAs (finerenone) in HFrEF and HFpEF.
- Optimal timing, sequencing, and combination strategies of foundational medical therapy (FMT) in hospitalized patients with acute decompensated heart failure.
- Definitive efficacy of primary prevention ICDs in non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy in the modern era of universal FMT and CRT.
- Role of conduction system pacing (His bundle and left bundle branch area pacing) compared to biventricular CRT in patients with heart failure and wide QRS complexes.
- Safety and long-term durability of transcatheter tricuspid valve interventions (TEER, replacement) in heart failure with severe functional tricuspid regurgitation.
