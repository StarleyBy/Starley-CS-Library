## Executive Summary: Top 10 Take-Home Messages</summary><div class="details-content">

1. **Multidisciplinary HCM Centers:** Management at comprehensive and primary hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) centers with specialized multidisciplinary expertise improves clinical outcomes. Referral is especially recommended for patients with drug-refractory symptoms, complex left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) anatomy, advanced heart failure, difficult-to-manage arrhythmias, or uncertain risk stratification.
2. **Shared Decision-Making:** Longitudinal shared decision-making is essential across all care pathways—including genetic testing, lifestyle and competitive athletic participation, primary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) placement, and invasive septal reduction therapies.
3. **Comprehensive Sudden Cardiac Death (SCD) Risk Assessment:** SCD risk stratification must be performed at initial evaluation and every 1 to 2 years thereafter. Major established clinical risk factors include: massive LV hypertrophy ($\ge 30\text{ mm}$), unexplained recent syncope, family history of premature HCM-related sudden death, LV apical aneurysm, systolic dysfunction (LVEF $< 50\%$), and extensive late gadolinium enhancement (LGE $\ge 15\%$ of LV mass on CMR).
4. **Patient Selection for Primary Prevention ICD:** In adult patients with HCM harboring $\ge 1$ major conventional risk factor, prophylactic ICD implantation is reasonable after an individualized discussion of arrhythmic risk versus device-related complications.
5. **First-Line Pharmacotherapy for Obstructive HCM:** Non-vasodilating beta-blockers titrated to heart rate and symptom response are the first-line pharmacologic agents. Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil or diltiazem) are recommended if beta-blockers are ineffective or contraindicated.
6. **Cardiac Myosin Inhibitors (Mavacamten):** In symptomatic patients with obstructive HCM who have persistent NYHA class II to III symptoms despite first-line beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers, the addition of a cardiac myosin inhibitor (mavacamten) is recommended to relieve dynamic LVOT gradient and improve functional class.
7. **Invasive Septal Reduction Therapy (SRT):** In patients with severe, drug-refractory symptoms (NYHA class III–IV or recurrent syncope) and a dynamic peak LVOT gradient $\ge 50\text{ mm Hg}$ (at rest or with provocation), surgical septal myectomy (preferred in younger patients or those with concomitant mitral/cardiac lesions) or alcohol septal ablation (ASA) performed at experienced centers is recommended.
8. **Atrial Fibrillation Management:** Due to the high risk of systemic thromboembolism in HCM, lifelong oral anticoagulation with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs, preferred over warfarin) is recommended for all patients with clinical or subclinical AF, irrespective of the CHA2DS2-VASc score. Antiarrhythmic drug therapy or catheter ablation is indicated for rhythm control.
9. **Physical Activity and Recreational Exercise:** Mild-to-moderate recreational physical activity is beneficial and strongly encouraged for all individuals with HCM. Participation in competitive sports can be considered after specialized evaluation and comprehensive shared decision-making.
10. **Genetic Testing and Cascade Family Screening:** Genetic testing with a targeted sarcomeric gene panel is recommended for all probands with HCM. Cascade genetic testing should be offered to first-degree relatives when a pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is identified; clinical screening (ECG and echocardiography) is recommended for all at-risk first-degree relatives.

## What Is New: Key Practice-Changing Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

The 2024 AHA/ACC Guideline updates and expands upon the 2020 recommendations with important practice-changing shifts:
* **Cardiac Myosin Inhibitors (Mavacamten):** Formally introduced as a Class 1 add-on recommendation for symptomatic obstructive HCM after pivotal Phase 3 trials (EXPLORER-HCM and VALOR-HCM) demonstrated dramatic gradient reduction, NYHA class improvement, and deferral/avoidance of surgical septal reduction.
* **Refined SCD Risk Stratification:** Strengthened role of cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging; late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) $\ge 15\%$ and left ventricular apical aneurysms are solidified as major primary prevention ICD triggers.
* **Extended Heart Rhythm Monitoring:** Upgraded from Class 2a to Class 1 in patients deemed high risk for AF (left atrial enlargement, advanced age, or validated risk score) to prevent cardioembolic stroke.
* **Routine Pediatric Stress Testing:** Exercise stress testing is now recommended (Class 1) for all pediatric HCM patients regardless of symptom status to assess functional capacity, dynamic obstruction, and blood pressure response.
* **Universal Anticoagulation in AF:** DOACs are recommended (Class 1) over warfarin for stroke prevention in HCM patients developing AF, confirming that traditional risk scores (CHA2DS2-VASc) significantly underestimate thromboembolic risk in this population.
* **Evolving Stance on Sports Participation:** Shared decision-making allows individualized consideration of competitive athletics for patients with low-risk clinical profiles.

## 1. Introduction: Scope, Methodology, and Recommendation System</summary><div class="details-content">

### 1.1 Scope of the Guideline
Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common monogenic cardiovascular disorder, characterized by unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy in the absence of abnormal loading conditions (e.g., severe aortic stenosis or uncontrolled systemic hypertension). This guideline provides a comprehensive, evidence-based roadmap for clinical diagnosis, advanced multimodality imaging, genetic testing, risk stratification for sudden cardiac death, personalized pharmacotherapy, interventional and surgical septal reduction, and lifelong surveillance.

### 1.2 Class of Recommendation and Level of Evidence

#### Table 3: Applying ACC/AHA Class of Recommendation and Level of Evidence
* **Class 1 (Strong):** Benefit >>> Risk. Treatment/intervention SHOULD be performed.
* **Class 2a (Moderate):** Benefit >> Risk. Treatment/intervention is REASONABLE.
* **Class 2b (Weak):** Benefit $\ge$ Risk. Treatment/intervention MAY BE CONSIDERED.
* **Class 3: No Benefit (Moderate):** Benefit = Risk. Treatment/intervention is NOT recommended (no demonstrated efficacy).
* **Class 3: Harm (Strong):** Risk > Benefit. Treatment/intervention causes HARM and should NOT be performed.

**Levels of Evidence (LOE):**
* **Level A:** High-quality evidence from $> 1$ randomized controlled trial (RCT), or meta-analyses of high-quality RCTs.
* **Level B-R (Randomized):** Moderate-quality evidence from 1 or more RCTs, or meta-analyses of moderate-quality RCTs.
* **Level B-NR (Nonrandomized):** Moderate-quality evidence from 1 or more well-designed nonrandomized studies, observational studies, or registries.
* **Level C-LD (Limited Data):** Randomized or nonrandomized observational or registry studies with limitations of design or execution.
* **Level C-EO (Expert Opinion):** Consensus of expert opinion based on clinical experience.

## 2. Definition, Etiology, Clinical Course, and Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

### 2.1 Prevalence and Epidemiology
* Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy occurs globally across all racial and ethnic backgrounds with an estimated phenotypic prevalence of approximately **1 in 500** (0.2%) in the general population based on echocardiographic screening.
* When incorporating cardiac magnetic resonance imaging and contemporary genetic cascade screening, the combined prevalence of clinical and subclinical (genotype-positive, phenotype-negative) HCM is estimated at **1 in 200 to 1 in 300** individuals.
* Most affected individuals remain asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic, and modern clinical outcomes demonstrate an annual cardiovascular mortality of $< 1\%$, dispelling historical notions that HCM is uniformly progressive or fatal.

### 2.2 Nomenclature and Differential Diagnosis (Phenocopies)
* The term **Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy** is defined as left ventricular wall thickness $\ge 15\text{ mm}$ (or $\ge 13\text{ mm}$ with a positive family history or pathogenic gene variant) that cannot be solely explained by abnormal loading conditions.
* In pediatric patients, the diagnosis is established with an LV wall thickness $z\text{-score} \ge 2.0$ or $> 2.5$ standard deviations above the body surface area-adjusted mean.
* **HCM Phenocopies (Mimics):** A crucial step in evaluation is differentiating sarcomeric HCM from systemic disorders that cause secondary myocardial thickening:
  - **Fabry Disease:** X-linked $\alpha$-galactosidase A deficiency; features include neuropathic pain, angiokeratomas, renal dysfunction, and short PR interval.
  - **Cardiac Amyloidosis (ATTR and AL):** Amyloid fibril deposition; marked apical sparing on strain echo, low ECG voltage despite thickened walls, elevated biomarkers.
  - **Danon Disease:** X-linked *LAMP2* deficiency; severe early-onset LV hypertrophy, prominent pre-excitation (WPW), skeletal myopathy, cognitive impairment.
  - **PRKAG2 Cardiomyopathy:** Glycogen storage cardiomyopathy with massive hypertrophy, AV block, and ventricular pre-excitation.
  - **RASopathies (Noonan Syndrome):** Facial dysmorphism, pulmonary valve stenosis, intellectual disability.

### 2.3 Sarcomeric Genetics and Etiology
* Approximately 40% to 60% of probands have an identifiable pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant in genes encoding the cardiac sarcomere, inherited in an **autosomal dominant** pattern with variable penetrance and expression.
* The two most common genes account for $\approx 70\%$ of all gene-positive cases:
  - ***MYH7*:** Encoding cardiac $\beta$-myosin heavy chain (thick filament).
  - ***MYBPC3*:** Encoding cardiac myosin-binding protein C (thick filament regulator).
* Less frequent sarcomeric genes include ***TNNT2*** (cardiac troponin T), ***TNNI3*** (cardiac troponin I), ***TPM1*** ($\alpha$-tropomyosin), ***ACTC1*** (cardiac $\alpha$-actin), ***MYL2*** (regulatory light chain), and ***MYL3*** (essential light chain).
* Non-sarcomeric genetic etiologies and polygenic/epigenetic modifiers contribute to phenotypic expression in gene-negative probands.

### 2.4 Clinical Course and Natural History
The clinical course of HCM encompasses four primary disease pathways:
1. **Normal Longevity / Stable Disease:** The majority of patients experience mild or no symptoms, preserve normal LV systolic function, and achieve a normal life expectancy with guideline-directed medical surveillance.
2. **Dynamic Outflow Obstruction and Exertional Symptoms:** Manifesting as dyspnea, fatigue, angina, and exercise intolerance due to LVOTO, mitral regurgitation, and secondary diastolic filling impairment.
3. **Progressive Heart Failure (End-Stage / Burned-Out HCM):** In approximately 5% to 10% of patients, extensive myocardial fibrosis leads to adverse LV remodeling, progressive wall thinning, cavity dilation, and systolic dysfunction (LVEF $< 50\%$).
4. **Arrhythmias and Sudden Cardiac Death:** Predisposition to ventricular arrhythmias (VT/VF) and atrial fibrillation, requiring systematic risk stratification and proactive rhythm monitoring.

## 3. Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

### 3.1 Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction (LVOTO)
* Dynamic LVOTO is present at rest or with physiological provocation in approximately **two-thirds** of patients with HCM:
  - **Mechanisms:** Characterized by systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve leaflets (anterior or posterior) contacting the hypertrophied basal interventricular septum. Venturi forces and flow drag draw elongated mitral leaflets into the high-velocity LVOT stream.
  - **Hemodynamic Thresholds:** A peak instantaneous LVOT gradient **$\ge 30\text{ mm Hg}$** defines dynamic obstruction. A gradient **$\ge 50\text{ mm Hg}$** (at rest or with Valsalva / exercise provocation) is the threshold for hemodynamic significance and guides consideration for invasive septal reduction therapy or cardiac myosin inhibitors.
  - **Dynamic Variability:** The LVOT gradient fluctuates with LV preload, afterload, and myocardial contractility. Dehydration, vasodilators, nitrates, and Valsalva maneuver exacerbate obstruction; volume loading, bradycardia, and increased afterload diminish it.

### 3.2 Diastolic Dysfunction
Diastolic dysfunction is nearly universal in HCM, driven by intrinsic cellular hypercontractility, impaired active myocardial relaxation, myofibrillar disarray, and interstitial collagen fibrosis:
* Decreased left ventricular compliance elevates left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) and causes secondary left atrial hypertension and dilatation.
* Increased left atrial volume index ($> 34\text{ mL/m}^2$) reflects chronically elevated LV filling pressures and strongly predicts risk of atrial fibrillation, stroke, and heart failure hospitalization.

### 3.3 Mitral Regurgitation
* Mitral regurgitation in obstructive HCM is typically **secondary (dynamic)**, resulting from incomplete leaflet coaptation caused by SAM.
* Because the anterior leaflet is pulled anteriorly and medially, the dynamic regurgitant jet is classically directed **posteriorly and laterally** into the left atrium during mid-to-late systole.
* An anteriorly or centrally directed jet should prompt suspicion of intrinsic primary mitral valve pathology (e.g., myxomatous leaflet prolapse, ruptured chordae tendineae, or leaflet calcification), which necessitates surgical repair rather than isolated septal reduction.

### 3.4 Myocardial Ischemia and Microvascular Dysfunction
Myocardial ischemia frequently occurs in the absence of epicardial atherosclerotic coronary artery disease:
* **Microvascular Angina:** Caused by severe coronary microvascular dysfunction, marked intimal hyperplasia and medial hypertrophy of intramural coronary arterioles, and myocardial capillary rarefaction.
* **Demand-Supply Mismatch:** Massive myocardial hypertrophy increases myocardial oxygen demand, while elevated intracavitary systolic pressure, shortened diastole, and prolonged relaxation compress intramural vessels and impair coronary perfusion.
* Repetitive subendocardial ischemia contributes over decades to replacement fibrosis (demonstrated as late gadolinium enhancement on CMR) and adverse ventricular remodeling.

### 3.5 Autonomic Dysfunction
Blunted or abnormal blood pressure responses during exercise (defined as failure to increase systolic blood pressure by $\ge 20\text{ mm Hg}$ or an exertional systolic blood pressure fall $> 20\text{ mm Hg}$) occurs in up to 25% of patients with HCM, reflecting inappropriate peripheral vasodilation and reflex baroreceptor abnormalities.

## 4. Shared Decision-Making</summary><div class="details-content">

### 4.1 Principles of Patient-Centered Shared Decision-Making
Shared decision-making is a foundational component of quality care in HCM. Because many management choices—including genetic testing, lifestyle adaptations, competitive sports participation, prophylactic ICD placement, and invasive septal reduction therapies—depend on individual risk tolerance, lifestyle preferences, and personal values, a paternalistic model of care is unacceptable:
* Clinicians must ensure full, balanced disclosure of expected benefits, procedural risks, long-term complications, and alternative management strategies.
* Patients and their families should be actively empowered to articulate personal life goals, athletic aspirations, and concerns regarding invasive procedures or device-related restrictions.

## 5. Multidisciplinary HCM Centers</summary><div class="details-content">

### 5.1 Care Delivery Model: Comprehensive and Primary HCM Centers
Given the anatomical heterogeneity, complex genetics, and technical demands of invasive interventions, optimal HCM management requires specialized multidisciplinary care teams:
* **Comprehensive HCM Centers:** Advanced tertiary/quaternary academic programs equipped with dedicated HCM cardiologists, multimodality imaging specialists (advanced 3D echocardiography, CMR, cardiac CT), dedicated HCM cardiac surgeons with high surgical myectomy volume ($> 20\text{--}30\text{ procedures/year}$), interventional cardiologists experienced in alcohol septal ablation, cardiovascular geneticists and genetic counselors, and advanced heart failure / electrophysiology teams.
* **Primary HCM Centers:** Regional centers capable of initial clinical evaluation, multimodality imaging, medical therapy optimization, and family cascade screening, maintaining formal collaborative referral pathways to comprehensive centers for complex procedures.
* **Procedural Volumes and Outcomes:** Landmark studies confirm an inverse relationship between institutional/operator volume and procedural mortality/complications. At high-volume surgical myectomy centers, 30-day procedural mortality is consistently $< 1\%$, complete relief of LVOT gradient is achieved in $> 90\%$, and permanent pacemaker dependency is $< 3\%$.

## 6. Diagnosis, Initial Evaluation, and Follow-Up</summary><div class="details-content">

### 6.1 Clinical Diagnosis and Initial Evaluation
The clinical diagnosis of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is established by identifying unexplained left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH) with cardiac imaging:
* **Diagnostic Thresholds:**
  - In adults: Maximal end-diastolic wall thickness **$\ge 15\text{ mm}$** in any myocardial segment (or **$\ge 13\text{ mm}$** in the presence of a confirmed family history of HCM or a verified pathogenic/likely pathogenic sarcomeric variant) in the absence of secondary causes of hypertrophy.
  - In children: LV wall thickness **$z\text{-score} \ge 2.0$** (or $> 2.5$ standard deviations above body surface area-adjusted mean).
* **Physical Examination:** A harsh, crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur at the left sternal border that characteristically **accentuates** with maneuvers decreasing LV preload or afterload (Valsalva strain phase, abrupt standing) and **attenuates** with maneuvers increasing preload or afterload (passive leg raise, squatting, handgrip).

### 6.2 Echocardiography
Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the primary first-line imaging modality for diagnosis, hemodynamic characterization, and longitudinal follow-up:
* **Comprehensive Baseline TTE:** Recommended for all patients with suspected or confirmed HCM (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR). Essential elements include:
  - Accurate measurement of maximal LV wall thickness across all segments from base to apex;
  - Evaluation of systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve;
  - Quantification of resting and provoked left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) gradients using continuous-wave Doppler;
  - Assessment of mitral regurgitation severity and jet direction (posterolaterally directed secondary MR vs central/anterior organic MR);
  - Determination of left atrial volume index ($> 34\text{ mL/m}^2$) and LV diastolic filling parameters;
  - Identification of right ventricular hypertrophy, apical pouching, or apical aneurysm.
* **Provocative Maneuvers for Latent Obstruction:** If the resting LVOT gradient is $< 50\text{ mm Hg}$, physiological provocation (Valsalva maneuver in sitting and standing positions) should be performed routinely. In symptomatic patients with gradients $< 50\text{ mm Hg}$ despite bedside maneuvers, **exercise stress echocardiography** is strongly recommended (Class 1, Level B-NR). Pharmacological provocation with dobutamine is not recommended because of non-physiological inotropic and chronotropic responses.
* **Periodic Surveillance TTE:** Recommended every **1 to 2 years** in clinically stable patients, and promptly upon any change in clinical status or new cardiovascular symptoms (Class 1, Level B-NR).

### 6.3 Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (CMR) Imaging
CMR imaging has emerged as an indispensable cornerstone for definitive diagnosis, phenotypic characterization, and risk stratification in HCM:
* **Indications for Baseline CMR:** Recommended in all patients with HCM when available (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR). CMR provides superior spatial and contrast resolution, particularly in segments poorly visualized by echocardiography (anterolateral free wall, apex, posterior septum).
* **Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE):** Quantifying myocardial fibrosis using LGE is a critical determinant of arrhythmic risk:
  - Extensive LGE involving **$\ge 15\%$** of total LV mass is an established major risk factor for sudden cardiac death (SCD) and supports primary prevention ICD implantation.
  - Patchy mid-wall LGE located at the junctions of the interventricular septum and right ventricular free wall is the classic hallmark of sarcomeric HCM.
* **Apical Aneurysm Detection:** CMR has substantially higher sensitivity than echocardiography for detecting small-to-medium **left ventricular apical aneurysms**, which confer high risks of both thromboembolism and monomorphic ventricular tachycardia.
* **Differentiating Phenocopies:** CMR tissue characterization (T1/T2 mapping, extracellular volume fraction [ECV]) is invaluable for distinguishing HCM from cardiac amyloidosis (diffuse subendocardial LGE, markedly elevated ECV), Fabry disease (low native myocardial T1, inferolateral basal LGE), and athlete's heart (homogeneous physiologic remodeling without LGE).

### 6.5 Heart Rhythm Assessment
Cardiac arrhythmias are major determinants of morbidity and mortality in HCM:
* **Ambulatory ECG Monitoring:** Continuous 24- to 48-hour ambulatory Holter monitoring is recommended at baseline evaluation for all patients with HCM to screen for asymptomatic non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT) and subclinical atrial fibrillation (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).

### 6.8 Genetics and Family Screening
Genetic evaluation provides definitive molecular diagnosis and enables cascade evaluation of at-risk relatives:
* **Pre- and Post-Test Genetic Counseling:** Expert counseling by a cardiovascular geneticist or genetic counselor is recommended for all patients undergoing genetic testing (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **Proband Genetic Testing:** Targeted next-generation sequencing panel testing of sarcomeric genes (*MYH7, MYBPC3, TNNT2, TNNI3, TPM1, ACTC1, MYL2, MYL3*) and major phenocopy genes (*GLA, PRKAG2, LAMP2, TTR*) is recommended for all probands with HCM (Class 1, Level B-NR).
* **Cascade Genetic Testing:** When a pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variant is identified in the proband, predictive cascade genetic testing should be offered to all first-degree relatives (Class 1, Level B-NR). Relatives testing negative for the familial P/LP variant can be safely discharged from longitudinal clinical surveillance.
* **Clinical Screening of Relatives:** In first-degree relatives of probands with HCM (or when genetic testing is non-informative or unavailable), longitudinal clinical screening with 12-lead ECG and 2D echocardiography is recommended:
  - **Children and Adolescents:** Every 1 to 2 years starting by age 8 to 10 years (or annually during pubertal growth spurts).
  - **Adult Relatives:** Every 3 to 5 years (or sooner if symptoms develop).

### 6.9 Individuals Who Are Genotype-Positive, Phenotype-Negative
Individuals who inherit a familial sarcomeric P/LP variant but do not manifest left ventricular hypertrophy or other phenotypic criteria are classified as **genotype-positive, phenotype-negative (G+/P-)**:
* **Longitudinal Surveillance:** Periodic clinical evaluation with 12-lead ECG and echocardiography every 2 to 3 years in children and every 3 to 5 years in adults is recommended to detect delayed phenotypic emergence (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **No Prophylactic Pharmacotherapy:** Prophylactic administration of beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, or other medications is **not recommended** to prevent the development of hypertrophy (Class 3: No Benefit, Level B-R; VANISH trial).
* **Unrestricted Lifestyle:** G+/P- individuals should be reassured that they do not have clinical cardiomyopathy, have an excellent prognosis, and should not be restricted from competitive athletics or recreational sports in the absence of phenotypic expression.

## 7. SCD Risk Assessment and Prevention</summary><div class="details-content">

### 7.1 Sudden Cardiac Death (SCD) Risk Assessment
Sudden cardiac death (SCD) from ventricular tachyarrhythmias (ventricular tachycardia / ventricular fibrillation) is the most devastating complication of HCM. Systematic risk stratification must be performed at baseline and repeated every **1 to 2 years** (or sooner if clinical status changes):
* **Established Major Clinical Risk Factors:**
  1. **Prior Ventricular Fibrillation, Sustained VT, or Resuscitated Cardiac Arrest:** Absolute indication for secondary prevention ICD.
  2. **Family History of Premature HCM-Related SCD:** Definite or probable sudden cardiac death in $\ge 1$ first-degree or close relative $\le 50$ years of age.
  3. **Massive Left Ventricular Hypertrophy:** Maximal wall thickness **$\ge 30\text{ mm}$** (or $z\text{-score} \ge 6$ in children) in any LV segment.
  4. **Unexplained Recent Syncope:** Syncope occurring within the preceding 6 to 12 months deemed not neurocardiogenic/vasovagal.
  5. **Left Ventricular Apical Aneurysm:** Regardless of aneurysm size, associated with a distinct arrhythmogenic scarred rim and thromboembolic propensity.
  6. **Systolic Dysfunction (End-Stage / Burned-Out):** Left ventricular ejection fraction **$< 50\%$**.
  7. **Extensive Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE):** Involving **$\ge 15\%$** of total LV myocardial mass on contrast CMR.
* **Contributory / Modifying Risk Markers:** Non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT) on ambulatory monitoring (especially if frequent, rapid, or prolonged), blunted or hypotensive blood pressure response to exercise, and markedly elevated left ventricular outflow tract gradient.

### 7.2 Patient Selection for ICD Placement
Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) are the only proven therapy for terminating malignant ventricular arrhythmias and preventing SCD in HCM:
* **Secondary Prevention (Class 1):** In patients with HCM who have survived a cardiac arrest due to VF or sustained VT, or experienced spontaneous sustained VT causing syncope or hemodynamic compromise, an ICD is strongly recommended (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **Primary Prevention in Adults:**
  - In patients harboring **$\ge 1$ major conventional risk factor** (massive LVH $\ge 30\text{ mm}$, family history of premature SCD, unexplained recent syncope, apical aneurysm, or LVEF $< 50\%$), ICD placement is reasonable (Class 2a, Level B-NR).
  - In patients with extensive LGE ($\ge 15\%$) on CMR or significant NSVT, ICD placement is reasonable (Class 2a, Level B-NR).
  - When clinical risk remains borderline or uncertain after conventional assessment, European (HCM Risk-SCD) or pediatric risk prediction calculators may provide supportive guidance, but clinical judgment and shared decision-making remain paramount.
* **Primary Prevention in Children:** In pediatric patients with $\ge 1$ major risk factor (family history of early SCD, massive LVH with $z\text{-score} \ge 6$, unexplained syncope, or NSVT), ICD placement is reasonable (Class 2a, Level B-NR).

## 8. Management of HCM</summary><div class="details-content">

### 8.1 Management of Symptomatic Patients With Obstructive HCM

#### 8.1.1 Pharmacological Management of Obstructive HCM
The primary objective of medical therapy is relieving symptoms of heart failure (dyspnea, fatigue) and chest pain by reducing the dynamic LVOT gradient, improving diastolic ventricular relaxation, and lengthening ventricular filling time:
* **First-Line Therapy — Beta-Blockers:** Non-vasodilating oral beta-blockers (e.g., metoprolol succinate, atenolol, propranolol) titrated to a resting heart rate of $55\text{--}65\text{ bpm}$ are recommended as initial first-line therapy (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR). Beta-blockers attenuate exercise-induced LVOT gradients by blunting sympathetic chronotropic and inotropic surges.
* **Alternative First-Line — Non-Dihydropyridine Calcium Channel Blockers:** In patients who are intolerant of or have contraindications to beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (**verapamil** or **diltiazem**) are recommended (Class 1, Level B-NR). Caution is warranted in patients with resting gradients $> 100\text{ mm Hg}$ or severe pulmonary congestion, as vasodilator effects can precipitate pulmonary edema.
* **Second-Line / Add-On Therapy — Cardiac Myosin Inhibitors (Mavacamten):**
  - In symptomatic patients with obstructive HCM who have persistent NYHA class II to III symptoms despite monotherapy with beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers, **mavacamten** is recommended as add-on therapy (Class 1, Level of Evidence A).
  - In the pivotal Phase 3 EXPLORER-HCM trial ($n=251$), mavacamten achieved the primary composite functional endpoint in 37% versus 17% on placebo ($P=0.0005$), reduced resting and provoked gradients to $< 30\text{ mm Hg}$ in 57% of patients, and markedly improved health status (KCCQ score).
  - In the VALOR-HCM trial ($n=112$), mavacamten reduced eligibility for invasive septal reduction therapy from 100% to 18% at 16 weeks ($P<0.001$).
  - **Safety and Monitoring:** Because mavacamten directly inhibits actin-myosin cross-bridging, it reversibly lowers LVEF. Periodic echocardiographic surveillance of LVEF is mandatory (at baseline, weeks 4, 8, 12, and every 12 weeks thereafter). Therapy must be temporarily withheld if LVEF drops below $50\%$. Mavacamten is metabolized by CYP2C19 and CYP3A4, requiring strict adherence to drug-interaction protocols.
* **Alternative Second-Line — Disopyramide:** In patients with persistent symptoms on beta-blockers or calcium channel blockers where cardiac myosin inhibitors are not available or not tolerated, disopyramide (Class Ia antiarrhythmic with negative inotropic properties) combined with an AV-nodal blocking agent is recommended (Class 1, Level B-NR).
* **Medications to AVOID (Class 3: Harm):** Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (nifedipine, amlodipine), oral nitrates, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, and cardiac digitalis glycosides (digoxin) cause peripheral vasodilation or positive inotropy, exacerbating dynamic LVOTO.

#### 8.1.2 Invasive Treatment of Symptomatic Patients With Obstructive HCM (Septal Reduction Therapy)
When severe symptoms (NYHA functional class III–IV, exertional presyncope, or syncope) persist despite maximally tolerated guideline-directed pharmacotherapy, and a dynamic peak LVOT gradient **$\ge 50\text{ mm Hg}$** (at rest or with provocation) is documented, **invasive septal reduction therapy (SRT)** is indicated:
* **Surgical Septal Myectomy (Morrow Procedure):**
  - The gold standard surgical procedure for obstructive HCM, performed via median sternotomy and transaortic approach (often augmented with transapical resection in midventricular/apical hypertrophy).
  - Recommended as the preferred intervention in younger, healthier patients, those with concomitant surgical indications (coronary artery disease requiring CABG, intrinsic mitral valve disease requiring repair/replacement, subaortic membrane, anomalous papillary muscles), or patients with massive basal septal thickness ($> 25\text{--}30\text{ mm}$) (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
  - High-volume surgical centers report procedural mortality $< 1\%$, permanent complete heart block rates $< 2\text{--}3\%$, and $> 90\%$ long-term freedom from recurrent obstruction.
* **Alcohol Septal Ablation (ASA):**
  - Catheter-based percutaneous technique involving selective balloon catheterization and infusion of $1\text{--}2\text{ mL}$ of absolute ethanol into a suitable septal perforator branch of the left anterior descending artery, inducing targeted focal septal infarction.
  - Recommended as an alternative to surgical myectomy in patients with advanced age, severe noncardiac comorbidities, high surgical risk, or strong informed preference, provided coronary anatomy is favorable and myocardial contrast echocardiography confirms precise localization to the basal septum contacting the mitral valve (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
  - Procedural complete heart block requiring permanent pacemaker implantation occurs in approximately $8\text{ to }12\%$ of cases.
* **Dual-Chamber Pacing:** In patients with drug-refractory symptoms who are not candidates for or decline myectomy, ASA, or cardiac myosin inhibitors, dual-chamber pacing with short AV delay may be considered to reduce gradients via paradoxical septal motion (Class 2b, Level B-R).

### 8.2 Management of Patients With Nonobstructive HCM With Preserved EF
Approximately one-third of HCM patients have nonobstructive physiology (resting and provoked peak LVOT gradients $< 30\text{ mm Hg}$):
* **Symptom Pathophysiology:** Exertional symptoms in nonobstructive HCM are primarily caused by severe diastolic dysfunction, elevated left atrial filling pressures, and microvascular ischemia.
* **First-Line Pharmacotherapy:** Beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil or diltiazem) are recommended to prolong diastole, improve ventricular compliance, and reduce myocardial oxygen consumption (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **Judicious Diuretics:** Oral loop or thiazide diuretics in low-to-moderate doses may be added to alleviate exertional dyspnea and orthopnea resulting from pulmonary congestion (Class 2a, Level B-NR). Overdiuresis must be avoided because reducing LV end-diastolic volume can precipitate cavity obliteration and dynamic hypotension.
* **SGLT2 Inhibitors:** Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are reasonable in patients with persistent heart failure symptoms (Class 2a, Level B-R).

### 8.3 Management of Patients With HCM and Advanced Heart Failure
In 5% to 10% of patients, extensive transmural fibrosis and adverse remodeling lead to the **"burned-out" or hypokinetic end-stage phenotype**, characterized by left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF $< 50\%$):
* **Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy for HFrEF:** Standard neurohormonal blockade—including angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI) or ACE inhibitors/ARBs, beta-blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), and SGLT2 inhibitors—is recommended (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **Cessation of Negative Inotropes:** Calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem), disopyramide, and cardiac myosin inhibitors must be discontinued once LVEF falls below 50%.
* **Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy (CRT):** In patients with LVEF $< 50\%$, NYHA class II to IV symptoms, and left bundle branch block (LBBB with QRS duration $\ge 130\text{--}150\text{ ms}$), CRT is recommended (Class 1, Level B-NR).
* **Advanced Therapies (Transplantation and LVAD):** In patients developing refractory Stage D heart failure despite optimal GDMT and CRT, evaluation for orthotopic heart transplantation or left ventricular assist device (LVAD) is recommended (Class 1, Level B-NR). Note that small cavity dimensions may pose anatomical challenges for continuous-flow LVAD inflow cannulation.

### 8.4 Management of Patients With HCM and Atrial Fibrillation
Atrial fibrillation (AF) develops in approximately 20% to 25% of patients with HCM and is associated with severe hemodynamic decompensation and a high risk of thromboembolic stroke:
* **Universal Oral Anticoagulation (DOACs Preferred):** In all patients with HCM who develop clinical or subclinical atrial fibrillation (or atrial flutter), lifelong oral anticoagulation is recommended (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR), **regardless of the CHA2DS2-VASc score**.
  - Direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban) are recommended in preference to vitamin K antagonists (warfarin) due to lower risks of major bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage.
* **Rhythm Control Strategy:** Because loss of atrial systolic contribution and rapid ventricular rates precipitate severe diastolic underfilling and pulmonary edema, an active rhythm control strategy (pharmacological cardioversion, electrical cardioversion, antiarrhythmic drugs, or catheter ablation) is recommended as the initial approach (Class 1, Level B-NR).
* **Antiarrhythmic Drug Therapy:** Amiodarone, sotalol, or dofetilide are reasonable options for maintaining sinus rhythm (Class 2a, Level B-NR). Class Ic antiarrhythmic drugs (flecainide, propafenone) should be used with extreme caution due to proarrhythmic risks in the presence of marked LVH.
* **Catheter Ablation:** Radiofrequency catheter ablation (pulmonary vein isolation) is recommended for symptomatic refractory AF (Class 1, Level B-NR). While recurrence rates are higher than in patients without structural heart disease, repeated ablation procedures achieve effective rhythm stabilization in $> 60\text{--}70\%$ of patients.
* **Surgical Maze Procedure:** In patients undergoing surgical septal myectomy who have concomitant paroxysmal or persistent AF, a concomitant surgical Cox-Maze procedure with left atrial appendage exclusion is recommended (Class 1, Level B-NR).

### 8.5 Management of Patients With HCM and Ventricular Arrhythmias
* **Non-Sustained VT (NSVT):** Asymptomatic NSVT detected on ambulatory monitoring does not warrant specific antiarrhythmic therapy, but serves as a contributory risk marker for SCD.
* **Sustained Ventricular Arrhythmias:** In patients experiencing sustained VT or VF, secondary prevention ICD implantation is mandatory (Class 1, Level A).
* **Suppressive Antiarrhythmic Therapy:** In patients with recurrent appropriate ICD therapies (shocks or ATP) for ventricular arrhythmias despite beta-blocker therapy, treatment with **amiodarone** or **sotalol** is reasonable (Class 2a, Level B-NR).
* **Catheter Ablation for Monomorphic VT:** Catheter ablation is reasonable for drug-refractory monomorphic VT or recurrent VT storms, frequently targeting scar borders around apical aneurysms or basal septal surgical/ablation margins (Class 2a, Level B-NR).

## 9. Lifestyle Considerations for Patients With HCM</summary><div class="details-content">

### 9.1 Recreational Physical Activity and Competitive Sports
Historically, individuals diagnosed with HCM were broadly restricted from vigorous exercise due to concerns about exercise-triggered sudden cardiac death. Contemporary evidence demonstrates that physical inactivity carries substantial adverse cardiovascular and metabolic risks, and that individualized exercise prescriptions are safe and beneficial:
* **Mild-to-Moderate Recreational Exercise:** Regular, low-to-moderate intensity physical activity (e.g., brisk walking, cycling, lap swimming, light jogging) is strongly recommended for all patients with HCM to maintain cardiorespiratory fitness, improve quality of life, and reduce metabolic morbidity (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **Vigorous Recreational Exercise and Competitive Sports:**
  - In individuals with HCM, participation in competitive sports or high-intensity athletic activities may be considered after a comprehensive clinical evaluation by an expert HCM specialist and a structured shared decision-making dialogue (Class 2a, Level of Evidence B-NR).
  - Landmark registry data (e.g., the prospective LIVE-HCM study of $> 1{,}600$ patients) demonstrated that individuals engaging in vigorous exercise or competitive sports had rates of death or life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias comparable to those engaging in moderate or non-vigorous exercise.
  - An individualized risk assessment must evaluate: history of syncope, dynamic LVOT obstruction, presence of an ICD, high-risk genetic mutations, and degree of myocardial hypertrophy.
* **Genotype-Positive, Phenotype-Negative (G+/P-):** Individuals who carry a pathogenic sarcomeric variant but exhibit no clinical or imaging signs of hypertrophy should not be restricted from competitive sports or vigorous physical activities (Class 3: No Benefit / Restriction Not Recommended, Level B-NR).

### 9.2 Occupation in Patients With HCM
* **Individualized Occupational Assessment:** Most individuals with HCM can safely pursue their chosen professions. Comprehensive evaluation of functional capacity, dynamic symptoms, and arrhythmic risk should guide employment fitness (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **High-Risk Safety-Critical Occupations:** Specific regulatory restrictions apply to commercial airline pilots, commercial motor vehicle operators, military combat personnel, and high-hazard industrial positions:
  - Regulatory standards (e.g., Federal Aviation Administration [FAA], Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration [FMCSA]) must be adhered to.
  - Patients should be counseled that heavy manual labor involving extreme burst isometric straining, rapid dehydration, or heat stress can exacerbate dynamic LVOT obstruction and provoke presyncope or syncope.

### 9.3 Pregnancy in Patients With HCM
Most women with HCM have favorable maternal and fetal outcomes when managed by a multidisciplinary Cardio-Obstetrics team:
* **Preconceptional Counseling:** Women of childbearing potential should receive comprehensive preconception counseling regarding maternal cardiac risks, inheritable genetic transmission (50% risk of transmitting autosomal dominant sarcomeric variants), and teratogenic medications (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **Multidisciplinary Team Care:** Pregnant patients with HCM should be managed collaboratively by cardiologists with HCM expertise, maternal-fetal medicine specialists, and obstetric anesthesiologists (Class 1, Level B-NR).
* **High-Risk Features:** Women with pre-existing severe symptoms (NYHA class III–IV), resting LVOT gradients $> 50\text{ mm Hg}$, LVEF $< 50\%$, or active atrial/ventricular arrhythmias have elevated risks of pulmonary edema and heart failure decompensation during late pregnancy and delivery.
* **Pharmacological Safety in Pregnancy:**
  - **Beta-Blockers:** Metoprolol or propranolol are the preferred agents for symptom management and rate control. **Atenolol must be avoided** due to documented associations with fetal growth restriction.
  - **Contraindicated Medications (Class 3: Harm):** ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists are strictly contraindicated due to fetal fetopathy and renal agenesis.
  - **Cardiac Myosin Inhibitors (Mavacamten):** Teratogenic in animal models; mavacamten **must not be administered during pregnancy or breastfeeding**, and effective contraception is mandatory in women of childbearing potential.
* **Labor and Delivery:** Vaginal delivery with effective regional (epidural) anesthesia is recommended as the preferred mode of delivery, reserving cesarean section for standard obstetric indications (Class 1, Level B-NR). Epidural anesthesia must be carefully titrated to avoid abrupt vasodilation and hypotension.

### 9.4 Patients With Comorbidities
Concurrent medical comorbidities interact synergistically with HCM pathology and require aggressive optimization:
* **Systemic Hypertension:** Present in $> 50\%$ of adult patients with HCM. Uncontrolled hypertension accelerates secondary concentric hypertrophy, worsens diastolic dysfunction, and increases stroke risk. First-line antihypertensive therapy should utilize beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers. If additional agents are required, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists can be carefully introduced in nonobstructive patients. Vasodilating agents (dihydropyridines, nitrates) should be avoided in obstructive physiology.
* **Obesity:** Excess adiposity dramatically increases left ventricular mass, worsens exertional dyspnea, and markedly escalates the incidence of atrial fibrillation and dynamic LVOTO. Structured lifestyle interventions, dietary counseling, and supervised exercise are recommended (Class 1, Level B-NR).
* **Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA):** Highly prevalent in patients with HCM. OSA increases nocturnal sympathetic surges, promotes systemic hypertension, and triggers atrial and ventricular arrhythmias. Routine screening for snoring, daytime somnolence, and nocturnal gasping, with subsequent polysomnography and continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy, is recommended (Class 1, Level B-NR).

## 10. Evidence Gaps and Future Directions</summary><div class="details-content">

Despite transformative advancements in the understanding and management of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, several pivotal clinical and biological questions remain unanswered:
1. **Refining the Diagnosis of HCM:** Developing machine learning algorithms and advanced automated echocardiographic/CMR strain metrics to distinguish early sarcomeric HCM from physiological athlete's heart and non-sarcomeric phenocopies with high specificity.
2. **Developing Disease-Modifying Therapies:** Determining whether early initiation of cardiac myosin inhibitors or next-generation small-molecule allosteric modulators in young patients or genotype-positive, phenotype-negative individuals can prevent the development or progression of hypertrophy, interstitial fibrosis, and diastolic dysfunction (e.g., ongoing gene-silencing, CRISPR base-editing, and AAV-mediated gene therapy platforms).
3. **Improving Care for Nonobstructive HCM:** While obstructive HCM has effective medical and surgical options, nonobstructive HCM remains an area of significant unmet therapeutic need. Novel therapies targeting myocardial energetics, microvascular ischemia, and advanced antifibrotic pathways are urgently needed.
4. **Improving and Expanding SCD Risk Stratification:** Enhancing risk algorithms in intermediate-risk adults and pediatric populations by integrating polygenic risk scores, serum circulating biomarkers, high-resolution CMR parametric mapping (ECV and diffuse interstitial fibrosis), and artificial intelligence-augmented electrocardiography.
5. **Arrhythmia Management:** Refining catheter ablation strategies for atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachycardia in structurally abnormal, fibrotic ventricles; determining the role of left atrial appendage closure devices in stroke prevention; and identifying optimal pharmacotherapies for preventing post-ablation recurrent arrhythmias.
6. **Expanding Understanding of Genetic Architecture:** Deciphering the genetic and epigenetic determinants of incomplete penetrance, variable phenotypic expression, and non-sarcomeric disease; identifying protective genetic modifiers that prevent severe hypertrophy in individuals harboring high-risk sarcomeric mutations.
