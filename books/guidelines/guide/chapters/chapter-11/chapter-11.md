## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### The AF-CARE Pathway: The New Central Paradigm
The 2024 ESC Guidelines introduce an integrated, patient-centred framework designated the **AF-CARE pathway**, which replaces previous diagnostic and therapeutic schemes (such as the 2020 CC to ABC scheme). AF-CARE establishes four continuous, interconnected pillars to structure patient management across the lifespan of atrial fibrillation (AF):
* **[C] Comorbidity and risk factor management:** Systematic identification and aggressive intervention on upstream cardiovascular risk factors and systemic comorbidities (arterial hypertension, heart failure, type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, obstructive sleep apnoea, chronic kidney disease, sedentary lifestyle, and excess alcohol consumption). This upstream management halts progressive atrial structural and electrical remodelling, decreases arrhythmia recurrence, and prevents systemic complications.
* **[A] Avoid stroke and thromboembolism:** Modernized thromboembolic risk assessment utilizing the updated **CHA2DS2-VA score**, preferential administration of Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) over Vitamin K Antagonists (VKAs), strict evidence-based indications for anticoagulation in subclinical AF / device-detected atrial high-rate episodes (AHRE), evaluation of bleeding risk via the HAS-BLED score exclusively to address modifiable factors, and expanded indications for surgical left atrial appendage (LAA) exclusion during cardiac surgery.
* **[R] Reduce symptoms by rate and rhythm control:** A personalized, shared-decision approach balancing lenient resting heart rate targets ($< 100	ext{--}110	ext{ b.p.m.}$) with early and assertive rhythm control. Upgraded indications position catheter pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) as a first-line Class I therapy in symptomatic paroxysmal AF, a Class I intervention in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), and endorse a structured "wait-and-see" rate-control strategy for acute-onset AF ($< 48	ext{ h}$).
* **[E] Evaluation and dynamic reassessment:** Structured clinical review recommended at 6 months following therapy initiation or adjustment, and at least annually thereafter, to dynamically reassess stroke and bleeding risks, comorbidity control, symptom burden, treatment adherence, and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs).

### Major Paradigm Shifts in the 2024 Guidelines
* **The CHA2DS2-VA Score (Removal of Female Sex):** Female sex is recognized as an age-dependent stroke risk modifier that amplifies risk only when $\ge 1$ non-sex risk factor is present, but does not independently confer excess stroke risk in isolation. Consequently, female sex ('Sc') has been removed from the score calculation, streamlining clinical stratification:
  * **Score $\ge 2$:** Oral anticoagulation (OAC) is **recommended** (Class I, Level A).
  * **Score = 1:** Oral anticoagulation (OAC) **should be considered** (Class IIa, Level B).
  * **Score = 0:** Antithrombotic therapy is **not recommended** (Class III, Level A).
* **DOAC Supremacy Over VKA:** DOACs (apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) are recommended in preference to VKAs (Class I, Level A) for all eligible patients with non-valvular AF, due to significantly reduced risks of intracranial haemorrhage, fatal bleeding, and all-cause mortality, alongside equal or superior thromboembolic protection.
* **Evidence-Based Management of Subclinical AF (AHRE):** Based on the randomized ARTESiA and NOAH-AFNET 6 trials, oral anticoagulation is only recommended for consideration in patients with device-detected subclinical AF (AHRE) when cumulative daily episode duration is $\ge 24	ext{ hours}$ AND the patient has an elevated baseline stroke risk (CHA2DS2-VA $\ge 2$) (Class IIa, Level B). For AHRE $< 24	ext{ hours}$, the risk of major bleeding outweighs stroke prevention, and routine OAC is not indicated.
* **HAS-BLED Score Role Clarified:** Bleeding risk assessment using the HAS-BLED score is indicated to identify and correct modifiable bleeding risk factors and flag patients for closer monitoring. A high bleeding risk score must **never** be used to withhold OAC from patients at elevated risk of stroke (Class III, Level A).
* **Stringent Upstream Targets:**
  * **Blood Pressure:** Target systolic blood pressure $< 130	ext{ mmHg}$ and diastolic $< 80	ext{ mmHg}$ in hypertensive patients with AF (Class I, Level B).
  * **Heart Failure:** Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are recommended for all patients with AF and heart failure regardless of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (Class I, Level A).
  * **Weight Loss:** Structured intensive lifestyle intervention targeting $\ge 10\%$ body weight reduction in overweight and obese individuals with AF (Class I, Level B).
  * **Exercise:** Tailored physical activity targeting at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity exercise (Class I, Level B).
  * **Alcohol Restriction:** Reduction of alcohol intake to $\le 3$ standard drinks ($\le 30	ext{ g}$ ethanol) per week (Class I, Level B).
* **First-Line Catheter Ablation:** Catheter pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is upgraded to a **Class I** first-line recommendation (alternative to antiarrhythmic drugs) in patients with symptomatic paroxysmal AF to prevent recurrence (Class I, Level A), as well as a Class I recommendation in AF patients with HFrEF.
* **Left Atrial Appendage (LAA) Interventions:**
  * **Surgical LAA Exclusion:** Class I, Level A recommendation during concomitant open or thoracoscopic cardiac surgery in patients with AF and elevated stroke risk (CHA2DS2-VA $\ge 2$), driven by the landmark randomized LAAOS III trial demonstrating a $33\%$ reduction in ischaemic stroke.
  * **Percutaneous LAA Occlusion (LAAO):** Restricted to Class IIb (Level B) only in patients with clear, documented, irreversible contraindications to long-term oral anticoagulation.

## 1. Preamble</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinical practice guidelines summarize and evaluate all available evidence on a particular issue at the time of the writing process, with the overarching aim of assisting healthcare professionals in selecting the best management strategies for an individual patient suffering from a given condition. 

These 2024 Guidelines on the management of Atrial Fibrillation were developed by an interdisciplinary Task Force appointed by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), with direct collaboration from the European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS), the European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA), and endorsed by the European Stroke Organisation (ESO).

The recommendations are grounded on an extensive synthesis of randomized clinical trials, observational cohorts, systematic reviews, and international registry data published up to 2024. Great care has been taken to avoid biases, manage potential conflicts of interest, and formulate concise, practical guidance. The Guideline provides actionable clinical algorithms structured around the patient pathway, ensuring equitable, evidence-based, and multidisciplinary delivery of cardiovascular healthcare.

## 2. Introduction</summary><div class="details-content">

### 2.1 Epidemiology and Disease Burden
Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most prevalent sustained cardiac arrhythmia in adults globally. Currently, the estimated lifetime risk of developing AF is approximately 1 in 3 in individuals of European ancestry aged 40 years and older. Worldwide, tens of millions of individuals are diagnosed with AF, and its prevalence continues to rise steeply due to population aging, enhanced surveillance technologies, and the escalating prevalence of predisposing lifestyle and cardiometabolic comorbidities.

AF is associated with substantial morbidity, premature mortality, and profound socioeconomic costs. Patients suffering from AF endure a 5-fold increase in the risk of ischaemic stroke, a 3-fold increase in the risk of heart failure (HF), a 2-fold increase in cardiovascular dementia and cognitive decline, a 1.5- to 2-fold elevation in all-cause mortality, and marked impairment in health-related quality of life.

### 2.2 Rationale for the 2024 Revision: The AF-CARE Paradigm
Since the publication of the 2020 ESC AF Guidelines, monumental advances have emerged from landmark clinical trials across the entire spectrum of AF management:
1. **Comorbidity modification:** Clear demonstrations that lifestyle optimization (weight reduction, alcohol cessation, physical conditioning) and metabolic pharmacotherapies (such as SGLT2 inhibitors) directly reduce arrhythmia burden and retard atrial remodelling.
2. **Stroke prevention refinements:** The publication of definitive trials on device-detected subclinical AF (NOAH-AFNET 6 and ARTESiA), surgical LAA exclusion (LAAOS III), and reappraisal of sex-specific stroke risks.
3. **Catheter ablation evidence:** Multiple randomized clinical trials evaluating early catheter ablation as a first-line rhythm control strategy and demonstrating survival advantages in patients with concomitant heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.
4. **Structured follow-up:** Increasing recognition that AF is a chronic, progressive, systemic condition requiring structured lifelong multidisciplinary evaluation rather than episodic arrhythmia-focused consultations.

To operationalize these advances, the Task Force formulated the **AF-CARE** pathway, consolidating patient-centred care into an intuitive, evidence-driven, dynamic architecture.

## 3. Definitions, Characterization, and Classification of AF</summary><div class="details-content">

### 3.1 Definition and Temporal Classification of AF
Atrial fibrillation is a supraventricular tachyarrhythmia characterized by uncoordinated atrial electrical activation and consequently ineffective atrial contraction. Electrocardiographic hallmarks include:
1. Irregularly irregular R-R intervals (in the absence of complete atrioventricular block or ventricular pacing).
2. Absence of distinct repeating P waves on the surface ECG.
3. Irregular atrial activations (fibrillatory waves / f waves) with cycle lengths typically $< 200	ext{ ms}$ ($> 300	ext{ b.p.m.}$).

#### Table 5: Definitions and Classifications for the Temporal Pattern of AF
| Temporal Pattern | Clinical Definition |
|---|---|
| **First-diagnosed AF** | AF that has not been diagnosed previously, regardless of the duration of the arrhythmia, presence or absence of symptoms, or temporal pattern. |
| **Paroxysmal AF** | AF that terminates spontaneously or with medical intervention within 7 days of onset. The majority of self-terminating paroxysms terminate within 48 hours. |
| **Persistent AF** | AF that continuously sustains for more than 7 days, including episodes that are terminated by pharmacological or electrical cardioversion at or after 7 days. |
| **Long-standing persistent AF** | Continuous AF lasting $\ge 12$ months when it is decided to adopt a rhythm control strategy (distinguishing it from permanent AF). |
| **Permanent AF** | AF that is accepted by both the patient and the physician, wherein no further attempts at restoring or maintaining sinus rhythm will be pursued. Permanent AF is a management decision rather than an intrinsic pathophysiological attribute. |
| **AF Burden** | The overall proportion of time spent in AF during a specific monitoring period, or the total duration and density of AF episodes recorded by wearable devices or CIEDs. |

#### Table 6: Other Clinical Concepts Relevant to AF
| Concept | Definition and Clinical Significance |
|---|---|
| **Subclinical AF / AHRE** | Asymptomatic atrial high-rate episodes detected by CIEDs (pacemakers, ICDs, CRT, insertable cardiac monitors) in patients without prior clinical AF. The 2024 Guidelines define significant AHRE as lasting $\ge 24	ext{ hours}$. |
| **Post-operative AF** | New-onset AF occurring during the immediate recovery phase following cardiac (incidence $30	ext{--}50\%$) or non-cardiac surgery (incidence $2	ext{--}10\%$). Often resolves, but carries long-term stroke risk and recurrent AF predisposition. |
| **Trigger-induced AF** | AF episodes precipitated by acute reversible non-cardiac stressors (e.g. sepsis, acute pulmonary embolism, severe pneumonia, thyrotoxic crisis, binge alcohol consumption ["holiday heart"], acute trauma). |
| **Pre-excited AF** | AF in patients with an antegrade-conducting accessory pathway (Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome), characterized by rapid, irregular, wide QRS complexes; a medical emergency carrying risk of ventricular fibrillation. |
| **"Lone AF"** | **OBSOLETE TERM:** The Task Force recommends that the term "lone AF" should no longer be used. Thorough diagnostic work-up invariably reveals underlying genetic, lifestyle, or occult cardiovascular drivers. |

### 3.2 Diagnostic Criteria for AF
A definitive clinical diagnosis of AF requires documentation of the arrhythmia on an electrocardiographic recording:
* Standard 12-lead ECG displaying AF characteristics.
* Single-lead or multi-lead ECG tracing showing AF with a continuous duration of $\ge 30	ext{ seconds}$ reviewed and confirmed by a physician or competent healthcare professional.

### Recommendation Table 1 — Recommendations for the Diagnosis of AF
| Recommendations | Class | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmation by an electrocardiogram (12-lead, multiple, or single leads $\ge 30	ext{ s}$) is recommended to establish the diagnosis of clinical AF and commence risk stratification and treatment. | **I** | **A** |

### 3.3 Symptoms Attributable to AF
Symptoms associated with AF vary widely, ranging from completely asymptomatic ("silent" AF) to severely debilitating. Common symptoms include palpitations, fatigue, dyspnoea on exertion, reduced exercise tolerance, dizziness, lightheadedness, chest discomfort, and sleep disturbances. Symptoms correlate poorly with arrhythmia burden; asymptomatic episodes are frequent even in highly symptomatic patients.

#### Table 7: The Modified European Heart Rhythm Association (mEHRA) Symptom Classification
| Score | Symptom Severity | Description of Functional Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **mEHRA I** | None | AF does not cause any symptoms. |
| **mEHRA IIa** | Mild | Normal daily activity is not affected by AF symptoms. |
| **mEHRA IIb** | Moderate | Normal daily activity is not affected, but the patient is troubled by symptoms. |
| **mEHRA III** | Severe | Normal daily activity is affected by AF symptoms. |
| **mEHRA IV** | Disabling | Normal daily activity is discontinued due to severe symptoms. |

|---|---|---|

### 3.4 Diagnostic Evaluation of New AF
Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation is essential to confirm the diagnosis, quantify symptom burden, characterize arrhythmia pattern, assess thromboembolic and bleeding risks, and identify underlying cardiovascular and systemic etiologies.

#### Table 8: Diagnostic Work-up for Patients with AF
| Modality | Diagnostic Role and Clinical Indications |
|---|---|
| **12-lead ECG** | Confirmation of rhythm, measurement of ventricular rate, assessment of conduction abnormalities (PR interval, QRS duration, bundle branch blocks), signs of ischaemia, chamber hypertrophy, and pre-excitation. |
| **Comprehensive Clinical History** | Assessment of symptoms (mEHRA), temporal pattern, prior episodes, family history of AF or cardiomyopathy, alcohol and tobacco use, athletic history, and comorbid conditions. |
| **Laboratory Testing** | Complete blood count, serum creatinine and eGFR, serum electrolytes ($	ext{K}^+$, $	ext{Mg}^{2+}$), liver function tests, thyroid function (TSH, free T4), fasting glucose/HbA1c, and lipid profile. |
| **Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE)** | Recommended in all patients to evaluate left ventricular size and systolic/diastolic function, left atrial dimensions/volume index, right ventricular size and function, valvular heart disease, and pericardial disease. |
| **Ambulatory ECG Monitoring (Holter / Wearables)** | Indicated when paroxysmal AF is suspected but unconfirmed, or to assess ventricular rate control and temporal correlation of symptoms with arrhythmia. |
| **Cardiac Magnetic Resonance (CMR) / CT** | Indicated in selected patients to assess structural heart disease, cardiomyopathies, or pre-procedural pulmonary vein and left atrial anatomy prior to catheter ablation. |

### 3.5 Adverse Events and Clinical Impact
Atrial fibrillation precipitates a vast spectrum of adverse clinical outcomes through loss of atrial transport, rapid irregular ventricular response, progressive atrial and ventricular cardiomyopathy, and intracardiac stasis.

## 5. [C] Comorbidity and Risk Factor Management</summary><div class="details-content">

### 5.1 Systemic Pathophysiology and Upstream Modulation
Atrial fibrillation is rarely an isolated electrical disorder; it represents the clinical manifestation of progressive atrial structural, electrophysiological, and autonomic remodelling driven by underlying cardiovascular disease, systemic comorbidities, and metabolic derangements. Chronic mechanical stretch, neurohormonal activation, microvascular ischaemia, systemic inflammation, and interstitial atrial fibrosis foster a substrate prone to electrical re-entry and ectopic firing.

Aggressive, structured identification and treatment of upstream risk factors is a non-negotiable pillar of the AF-CARE pathway. Comprehensive lifestyle modification and target-driven medical management have been demonstrated to reduce AF symptom burden, lower episode frequency and duration, halt progression from paroxysmal to persistent AF, enhance success rates of catheter ablation, and significantly reduce cardiovascular hospitalizations and mortality.

![Figure 8: Management of key comorbidities to reduce AF recurrence and progression](images/fig08_management_key_comorbidities.jpg)

### 5.2 Arterial Hypertension
Hypertension is the most prevalent predisposing comorbidity for AF, affecting over $70\text{--}80\%$ of AF cohorts. Elevated systolic and pulse pressures augment left ventricular afterload, promoting left ventricular hypertrophy, impaired diastolic relaxation, elevated left ventricular filling pressures, and secondary left atrial enlargement and stretch.
* **Target Blood Pressure:** Blood pressure lowering treatment is recommended to achieve a resting office blood pressure $< 130/80\text{ mmHg}$ (specifically systolic $120\text{--}129\text{ mmHg}$ and diastolic $70\text{--}79\text{ mmHg}$ if tolerated, but avoiding SBP $< 120\text{ mmHg}$).
* **Preferred Pharmacotherapy:** Renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors (ACE inhibitors or ARBs) combined with dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers or thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics represent the first-line therapeutic foundation. RAAS blockade confers supplementary anti-fibrotic and anti-remodelling benefits in atrial tissue.

### 5.3 Heart Failure
Heart failure (HF) and AF share a bidirectional vicious circle: AF predisposes to HF through loss of atrial kick, rapid irregular ventricular response, and tachycardiomyopathy, while HF predisposes to AF through chronic atrial hypertension, stretch-mediated fibrosis, and neurohumoral activation.
* **Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT):** In AF patients with HF and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, LVEF $\le 40\%$) or mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF, LVEF $41\text{--}49\%$), four-pillar neurohormonal therapy (ARNI/ACEi, evidence-based beta-blocker, MRA, and SGLT2 inhibitor) is recommended to reduce mortality, HF hospitalizations, and AF recurrence.
* **SGLT2 Inhibitor Breakthrough:** Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) are recommended across the entire spectrum of HF (HFrEF, HFmrEF, and HFpEF) in patients with AF, based on overwhelming evidence from DAPA-HF, DELIVER, EMPEROR-Reduced, and EMPEROR-Preserved trials demonstrating consistent reductions in cardiovascular death and worsening HF.
* **Congestion Relief:** Loop diuretics are recommended to relieve congestion, optimize volume status, and facilitate safe uptitration of GDMT and rate-controlling medications.

### 5.4 Diabetes Mellitus and Glycaemic Control
Type 2 diabetes mellitus is an independent predictor of incident AF, thromboembolism, and cardiovascular death. Diabetic autonomic neuropathy, advanced glycation end-products, and epicardial adipose tissue inflammation promote atrial arrhythmogenesis. Structured glycaemic optimization (targeting HbA1c $< 7.0\%$ / $< 53\text{ mmol/mol}$) using modern agents with proven cardiovascular benefits (SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists) is recommended as part of comprehensive risk factor management.

### 5.5 Obesity and Weight Reduction
Obesity (BMI $\ge 30\text{ kg/m}^2$) significantly expands epicardial fat volume, precipitates left atrial diastolic overload, promotes fibro-fatty infiltration of the atrial myocardium, and doubles the risk of post-ablation arrhythmia recurrence.
* **Target Weight Loss:** A structured, multidisciplinary weight reduction programme aiming for $\ge 10\%$ body weight reduction (or BMI $< 27\text{ kg/m}^2$) is recommended in overweight and obese AF patients. Such weight loss yields sustained reductions in AF burden, reversal of atrial remodelling, and improved symptom control.
* **Bariatric Surgery:** In morbidly obese individuals (BMI $\ge 40\text{ kg/m}^2$ or BMI $\ge 35\text{ kg/m}^2$ with obesity-related comorbidities) scheduled for rhythm control, metabolic/bariatric surgery may be considered when medical and lifestyle measures fail.

### 5.6 Physical Activity and Cardiorespiratory Fitness
Physical inactivity promotes systemic vascular stiffness, obesity, and hypertension. Conversely, regular, moderate-intensity aerobic exercise enhances autonomic balance, improves cardiorespiratory fitness ($VO_{2\text{peak}}$), and decreases AF recurrence:
* A structured aerobic exercise programme of at least $150\text{ minutes/week}$ of moderate-intensity exercise (or $75\text{ minutes/week}$ of vigorous exercise) is recommended.
* **Caution Regarding Extreme Endurance Sports:** High-volume, chronic lifetime endurance sports (marathon running, ultra-distance cycling) confer an elevated risk of incident AF due to chronic bi-atrial dilatation, heightened vagal tone, and localized micro-fibrosis.

### 5.7 Alcohol Restriction
Alcohol exerts direct electrophysiological cardiotoxicity, shortening atrial refractory periods, impairing intracellular calcium handling, and precipitating nocturnal vagal/adrenergic surges. Prospective randomized trials (such as the Australian Alcohol-AF trial) demonstrate that complete abstinence or substantial reduction in drinking dramatically cuts AF recurrences.
* **Guideline Threshold:** Restricting alcohol consumption to $\le 3$ standard drinks per week ($\le 30\text{ g}$ of pure ethanol per week), or ideally complete abstinence, is recommended.

### 5.8 Obstructive Sleep Apnoea (OSA)
OSA affects up to $50\%$ of AF patients and is characterized by repetitive nocturnal upper airway collapses generating severe negative intrathoracic pressure swings, hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, and autonomic surges that trigger nocturnal AF paroxysms and attenuate ablation efficacy:
* Formal diagnostic testing (overnight polysomnography or home polygraphy) is recommended in patients with suspected OSA. Symptom-based questionnaires alone (e.g. Epworth, STOP-Bang) exhibit poor specificity in AF populations and should not be relied upon exclusively.
* Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy should be considered to reduce nocturnal desaturation, improve quality of life, and reduce post-ablation AF recurrence.

## 6. [A] Avoid Stroke and Thromboembolism</summary><div class="details-content">

### 6.1 Pathophysiology of Thromboembolism in AF
Atrial fibrillation fulfills all components of Virchow's triad:
1. **Endothelial Dysfunction:** Loss of normal endocardial shear stress, subendothelial collagen exposure, and elevated von Willebrand factor.
2. **Stasis of Blood:** Ineffective mechanical atrial contraction and blood pooling, predominantly localized within the trabeculated left atrial appendage (LAA), where over $90\%$ of cardiac thrombi originate in non-rheumatic AF.
3. **Hypercoagulability:** Systemic platelet activation, elevated prothrombin fragments F1+2, and circulating D-dimer.

Without anticoagulation, clinical AF confers a 5-fold increase in the risk of ischaemic stroke. AF-related strokes are characteristically larger, more disabling, more frequently fatal, and prone to early hemorrhagic transformation compared to non-cardioembolic strokes.

### 6.2 Modernized Stroke Risk Assessment: The CHA2DS2-VA Score
Previous guidelines utilized the CHA2DS2-VASc score, which allocated 1 point for female sex ('Sc'). Extensive contemporary epidemiological data confirm that female sex is an age-dependent risk modifier that amplifies stroke risk only in the presence of $\ge 1$ other non-sex risk factor; in the absence of other risk factors (age $< 65$ and no comorbidities), women do not exhibit an increased risk of stroke compared to men. To eliminate clinical confusion and establish unified risk thresholds, the 2024 ESC Guidelines adopted the **CHA2DS2-VA score**, removing female sex from the point tally.

* **CHA2DS2-VA = 0:** Oral anticoagulation is **not recommended** (Class III, Level A).

### 6.3 Oral Anticoagulation: Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) vs VKAs
Direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) have fundamentally supplanted Vitamin K Antagonists (VKAs) across all non-valvular AF clinical trials. In a pooled meta-analysis of pivotal Phase III trials ($> 71,000$ patients), DOACs demonstrated:
* $19\%$ significant reduction in stroke and systemic embolism.
* $51\%$ reduction in haemorrhagic stroke.
* $52\%$ reduction in intracranial haemorrhage.
* $10\%$ reduction in all-cause mortality, with similar rates of major bleeding compared to well-managed warfarin.

*Valvular AF Exception:* VKAs (target INR 2.0–3.0 or higher depending on valve prosthesis position) remain the mandatory standard of care in patients with mechanical prosthetic heart valves or moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis. In all other patients, DOACs are recommended in preference to VKAs (Class I, Level A).

### 6.4 Bleeding Risk Assessment and Clinical Management
Evaluation of bleeding risk must be performed in all patients initiating or receiving OAC. The **HAS-BLED score** (Hypertension, Abnormal renal/liver function, Stroke, Bleeding history, Labile INR, Elderly [$> 65$], Drugs/alcohol) remains the validated clinical tool to systematically identify and correct modifiable bleeding risk factors:
* **Modifiable Factors:** Uncontrolled hypertension ($SBP \ge 160\text{ mmHg}$), labile INR, co-prescription of NSAIDs or antiplatelet agents, heavy alcohol abuse, and anemia.
* **Clinical Principle:** A high HAS-BLED score ($\ge 3$) indicates the need for aggressive mitigation of modifiable risks and closer follow-up (e.g. every 1–3 months), but must **never** be used to withhold or discontinue OAC, as patients at highest bleeding risk derive the greatest absolute reduction in ischaemic stroke and mortality from anticoagulation.

#### Management of Active Bleeding Under OAC
Management depends on bleeding severity:
* **Minor Bleeding:** Delay or omit one dose of DOAC; apply local haemostatic measures.
* **Moderate-to-Severe Bleeding:** Discontinue OAC; perform mechanical compression, endoscopic, or surgical haemostasis; administer fluid resuscitation and blood products.
* **Life-Threatening / Critical Organ Bleeding:**
  * For Dabigatran: Administer specific reversal agent **Idarucizumab** ($5\text{ g}$ IV in two $2.5\text{ g}$ boluses) (Class I, Level B).
  * For Factor Xa Inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban): Administer specific antidote **Andexanet alfa** (or 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate [4F-PCC] $25\text{--}50\text{ IU/kg}$) (Class IIa, Level B).
  * For VKAs: Administer 4F-PCC ($25\text{--}50\text{ IU/kg}$) and intravenous Vitamin K ($5\text{--}10\text{ mg}$) (Class I, Level A).

Cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) with atrial leads detect asymptomatic subclinical atrial tachyarrhythmias (AHRE, typically rate $> 175\text{--}220\text{ b.p.m.}$). 1. In AHRE lasting $< 24\text{ hours}$, the absolute stroke rate is low ($\approx 1\%/\text{year}$), and DOAC therapy produces an excess in major bleeding without a statistically significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality.

## 7. [R] Reduce Symptoms by Rate and Rhythm Control</summary><div class="details-content">

### 7.1 Management of Heart Rate in Patients with AF

#### 7.1.1 Indications and Target Heart Rate
Heart rate control is an essential component of AF management, indicated in virtually all patients to alleviate palpitations, fatigue, and dyspnoea, optimize ventricular filling dynamics, and prevent tachycardiomyopathy (tachycardia-induced heart failure).
* **Initial Lenient Target:** Guided by the randomized RACE II trial, an initial lenient resting ventricular rate target of $< 100\text{--}110\text{ b.p.m.}$ is recommended (Class IIa, Level B). Lenient rate control avoids unnecessary iatrogenic bradycardia, syncope, and excessive polypharmacy, and is clinically non-inferior to strict control in terms of major cardiovascular events.
* **Strict Target Criteria:** A stricter resting heart rate target ($< 80\text{ b.p.m.}$ at rest and $< 110\text{ b.p.m.}$ during moderate exertion) should be pursued only if the patient remains symptomatic on lenient control, or if progressive left ventricular systolic dysfunction / tachycardiomyopathy is documented.

#### 7.1.2 Acute and Long-Term Pharmacological Rate Control
The choice of rate-controlling agent is governed by haemodynamic stability, presence of heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, LVEF $\le 40\%$), and baseline comorbidities:
* **Beta-Blockers:** First-line agents for rate control in the majority of patients, particularly in the presence of coronary artery disease, prior myocardial infarction, or HFrEF (using evidence-based agents: bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate, carvedilol, or nebivolol).
* **Non-Dihydropyridine Calcium Channel Blockers (Diltiazem, Verapamil):** Highly effective first-line agents in patients with preserved left ventricular systolic function (LVEF $> 40\%$), especially in patients with severe reactive airway disease (asthma) where beta-blockers are contraindicated. *Strict Contraindication:* Diltiazem and verapamil must **never** be administered to patients with HFrEF (LVEF $\le 40\%$) due to their negative inotropic effects triggering acute haemodynamic collapse.
* **Digoxin:** Acts via central vagal activation to slow AV nodal conduction. Digoxin is particularly effective at controlling resting (but not exertional) heart rate. It is recommended as second-line or add-on therapy in heart failure or sedentary elderly patients. Strict therapeutic monitoring is mandatory to maintain serum digoxin trough levels between $0.5\text{--}0.9\text{ ng/mL}$ to avert toxicity and excess mortality.
* **Intravenous Amiodarone:** Reserved as an acute rate-controlling agent solely in haemodynamically unstable or critically ill patients with severe systolic dysfunction when other therapies are ineffective, contraindicated, or contraindicated by severe hypotension.

#### 7.1.3 Atrioventricular Node Ablation and Pacing ("Ablate and Pace")
Catheter ablation of the AV node coupled with permanent pacemaker implantation completely uncouples the ventricles from the fibrillating atria, providing definitive, highly regularized rate control. It is recommended in patients refractory or intolerant to intensive rate- and rhythm-control pharmacotherapy or catheter ablation.
* **Pacing Modality:** Because mandatory lifelong right ventricular (RV) apical pacing induces dyssynchrony, heart failure, and adverse remodelling in up to $20\%$ of patients, **biventricular pacing (CRT)** or **conduction system pacing (CSP)**—specifically His-bundle pacing (HBP) or left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP)—is recommended (Class I, Level B) in patients with LVEF $\le 40\%$, and should be considered in all patients with expected high ventricular pacing burdens.

### 7.2 Rhythm Control Strategies in Patients with AF

#### 7.2.1 General Principles and Anticoagulation Around Cardioversion
Rhythm control aims to restore and maintain sinus rhythm to eliminate symptoms, improve exercise capacity, enhance quality of life, and retard electrical and mechanical atrial remodelling. The randomized **EAST-AFNET 4** trial established that early rhythm control (initiated within 1 year of AF diagnosis) significantly reduces cardiovascular death, stroke, and HF hospitalizations compared to usual care.
* **Anticoagulation Rules Around Cardioversion:**
  * In AF episodes lasting $\ge 48\text{ hours}$ (or of unknown duration), therapeutic oral anticoagulation for at least 3 consecutive weeks prior to electrical or pharmacological cardioversion is mandatory (Class I, Level B).
  * Alternatively, transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) may be performed immediately to rule out left atrial / LAA thrombus, followed by immediate cardioversion, provided that therapeutic anticoagulation has been initiated beforehand (Class I, Level B).
  * After successful or unsuccessful cardioversion, therapeutic oral anticoagulation must be continued for at least 4 weeks in all patients (Class I, Level B). Beyond 4 weeks, long-term OAC is maintained indefinitely based on the patient's CHA2DS2-VA score (Class I, Level A).
* **The "Wait-and-See" Approach for Acute AF:**
  In haemodynamically stable patients presenting with acute-onset AF $< 48\text{ hours}$ in duration, the landmark RACE 7 ACWAS trial demonstrated that initial rate control with outpatient observation and delayed cardioversion within 48 hours is non-inferior to immediate cardioversion, as up to $70\%$ will spontaneously convert to sinus rhythm without requiring electrical shock.

![Figure 12: Approaches for cardioversion in patients with AF](images/fig12_cardioversion_approaches.jpg)

#### 7.2.2 Electrical Cardioversion
Direct current cardioversion (DCCV) using synchronized biphasic waveforms is the most reliable method for restoring sinus rhythm, with acute success rates $> 90\%$:
* **Pad Configuration:** An anteroposterior pad configuration (anterior electrode over the sternum, posterior electrode interscapularly) is preferred as it delivers lower transthoracic impedance and higher shock efficacy compared to anterolateral placement.
* **Pre-Treatment:** Pre-treatment with antiarrhythmic drugs (e.g. amiodarone, flecainide, ibutilide) should be considered prior to elective DCCV to decrease defibrillation threshold and prevent immediate recurrence of AF.

#### 7.2.3 Pharmacological Cardioversion
Pharmacological cardioversion restores sinus rhythm without general anaesthesia, using intravenous or oral antiarrhythmic agents:
* **Patients WITHOUT Structural Heart Disease:** Intravenous vernakalant, flecainide, or propafenone are recommended (Class I, Level A). Vernakalant acts rapidly (median conversion time 10–14 minutes). Flecainide and propafenone convert within 3–6 hours.
* **Patients WITH Structural Heart Disease / Heart Failure:** Intravenous amiodarone is recommended (Class I, Level A). Amiodarone exhibits delayed conversion kinetics (typically 8–24 hours).
* **"Pill-in-the-Pocket" Strategy:** Outpatient self-administration of a single oral dose of flecainide ($200\text{--}300\text{ mg}$) or propafenone ($450\text{--}600\text{ mg}$) is recommended in selected patients with infrequent, symptomatic paroxysmal AF without structural heart disease, provided that its safety and efficacy have previously been confirmed in a monitored hospital setting (Class IIa, Level B). An AV-nodal blocking agent (beta-blocker or CCB) must be co-administered to prevent 1:1 conducting atrial flutter.

#### 7.2.4 Catheter Ablation of Atrial Fibrillation
Catheter pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) represents the cornerstone of interventional rhythm control. PVI creates durable, transmural circumferential lesions around the antrum of all four pulmonary veins, electrically isolating the ectopic triggers responsible for initiating AF:
* **First-Line Indication in Paroxysmal AF:** Based on multiple randomized trials (EARLY-AF, STOP-AF, PROGRESSIVE-AF) demonstrating significantly lower arrhythmia recurrence, reduced progression to persistent AF, and superior quality of life compared to AADs, **catheter ablation is recommended as first-line therapy (Class I, Level A)** in patients with symptomatic paroxysmal AF.
* **Indication in Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF):** In patients with AF and HFrEF, randomized trials (CASTLE-AF, CASTLE-HTx, AATAC) proved that catheter ablation achieves dramatic reverse left ventricular remodelling (average LVEF increase $> 8\text{--}10\%$), substantial reductions in HF hospitalizations, and significant improvements in all-cause mortality. Catheter ablation is **recommended as Class I (Level B)** in this population.
* **Energy Modalities:** PVI may be performed using point-by-point radiofrequency (RF) with contact-force sensing, cryoballoon ablation, or non-thermal **pulsed field ablation (PFA)** (irreversible electroporation), which offers tissue selectivity with ultra-rapid lesion creation and minimal risk of collateral damage to the oesophagus or phrenic nerve.
* **Periprocedural Anticoagulation:** Catheter ablation must be performed under **uninterrupted oral anticoagulation** (uninterrupted DOAC or VKA with INR 2.0–2.5) to minimize both thromboembolic and bleeding risks (Class I, Level A). Following ablation, OAC must be continued for at least 2 months in all patients, and continued indefinitely according to baseline CHA2DS2-VA score, irrespective of apparent rhythm success.

#### 7.2.5 Concomitant Surgical and Hybrid AF Ablation
* **Concomitant Surgical Ablation:** In patients with symptomatic or asymptomatic AF undergoing open or minimally invasive cardiac surgery (CABG, valve replacement/repair), concomitant surgical ablation (bipolar RF or cryothermal Cox-Maze procedure) is **recommended (Class I, Level A)** to restore sinus rhythm and improve long-term outcomes.
* **Hybrid / Thoracoscopic Epicardial-Endocardial Ablation:** In patients with long-standing persistent AF or failed prior transcatheter ablation, a staged or simultaneous hybrid approach combining minimally invasive thoracoscopic epicardial ablation with transvenous endocardial mapping and touch-up should be considered (Class IIa, Level B).

## 8. [E] Evaluation and Dynamic Reassessment</summary><div class="details-content">

### 8.1 Implementation of Dynamic Care
Atrial fibrillation is a chronic, progressive, systemic condition with dynamic thromboembolic and bleeding risk profiles. Aging, progression of subclinical vascular disease, newly acquired comorbidities (e.g. incident hypertension, diabetes, or worsening chronic kidney disease), and changes in medication adherence continually alter patient prognosis.

The 2024 ESC Guidelines mandate **structured clinical reassessment** at 6 months after starting or modifying treatment, and at least annually thereafter. This dynamic review encompasses:
1. Re-calculation of the CHA2DS2-VA score and verification of OAC indication and dosing.
2. Re-evaluation of the HAS-BLED score to eliminate newly emerging modifiable bleeding hazards.
3. Review of renal function (eGFR) and hepatic function to ensure correct DOAC dosing.
4. Objective appraisal of resting and exertional heart rate control and symptom burden (mEHRA score).
5. Audit of lifestyle interventions (blood pressure, weight, physical activity, alcohol).
6. Medication adherence monitoring and management of adverse drug reactions.

## 9. The AF-CARE Pathway in Specific Clinical Settings</summary><div class="details-content">

### 9.1 AF-CARE in Unstable Patients
Haemodynamic instability occurring in the setting of rapid AF (severe hypotension, cardiogenic shock, acute pulmonary edema, ongoing myocardial ischaemia, or acute syncope) is a medical emergency. Emergency electrical cardioversion (synchronized biphasic DCCV) is recommended immediately (Class I, Level B) to restore sinus rhythm. Concurrently, therapeutic anticoagulation (e.g. intravenous unfractionated heparin bolus) should be initiated as soon as possible, but must not delay life-saving electrical shock.

### 9.2 AF-CARE in Acute and Chronic Coronary Syndromes (ACS & CCS) Undergoing PCI
Managing AF in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for ACS or CCS requires carefully balancing ischemic recurrent cardiac events against catastrophic major bleeding hazards:
* **The Dominance of Dual Antithrombotic Therapy (DAT):** Landmark randomized trials (PIONEER AF-PCI, RE-DUAL PCI, AUGUSTUS, ENTRUST-AF PCI) demonstrated that early cessation of aspirin and continuation of a **DOAC plus a single P2Y12 inhibitor (clopidogrel $75\text{ mg}$ once daily)** substantially reduces major bleeding and intracranial haemorrhage by $\approx 50\%$ without a statistically significant increase in stent thrombosis or ischemic events compared to prolonged triple therapy.
* **Duration of Triple Therapy (TAT: DOAC + Aspirin + Clopidogrel):** Routine triple therapy should be restricted to the peri-procedural in-hospital phase only (up to discharge or maximum 1 week). Only in rare patients with very high ischemic risk (e.g. prior stent thrombosis, complex bifurcations, left main PCI) and low bleeding risk may triple therapy be extended up to 1 month.
* **Duration of Dual Therapy (DAT: DOAC + Clopidogrel):**
  * **In ACS undergoing PCI:** Dual therapy is recommended for **up to 12 months** (Class I, Level A), followed by lifelong **DOAC monotherapy** (Class I, Level A).
  * **In CCS undergoing PCI:** Dual therapy is recommended for **6 months** (or 1–3 months if bleeding risk is high) (Class I, Level A), followed by lifelong **DOAC monotherapy** (Class I, Level A).
* **Choice of P2Y12 Inhibitor:** Clopidogrel ($75\text{ mg}$ od) is the P2Y12 inhibitor of choice. Potent P2Y12 inhibitors (**prasugrel and ticagrelor**) are **not recommended** as part of triple therapy (Class III, Level B) due to unacceptably high major bleeding rates.

### 9.4 AF-CARE in Acute Stroke or Intracranial Haemorrhage

#### 9.4.1 Acute Ischaemic Stroke
* **Timing of OAC Initiation / Resumption:** Immediate full anticoagulation during the hyperacute phase of ischaemic stroke is contraindicated due to the risk of symptomatic hemorrhagic transformation. Based on recent randomized trials (TIMING, ELAN), anticoagulation should be initiated based on stroke severity:
  * Minor stroke (NIHSS $< 8$) or TIA: Initiate OAC on **day 3–4** post-stroke.
  * Moderate stroke (NIHSS 8–15): Initiate OAC on **day 6–7** post-stroke.
  * Severe stroke (NIHSS $\ge 16$ or large infarct): Perform follow-up CT/MRI to rule out hemorrhagic transformation, then initiate OAC on **day 12–14** post-stroke.
* **Secondary Prevention:** Long-term DOAC therapy is recommended in preference to VKA to reduce the risk of recurrent ischaemic stroke and hemorrhagic transformation (Class I, Level A).

#### 9.4.2 Intracranial Haemorrhage (ICH)
In patients with AF who survive an acute spontaneous or traumatic ICH, oral anticoagulation must be stopped immediately during the acute bleeding phase. Following complete hematoma stabilization and resolution, a multidisciplinary team (stroke neurologist, neurosurgeon, cardiologist) should review the case:
* If the bleeding source was reversible (e.g. trauma, severe hypertensive crisis now controlled, aneurysm surgically clipped) and the patient has an elevated stroke risk, resumption of DOAC therapy should be considered after **4–8 weeks**.
* In patients with non-modifiable cerebral amyloid angiopathy or recurrent lobar ICH, long-term OAC is contraindicated; **percutaneous LAA occlusion (LAAO)** should be considered as a non-pharmacological alternative (Class IIb, Level B).

| **Endocrine & Metabolic** | Thyrotoxicosis / hyperthyroidism, phaeochromocytoma, diabetic ketoacidosis, severe hypokalaemia / hypomagnesaemia. |
| **Exogenous Toxins & Drugs** | Binge alcohol consumption (\"holiday heart syndrome\"), cocaine, amphetamines, high-dose intravenous inotropes. |

*Management Principles:* The acute trigger must be identified and corrected immediately. Rate control with beta-blockers is the first-line symptomatic intervention. Although triggered AF often resolves upon recovery, extensive registry evidence demonstrates that over $50\%$ of patients develop recurrent AF within 1–2 years, and long-term stroke risk matches that of non-triggered AF.
* **Anticoagulation:** Long-term oral anticoagulation should be considered in patients with trigger-induced AF if the baseline CHA2DS2-VA score is $\ge 2$ (Class IIa, Level C).

|---|---|---|

### 9.6 AF-CARE in Post-Operative Patients
Post-operative AF (POAF) is the most frequent complication of cardiac surgery (occurring in $30\text{--}50\%$ of patients, peaking on post-operative days 2–4) and non-cardiac thoracic surgery ($10\text{--}20\%$). POAF is driven by surgical trauma, pericardial inflammation, elevated adrenergic tone, fluid shifts, and autonomic imbalance:
* **Prevention:** Peri-operative continuation or pre-operative initiation of oral beta-blockers is recommended in patients undergoing cardiac surgery (Class I, Level A).
* **Treatment:** Rate control with beta-blockers or amiodarone is standard. In hemodynamically unstable patients, prompt electrical cardioversion is indicated.
* **Anticoagulation:** Because POAF is associated with a 3- to 4-fold increase in long-term stroke and recurrent AF, therapeutic oral anticoagulation is recommended for at least 4 weeks post-cardiac surgery in patients with POAF and elevated stroke risk, with continuation based on CHA2DS2-VA score (Class IIa, Level B).

### Recommendation Table 28 — Recommendations for Management of Post-Operative AF
| Recommendations | Class | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Peri-operative beta-blocker therapy is recommended in patients undergoing cardiac surgery to reduce the incidence of post-operative AF. | **I** | **A** |
| Oral anticoagulation should be considered in patients with post-operative AF following cardiac surgery who have an elevated stroke risk, weighing the risk of post-operative bleeding. | **IIa** | **B** |

### 9.7 Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source (ESUS)
Patients presenting with cryptogenic stroke fulfilling ESUS criteria should not receive empiric oral anticoagulation without documented AF (NAVIGATE ESUS and RE-SPECT ESUS trials demonstrated excess bleeding without stroke reduction for rivaroxaban and dabigatran vs aspirin). Prolonged continuous cardiac monitoring (using wearable patches for up to 30 days or insertable cardiac monitors [ICMs] for up to 3 years) is recommended to identify subclinical AF and guide targeted OAC initiation (Class I, Level B).

### 9.8 AF-CARE During Pregnancy
AF during pregnancy is rare but poses severe maternal and fetal risks, especially in women with underlying congenital or valvular heart disease:
* **Rate Control:** Selective $\beta_1$-blockers (metoprolol, bisoprolol) are the preferred first-line agents. Digoxin is safe. Verapamil should be used with caution. Diltiazem and atenolol are teratogenic/fetotoxic and contraindicated.
* **Cardioversion:** Synchronized electrical cardioversion is safe and effective in all trimesters when haemodynamic compromise or rapid ventricular rates threaten maternal-fetal stability.
* **Anticoagulation:** **DOACs are strictly contraindicated during pregnancy and breastfeeding** due to teratogenicity and placental transfer (Class III, Level C). Therapeutic subcutaneous Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (LMWH) with anti-Xa monitoring is the preferred anticoagulant during the first trimester and the final month of gestation. Adjusted-dose VKA (with target INR 2.0–3.0) may be considered during the second trimester if maternal stroke risk is exceptionally high.

### Recommendation Table 29 — Recommendations for AF in Pregnancy
| Recommendations | Class | Level |
|---|---|---|

| Therapeutic anticoagulation with low-molecular-weight heparin is recommended during pregnancy in women with AF at elevated thromboembolic risk. | **I** | **C** |
| Direct oral anticoagulants are not recommended during pregnancy and breastfeeding due to potential fetal toxicity and maternal-fetal bleeding. | **III** | **C** |

### 9.9 AF-CARE in Adult Congenital Heart Disease (ACHD)
Supraventricular tachyarrhythmias (AF and intra-atrial re-entrant tachycardia [IART]) are the leading cause of morbidity and hospitalization in adults with congenital heart disease (ACHD). In patients with severe complexity (e.g. Fontan palliation, cyanotic lesions, transposition of the great arteries with systemic right ventricle), oral anticoagulation should be considered even in the presence of a low conventional CHA2DS2-VA score (Class IIa, Level C). Catheter ablation in specialized ACHD electrophysiology centers is recommended.

### 10.2 Screening Strategies: Opportunistic vs Systematic
* **Opportunistic Screening:** Routine pulse palpation or single-lead ECG recording during routine healthcare encounters is recommended in all individuals aged $\ge 65$ years (Class I, Level B).
* **Systematic Screening:** Targeted systematic screening programmes using prolonged or intermittent ECG monitoring should be considered in high-risk individuals aged $\ge 75$ years or those with elevated cardiovascular risk profiles (Class IIa, Level B) (e.g. STROKESTOP trial).
* **Diagnostic Gold Standard:** Wearable devices and smartwatches provide screening notifications only. **Confirmation by a physician reviewing a standard 12-lead ECG or single-lead ECG tracing $\ge 30\text{ seconds}$ is mandatory** before establishing a formal clinical diagnosis and initiating lifelong anticoagulation (Class I, Level B).

## 11. Key Messages</summary><div class="details-content">

1. **The AF-CARE Pathway:** Comprehensive AF care requires integrated, patient-centred implementation across four continuous pillars: [C] Comorbidity management, [A] Avoid stroke, [R] Reduce symptoms, and [E] Evaluation.
2. **Simplified Stroke Risk (CHA2DS2-VA):** Female sex has been removed from the risk calculation. Patients with a CHA2DS2-VA score $\ge 2$ should receive OAC (Class I), and those with score = 1 should be considered for OAC (Class IIa).
3. **DOAC Preference:** DOACs are recommended over VKAs for all eligible AF patients, reducing fatal bleeds and intracranial haemorrhage.
4. **Subclinical AF (AHRE):** Routine OAC is only indicated when device-detected AHRE duration is $\ge 24\text{ hours}$ in patients with a CHA2DS2-VA score $\ge 2$.
5. **Bleeding Risk (HAS-BLED):** Use HAS-BLED exclusively to address modifiable bleeding risks; never withhold OAC based on a high bleeding score.
6. **Aggressive Comorbidity Targets:** Blood pressure $< 130/80\text{ mmHg}$, SGLT2 inhibitors for all HF phenotypes, $\ge 10\%$ weight loss in obesity, and alcohol $\le 3$ drinks/week.
7. **First-Line Catheter Ablation:** Pulmonary vein isolation is a first-line Class I therapy in symptomatic paroxysmal AF and in HFrEF.
8. **Surgical LAA Exclusion:** Class I recommendation during concomitant cardiac surgery based on the LAAOS III trial.
9. **Dynamic Follow-Up:** Mandatory structured reassessment at 6 months and at least annually thereafter.

## 12. Gaps in Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

1. **Optimal Management of Short AHRE ($< 24\text{ hours}$):** Further trials are needed to identify biomarkers or left atrial mechanical imaging parameters that identify which patients with short AHRE derive a net benefit from OAC.
2. **Role of Percutaneous LAAO vs DOAC:** Large ongoing randomized trials (CHAMPION-AF, CATALYST) are needed to determine if percutaneous LAAO is non-inferior to DOACs in non-contraindicated patients.
3. **Pulsed Field Ablation Long-Term Efficacy:** Long-term durability and five-year freedom from arrhythmia following pulsed field ablation compared to radiofrequency and cryoballoon ablation.
4. **Substrate Ablation in Persistent AF:** Identifying definitive, reproducible lesion sets beyond PVI in advanced persistent and long-standing persistent AF.
5. **AI and Wearable-Guided Intermittent Anticoagulation:** Whether \"pill-in-the-pocket\" OAC guided by continuous smartwatch surveillance is safe compared to continuous lifelong OAC in low-burden paroxysmal AF.
6. **Optimal Timing of OAC Post-ICH:** Prospective randomized trials are needed to establish the exact safety window and patient selection for DOAC resumption following spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage.
