## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Document Nature and Core Scope
* **Document Status:** This document is an **Expert Consensus Document (ECD)** developed by 15 cardiac surgeon members of the American Association for Thoracic Surgery (AATS) using a rigorous modified Delphi consensus process (requiring a 75% consensus rate with at least 80% voting participation). It provides targeted, expert-agreed recommendations for acute surgical triage and operative management, complementing broader clinical practice guidelines.
* **Definition of ATAAD:** Acute aortic dissection involving the ascending aorta (proximal to the brachiocephalic artery) diagnosed within 14 days of symptom onset, regardless of the site of the primary intimal entry tear.
* **Mortality and Urgency:** Nonoperative management carries a 30-day mortality approaching 60% (~ 1% to 2% per hour initially). Prompt open surgical repair remains the undisputed standard of care.

### Initial Medical and Anti-Impulse Therapy
* **Anti-Impulse Targets:** Immediate blood pressure and heart rate reduction to minimize aortic wall shear stress ($dP/dt$) and retard propagation of the false lumen. Target systolic blood pressure (SBP): 100–120 mmHg; target heart rate: < 60 bpm.
* **First-Line Agents:** Intravenous $\beta$-blockers (esmolol, labetalol, metoprolol, propranolol). In patients with severe AR, use with caution.
* **Second-Line Agents & Alternatives:** Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem) for $\beta$-blocker contraindications. Vasodilators (e.g., intravenous nicardipine, sodium nitroprusside) should only be administered *after* adequate heart rate control is established to avoid reflex tachycardia.
* **Hypotension & Tamponade:** In hypotension, volume resuscitation targeting a permissive SBP of ~ 90 mmHg is reasonable. Routine pericardiocentesis for hemopericardium is contraindicated as it may provoke recurrent catastrophic bleeding; it is reserved exclusively for imminent cardiac arrest.
* **Analgesia:** Rapid intravenous opioid titration to blunt sympathetic catecholamine surges.

### Interhospital Transfer and Comprehensive Aortic Centers
* **Regionalization Benefit:** High-volume Comprehensive Aortic Centers (CACs, > 10–13 ATAAD repairs/year) demonstrate significantly reduced operative mortality (< 10% vs 20–34% in low-volume centers).
* **Transfer Indications:** Transfer to a CAC is recommended when on-site cardiac surgery is absent or unavailable, and is reasonable for complicated ATAAD (distal malperfusion, previous cardiac surgery, extensive comorbidities) in hemodynamically stable patients.

### Surgical Triage and Special Clinical Scenarios
* **Emergency Surgery:** Standard for virtually all ATAAD patients.
* **Acute Type A Intramural Hematoma (IMH):** Immediate surgery is recommended if high-risk features are present: total aortic diameter > 50 mm, hematoma thickness > 11 mm, pericardial effusion, significant AR, or ulcer-like projections (ULP). In high-comorbidity patients lacking these features, close expectant surveillance is an option.
* **Brain Malperfusion / Stroke / Coma:** Immediate surgical repair can restore cerebral perfusion, reduce mortality to rates comparable to patients without stroke, and achieve complete neurologic recovery in up to 50% of cases, including comatose patients.
* **Catheter-Induced / Iatrogenic ATAAD:** If localized strictly to the aortic root around the coronary ostium without distal progression or myocardial ischemia, conservative expectant medical management is reasonable; extension beyond the root warrants immediate surgical repair.
* **Retrograde ATAAD:** In carefully selected patients where the primary intimal tear is distal to the left subclavian artery with retrograde propagation into the ascending aorta, descending TEVAR alone may be reasonable.

### Cannulation and Perfusion Strategies
* **Arterial Cannulation:** Right axillary artery cannulation is preferred for stable patients, providing antegrade systemic flow and facilitating selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP). Direct ascending aortic cannulation via the Seldinger technique under transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or epiaortic ultrasound guidance is an effective central alternative. Femoral cannulation is reserved for rapid bypass initiation in hemodynamically collapsed or unstable patients.
* **Brain Protection:** Either ACP (unilateral or bilateral) or retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP) is superior to hypothermic circulatory arrest alone. Moderate hypothermia with ACP is safe and comparable in neuroprotection to deep hypothermia.

### Aortic Root and Valve Management
* **Root Preservation:** Sinus segment preservation with aortic valve resuspension (transmural horizontal mattress pledgeted sutures at the commissural tips) and false lumen obliteration (neomedia construction with Teflon felt or bovine pericardium) is effective in the majority of patients.
* **Root Replacement Indications:** Severe root dilatation / aneurysm (> 45 mm), extensive dissection involving all 3 sinuses, primary intimal tear in the root, or underlying hereditary thoracic aortic disorder (HTAD / Marfan syndrome).
* **Valve-Sparing Root Replacement (VSARR / David):** Reasonable in young, physiologically fit patients in high-volume expert centers with durable cusps and absence of severe leaflet fenestration/calcification.
* **Coronary Malperfusion:** Immediate surgical restoration of coronary perfusion via direct repair, neomedia reinforcement, or emergent coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) before leaving the operating room.

### Ascending Aorta and Distal Anastomosis
* **Open Distal Anastomosis:** Mandatory under circulatory arrest to allow complete inspection of the arch, rule out secondary entry tears, and excise the entire ascending aorta and primary entry tear. Clamped distal anastomosis carries worse downstream aortic remodeling and higher late reoperation rates.
* **Hemiarch Reconstruction:** Preferred whenever the primary tear extends into the lesser curvature of the aortic arch.
* **Ascending TEVAR:** Currently investigational; off-label for inoperable candidates in specialized feasibility studies.

### Aortic Arch and Frozen Elephant Trunk
* **Extended Arch Replacement:** Indicated for primary entry tears in the arch or proximal descending aorta, cerebral or arch-vessel branch malperfusion, arch aneurysms (> 5.5 cm), or arch rupture.
* **Frozen Elephant Trunk (FET):** Promotes true lumen expansion, covers distal entry tears, induces false lumen thrombosis in up to 85% of treated thoracic segments, and provides a stable landing zone for subsequent interventions.
* **Spinal Cord Protection:** Limiting FET coverage to at or above the T6 vertebra (or stent graft length ≤ 10 cm) significantly reduces the risk of spinal cord ischemia (SCI).

### Malperfusion Management
* **Malperfusion Syndrome:** Affects 15% to 40% of ATAAD cases (cerebral, mesenteric, renal, limb).
* **Mesenteric Malperfusion Paradigm:** Operative mortality with immediate open proximal repair in severe mesenteric malperfusion exceeds 40%–100%. In hemodynamically stable patients with visceral malperfusion and end-organ dysfunction, an initial percutaneous or endovascular reperfusion strategy (descending TEVAR, true lumen aortic fenestration, branch stenting) followed by delayed elective open proximal aortic repair reduces operative mortality.
* **Limb Ischemia:** In dynamic obstruction, central true lumen restoration often re-establishes limb flow; in static obstruction or profound ischemia with motor/sensory deficit, immediate peripheral revascularization before or concomitant with central repair prevents irreversible ischemia-reperfusion injury.

### Postoperative Medical Therapy and Surveillance
* **Pharmacotherapy:** Lifelong $\beta$-blocker therapy is essential to reduce late mortality, reoperation, and aortic expansion.
* **Fluoroquinolone Black-Box Contraindication:** Systemic fluoroquinolones are strictly contraindicated in aortic dissection and aneurysm patients due to collagen degradation and increased risk of rupture.
* **Imaging Surveillance:** Multiphase contrast CT or MRI predischarge, at 6 months, 12 months, and annually thereafter.
* **Genetics and Family Screening:** Transthoracic echocardiographic screening of all first-degree relatives every 5–10 years. Formal genetic assessment and testing for patients < 50 years, syndromic features, or positive family history.

## Definitions and Scope</summary><div class="details-content">

1. **Aortic Dissection (AD):** Disruption of the tunica intima that results in blood flow into the tunica media, propagating along the length of the aorta and into branch vessels. AD belongs to the spectrum of acute aortic syndromes alongside penetrating aortic ulcer (PAU) and intramural hematoma (IMH). These syndromes represent life-threatening, inter-related pathological entities with shared clinical features.
2. **Type A Aortic Dissection:** Involvement of the ascending aorta (proximal to the brachiocephalic artery), regardless of the primary entry tear location according to the Stanford classification, corresponding to DeBakey type I or type II depending on distal extent. Newer classification systems characterize AD by entry tear site, false lumen extent, and malperfusion syndromes. Conflicting definitions exist; for instance, retrograde extension of a descending dissection into the ascending aorta is classified as type B by STS/SVS reporting standards, but as type A by EACTS/ESC definitions.
3. **Acute Type A Aortic Dissection (ATAAD):** Defined as dissection diagnosed within 14 days of symptom onset, standard across all international classifications. For the purposes of this document, any dissection involving the ascending aorta diagnosed within 14 days is considered ATAAD regardless of primary entry tear location. Subacute and chronic dissections are outside the scope of this document.
4. **Scope of Document:** Focused on the acute surgical triage and operative management of ATAAD. Detailed evaluation, pre-hospital algorithms, and non-surgical diagnostic pathways were not evaluated exhaustively.
5. **Perioperative Spectrum:** Concise perioperative medical optimization, systemic cannulation, brain protection, and long-term postoperative surveillance protocols are included.
6. **Endorsement of Prior Guidelines:** The writing committee acknowledges and endorses established evidence-based recommendations, including the 2010 ACCF/AHA/AATS thoracic aortic disease guidelines, the 2011 Japanese Circulation Society guidelines, the 2014 European Society of Cardiology guidelines, and the 2016 Canadian Cardiovascular Society/Canadian Society of Cardiac Surgeons/CSVS joint position statement.
7. **Patient-Centered Decision Making:** Patient values, physiological status, and anatomical factors must be integrated with evidence-based surgical judgment.
8. **Dynamic Nature:** When new high-level clinical trial or registry data become available, these consensus statements are subject to periodic revision.

## Methodology</summary><div class="details-content">

The writing panel adhered to the recommendations of the AATS/STS position statement on developing clinical practice documents. An expert panel consisting of 15 cardiac surgeons, all active members of the AATS, utilized an Expert Consensus Document (ECD) pathway to formulate consensus statements graded by Class of Recommendation (COR) and Level of Evidence (LOE).

### Consensus Process and Delphi Method
Literature searches were conducted through Ovid MEDLINE for all articles published since 2000, supplemented by seminal landmark historical publications. Section-specific evidence was synthesized under the guidance of an epidemiologist/methodologist. Recommendations were submitted for formal voting among writing committee members using the Delphi method. Approval required a minimum **75% consensus threshold** with at least **80% voting participation**. Controversies were debated during structured online video conferences with iterative re-voting. Following committee finalization, external review was conducted by a dedicated panel of 10 international peer reviewers before formal endorsement by the AATS Clinical Practice Standards Committee and the AATS Board of Directors.

### Classes of Recommendation and Levels of Evidence
* **Class I (Strong):** Benefit >>> Risk. The procedure or treatment should be performed or administered.
* **Class IIa (Moderate):** Benefit >> Risk. It is reasonable to perform the procedure or administer the treatment.
* **Class IIb (Weak):** Benefit $\ge$ Risk. The procedure or treatment may be considered.
* **Class III (No Benefit / Harm):** Risk $\ge$ Benefit. Not recommended or potentially harmful.
* **Level of Evidence A:** High-quality data from more than 1 randomized controlled trial (RCT) or meta-analyses of high-quality RCTs.
* **Level of Evidence B:** Moderate-quality data from 1 randomized trial or robust nonrandomized registries and cohort studies.
* **Level of Evidence C:** Consensus opinion of experts, case series, or established standard of care.

## Section 1: Initial Medical Therapy</summary><div class="details-content">

Initial medical therapy ([Table 1](#table-1)) should optimize patient stabilization during interhospital transfer and surgical triage. As soon as a diagnosis of ATAAD is established, immediate control of blood pressure, heart rate, and pain must be initiated. Medical therapy is directed at decreasing excessive shear stress ($dP/dt$) on the dissected aortic layers, thereby arresting false lumen propagation and preventing catastrophic intrapericardial rupture. Additionally, patients can present with acute complications such as severe AR, hypotension, or cardiac tamponade; medical treatment must therefore be tailored to the presenting clinical phenotype. Medical management is never a substitute for emergency surgery, but when deployed rapidly, is essential for initial stabilization.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anti-Impulse Therapy and Target Hemodynamics</summary><div class="details-content">

#### What are appropriate agents for anti-impulse therapy?
In normotensive or hypertensive patients, the goal of initial therapy is focused on decreasing shear stress on the aortic wall, suppressing false lumen propagation, and preventing rupture. Forces that acutely drive dissection propagation include heart rate, systemic blood pressure, and the velocity of left ventricular contraction ($dP/dt$). These parameters are stabilized through early $\beta$-blockade targeting a systolic blood pressure between 100 and 120 mmHg and a heart rate below 60 bpm. By diminishing afterload and reducing myocardial oxygen consumption, anti-impulse therapy also improves coronary perfusion.

Preferred intravenous agents include esmolol, labetalol, metoprolol, or propranolol. Esmolol has a uniquely short half-life (~ 9 minutes), facilitating rapid dose titration and immediate discontinuation if hypotension or heart failure ensues. Labetalol, offering combined $\alpha_1$- and nonselective $\beta$-adrenergic antagonism with partial intrinsic sympathomimetic activity, provides concurrent heart rate and blood pressure reduction, potentially eliminating the need for a secondary vasodilator.

Contraindications to $\beta$-blockers include severe bronchial asthma, decompensated heart failure, or high-degree atrioventricular block. In patients unable to tolerate $\beta$-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil or diltiazem) serve as effective alternatives. Both agents must be used cautiously in the presence of severe acute AR, where excessive bradycardia or reflex tachycardia could precipitate hemodynamic collapse.

#### What are appropriate second-line agents?
When blood pressure remains refractory despite maximal $\beta$-blockade, secondary vasodilators may be added. Crucially, vasodilators must never be initiated before adequate chronotropic control is established, as isolated vasodilation provokes reflex tachycardia and sharp increases in $dP/dt$, accelerating tear propagation. Intravenous nicardipine (a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker) is highly effective, rapidly reducing blood pressure without inducing reflex tachycardia or precipitous drops in diastolic perfusion pressure. Intravenous sodium nitroprusside is also effective, though prolonged infusion carries the risk of cyanide toxicity. Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers are alternatives, but their intravenous titration is less predictable in the hyperacute setting.

### Management of Hypotension and Pain</summary><div class="details-content">

#### How should hypotension in patients with ATAAD be managed?
Hypotension at presentation is a critical clinical sign, associated with significantly increased in-hospital mortality and neurological morbidity. Underlying etiologies include cardiac tamponade, acute severe AR, acute myocardial infarction due to coronary ostial disruption, or overt aortic rupture. Each requires emergency surgical intervention.

The initial management of hypotension consists of cautious intravenous fluid resuscitation to augment preload, stroke volume, and cardiac output while maintaining vital end-organ perfusion. Permissive hypotension targeting a systolic blood pressure of approximately 90 mmHg is reasonable, as aggressive volume loading or excessive vasopressor administration elevates aortic wall tension and promotes false lumen extension. Vasopressors may be used as an adjunct to judicious fluid administration if profound shock persists, but caution is warranted.

Pericardiocentesis in dissection-related hemopericardium remains highly controversial. Aspiration of pericardial blood reduces intrapericardial pressure, which may dislodge a provisional thrombus at the aortic rupture site and induce fatal exsanguination. Routine pericardiocentesis should never delay transfer to the operating theater. However, in patients experiencing pulseless electrical activity or imminent cardiac arrest, controlled aspiration of small volumes (e.g., 20–50 mL) of pericardial fluid may relieve subacute tamponade, restore minimal perfusion, and bridge the patient directly onto cardiopulmonary bypass.

#### How to manage pain in patients with ATAAD?
Patients with ATAAD typically present with excruciating chest or back pain accompanied by intense anxiety. Severe pain triggers massive sympathetic activation, releasing endogenous catecholamines that elevate heart rate and blood pressure, directly neutralizing anti-impulse therapy. Intravenous opioids should be administered promptly upon presentation and titrated rapidly. Analgesia not only augments the efficacy of anti-impulse agents, but also relieves agitation, tachypnea, and respiratory distress.

## Section 2: Interhospital Transfer</summary><div class="details-content">

Current data suggest that patients with ATAAD benefit substantially from transfer to a Comprehensive Aortic Center (CAC) ([Table 2](#table-2)). The definitive criteria defining such centers include case volume and institutional expertise across both elective and emergency complex aortic pathology. Surgery for ATAAD is an uncommon procedure in most community facilities, with a median hospital case volume of only 3 cases per year. Furthermore, only approximately 10% of hospitals perform more than 10 ATAAD repairs annually.

The relationship between institutional case volume and clinical outcomes has consistently demonstrated that high-volume centers achieve superior operative survival compared with low-volume centers. Outcomes are further optimized when patients are treated at centers that maintain high volumes of complex elective aortic surgery. CACs commit dedicated multidisciplinary resources, specialized intensive care, perfusion expertise, and advanced surgical and endovascular infrastructure. Streamlined triage algorithms and superior quality metrics are documented advantages. Consequently, regionalization of care to CACs has strong merit, but must be judiciously balanced against the risk of transport delays and rupture during transfer.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Criteria and Indications for Regional Transfer</summary><div class="details-content">

#### When should patients with ATAAD be transferred to a CAC?
When patients with ATAAD present to hospitals lacking on-site cardiac surgical capabilities or when cardiac surgery is not immediately available, urgent interhospital transfer should be arranged without hesitation. Decision-making is straightforward when the nearest receiving center is a CAC. A substantial body of evidence demonstrates an inverse relationship between hospital/surgeon volume and operative mortality. Large multi-institutional databases report overall operative mortality for ATAAD repair between 17% and 20%, whereas dedicated high-volume aortic centers consistently achieve mortality rates below 10%.

In an analysis of more than 5,000 patients from the National Inpatient Sample database stratified by annual hospital ATAAD volume quartiles (lowest: < 3 cases; low: 3–8; high: 8–13; highest: > 13), operative mortality was significantly lower in high-volume hospitals compared with low-volume facilities (16% vs 27%, $P < .001$). Implementation of specialized thoracic aortic surgery teams—concentrating procedural volume among experienced surgeons, standardizing perioperative management, and establishing dedicated protocols—has been shown to reduce mortality dramatically from 33.9% to 2.8%.

Bypassing a community hospital in favor of a distant CAC requires balancing the known risk of mortality during transit against the survival advantage of specialized care. A study of 16,886 Medicare beneficiaries with ATAAD revealed that 40% required interhospital transfer. Patients transferred to high-volume centers (> 105 total open thoracic aortic cases annually) achieved a 7.2% absolute risk reduction in operative mortality (95% CI, 4.1%–10.3%) compared with those managed at low-volume hospitals, a benefit that persisted during long-term follow-up. Sensitivity analyses indicated that more than 4.4% of patients would have to die during transit before the benefits of transfer to a high-volume center were negated.

#### When should patients with complicated ATAAD be transferred to a CAC?
Regional aortic transfer networks linking community emergency departments with tertiary referral centers have demonstrated excellent safety profiles for stable patients. In an Ohio regional network of 359 patients transferred across 84 hospitals (median distance: 66 km), zero transfer-related deaths were observed. Similarly, a multidisciplinary regional network in Minnesota encompassing 32 hospitals reduced median time to diagnosis by 43% (from 279 to 160 minutes, $P = .014$) and decreased door-to-incision time by 55%, while simultaneously increasing the proportion of patients receiving appropriate anti-impulse therapy.

Patients presenting with hemodynamic collapse secondary to tamponade, acute severe AR, or massive myocardial infarction are often too unstable to survive interhospital transit and require emergency on-site intervention if local cardiac surgery is available. Conversely, hemodynamically stable patients presenting with complex comorbidities, prior cardiac surgery, extensive arch involvement, or distal malperfusion syndromes are prime candidates for escalation to a CAC, where advanced techniques (e.g., valve-sparing root replacement, total arch reconstruction with frozen elephant trunk, or hybrid endovascular revascularization) can be safely executed.

## Section 3: Surgical Triage</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical triage is predicated upon clinical findings and cross-sectional imaging: computed tomographic angiography (CTA), magnetic resonance angiography (MRA), or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). Diagnostic imaging must be scrutinized systematically for:
1. Confirmation of aortic dissection versus intramural hematoma (IMH);
2. Anatomical location of the primary intimal entry tear;
3. Proximal and distal longitudinal and circumferential extent of the false lumen; and
4. Radiological hallmarks of branch-vessel malperfusion or impending/contained aortic rupture.

Clinical findings must be integrated with imaging to identify high-risk complications: hemopericardium, cardiac tamponade, severe AR, organ malperfusion syndromes, or shock.

Emergency surgical repair remains the standard of care for ATAAD based on decades of worldwide data demonstrating that surgery dramatically outperforms medical therapy. Historical and contemporary series confirm a 30-day mortality of ~ 60% with medical therapy alone compared with 15%–25% with prompt surgical intervention. Emergency operation without delay is recommended for the vast majority of patients. Nevertheless, emerging evidence supports nuanced management for specific subgroups, including acute type A IMH, catheter-induced dissection, retrograde dissection, and select presentations of severe visceral malperfusion ([Table 3](#table-3)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Intramural Hematoma, Cerebral Malperfusion, and Special Scenarios</summary><div class="details-content">

#### How should IMH be managed?
Acute type A IMH can occasionally regress or reabsorb spontaneously under medical therapy alone. However, medical management is fraught with a 37% complication rate and a 29% to 46% risk of delayed progression to classic overt dissection or rupture. Meta-analyses have confirmed that overall survival is significantly superior with upfront emergency surgical repair compared with expectant medical management.

Specific morphological risk factors predict failure of medical management and rapid progression:
* Maximum ascending aortic diameter > 50 mm;
* Maximum hematoma wall thickness > 11 mm;
* Presence of pericardial effusion;
* Significant aortic regurgitation;
* Presence of ulcer-like projections (ULP) or focal intimal disruptions on contrast imaging.

In the presence of any of these high-risk features, prompt surgical repair is recommended. In elderly patients or those with severe prohibitive comorbidities who lack high-risk features, expectant medical therapy with anti-impulse management, close intensive care unit monitoring, and serial cross-sectional imaging may be considered.

#### How should ATAAD with brain malperfusion be managed?
According to the International Registry of Acute Aortic Dissection (IRAD), cerebral malperfusion occurs in approximately 15% of patients with ATAAD, presenting across a clinical spectrum from transient focal deficits to dense hemispheric stroke or profound coma. A primary clinical concern has been that systemic heparinization and cardiopulmonary bypass during emergency aortic repair might precipitate catastrophic hemorrhagic transformation of an acute ischemic cerebral infarction.

However, clinical experience indicates that emergency surgical repair can be performed with acceptable safety in selected patients with stroke, reducing operative mortality to levels comparable to patients without preoperative neurological deficits. Crucially, immediate restoration of true lumen cerebral blood flow can reverse neurological deficits, and waking from coma has been repeatedly documented following early surgery. Nonetheless, cerebral malperfusion remains associated with a 37% to 50% incidence of persistent neurological deficit. Complete common or internal carotid artery thrombosis is a high-risk feature associated with cerebral edema and herniation. Carotid reperfusion strategies, including direct carotid artery bypass or arch vessel reconstruction, can salvage ischemic brain tissue. In the absence of established, irreversible cerebral infarction with massive hemispheric edema on pre-repair head CT, emergency proximal aortic repair remains reasonable for most patients presenting with brain malperfusion.

#### How should iatrogenic ATAAD be managed?
Iatrogenic catheter-induced ATAAD occurs as a rare complication during diagnostic coronary angiography or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI). When the dissection is localized strictly to the sinus of Valsalva immediately surrounding the coronary ostium without extension into the tubular ascending aorta or persistent myocardial ischemia, conservative medical therapy with meticulous blood pressure control and serial imaging observation is reasonable. These localized retrograde lesions frequently thrombose and heal spontaneously once the guiding catheter is withdrawn.

Conversely, if the catheter-induced dissection flap propagates into the tubular ascending aorta beyond the sinotubular junction, or if ongoing myocardial ischemia is present, immediate emergency surgical repair is indicated.

#### What is the preferred management of retrograde ATAAD?
De novo retrograde ATAAD occurs when the primary intimal entry tear is located in the descending thoracic aorta (distal to the left subclavian artery) and the false lumen propagates retrograde into the aortic arch and ascending aorta. In carefully selected patients with favorable landing-zone anatomy, isolated thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) covering the descending entry tear has been shown to depressurize the false lumen, achieve retrograde thrombosis of the ascending aorta, and avoid high-risk open sternotomy.

Iatrogenic retrograde ATAAD following TEVAR performed for descending aneurysm or type B dissection is typically caused by stent-graft-induced injury to the lesser curvature of the aortic arch. Risk factors include proximal stent "bird-beaking," underlying connective tissue disorders, and ascending aortic diameter > 40 mm. Post-TEVAR retrograde ATAAD carries high mortality and requires immediate open ascending aortic repair unless patient anatomy uniquely allows for proximal endovascular extension into the arch.

#### Can ATAAD after previous coronary artery bypass grafting be delayed for coronary imaging?
Patients presenting with ATAAD after previous CABG present unique surgical challenges due to patent retrosternal vein grafts, internal thoracic artery conduits, and dense pericardial adhesions. Although historical reports postulated that pericardial adhesions protect against cardiac tamponade and rupture, contemporary registry analyses demonstrate that these patients remain at substantial risk for rupture, AR, and organ malperfusion.

Delaying surgery to perform invasive coronary angiography is generally unnecessary and potentially hazardous. Modern thin-slice contrast-enhanced CTA of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis performed for dissection diagnosis routinely visualizes the patency, course, and origin of prior bypass grafts, providing sufficient anatomical guidance for safe re-entry sternotomy without delaying life-saving surgical repair.

## Section 4: Cannulation and Perfusion Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

Circulatory management and cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) conduct are pivotal determinants of operative success in ATAAD repair ([Table 4](#table-4)). Guiding principles include the establishment of physiologic antegrade systemic perfusion whenever possible, rapid decompression of the false lumen, and swift relief of end-organ malperfusion through true lumen restoration. Organ protection during periods of circulatory arrest and aortic cross-clamping relies upon systemic hypothermia, cold blood cardioplegia, and dedicated cerebral perfusion strategies.

Arterial cannulation affects intra-aortic flow dynamics and dictates how cerebral perfusion is delivered during hemiarch or total arch reconstruction. With advancements in brain protection techniques, perfusion strategies have evolved, allowing warmer systemic hypothermic arrest temperatures when coupled with continuous selective antegrade cerebral perfusion.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Arterial Cannulation Sites and Brain Protection</summary><div class="details-content">

#### What is the preferred cannulation site for ATAAD surgery?
Arterial cannulation can be established via peripheral arteries (right axillary, femoral) or directly through the ascending aorta. Right axillary artery cannulation is the preferred modality in many international centers (> 50% usage in Europe, 36% in North America). Although randomized trials are lacking, large multi-center registries and meta-analyses consistently show that axillary cannulation is associated with a significantly reduced risk of intraoperative stroke and in-hospital mortality compared with femoral cannulation. The right axillary artery delivers physiologic antegrade flow through the true lumen, avoids retrogradely dislodging atheroma or dissection flaps, and provides an immediate conduit for selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP) during circulatory arrest without requiring additional arterial cannulas. Technical limitations include small caliber vessel size, prolonged cutdown time, and potential extension of the dissection into the right subclavian/axillary system, which warrants alternative access.

Direct central ascending aortic cannulation has gained increasing adoption. Utilizing the Seldinger technique under real-time transesophageal echocardiographic (TEE) or epiaortic ultrasound guidance, a needle and wire are placed directly into the true lumen of the dissected ascending aorta, followed by serial dilation and cannula insertion. This technique provides rapid, true antegrade central flow without a peripheral incision. Large cohort studies (e.g., Kreibich et al., Jormalainen et al.) demonstrate that central direct aortic cannulation is safe and yields stroke and mortality rates equivalent to axillary cannulation.

Femoral artery cannulation has historical prominence and remains an indispensable technique for rapid cardiopulmonary bypass initiation in hemodynamically unstable, collapsed, or moribund patients. Its major limitation is retrograde perfusion through the thoracoabdominal aorta, which carries an unpredictable risk of pressurizing the false lumen, dynamic true lumen collapse, visceral malperfusion, and retrograde embolization of thrombus or atheroma into the cerebral circulation. Analyses of the STS Adult Cardiac Surgery Database show higher unadjusted stroke rates with femoral cannulation; however, femoral access remains life-saving when rapid cannulation is required to rescue an unstable patient in extremis.

#### Should adjunctive brain perfusion be used during circulatory arrest in ATAAD repair?
Over the past two decades, neuroprotective adjuncts during hypothermic circulatory arrest have evolved from isolated deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) to routine cerebral perfusion with either retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP) via the superior vena cava or selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP) via the axillary or innominate/carotid arteries.

Large database analyses, including an STS registry analysis of 6,387 patients undergoing ATAAD repair, demonstrate that the addition of either ACP or RCP during circulatory arrest significantly reduces mortality and permanent stroke compared with DHCA alone. In elective arch reconstruction, randomized and propensity-matched studies demonstrate comparable neuroprotection between ACP and RCP. However, for complex arch reconstructions requiring circulatory arrest times exceeding 30–40 minutes, ACP provides sustained metabolic support and is generally preferred.

#### How should systemic temperature be managed during circulatory arrest during ATAAD repair?
When extended circulatory arrest is anticipated, the application of ACP permits milder degrees of hypothermia. Multiple comparative studies and meta-analyses demonstrate that moderate hypothermia (nasopharyngeal temperature 24°C–28°C or bladder temperature 26°C–30°C) with ACP provides neurological protection equivalent to deep hypothermia (temperatures < 20°C–24°C). Moderate hypothermia shortens CPB cooling and rewarming durations, reduces hypothermia-induced coagulopathy, decreases bleeding, and limits transfusion requirements.

Regarding ACP perfusion technique, multiple studies confirm that unilateral ACP (via the right axillary or innominate artery) achieves neurological outcomes comparable to bilateral ACP for circulatory arrest durations up to 40–50 minutes. Bilateral ACP (by cannulating the left common carotid artery) is reasonable when prolonged arch arrest (> 50 minutes) is anticipated, or if cerebral oximetry (near-infrared spectroscopy, NIRS) indicates left hemispheric desaturation during unilateral perfusion.

## Section 5: Aortic Root Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Management of the aortic root in ATAAD is determined by the patient's hemodynamic stability, the extent of root dissection, and the structural integrity of the aortic valve leaflets ([Table 5](#table-5)). Guiding principles are:
1. Restoration of a competent, stable aortic valve;
2. Obliteration of the root false lumen to eliminate the risk of late pseudoaneurysm; and
3. Expeditious relief of coronary ostial malperfusion.

Echocardiographic and direct intraoperative inspection assesses the mechanism of aortic regurgitation (commissural prolapse vs primary leaflet disease), the circumferential involvement of the sinuses of Valsalva, and the condition of the coronary buttons. Surgical strategy must balance the immediate imperative of minimizing early operative mortality against the long-term durability of the repair.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

<span id="fig-2"></span>![](fig2_aortic_root_management.jpg)

#### What is the preferred management of the aortic root and for the aortic valve in ATAAD?
For the majority of patients presenting with ATAAD, a sinus segment-preserving approach with aortic valve resuspension and false lumen obliteration is appropriate ([Fig. 1](#fig-1), [Fig. 2](#fig-2)). Acute AR in ATAAD is most frequently caused by detachment and downward prolapse of one or more valve commissures due to extension of the dissection flap below the sinotubular junction ([Fig. 1](#fig-1)A), rather than intrinsic leaflet pathology.

Valve resuspension is accomplished by placing transmural horizontal mattress sutures supported by Teflon pledgets through the apex of each detached commissure, anchoring them securely back to the external aortic wall ([Fig. 2](#fig-2)). The false lumen within the involved sinus segments (most commonly the non-coronary and right coronary sinuses) is obliterated by constructing a neomedia using Teflon felt, autologous/bovine pericardium, or biologic glue ([Fig. 2](#fig-2)). Surgical adhesives (e.g., gelatin-resorcinol-formalin or glutaraldehyde-based glues) must be used judiciously and sparingly; excessive application has been linked to delayed aortic wall necrosis, redissection, pseudoaneurysm formation, and high-degree atrioventricular heart block from chemical injury to the conduction system.

When aortic valve cusps are normal and root dilatation is absent, valve resuspension combined with supracoronary ascending graft replacement yields excellent early survival and durable mid-term valve competence. If the aortic valve leaflets are structurally degenerated, calcified, or fenestrated, valve replacement is required.

Formal aortic root replacement (ARR) is indicated when:
* There is a pre-existing root aneurysm (> 45 mm);
* The primary intimal entry tear is located within the aortic root or sinuses of Valsalva;
* Dissection involves all 3 sinuses with circumferential destruction of root tissue;
* The patient has Marfan syndrome or another confirmed hereditary thoracic aortic disorder (HTAD).

When root replacement is mandatory, composite valve-graft replacement (Bentall-De Bono procedure using a mechanical or biologic valved conduit) represents the standard gold-standard approach. In specialized centers with extensive experience, valve-sparing aortic root replacement (VSARR, utilizing David reimplantation or Yacoub remodeling techniques) can be performed with low mortality and excellent long-term freedom from reoperation in carefully selected young, stable patients with pliable, non-fenestrated aortic cusps.

#### What is the preferred management of the aortic root in patients with ATAAD and HTAD?
Patients with connective tissue disorders, such as Marfan syndrome, Loeys-Dietz syndrome, or vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, carry a fragile aortic wall substrate. Supracoronary ascending replacement in HTAD patients leaves residual diseased sinus tissue that is prone to progressive dilatation, severe secondary AR, and late root rupture, necessitating secondary root reoperation in over 40% of cases.

Consequently, comprehensive aortic root replacement with either a composite valved graft or valve-sparing reimplantation (David procedure) is recommended in patients with known or suspected HTAD presenting with ATAAD, even if root dilatation is mild.

#### How should bicuspid aortic valve be managed in a setting of ATAAD?
Patients with bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) presenting with ATAAD are typically younger and have larger root diameters than their tricuspid counterparts. However, valve resuspension and limited root repair can be successfully applied in BAV patients if the valve has normal baseline function and root aneurysmal disease is absent. If the BAV is calcified, stenotic, or severely regurgitant, or if root dilatation is present, aortic root replacement with a composite valved conduit is indicated. Early and late survival after ATAAD repair in BAV patients is comparable to that observed in patients with tricuspid aortic valves.

#### What is the preferred management of ATAAD complicated by persistent coronary malperfusion?
Coronary malperfusion occurs in 10% to 15% of ATAAD cases and carries extreme operative mortality. The mechanism may be static (circumferential false lumen thrombosis compressing the coronary ostium) or dynamic (prolapse of the dissection flap over the coronary orifice during cardiac cycle phases) ([Fig. 1](#fig-1)B).

Diagnosis is established via preoperative ECG changes, regional wall motion abnormalities on echocardiography, coronary disruption on CTA, or direct intraoperative visualization of coronary ostial detachment. Management requires rapid restoration of coronary perfusion. Techniques include:
* Fenestration and obliteration of the false lumen during commissural repair;
* Unroofing and patch reconstruction of the coronary ostium with pericardium or felt;
* Button excision and reimplantation during root replacement.

If direct surgical ostial reconstruction fails to restore robust antegrade coronary flow, or if regional myocardial hypokinesia and electrical instability persist after weaning from CPB, emergency coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) with a saphenous vein conduit must be performed expeditiously prior to chest closure.

## Section 6: Ascending Aorta and Distal Anastomosis</summary><div class="details-content">

The tubular ascending aorta is the segment at highest risk for rupture in ATAAD. Core surgical objectives during ATAAD repair are:
1. Resection of the entire vulnerable tubular ascending aorta including the primary intimal tear;
2. Prevention of proximal intrapericardial rupture; and
3. Redirection of systemic blood flow exclusively into the distal true lumen ([Table 6](#table-6)).

An open distal anastomosis under hypothermic circulatory arrest permits complete resection of the ascending aorta and enables a beveled hemiarch reconstruction if the intimal tear extends into the concavity of the aortic arch ([Fig. 3](#fig-3)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anastomotic Techniques and Resection Extent</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-3"></span>![](fig3_ascending_aorta_distal_anastomosis.jpg)

**FIGURE 3.** Surgical principles of ascending aorta resection and distal open anastomosis. Complete resection of the ascending aorta eliminates the vulnerable tissue and primary entry tear under circulatory arrest. Distal reconstruction restores true lumen perfusion by meticulous reapposition of the dissected aortic wall layers using Teflon felt or bovine pericardial neomedia reinforcement, fine monofilament suture lines, and adventitial collar buttressing.

#### Should the distal aortic anastomosis be performed open or clamped?
Historically, some surgeons performed distal anastomosis with an aortic cross-clamp in place without hypothermic circulatory arrest. However, cross-clamping a dissected aorta carries substantial risks: the clamp can crush fragile aortic tissue, generate new intimal tears, prevent direct visualization of the distal arch, and risk leaving residual entry tears in the proximal arch.

An open distal anastomosis under circulatory arrest is the accepted standard of care. It provides unhindered visualization of the aortic arch, facilitates complete resection of the primary entry tear, and ensures precise reconstitution of the aortic wall layers ([Fig. 3](#fig-3)). Multiple large registry analyses (e.g., Lawton et al., Malvindi et al.) demonstrate that open distal anastomosis is associated with superior downstream aortic remodeling, lower rates of distal aortic enlargement, and significantly improved long-term survival compared with clamped repair. In an analysis from the Nordic Consortium (NORCAAD), clamped repair was associated with higher unadjusted mortality, although patients in that cohort presented more frequently with shock and severe comorbidities. Clamped anastomosis may remain an acceptable option in highly selected circumstances, such as elderly patients in profound cardiogenic shock at community hospitals where facilities for safe circulatory arrest and cerebral perfusion are constrained.

#### What extent of resection should be performed in ATAAD?
The extent of resection should encompass the entire tubular ascending aorta from the sinotubular junction to the origin of the brachiocephalic artery, incorporating the primary entry tear ([Fig. 3](#fig-3)). Incomplete resection that leaves residual dissected ascending aorta below or above the graft is a potent risk factor for progressive aneurysmal degeneration, pseudoaneurysm formation, and early reoperation.

When the intimal tear extends into the lesser curve of the arch, a beveled hemiarch reconstruction under circulatory arrest with ACP is standard. Hemiarch replacement effectively eliminates the tear, restores distal true lumen flow, and minimizes early reoperation risk without significantly increasing operative complexity. Furthermore, complete resection of the ascending aorta provides a straight, robust Dacron landing zone that facilitates subsequent endovascular or hybrid interventions if the downstream aorta undergoes late aneurysmal degeneration.

Techniques for preparing the distal aortic stump include sandwich reconstruction with internal and external Teflon felt strips, bovine pericardial gaskets, or adventitial inversion buttressing ([Fig. 3](#fig-3)). The quality and precision of the continuous monofilament suture line are paramount to prevent anastomotic leaks and avoid secondary tear generation.

#### What is the role of ascending TEVAR in ATAAD?
Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) for the ascending aorta (Zone 0 TEVAR) is currently investigational. While up to 20% of patients with ATAAD do not undergo open surgery due to extreme age, prohibitive frailty, or fatal comorbidity, dedicated commercial ascending endovascular graft devices with active fixation and conformability to the hyperdynamic aortic root are not yet approved. Early feasibility trials and off-label series have demonstrated technical feasibility with 30-day mortality ranging from 0% to 13%, but long-term data regarding durability, stroke, and retrograde redissection are lacking. Zone 0 TEVAR remains strictly investigational.

## Section 7: Aortic Arch Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic arch reconstruction in ATAAD represents a strategic balance between the operative risks of extended repair and the long-term benefits of eliminating downstream aortic pathology ([Table 7](#table-7)). Most ATAAD patients can be treated safely and definitively with hemiarch reconstruction combined with complete ascending aortic replacement. However, extended arch repair (total arch replacement with branched graft or Frozen Elephant Trunk [FET]) is indicated in specific clinical and anatomical scenarios.

Extended arch repair requires longer periods of circulatory arrest and profound hypothermia; however, in high-volume comprehensive aortic centers, these risks are minimized through systematic ACP neuroprotection and refined surgical algorithms.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Extended Arch Replacement and Frozen Elephant Trunk</summary><div class="details-content">

#### When should extended arch surgery be performed in patients with ATAAD?
Extended aortic arch replacement is recommended in patients presenting with:
1. Primary intimal entry tears located in the transverse arch or proximal descending thoracic aorta that cannot be resected or excluded via hemiarch repair (observed in 13% to 32% of ATAAD cases);
2. Cerebral or arch branch-vessel malperfusion due to static dissection, severe true lumen collapse, or circumferential disruption of arch vessels;
3. Pre-existing transverse arch aneurysm (> 55 mm) or extensive aneurysmal degeneration;
4. Traumatic or acute rupture of the aortic arch.

In these clinical scenarios, hemiarch repair fails to exclude the entry tear or relieve arch-vessel obstruction, leaving the patient at high risk of early persistent malperfusion or progressive distal aortic expansion.

#### Is it reasonable to use FET in addition to ATAAD surgery?
The Frozen Elephant Trunk (FET) technique combines open surgical total arch replacement with antegrade deployment of a stented endovascular graft into the proximal descending thoracic aorta under direct vision during circulatory arrest. FET offers several distinct physiological advantages:
* It expands the collapsed true lumen and re-establishes distal perfusion;
* It covers and seals secondary entry tears in the proximal descending thoracic aorta;
* It induces early and sustained false lumen thrombosis around the stent graft in up to 85% of patients, promoting positive aortic remodeling;
* It provides a secure, hemostatic landing zone for future open thoracoabdominal or endovascular (TEVAR) reinterventions.

A primary concern with the FET procedure is spinal cord injury (SCI, paraplegia/paraparesis), which occurs when extensive descending aortic coverage compromises critical intercostal arterial feeders to the anterior spinal artery (artery of Adamkiewicz). Large multicenter registries and meta-analyses demonstrate that SCI risk is directly correlated with the distal extent of coverage and graft length. Limiting FET deployment to at or above the **sixth thoracic vertebra (T6)**—conveniently matching the anatomical level of the native aortic valve—or utilizing **10-cm length stent grafts** rather than 15-cm devices significantly reduces the incidence of paraplegia while preserving favorable remodeling benefits.

#### How should the arch be managed in patients with ATAAD and HTAD?
Patients with hereditary thoracic aortic disorders (HTAD), such as Marfan syndrome, present with ATAAD at a markedly younger age. Following conventional ascending or hemiarch repair, over 50% of HTAD patients develop progressive aneurysmal dilation of the residual dissected arch and descending aorta, and over 40% ultimately require extensive secondary aortic reoperations throughout their lifetime.

In young, physiologically fit patients with HTAD presenting with uncomplicated ATAAD, extended aortic arch replacement with an elephant trunk procedure (classical or frozen) may be considered at the index operation. This planned, staged strategy simplifies subsequent elective thoracoabdominal aortic repair, minimizes emergency reoperations, and improves lifetime survival.

## Section 8: Malperfusion Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Organ malperfusion is among the most lethal complications of ATAAD, occurring in 15% to 40% of patients ([Table 8](#table-8)). While coronary and cerebral malperfusion are immediately life-threatening, peripheral malperfusion (mesenteric in 4%–6%, renal in 6%–9%, spinal cord in 2%–5%, and lower extremity in 10%–15%) carries profound morbidity and mortality. Ischemia of vital vascular beds leads to systemic malperfusion syndrome, characterized by severe metabolic acidosis, lactic acidemia, systemic inflammatory response, and irreversible end-organ infarction.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology of Branch Vessel Obstruction and Revascularization Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-4"></span>![](fig4_branch_vessel_obstruction.jpg)

**FIGURE 4.** Mechanisms of branch-vessel obstruction in acute aortic dissection. **(Left panel)** *Static obstruction:* The pressurized false lumen circumferentially compresses the branch vessel origin to complete stenosis, often leading to secondary distal thrombosis; static obstruction is rarely relieved by proximal aortic repair alone and requires direct intervention. **(Right panel)** *Dynamic obstruction:* High pressure in the false lumen intermittently displaces the mobile intimal flap over the vessel orifice; dynamic obstruction is typically resolved by central proximal repair and true lumen decompression.

Branch vessels may be compromised by two distinct mechanisms ([Fig. 4](#fig-4)):
* **Dynamic obstruction:** The pressurized false lumen prolapses across the branch ostium like a curtain, intermittently obstructing true lumen flow without intrinsic vessel wall disruption ([Fig. 4](#fig-4), right). Dynamic obstruction is usually relieved promptly by central surgical repair and decompression of the false lumen.
* **Static obstruction:** The dissection flap enters the branch vessel directly, or the pressurized false lumen circumferentially constricts the vessel origin, causing complete luminal collapse and secondary distal thrombosis ([Fig. 4](#fig-4), left). Static obstruction persists despite successful central repair and mandates direct catheter-based or surgical revascularization.

#### How should ATAAD patients with malperfusion and end-organ dysfunction be managed?
Mesenteric malperfusion is exceptionally lethal. Historical cohorts treated with immediate open proximal aortic repair followed by secondary laparotomy suffered operative mortality rates ranging from 41% to 100%. In an IRAD analysis of 1,809 ATAAD patients, mesenteric malperfusion occurred in 4% and carried a 63% mortality. In patients with established splanchnic ischemia, cardiopulmonary bypass, systemic heparinization, and hypothermia induce severe reperfusion injury, intractable coagulopathy, metabolic acidosis, and multi-organ failure.

To address this devastating paradox, an alternative paradigm—**upfront endovascular revascularization followed by delayed open proximal repair**—has been established. First described by Deeb and colleagues and recently validated in a 20-year experience by Yang et al., stable ATAAD patients presenting with mesenteric malperfusion undergo immediate percutaneous intervention:
* True lumen aortic fenestration;
* Stenting of the celiac and superior mesenteric arteries; or
* Antegrade descending TEVAR ("TEVAR-first" approach).

Once visceral organ perfusion is restored and metabolic derangements (lactate, liver enzymes, acidosis) normalize, the patient is stabilized in the ICU and undergoes delayed open proximal aortic repair. In the University of Michigan series, in-hospital mortality for patients surviving to delayed open surgery was reduced to 2%–10%, although ~ 10%–13% of patients suffered fatal aortic rupture while awaiting open repair. A hybrid approach—immediate endovascular mesenteric reperfusion in a hybrid suite immediately followed by open central repair under the same anesthetic—aims to eliminate the delay and eliminate rupture risk. Upfront percutaneous/endovascular revascularization is reasonable in hemodynamically stable patients with documented mesenteric malperfusion and metabolic dysfunction.

#### How should ATAAD patients with limb ischemia be managed?
Acute lower extremity malperfusion occurs in 10% to 15% of ATAAD cases due to dynamic true lumen compression or static iliofemoral branch thrombosis. While dynamic obstruction resolves after central aortic repair and true lumen pressurization, static obstruction results in progressive muscle necrosis, rhabdomyolysis, myoglobinuric renal failure, and limb loss.

In patients presenting with severe, profound limb ischemia accompanied by motor or sensory deficits, prolonged delay to central repair risks irreversible ischemic contracture. Immediate peripheral revascularization (e.g., extra-anatomic femorofemoral bypass, axillofemoral bypass, or percutaneous iliac stenting) before or concomitant with central aortic repair is reasonable to prevent rhabdomyolysis and severe reperfusion injury.

## Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

Optimal postoperative management of patients surviving ATAAD repair requires rigorous lifelong blood pressure control and systematic serial imaging surveillance of the entire aorta. Despite successful repair of the proximal aorta, the majority of patients harbor a residual patent false lumen in the downstream thoracic and abdominal aorta, placing them at continued lifetime risk of aneurysmal degeneration, redissection, and late rupture.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Long-Term Medical Therapy and Surveillance</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Medical Therapy and Blood Pressure Targets
Strict blood pressure management dramatically improves long-term survival after ATAAD repair. $\beta$-Adrenergic blockade reduces both heart rate and blood pressure, attenuating aortic wall shear stress ($dP/dt$) independent of systemic pressure effects. In a nationwide propensity-matched analysis of 396 patient pairs by Chen et al., postoperative $\beta$-blocker therapy conferred a highly significant survival benefit (late mortality 16% vs 27%, $P < .001$), with strict adherence directly correlating with freedom from aortic reoperation. IRAD registry analyses likewise confirm that post-discharge $\beta$-blocker use significantly improves survival ($P = .006$). Target blood pressure should be maintained below 120/80 mmHg.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Black-Box Warning: Strict Contraindication of Systemic Fluoroquinolones**  
> A growing body of clinical and experimental evidence demonstrates that fluoroquinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) cause severe disruption of collagen and elastin within the aortic extracellular matrix. Experimental studies confirm that fluoroquinolones exacerbate aortic wall degradation and induce acute rupture in murine dissection models. Clinical epidemiological studies have demonstrated a 2- to 3-fold increased risk of aortic aneurysm, dissection, and rupture in patients prescribed fluoroquinolones. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) have issued strict black-box warnings: systemic fluoroquinolones must **never** be prescribed to patients with pre-existing aortic dissection, aortic aneurysm, or genetic aortopathies unless no alternative antimicrobial therapy exists.

#### Postoperative Imaging Surveillance Protocol
Lifelong imaging surveillance is imperative for all patients surviving ATAAD repair. Predictors of late aortic growth and distal reintervention include a patent downstream false lumen, large initial aortic diameter (> 40 mm), and persistent proximal re-entry tears.

Recommended cross-sectional imaging schedule (contrast-enhanced CTA or MRA of the entire aorta):
1. **Predischarge:** Baseline assessment before hospital discharge (if renal function permits);
2. **6 Months:** Early surveillance for rapid aneurysmal expansion or anastomotic pseudoaneurysm;
3. **12 Months:** Confirmation of stable aortic remodeling;
4. **Annually thereafter:** Lifelong annual CTA or MRA surveillance.

Imaging should incorporate non-contrast, arterial, and delayed venous phases to evaluate false lumen perfusion, thrombus formation, and visceral branch supply. Serial transthoracic echocardiography is indicated to monitor aortic valve function, left ventricular dimensions, and ejection fraction.

#### Genetic Testing and Family Screening
Thoracic aortic aneurysms and dissections exhibit significant familial clustering. All first-degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) of patients who suffer an ATAAD should be screened with transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) to evaluate the aortic root and ascending aorta. If the initial echocardiogram is normal, repeat screening is recommended every 5 to 10 years.

Patients presenting with ATAAD who meet any of the following criteria should be referred for formal genetic counseling and comprehensive thoracic aortic gene panel testing:
* Age younger than 50 years at presentation (or 50–60 years in the absence of chronic hypertension);
* Physical syndromic features (e.g., Marfanoid habitus, ectopia lentis, craniofacial features, pectus deformity, skin hyperelasticity);
* Positive family history of thoracic aortic aneurysm, dissection, or unexplained sudden premature death.

If a definitive pathogenic or likely pathogenic variant is identified (e.g., in *FBN1*, *TGFBR1*, *TGFBR2*, *SMAD3*, *ACTA2*, *MYH11*, *COL3A1*), targeted cascade genetic testing should be offered to all at-risk family members.

## Conclusions and Future Directions</summary><div class="details-content">

Because of the lethal nature of ATAAD, a true surgical emergency that is uncommon and rarely encountered in individual non-specialized centers, this expert consensus document was developed to provide evidence-based, consensus-derived guidance for practicing cardiac surgeons, emergency physicians, and multidisciplinary teams.

Core consensus principles include:
1. **Anti-Impulse Therapy:** Immediate administration of intravenous $\beta$-blockade and rapid analgesia before surgical repair to diminish aortic wall shear stress.
2. **Expeditious Surgery:** Urgent open surgical repair remains the undisputed standard of care for virtually all patients with ATAAD, including selected patients presenting with acute brain malperfusion or coma.
3. **Surgical Conduct:** The operation should be performed safely using techniques familiar to the operating surgeon.
4. **Perfusion and Cannulation:** Right axillary and direct aortic cannulation are preferred; systemic antegrade perfusion and routine cerebral perfusion (ACP or RCP) during circulatory arrest are paramount.
5. **Open Distal Anastomosis:** Resection of the entire ascending aorta and primary intimal tear using an open distal anastomosis under circulatory arrest is essential for durable outcomes.
6. **Aortic Root Management:** Aortic valve resuspension with neomedia false lumen obliteration is appropriate for most patients; formal root replacement is indicated for root aneurysms (> 45 mm), intimal tears in the root, or hereditary thoracic aortic disorders.
7. **Malperfusion Syndrome:** Mesenteric and severe limb malperfusion represent profound challenges; in stable patients with end-organ dysfunction, upfront percutaneous or endovascular revascularization before delayed open proximal repair reduces operative mortality.
8. **Lifelong Optimization:** Lifelong $\beta$-blockade, strict blood pressure control, absolute avoidance of fluoroquinolones, serial multi-phase CT/MRI surveillance, and family screening are vital to curb late mortality.

### Future Directions
Future efforts must focus on:
* Developing regionalized aortic care networks with streamlined diagnostic protocols;
* Conducting multicenter prospective registries and comparative effectiveness trials to refine patient selection for extended arch replacement and Frozen Elephant Trunk;
* Investigating next-generation endovascular ascending aortic stent-grafts for anatomically suited inoperable patients;
* Establishing structured postoperative rehabilitation and long-term surveillance clinics to maximize patient compliance and improve late survival.
