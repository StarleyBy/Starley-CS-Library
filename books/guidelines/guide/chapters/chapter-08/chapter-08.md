# The Society for Vascular Surgery clinical practice guideline on the <strong><span class="t-green">management of blunt thoracic aortic injury</span></strong>: Focused update

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Rationale
* **Blunt thoracic aortic injury (BTAI):** Devastating and potentially fatal complication of major trauma requiring expedient diagnosis and management by a collaborative team of trauma and vascular specialists. Most commonly encountered after major deceleration events (motor vehicle, auto vs pedestrian, fall-related injuries). Isolated BTAI is exceedingly rare; commonly associated injuries include traumatic brain injury (TBI), solid organ injury (SOI), and pelvic fracture.
* **Associated injuries:** 30% to 50% of patients with BTAI have coincident TBI or SOI, associated with significantly greater mortality. 28% of patients with BTAI have simultaneous TBI, with more severe shock, greater injury severity score, worse Glasgow Coma Scale, and higher likelihood of acute respiratory distress syndrome, stroke, and cardiac arrest.

### Classification of BTAI (SVS Grading)
* **Grade 1:** Intimal tear — minimal aortic injury (MAI).
* **Grade 2:** Intramural hematoma — minimal aortic injury (MAI).
* **Grade 3:** Pseudoaneurysm.
* **Grade 4:** Rupture.

### Management Summary
* **Grade 1 and 2 (MAI):** Definitive nonoperative management (NOM) recommended over TEVAR or open repair (1C). Grade 1 requires no routine follow-up imaging. Grade 2 should undergo one follow-up CTA to ensure resolution.
* **Grade 3 (hemodynamically stable):** Delayed TEVAR (> 24 hours) suggested to allow for management of other associated traumatic injuries (2C). In unstable patients where BTAI is the specific cause of instability, urgent (< 24 hours) or emergent intervention recommended (1B).
* **Grade 4:** Emergent repair recommended (1C).
* **High-risk features for early intervention in grade 3:** Hemothorax, mediastinal hematoma, aortic coarctation, lesion/normal aortic diameter ratio > 1.4, admission lactate > 4 mM.

### Left Subclavian Artery (LSA) Management
* **LSA coverage during TEVAR:** Decision regarding revascularization based on feasibility and factors: patent prior left internal mammary to coronary bypass, detection of dominant left vertebral artery on preoperative or intraoperative imaging, or aortic origin of left vertebral artery (2C).
* **Indications for revascularization:** (1) Patent prior left internal mammary to coronary bypass to prevent major cardiac event; (2) Dominant left vertebral artery (diminutive or occluded right vertebral artery, or end posterior inferior cerebellar artery) to mitigate posterior circulation stroke; (3) Aortic origin of left vertebral artery to avoid limb-threatening left arm ischemia.
* **Revascularization options:** Branched devices, open bypass, or transposition.

### Intraoperative Anticoagulation
* **Heparinization during TEVAR:** Can be used at surgeon's discretion after considering risk of bleeding and thrombotic complications (2C). Full heparinization did not increase mortality; VQI database indicated mortality benefit. No difference in stroke, thrombotic events, spinal cord ischemia, hematoma, or endoleak related to systemic heparinization.
* **In TBI/SOI:** Collaborative approach for intraprocedural anticoagulation; decisions individualized based on multidisciplinary collaboration (best practice statement).

### Imaging Surveillance
* **Grade 1 BTAI treated nonoperatively:** Against routine surveillance imaging (2C). 93% of grade I injuries demonstrate resolution or stability by median postinjury day 11; additional 6% by day 31.
* **Grade 2 BTAI treated nonoperatively:** At least one follow-up surveillance imaging study; repeat imaging at 1 to 3 months after injury (2C). Median resolution time 39 days.
* **Post-TEVAR:** Postoperative surveillance imaging suggested (2C). No differences in survival, graft-related complications, or need for reintervention between first reimaging at 12–18 months vs after 18 months.

### Concomitant TBI/SOI Management
* **Grade 1 or 2 BTAI with TBI:** Against anti-impulse therapy for BTAI; management of blood pressure should prioritize TBI over BTAI (2C).
* **Grade 3 BTAI with TBI:** Decision to use anti-impulse therapy individualized based on collaborative management (2C).
* **Grade 3 BTAI with TBI or SOI:** Timing for TEVAR determined in collaborative fashion with trauma and neurosurgery (2C).
* **Grade 4 BTAI:** Emergent repair recommended (1C).
* **Intraprocedural anticoagulation in TBI/SOI:** Collaborative approach; heparinization not associated with worse intracranial bleeding or neurologic outcomes, but certainty in evidence is very low.

### Anti-Impulse Therapy
* **Grade 3 BTAI:** Anti-impulse therapy suggested as stabilizing measure until TEVAR performed if concomitant injuries do not preclude this approach (2C). Continuous titratable infusion of antihypertensive medication used in 49.1% of patients; β-blockers most used class (74.6%). Target systolic blood pressure goal < 120 mm Hg achieved in only 64% of patients.

### Methods
* **GRADE approach:** Certainty in estimates assessed using GRADE. SVS assigns labels A, B, C to high, moderate, and low/very low certainty. Recommendations strong (recommend) or conditional (suggest).
* **Systematic reviews:** Search strategies developed by methodology team and medical librarians; multiple databases from inception to April 15, 2024. Meta-analyses conducted when two or more studies addressed same outcome. Risk of bias assessed using Newcastle Ottawa Scale.
* **Evidence gaps:** Comparative effectiveness studies and large-scale randomized trials severely lacking; published literature relies on retrospective data and multicenter registry collaborations.

## Summary of recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

(1) In patients with grade 1 and 2 blunt thoracic aortic injury (BTAI) (minimal aortic injury [MAI]), we recommend definitive nonoperative management (NOM) over thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) or open repair ([Fig 1](#fig-1)). (IC) Grade 1 injuries require no routine follow-up imaging. We suggest grade 2 injuries undergo one follow-up computed tomography angiography (CTA) to ensure resolution.

(2) In patients with hemodynamically stable grade 3 BTAI, we suggest delayed TEVAR (> 24 hours) to allow for management of other associated traumatic injuries if needed. (2C) In an unstable patient in whom there is a concern that the BTAI is the specific cause of the patient's instability, we recommend urgent (< 24 hours) or emergent intervention. (IB)

(3) Among patients requiring left subclavian artery (LSA) coverage for TEVAR after BTAI, we suggest that the decision regarding LSA revascularization be based on feasibility and factors such as a patient prior left internal mammary to coronary bypass, detection of a dominant left vertebral artery on preoperative or intraoperative imaging, or an aortic origin of the left vertebral artery. (2C)

(4) In patients with BTAI undergoing TEVAR, we suggest that intraoperative anticoagulation can be used at the surgeon's discretion after considering the risk of bleeding and thrombotic complications. (2C)

(5a) In all patients with grade 1 BTAI treated nonoperatively, we suggest against routine surveillance imaging. (2C)

(5b) In patients with grade 2 BTAI treated nonoperatively, we suggest at least one follow-up surveillance imaging study. We suggest repeat imaging at 1 to 3 months after injury. (2C)

(5c) In patients who had TEVAR for BTAI, we suggest postoperative surveillance imaging. (2C)

(6a) In patients with grade 1 or 2 BTAI (MAI) and concomitant traumatic brain injury (TBI), we suggest against anti-impulse therapy for BTAI. Management of blood pressure should prioritize TBI over BTAI. (2C)

(6b) In patients with grade 3 BTAI and concomitant TBI, we suggest that the decision to use anti-impulse therapy should be individualized based on collaborative management with other surgical and medical specialties. (2C)

(6c) In patients with grade 3 BTAI and concomitant TBI or solid organ injury (SOI), we suggest that timing for TEVAR should be determined in a collaborative fashion in consultation with other stakeholders, including trauma and neurosurgery. (2C)

(6d) In patients with grade 4 BTAI, we recommend emergent repair. (IC)

(6e) For patients with BTAI and concomitant TBI or SOI, we suggest a collaborative approach for intraprocedural anticoagulation during TEVAR. Although current observational studies do provide some reassurance that heparinization is not associated with worse intracranial bleeding or neurologic outcomes, the certainty in this evidence is very low. Decisions about intraoperative anticoagulation in patients with TBI should be individualized based on multidisciplinary collaboration. (best practice statement)

(7) In patients with grade 3 BTAI, we suggest anti-impulse therapy as a stabilizing measure until TEVAR is performed if the concomitant injuries do not preclude this approach. (2C)

## INTRODUCTION AND RATIONALE</summary><div class="details-content">

In 2011, the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) published comprehensive clinical practice guidelines (CPG) on the management of BTAI with TEVAR. BTAI remains an infrequent but potentially lethal sequela of major trauma — most commonly encountered after major deceleration events, including major motor vehicle, auto vs pedestrian or fall-related injuries. Because of the nature of the kinetic energy exerted on the human body in these mechanisms of injury, isolated BTAI is exceedingly rare. Commonly associated injuries including TBI, SOI, and pelvic fracture represent major risk for hemorrhagic complications, including death. Accordingly, these injuries often take precedence over initial BTAI management depending on a complex assessment of factors that must be conducted rapidly and in a collaborative fashion with the key stakeholders of care for these patients. This CPG was undertaken to provide clinicians caring for victims of trauma with the best available contemporary data on the diagnosis, initial management, definitive care, and follow-up for these patients.

In planning this update, the committee considered the scope of clinical research advances in the management of BTAI since the prior publication in 2011. The areas for focus concern primarily the role and conduct of TEVAR in the management of BTAI. Comparative effectiveness studies and large-scale randomized trials in this specific population of patients remain severely lacking. The published literature on the selection for, timing of, and conduct of TEVAR for patients with BTAI relies on retrospective data and multicenter registry collaborations. These limitations were highlighted during the systematic data review undertaken, impacting the strength of recommendations.

</div></details>

## METHODS</summary><div class="details-content">

The SVS appointed the chair and approved members of the writing committee with specific expertise in the management of BTAI. Committee members' conflicts of interest were declared and reviewed on a regular basis in accordance with SVS policies. Methodological support was provided by the Mayo Clinic Evidence-based Practice Center, which included facilitation of developing structured clinical questions using the PICOS format (population, intervention, comparison, outcomes, subgroups), identification of patient-important outcomes, conducting systematic reviews, and support in the evidence-to-decision (EtD) process. The committee used expert consensus to prioritize seven clinical questions deemed to be of highest importance to current practice.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Conducting systematic reviews and rating certainty of evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Search strategies were developed by the methodology team and medical librarians. The strategies used structured controlled vocabulary terms and text words to search multiple databases from each database's inception to April 15, 2024. Eligible studies were selected by two independent reviewers based on prior established inclusion criteria consistent with each PICO question. Meta-analyses were conducted when two or more studies addressed the same outcome. Risk of bias was assessed using items selected from the Newcastle Ottawa Scale. The certainty in the estimates was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach, consistent with SVS adoption of GRADE in 2007.

The GRADE approach starts with a provisional high certainty rating for outcomes derived from randomized trials and low certainty from nonrandomized studies. This level of certainty can be reduced or increased based on several domains, such as the risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, publication bias, and magnitude of effect. SVS assigns the labels of A, B, and C to high, moderate, and low/very low certainty.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Developing recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

SVS uses the GRADE EtD framework to transform evidence to recommendations. These actionable statements are based on certainty of the evidence, balance of effects, values and preferences, feasibility (eg, availability of surgical expertise), acceptability by stakeholders, and other contextual factors. Recommendations are either strong or conditional, denoted with the verbs "recommend" and "suggest," respectively. The rationale for the recommendations is provided in EtD worksheets (see the [Appendix](#appendix), online only). Strong recommendations imply that the desirable consequences clearly outweigh the undesirable consequences in most settings and that most patients in this situation would want the recommended course of action.

</div></details>

## RESPONSES TO PICO QUESTIONS, DATA REVIEW, AND RECOMMENDATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### PICO question #1: should patients with SVS grade 1 and 2 (MAIs) be treated with NOM alone or TEVAR?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Background and rationale</summary><div class="details-content">

The previous SVS guidelines recommended that NOM be used as definitive care for SVS grade 1 injuries (see [Fig 2](#fig-2)). Since this 2011 publication, several studies have suggested that both grade 1 and 2, together referred to as MAIs, may be safely treated with NOM.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Several observational studies have demonstrated no difference in aortic-related mortality or outcomes when SVS grade 1 and 2 injuries (MAIs) receive NOM. These studies demonstrated that MAIs were associated with low aortic-related mortality and excellent intermediate-term outcomes. One study followed MAIs with surveillance CTA imaging and showed that the majority resolve within 8 weeks. An approach using NOM with follow-up imaging of grade 2 injuries allows for detection of the injury progression in these patients and avoids the potential complications that can result from the conduct of TEVAR, including access complications.

Multiple studies suggest that follow-up imaging for grade 1 injuries may not be required. Most notably, Jinadasa et al found that more than 93% of patients with grade I injuries demonstrated achievement of resolution or stability of their injury by median postinjury day 11; an additional 6% showed resolution by median postinjury day 31. Of the minority that showed initial worsening of their injury, 85% went on to show injury resolution. Sandhu et al found that grade I injuries had a median resolution time of 39 days.

Although one retrospective review of the Aortic Trauma Foundation (ATF) Registry from 2016 to 2021 showed a difference between NOM and TEVAR with regard to mortality, the SVS commissioned systematic review combining multiple studies showed no difference in mortality, with an increased aortic-related mortality with TEVAR.

The weight of present data favors NOM for SVS grade 1 and 2 injuries, despite the need for additional research on this topic. The committee made a strong recommendation considering the feasibility, availability, and resource use associated with NOM.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recommendation</summary><div class="details-content">

1. In patients with grades 1 and 2 BTAI (MAI), we recommend definitive NOM over TEVAR or open repair. (IC) Grade 1 injuries require no routine follow-up imaging. We suggest grade 2 injuries undergo one follow-up CTA to ensure resolution.

</div></details>

### PICO question #2: should SVS grade 3 BTAI undergo early or delayed TEVAR?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Background and rationale</summary><div class="details-content">

The previous SVS CPG suggested urgent repair (< 24 hours) for all SVS grade 3 injuries. Since this 2011 guideline, several studies have examined outcomes among both early and delayed TEVAR in these patients. Patients presenting with SVS grade 3 injuries rarely have isolated BTAI. There is frequently a question of which injury should be addressed first, and often, significant time in the operating room is required to address all of the associated traumatic injuries. The rationale of a delayed approach would allow for patient stabilization and permit management of coexisting traumatic injuries that may represent a greater threat to a detrimental outcome.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Updated review since the previous CPG yields additional research suggesting that early TEVAR (< 24 hours) is associated with increased overall mortality in SVS grade 3 BTAI. In addition, there was no difference in aortic-related mortality in the early vs late cohorts.

Despite these findings favoring delayed TEVAR for grade 3 BTAI, it is important to consider that several factors may provide solid indication for earlier timing of TEVAR in select patients. Early TEVAR is advised in unstable patients with SVS grade 3 BTAI where the BTAI is deemed the specific cause for the patient's instability. In addition, if the aortic injury demonstrates certain high-risk features, urgent (< 24 hours) intervention would be necessary, because indirect evidence implies that delay in the presence of such features may be associated with a higher risk of complications, including death. Such reported high-risk features include hemothorax, mediastinal hematoma, aortic coarctation, lesion/normal aortic diameter ratio > 1.4 and admission lactate > 4 mM.

Taken in total, these existing data suggest that early TEVAR is associated with worse outcomes than a more delayed approach. Additional study is required to further define the optimal timing of TEVAR within the context of specific global injury patterns, hemodynamic parameters, and patient-specific risk factors.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recommendation</summary><div class="details-content">

2. In patients with hemodynamically stable grade 3 BTAI, we suggest delayed TEVAR (> 24 hours) to allow for management of other associated traumatic injuries if needed. (2C) In an unstable patient in whom there is a concern that the BTAI is the specific cause of the patient's instability, we recommend urgent (< 24 hours) or emergent intervention. (1B)

</div></details>

### PICO question #3: do patients undergoing left subclavian artery coverage (zone 2) for TEVAR after BTAI require left subclavian revascularization?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Background and rationale</summary><div class="details-content">

The previous SVS CPG favored selective revascularization (either before or after TEVAR) depending on the status of the vertebral anatomy, with a minority of experts favoring revascularization. The rationale of revascularization approaches has included (1) the preservation of antegrade perfusion to the cerebrovascular system to specifically decrease the risk of posterior circulation strokes and (2) to mitigate the risk of left arm ischemia. Early revascularization is reasonably of particular importance in the setting of prior left internal mammary to coronary artery bypass or left arm dialysis access.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Several studies have been conducted on this topic since the previous guidelines, although not all met criteria for inclusion in our analysis because populations that included non-BTAI patients. One included study from the ATF International BTAI registry examined 364 patients undergoing TEVAR for BTAI: 26.6% of whom underwent left subclavian artery (LSCA) coverage without revascularization and 2.7% (10) underwent coverage with subsequent LSCA revascularization. These investigators found a lower incidence of stroke or paralysis with revascularization during the index TEVAR procedure compared with those patients who did not require LSCA coverage.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recommendation</summary><div class="details-content">

3. Among patients requiring LSA coverage for TEVAR after BTAI, we suggest that the decision about LSA revascularization be based on feasibility and factors such as a patent prior left internal mammary to coronary bypass, detection of a dominant left vertebral artery on preoperative or intraoperative imaging, or an aortic origin of the left vertebral artery. (2C)

Revascularization should be considered in (1) circumstances of a patent prior left internal mammary to coronary bypass to prevent a major cardiac event, (2) when a dominant left vertebral artery is detected (a diminutive or occluded right vertebral artery, or an end posterior inferior cerebellar artery) on preoperative or intraoperative imaging to mitigate the risk of posterior circulation stroke, and (3) in the case of an aortic origin of the left vertebral artery to avoid limb-threatening left arm ischemia. Multiple LSA revascularization options exist, each with its own unique risks and procedural considerations, including but not limited to branched devices, open bypass, or transposition. The urgency of repair (injury grade), the condition of the patient, the availability of devices, and expertise may impact the feasibility and timing of revascularization.

</div></details>

### PICO question #4: what is the optimal role of intraoperative systemic anticoagulation in patients with BTAI undergoing TEVAR?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Background and rationale</summary><div class="details-content">

The rationale for heparinization in the context of TEVAR is to mitigate the risk of thrombotic complications, particularly of the lower extremities, in the setting of larger-bore sheath requirements. The safety of systemic intravenous heparinization during TEVAR in a polytrauma patient with a TBI or SOI, however, remains a significant area of concern for all clinical stakeholders caring for these patients.

In the 2011 SVS CPG, the majority of experts suggested that heparin be used routinely, but at lower doses than used in the context of elective TEVAR. The previous CPG group also yielded a minority opinion expressing that heparin may not be necessary, given that the rapidity with which TEVAR can be performed in these cases mitigates the risk for thrombotic events. In the final analysis of the 2011 CPG writing group, they concluded that the decision for heparinization must be individualized based on the balance of the perceived risks of bleeding for the specific patient.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Two recently published nonrandomized studies have investigated the use of heparin vs no systemic heparinization during TEVAR. Full heparinization did not increase mortality, and analysis of the Vascular Quality Initiative database indicated a mortality benefit associated with the practice. Further, the risks pertaining to the use of heparin during TEVAR were demonstrated to be similar across all grades of aortic injury. There was no difference in stroke, thrombotic events, spinal cord ischemia, hematoma, or endoleak related to systemic heparinization during TEVAR.

### PICO question #5: what is the optimal imaging surveillance protocol for patients with BTAI after TEVAR or NOM?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Background and rationale</summary><div class="details-content">

Although the indications for surveillance imaging after BTAI treated with NOM are generally recognized as including detection of injury progression or resolution, the optimal frequency, duration, and modality of that imaging have not been clearly defined. Previous SVS guidelines lacked sufficient data to justify substantive guidelines.

Since that time, conservative management of grade 1 and 2 BTAI (MAI) has been proposed by investigators, making the need for diligent and standardized surveillance practices of these injuries more pressing.

Similarly, guidelines for postoperative surveillance of patients with BTAI treated with TEVAR have not been clearly established. Surveillance practices for TEVAR after aneurysm or dissection are predicated on pathology-specific risks that may not pertain to patients with BTAI. Moreover, the relative youth of the trauma population attaches substantially higher cumulative radiation exposure and cost to that surveillance protocol. On the other hand, the long-term durability and effectiveness of endografts placed for trauma are largely unknown.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Gharaie et al performed a retrospective review of 17,579 patients who underwent chest CT angiography as part of their blunt trauma admission workup, of whom 113 were found to have MAIs. Of these patients, 93% had at least one follow-up scan. Forty-three percent of these showed resolution on their first follow-up scan at a median of 2 days. By the third follow-up scan at a median of 28 days, 64% of the patients' injuries had resolved. By the final scan (median 261 days), 69% of injuries had completely resolved, 11% had improved, and 20% were stable.

Jinadasa et al reported on the natural history of 83 patients with grade 1 BTAI and follow-up imaging. Seventy-eight percent of these patients had improvement or resolution demonstrated by median day 5. Seven patients showed worsening that was demonstrated on median day 3, six of whom went on to show resolution or improvement, with the last undergoing TEVAR that would not have been performed under current practice patterns.

Among patients who undergo TEVAR for BTAI, evidence is sparse but encouraging. Spiliotopoulos et al investigated the optimal follow-up imaging schedule based on 76 patients who underwent TEVAR for BTAI over 11 years, all but six of whom underwent follow-up imaging. No differences were seen in survival, graft-related complications, or need for reintervention between those patients who were first reimaged at 12 to 18 months and those who had their first follow-up scans after 18 months.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

(5a) In all patients with grade 1 BTAI treated nonoperatively, we suggest against routine surveillance imaging. (2C)

(5b) In patients with grade 2 BTAI treated nonoperatively, we suggest at least one follow-up surveillance imaging study. We suggest repeat imaging at 1 to 3 months after injury. (2C)

(5c) In patients who had TEVAR for BTAI, we suggest postoperative surveillance imaging. (2C)

Considering the scarcity of evidence, the committee suggests choosing surveillance schedules based on clinical experience, surgeon judgment, and patient-specific factors, including the device used for repair. The individualized protocol should balance the low risk of longterm device complications compared with other burdens, including the cumulative radiation exposure to the patient.

</div></details>

### PICO question #6: what is the optimal treatment of patients with BTAI with concomitant solid organ injury or traumatic brain injury?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Background and rationale</summary><div class="details-content">

Often, the injured patient has concomitant injuries, each of which is lifethreatening. The challenge of caring for such patients is particularly complex when the optimal treatments for these injuries are in direct conflict with each other. For instance, TBI is often treated with permissive hypertension; in the presence of a BTAI for which relative hypotension is desirable, the physician is ostensibly faced with a critical choice. Moreover, although TEVAR for BTAI may enable permissive hypertension, the procedure itself may carry additional risks, including stroke and reduced cerebral perfusion if LSA coverage is required. In addition, the risks of procedural anticoagulation during TEVAR are not only relevant to concomitant intracranial hemorrhage but any significant SOI or musculoskeletal injury.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

It is estimated that 30% to 50% of patients who have BTAI also have coincident TBI or SOI, and the combination of these injuries is associated with significantly greater mortality. An analysis of data from the Trauma Quality Improvement Program found that 28% of patients with BTAI had simultaneous TBI; these patients predictably exhibited more severe shock, a greater injury severity score, and a worse Glasgow Coma Scale. They were significantly more likely to develop acute respiratory distress syndrome, stroke, and cardiac arrest, and had a higher overall mortality than patients with BTAI alone. This group found that patients with TBI were less likely to undergo TEVAR than patients with BTAI without brain injury. In addition, patients with TBI who did undergo aortic repair had a higher Glasgow Coma Scale and lower injury severity score than patients with BTAI/TBI managed nonoperatively.

Patients with BTAI/TBI undergoing TEVAR were more likely to develop acute renal failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary embolism, and stroke than those managed nonoperatively, yet had a lower incidence of in-hospital cardiac arrest and had a substantial and significant reduction in mortality.

Emergent, rather than urgent, TEVAR in the presence of TBI is often justified by the allowance of a shorter period of anti-impulse control; the sooner TEVAR is performed, the more rapidly the blood pressure may be increased for the treatment of TBI. Indeed, the use of antihypertensive agents has been identified as a risk factor for mortality in TBI.

The optimal timing for TEVAR in the setting of concomitant TBI has been examined by several groups. In one single-center study of 20 patients with combined BTAI/TBI, Zambetti et al identified a possible increase in mortality associated with TEVAR performed before 14 hours. In a subsequent examination of 100 patients with BTAI/TBI from the multicenter ATF Registry, however, Arbabi et al found that emergent vs urgent TEVAR for BTAI in patients with TBI showed no difference in the outcomes of delayed cerebral ischemic events, in hospital mortality, or aortic-related mortality, and found zero incidence of delayed hemorrhagic stroke. In patients with grade 4 BTAI, emergent repair, even in the presence of TBI, is recommended.

Data regarding intraoperative anticoagulation during TEVAR for BTAI in the setting of other injuries are inconclusive. Zambetti et al evaluated TEVAR timing and procedural anticoagulation in patients with TBI and found that universal intraprocedural heparinization was not associated with any change in neurologic function.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

6a. In patients with grade 1 or 2 BTAI (MAI) and concomitant TBI, we suggest against anti-impulse therapy for BTAI. Management of blood pressure should prioritize TBI over BTAI. (2C)

6b. In patients with grade 3 BTAI and concomitant TBI, we suggest that the decision to use anti-impulse therapy should be individualized based on collaborative management with other surgical and medical specialties. (2C)

6c. In patients with grade 3 BTAI and concomitant TBI or SOl, we suggest that timing for TEVAR should be determined in a collaborative fashion in consultation with other stakeholders, including trauma and neurosurgery. (2C)

6d. In patients with grade 4 BTAI, we recommend emergent repair. (1C)

6e. For patients with BTAI and concomitant TBI or SOl, we suggest a collaborative approach for intraprocedural anticoagulation during TEVAR. Although current observational studies do provide some reassurance that heparinization is not associated with worse intracranial bleeding or neurologic outcomes, the certainty in this evidence is very low. Decisions about intraoperative anticoagulation in patients with TBI should be individualized based on multidisciplinary collaboration. (best practice statement)

### PICO question #7: what is the role of anti-impulse therapy in the nonoperative management of BTAI?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Background and rationale</summary><div class="details-content">

Anti-impulse therapy has been advocated in a variety of acute aortic pathologies, including BTAI, to decrease shear wall stress on the compromised aortic architecture and mitigate against injury progression. The therapeutic foundation of anti-impulse approaches consists of antihypertensive and negative inotropic therapy and, when needed, supplementation with a vasodilator.

The effective use of anti-impulse therapy strategies in some trauma patients may, however, be problematic. Specifically, associated brain and spinal cord injuries have been shown to benefit from pharmacologic elevation of blood pressure to optimize perfusion of recovering neurologic tissues. Such a conflict of potential strategies between BTAI and neurologic injuries in the same patient requires multidisciplinary collaboration to resolve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Data examining the optimal use of anti-impulse therapy are lacking. In the largest pertinent review reporting practice and outcomes from the ATF Registry, continuous titratable infusion of antihypertensive medication was used in 49.1% of patients with BTAI, with β-blockers proving the most used class of medications (74.6%). In this study, the most often cited target objective was a systolic blood pressure goal of < 120 mm Hg. This was only documented as achieved, however, in 64% of patients with BTAI.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recommendation</summary><div class="details-content">

7. In patients with grade 3 BTAI, we suggest anti-impulse therapy as a stabilizing measure until TEVAR is performed if the concomitant injuries do not preclude this approach. (2C)

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Limitations</summary><div class="details-content">

This guideline represents the diligent work of our methodology team and a committee of dedicated SVS expert volunteers and staff. As outlined in the disclaimer at the beginning of this document, the recommendations included herein are based on a diligent review of the available body of evidence. The applicability of these guidelines is subject to reassessment and revision as new data emerge and highlights a clear need for ongoing research into a host of variables pertinent to optimal care of patients sustaining BTAI. Individualized consideration of the specific patient and the clinical environment by capable clinicians will continue to be of critical importance in advancing the care of BTAI.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## CONCLUSIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

The understanding of BTAI and its optimal management, both medical and surgical, continues to evolve as data accumulate and as endovascular technology advances. Moreover, the presence and nature of associated injuries can substantially impact treatment strategies. Although this contextual variability precludes comprehensive evidence that applies to all possible presentations, sufficient data exist to support the above recommendations and help guide the care teams who treat patients with BTAI.

</div></details>
