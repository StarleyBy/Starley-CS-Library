## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Paradigm Shifts and Top 10 Take-Home Messages of the 2025 Guidelines
The 2025 ACC/AHA/ACEP/NAEMSP/SCAI Guideline for the Management of Patients With Acute Coronary Syndromes provides a contemporary, comprehensive, and evidence-based synthesis of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for patients presenting across the entire spectrum of acute coronary syndromes (STEMI, NSTEMI, and unstable angina):

1. **Streamlined Systems of Care & Prehospital Triage:** A 12-lead ECG must be acquired and interpreted within 10 minutes of first medical contact (FMC). In suspected STEMI, immediate emergency medical services (EMS) routing directly to a PCI-capable center with advance hospital notification is recommended to achieve an FMC-to-first-device activation time $\le 90	ext{ minutes}$.
2. **Potent P2Y12 Inhibitors Preferred:** Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and an oral P2Y12 inhibitor is recommended for all ACS patients. In patients undergoing PCI who do not require chronic oral anticoagulation and are not at high bleeding risk, potent P2Y12 inhibitors (**prasugrel** $60	ext{ mg}$ load / $10	ext{ mg}$ daily or **ticagrelor** $180	ext{ mg}$ load / $90	ext{ mg}$ twice daily) are recommended in preference to clopidogrel.
3. **Universal High-Intensity Statin & Early Non-Statin Intensification:** High-intensity statin therapy (atorvastatin $80	ext{ mg}$ or rosuvastatin $40	ext{ mg}$) is recommended in all ACS patients regardless of baseline LDL-C. In patients who do not achieve an LDL-C target $< 55	ext{ mg/dL}$ ($< 1.4	ext{ mmol/L}$) or $\ge 50\%$ reduction from baseline, early initiation of nonstatin therapies (**ezetimibe** and **PCSK9 inhibitors**) during hospitalization or within 4–12 weeks postdischarge is recommended.
4. **Radial-First Revascularization:** Radial artery access is a definitive Class 1 recommendation for all diagnostic coronary angiography and PCI procedures in both STEMI and NSTE-ACS to minimize access-site bleeding, vascular complications, and all-cause mortality.
5. **Complete Revascularization in Multivessel Disease:** In hemodynamically stable patients with STEMI or NSTE-ACS and multivessel coronary artery disease, complete revascularization (staged or index-procedure treatment of nonculprit angiographically significant lesions) is recommended over culprit-only revascularization to reduce cardiovascular death and recurrent myocardial infarction (supported by COMPLETE and FIRE trials).
6. **Risk-Tailored Invasive Strategy in NSTE-ACS:** Routine invasive coronary angiography within 24 hours of admission is recommended for intermediate-to-high risk NSTE-ACS. Immediate coronary angiography ($< 2	ext{ hours}$) is indicated for very high-risk criteria: hemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, recurrent refractory chest pain, sustained ventricular arrhythmias, or mechanical complications.
7. **Cardiogenic Shock & Mechanical Circulatory Support:** Revascularization in ACS-associated cardiogenic shock should be restricted to the **culprit lesion only** during the acute phase (CULPRIT-SHOCK trial). In selected STEMI patients presenting with cardiogenic shock, routine use of a **microaxial flow pump (Impella)** is recommended based on the randomized **DanGer Shock trial** demonstrating a significant reduction in 180-day all-cause mortality (with careful vigilance for major bleeding and limb ischemia). Routine use of intra-aortic balloon counterpulsation (IABP) is not recommended.
8. **Individualized DAPT De-escalation & Shortened DAPT:** While standard DAPT remains 12 months, personalized strategies are endorsed:
   * In patients with **High Bleeding Risk (HBR)** (ARC-HBR criteria), DAPT should be shortened to **1 to 3 months**, followed by single antiplatelet therapy (P2Y12 inhibitor or aspirin monotherapy).
   * In non-HBR patients, early de-escalation from prasugrel/ticagrelor to clopidogrel or transitioning to **P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy (ticagrelor)** after 1–3 months reduces major bleeding while maintaining ischemic protection.
9. **Double Therapy in Anticoagulated Patients (AF + PCI):** In ACS patients requiring long-term oral anticoagulation (OAC) undergoing PCI, triple therapy (DOAC + Aspirin + Clopidogrel) is strictly minimized to the peri-PCI in-hospital period (up to 1–4 weeks), followed immediately by **double antithrombotic therapy (DOAC + Clopidogrel $75	ext{ mg}$ once daily)** for up to 12 months, then lifelong DOAC monotherapy. Prasugrel and ticagrelor are contraindicated as part of triple therapy.
10. **Comprehensive Secondary Prevention Pharmacotherapy:** Routine prescription of evidence-based beta-blockers (for LVEF $\le 40\%$), RAAS inhibitors, SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists in diabetic/HF patients, low-dose **colchicine** ($0.5	ext{ mg/day}$) for residual inflammatory risk reduction, automatic referral to structured **cardiac rehabilitation**, and annual immunization (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19).

## 1. Introduction</summary><div class="details-content">

### 1.1 Methodology and Evidence Review
Clinical practice guidelines provide evidence-based recommendations to assist healthcare professionals in the diagnosis and management of patients with cardiovascular diseases. This 2025 guideline was commissioned by the American College of Cardiology (ACC) and American Heart Association (AHA) Joint Committee on Clinical Practice Guidelines, developed in direct collaboration with and endorsed by the American College of Emergency Physicians (ACEP), the National Association of EMS Physicians (NAEMSP), and the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI).

The Writing Committee performed comprehensive, systematic literature searches across MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, and the Cochrane Library through 2024. Evidence synthesis strictly prioritized prospective randomized controlled trials, well-designed observational registries, and systematic meta-analyses. Strict conflict-of-interest policies were maintained, with members recusing themselves from deliberations and voting on topic sections to which commercial relationships could apply.

### 1.2 Scope of the Guideline and Health Equity
The scope of this document encompasses the entire continuum of Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS) in adults, including:
* ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)
* Non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI)
* Unstable angina (UA)
* Myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA)
* Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD)
* Complications of myocardial infarction, in-hospital care pathways, and transition to long-term secondary prevention.

The guideline explicitly underscores the vital principle of **health equity**. Racial and ethnic minority populations, women, individuals of lower socioeconomic status, and rural residents experience documented disparities in time-to-presentation, access to primary PCI, utilization of potent antiplatelet therapies, and referral to cardiac rehabilitation. Standardized institutional clinical protocols, regionalized transfer networks, and implicit bias education are recommended to eliminate these pervasive inequities.

## 2. Overview of ACS</summary><div class="details-content">

### 2.1 Pathobiology of Type 1 Myocardial Infarction Due to Atherosclerosis
Acute coronary syndromes most commonly result from the acute disruption of an unstable, lipid-rich atherosclerotic plaque in an epicardial coronary artery. 

Plaque disruption occurs primarily through two distinct mechanisms:
1. **Plaque Rupture:** Typically characterized by a thin fibrous cap ($< 65\ \mu	ext{m}$), extensive macrophage infiltration, apoptosis, and a large necrotic lipid core. Disruption exposes thrombogenic subendothelial core contents (tissue factor, collagen) directly to circulating blood.
2. **Plaque Erosion:** Characterized by an intact fibrous cap with denudation of overlying endothelial cells, enriched with proteoglycans and hyaluronan, and associated with neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs).

Exposure of thrombogenic matrix triggers rapid platelet adhesion via glycoprotein receptors (GP Ib/IX/V, GP VI), platelet activation with degranulation and release of thromboxane A2 and ADP, and conformational activation of GP IIb/IIIa receptors, mediating platelet cross-linking via fibrinogen. Simultaneously, tissue factor activates the extrinsic coagulation cascade, generating a thrombin burst that converts fibrinogen into a cross-linked fibrin clot. When the thrombus is totally occlusive, acute transmural ischemia and STEMI typically result. When thrombus is partially occlusive, dynamic, or complicated by distal microembolization, NSTEMI or unstable angina develops.

## 3. Initial Evaluation and Management of Patients With Suspected or Confirmed ACS</summary><div class="details-content">

### 3.1 Clinical Assessment, Prehospital Triage, and Initial In-Hospital Care
Early identification of ACS is vital, as myocardial salvage is strictly time-dependent ("time is muscle"). Patients experiencing ischemic symptoms (retrosternal pressure, squeezing, fullness, radiation to neck, jaw, shoulders, or arms, diaphoresis, dyspnea, nausea, or syncope) should immediately activate 911/EMS rather than driving to the hospital.

Emergency Medical Services (EMS) personnel play an indispensable role in the survival chain by obtaining a prehospital 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of first medical contact (FMC), initiating telemetric transmission to the receiving facility, activating the cardiac catheterization team directly from the field, and bypassing non-PCI hospitals for patients with suspected STEMI.

### 3.2 Analgesia and Oxygen Therapy
* **Supplemental Oxygen:** Routine administration of supplemental oxygen in non-hypoxemic patients ($SaO_2 \ge 90\%$) is **not recommended** (Class 3: No Benefit). Hyperoxia induces coronary vasoconstriction, enhances free radical generation, and increases infarct size and myocardial injury. Supplemental oxygen is indicated exclusively for patients with documented arterial hypoxemia ($SaO_2 < 90\%$, $PaO_2 < 60	ext{ mmHg}$) or respiratory distress.
* **Nitrates:** Sublingual nitroglycerin ($0.4	ext{ mg}$ every 5 minutes up to 3 doses) is reasonable for ongoing ischemic chest discomfort. Intravenous nitroglycerin is indicated for recurrent ischemia, uncontrolled hypertension, or acute heart failure. *Strict Contraindication:* Nitrates must **never** be administered to patients who have taken phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors (sildenafil/vardenafil within 24 hours, tadalafil within 48 hours) due to life-threatening refractory hypotension, or in severe aortic stenosis or right ventricular infarction.
* **Opioids (Morphine):** Intravenous morphine sulfate should be reserved for refractory ischemic chest pain unresponsive to anti-ischemic medications. Routine administration should be approached with caution, as morphine delays gastric emptying, impairs absorption, and blunts peak antiplatelet effects of oral P2Y12 inhibitors.
* **NSAIDs:** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (except aspirin) should be withheld and discontinued during acute hospitalization due to increased risks of myocardial rupture, recurrent infarction, heart failure, and mortality.

## 4. Standard Medical Therapies for STEMI and NSTE-ACS</summary><div class="details-content">

### 4.1 Aspirin
Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) remains the fundamental antiplatelet foundation in ACS management. By irreversibly acetylating platelet cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), aspirin inhibits the synthesis of thromboxane A2 for the lifespan of the platelet:
* **Initial Loading Dose:** A non–enteric-coated chewable aspirin loading dose of **$162\text{ to }325\text{ mg}$** is recommended as soon as possible after presentation in all patients with confirmed or suspected ACS (Class 1, Level of Evidence A). If oral ingestion is not feasible, intravenous aspirin ($250\text{--}500\text{ mg}$) or rectal administration ($300\text{--}600\text{ mg}$) is reasonable.
* **Maintenance Dose:** Lifelong low-dose aspirin (**$81\text{ mg}$ once daily**) is recommended. High maintenance doses ($> 100\text{ mg/day}$) confer no additional ischemic protection but significantly increase gastrointestinal bleeding risks.

### 4.2 Oral P2Y12 Inhibitors During Hospitalization
Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) combining aspirin with an oral P2Y12 receptor inhibitor is indicated across all ACS clinical pathways. The choice of P2Y12 inhibitor depends on the planned revascularization strategy, bleeding risk, and need for chronic oral anticoagulation:
* **Potent P2Y12 Inhibitors Preferred:** In patients with ACS undergoing PCI who are not at high bleeding risk and do not require chronic oral anticoagulation, potent P2Y12 inhibitors—**prasugrel** ($60\text{ mg}$ loading dose, followed by $10\text{ mg}$ once daily) or **ticagrelor** ($180\text{ mg}$ loading dose, followed by $90\text{ mg}$ twice daily)—are recommended in preference to clopidogrel (Class 1, Level B-R), driven by TRITON-TIMI 38, PLATO, and ISAR-REACT 5 trials demonstrating superior ischemic event reduction and stent thrombosis suppression.
* **Pretreatment Considerations in NSTE-ACS:** In patients with NSTE-ACS scheduled for an early invasive strategy, routine unselected pre-treatment with prasugrel before diagnostic coronary angiography is **not recommended** (Class 3: Harm), as it increases bleeding without ischemic benefit (ACCOAST trial) and complicates urgent CABG. Pre-treatment with ticagrelor or clopidogrel may be considered when an invasive strategy is delayed $> 24\text{ hours}$.
* **Clopidogrel Indications:** Clopidogrel ($300\text{--}600\text{ mg}$ load, $75\text{ mg}$ daily) is recommended in patients receiving fibrinolytic therapy, those with high bleeding risk (HBR), prior stroke/TIA (contraindication to prasugrel), or those requiring concomitant oral anticoagulation.

### 4.4 Parenteral Anticoagulation
Parenteral anticoagulation is indicated for all ACS patients during acute management:
* **Primary PCI for STEMI:** Unfractionated heparin (UFH) ($70\text{--}100\text{ units/kg}$ IV bolus targeting ACT $250\text{--}300\text{ s}$) or Bivalirudin ($0.75\text{ mg/kg}$ bolus, $1.75\text{ mg/kg/h}$ infusion with post-PCI full-dose infusion for 2–4 hours) is recommended.

### 4.5 Lipid Management
Atherosclerotic cardiovascular plaque stabilization requires aggressive, early lipid lowering:
* **Universal High-Intensity Statin:** High-intensity statin therapy (atorvastatin $80\text{ mg}$ daily or rosuvastatin $40\text{ mg}$ daily) is recommended in all patients presenting with ACS, irrespective of baseline LDL-C levels (Class 1, Level A).

### 4.6 Beta-Blocker Therapy
Oral beta-blocker therapy (metoprolol succinate, carvedilol, or bisoprolol) should be initiated within the first 24 hours in patients without acute decompensated heart failure, signs of hypoperfusion, bradycardia, or high-degree AV block. 
* In patients with left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF $\le 40\%$), long-term beta-blocker therapy is recommended (Class 1, Level A) to reduce mortality. 
* In patients with preserved ejection fraction (LVEF $> 50\%$) and complete revascularization, recent randomized trials (REDUCE-AMI) suggest that long-term beta-blocker therapy beyond 1 year may not confer a mortality benefit, and reassessment is appropriate.

## 6. NSTE-ACS: Routine Invasive or Selective Invasive Initial Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

### 6.1 Rationale and Timing for a Routine Invasive or Selective Invasive Approach
Management of non–ST-segment elevation acute coronary syndromes (NSTE-ACS) requires risk-directed decision-making regarding diagnostic coronary angiography and subsequent revascularization:
* **Routine Invasive Strategy:** In patients with confirmed NSTE-ACS who are at intermediate or high risk of death or ischemic events, an initial routine invasive approach (diagnostic catheterization with intent to revascularize) is recommended (Class 1, Level of Evidence A) to reduce refractory angina, recurrent hospitalizations, and long-term ischemic complications.
* **Immediate Invasive Strategy ($< 2\text{ hours}$):** Urgent coronary angiography is recommended for patients presenting with:
  1. Refractory or recurrent angina despite intensive medical therapy;
  2. Hemodynamic instability or cardiogenic shock;
  3. Life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias (sustained VT or VF);
  4. Dynamic new ST-segment depression or transient ST-segment elevation;
  5. Mechanical complications or acute acute heart failure with new or worsening mitral regurgitation.
* **Early Invasive Strategy ($< 24\text{ hours}$):** An early invasive approach within 24 hours of hospital presentation is reasonable (Class 2a, Level A) in stable high-risk patients, defined by:
  - Elevated cardiac troponin temporal rise/fall;
  - GRACE risk score $> 140$;
  - Dynamic ST-segment or T-wave ischemic changes.
* **Selective Invasive (Ischemia-Guided) Strategy:** In low-risk patients (e.g., GRACE risk score $< 109$, TIMI risk score $0\text{ to }1$, normal serial troponins and ECGs), or those with severe noncardiac comorbidities where revascularization would not improve functional status or survival, noninvasive functional or anatomical testing (CCTA, stress imaging) is recommended before deciding on catheterization.
* **Class 3 (Harm):** A routine invasive strategy is **not recommended** in patients with extensive comorbidities (e.g., advanced hepatic, renal, or pulmonary failure, end-stage malignancy) where procedural morbidity and bleeding hazards clearly outweigh any potential ischemic benefit.

## 7. Catheterization Laboratory Considerations in ACS</summary><div class="details-content">

### 7.1 Vascular Access Approach for PCI
* **Radial Artery Access Preferred:** In patients with ACS undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), **radial artery access** is recommended in preference to femoral artery access (Class 1, Level of Evidence A). Landmark trials (RIVAL, MATRIX) and large meta-analyses demonstrate that transradial intervention significantly reduces access-site bleeding, vascular complications, blood transfusion requirements, and all-cause mortality, particularly in STEMI and high-risk NSTE-ACS cohorts.
* Femoral access should be reserved for cases with anatomical radial artery failure, extreme tortuosity, severe radial spasm, or when large-bore mechanical circulatory support devices (e.g., Impella, ECMO) are concurrently required.

### 7.2 Use of Aspiration Thrombectomy
* **Routine Use Harmful / No Benefit:** Routine manual aspiration thrombectomy prior to primary PCI in STEMI is **not recommended** (Class 3: No Benefit, Level of Evidence A). Large-scale prospective randomized clinical trials (TOTAL [$n=10{,}732$] and TASTE [$n=7{,}244$]) demonstrated that routine aspiration thrombectomy did not reduce cardiovascular mortality, recurrent MI, or stent thrombosis, but was associated with a statistically significant increase in the risk of stroke or transient ischemic attack (TOTAL trial).
* **Selective / Bailout Thrombectomy:** Aspiration thrombectomy may be considered in carefully selected cases only as a bailout maneuver for massive residual intracoronary thrombus burden or refractory no-reflow following stent deployment/balloon dilation (Class 2b, Level C-LD).

### 7.3 Use of Intracoronary Imaging
Intravascular imaging modalities—including **optical coherence tomography (OCT)** and **intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)**—have transformed procedural precision and diagnostic clarity in ACS:
* **Procedural Guidance:** In patients with ACS undergoing coronary stent implantation, procedural guidance using IVUS or OCT is recommended (Class 2a, Level of Evidence A). Imaging guidance ensures accurate reference vessel sizing, optimal stent expansion, identification of malapposition, and detection of edge dissections, resulting in significantly lower rates of target vessel failure, stent thrombosis, and cardiac death (supported by RENOVATE-COMPLEX-PCI, OCTOBER, and ILUMIEN IV trials).
* **Diagnostic Clarification:** Intracoronary imaging is invaluable for defining underlying culprit lesion morphology—such as distinguishing plaque rupture from plaque erosion, identifying calcified nodules, or diagnosing spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) and myocardial infarction with nonobstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA).

### 7.4 Management of Multivessel CAD in ACS
Multivessel coronary artery disease (MVD) is present in approximately 40% to 50% of STEMI and 50% to 60% of NSTE-ACS patients:

#### 7.4.1 Multivessel CAD in STEMI
* **Hemodynamically Stable Patients:** In selected, hemodynamically stable patients with STEMI and multivessel disease, **complete revascularization** of non-infarct-related (non-culprit) arteries with PCI is recommended (Class 1, Level of Evidence A). Staged PCI of non-culprit lesions may be performed during the index hospitalization or within 45 days after discharge, as demonstrated by the COMPLETE trial ($n=4{,}041$), which showed a significant 26% reduction in cardiovascular death or new myocardial infarction ($7.8\%\text{ vs }10.5\%$, $P=0.004$).
* **Timing of Non-Culprit PCI:** Non-culprit PCI may be performed at the time of primary PCI or as a staged procedure during index hospitalization (Class 2a, Level B-R).
* **Complex Anatomy / CABG:** In patients with STEMI and complex multivessel disease (e.g., severe unprotected left main disease, high SYNTAX score) not amenable to PCI, urgent coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery is indicated after Heart Team evaluation (Class 2a, Level B-NR).
* **Cardiogenic Shock (Class 3: Harm):** In patients with STEMI complicated by cardiogenic shock, routine immediate PCI of non-culprit arteries during primary PCI is **not recommended** (Class 3: Harm, Level B-R), driven by the CULPRIT-SHOCK trial which demonstrated higher 30-day mortality and renal replacement therapy with immediate multivessel PCI compared to culprit-only PCI ($54.7\%\text{ vs }45.9\%$, $P=0.01$).

## 8. Cardiogenic Shock Management</summary><div class="details-content">

### 8.1 Revascularization in ACS With Cardiogenic Shock
Cardiogenic shock complicates 5% to 10% of ACS cases and remains the leading cause of in-hospital death:
* **Emergency Culprit Revascularization:** In patients with ACS complicated by cardiogenic shock or hemodynamic instability, emergency revascularization of the culprit coronary artery (via primary PCI or emergency CABG) is strongly recommended irrespective of the time delay from symptom onset (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-R). The landmark SHOCK trial demonstrated that early emergency revascularization yields significant long-term survival advantages (6-year survival 32.8% vs 19.6%).
* **Culprit-Only Strategy during Acute Phase:** In patients presenting with ACS and cardiogenic shock, routine immediate PCI of non-culprit arteries is **harmful** and **not recommended** (Class 3: Harm, Level B-R). In the CULPRIT-SHOCK trial ($n=706$), immediate multivessel PCI significantly increased 30-day all-cause mortality and renal failure requiring dialysis compared to culprit-lesion-only PCI. Non-culprit revascularization should only be performed in staged fashion once shock has resolved, or acutely if an identifiable second vessel displays critical TIMI flow 0/1 with ongoing extensive ischemia.

|---|:---:|:---:|

### 8.2 Mechanical Circulatory Support (MCS) in ACS and Cardiogenic Shock
The landscape of temporary mechanical circulatory support (tMCS) has evolved with paradigm-shifting clinical trial data:
* **Microaxial Left Ventricular Flow Pump (Impella CP):** In carefully selected patients with STEMI-related cardiogenic shock (SCAI stage C, D, or E), routine implantation of a microaxial flow pump is reasonable to reduce 180-day all-cause mortality (Class 2b, Level of Evidence B-R). The landmark DanGer Shock trial ($n=355$) demonstrated that routine microaxial flow pump placement reduced 180-day mortality from 58.5% to 45.8% (hazard ratio 0.74, 95% CI 0.55–0.99; $P=0.04$). However, this survival advantage is accompanied by significantly higher risks of severe bleeding, limb ischemia, vascular injury, and renal replacement therapy, emphasizing the need for meticulous vascular access, ultrasound guidance, and institutional shock team protocols.
* **Bridge to Surgery for Mechanical Complications:** In patients with mechanical complications of ACS (acute ventricular septal rupture or severe papillary muscle rupture with acute mitral regurgitation), temporary MCS (e.g., intra-aortic balloon pump [IABP], Impella, or venoarterial ECMO) is reasonable as a hemodynamic bridge to emergency surgery (Class 2a, Level B-NR).
* **Routine IABP (Class 3: No Benefit):** In patients with acute myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock *without* mechanical complications, routine placement of an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) is **not recommended** (Class 3: No Benefit, Level of Evidence A). The randomized IABP-SHOCK II trial ($n=600$) established that routine IABP did not improve 30-day, 1-year, or 6-year mortality or hemodynamic parameters compared to standard medical therapy.

## 9. ACS Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

### 9.1 Mechanical Complications of Myocardial Infarction
Mechanical complications occur in $< 1\%$ of reperfused ACS cases but carry catastrophic mortality ($> 40\text{--}80\%$ without surgical intervention). They typically develop within the first 1 to 14 days post-MI:
* **Clinical Presentation:** Acute hemodynamic collapse, sudden pulmonary edema, new harsh holosystolic murmur (ventricular septal rupture or acute mitral regurgitation), or electromechanical dissociation / cardiac tamponade (free wall rupture).
* **Emergency Multidisciplinary Care:** Patients with mechanical complications must immediately be evaluated by an interdisciplinary Heart Team (cardiac surgery, interventional cardiology, advanced heart failure, and critical care) and transferred to a high-volume surgical center (Class 1, Level B-NR).
* **Emergency Echocardiography:** Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) or transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) should be performed immediately to confirm diagnosis, characterize anatomical defect size, assess biventricular function, and evaluate pericardial effusion.
* **Definitive Repair:** Emergency surgical or transcatheter repair is recommended for ventricular septal rupture (VSR) and acute papillary muscle rupture / flail leaflet causing severe mitral regurgitation (Class 1, Level B-NR). Free wall rupture requires immediate emergency pericardial decompression and surgical cardiorrhaphy.
* **Preoperative Hemodynamic Support:** Initiation of temporary mechanical circulatory support (IABP, Impella, or VA-ECMO) is reasonable to achieve afterload reduction, stabilize end-organ perfusion, and bridge the patient to definitive surgery (Class 2a, Level B-NR).

### 9.2 Electrical Complications and Prevention of Sudden Cardiac Death After ACS
Ventricular arrhythmias and conduction system disturbances are frequent acute complications of ACS:
* **Secondary Prevention ICD:** In patients experiencing sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT) or ventricular fibrillation (VF) occurring **$> 48\text{ hours}$** after acute MI, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) therapy is recommended for secondary prevention (Class 1, Level of Evidence A), provided the arrhythmia is not solely attributable to acute transient, reversible myocardial ischemia or reinfarction.
* **Primary Prevention Prophylactic ICD:**
  - Prophylactic ICD implantation is indicated in patients with persistent left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) $\le 35\%$ and NYHA class II or III heart failure symptoms (or LVEF $\le 30\%$ with NYHA class I symptoms) measured at least **$40\text{ days}$** after MI (or at least **$90\text{ days}$** after surgical or percutaneous revascularization) while on optimal guideline-directed medical therapy (Class 1, Level of Evidence A).
  - Routine early prophylactic ICD implantation ($< 40\text{ days}$ post-MI) is **not recommended** (Class 3: No Benefit, Level B-R), as the DINAMIT and IRIS randomized trials demonstrated that while early ICDs reduced arrhythmic deaths, non-arrhythmic mortality was increased, yielding no overall survival advantage.
* **Wearable Cardioverter-Defibrillator (WCD):** In selected patients early after MI with severe LV dysfunction (LVEF $\le 35\%$) or awaiting delayed ICD reassessment, a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator may be considered as a temporary protective bridge (Class 2b, Level B-R; VEST trial).
* **Conduction Disturbances:** High-grade atrioventricular (AV) block or sinus node arrest during acute inferior MI is frequently transient and responds to atropine or temporary pacing. In contrast, new-onset bundle branch block or bifascicular block with anterior MI indicates extensive septal necrosis and warrants close hemodynamic and electrophysiological surveillance.

### 9.3 Pericarditis Management After MI
Pericarditis after myocardial infarction occurs either early (peri-infarction pericarditis, within days of extensive transmural necrosis) or late (Dressler syndrome / post-cardiac injury syndrome, weeks to months post-MI, autoimmune etiology):
* **Diagnostic Criteria:** Diagnosis requires at least 2 of 4 classic criteria: (1) positional chest pain (sharp, pleuritic, improved by sitting up and leaning forward); (2) pericardial friction rub; (3) dynamic ECG changes (widespread ST-segment elevation with PR depression); (4) new or worsening pericardial effusion on echocardiography.
* **First-Line Pharmacotherapy:** High-dose **aspirin** ($650\text{ to }1000\text{ mg}$ orally every 6 to 8 hours) combined with **colchicine** ($0.5\text{ to }0.6\text{ mg}$ once or twice daily for 3 months) is the standard recommended regimen.
* **Avoidance of NSAIDs and Glucocorticoids:** Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., ibuprofen, indomethacin) and systemic corticosteroids should be strictly avoided in the acute peri-infarction window, as experimental and clinical data indicate they impair myocardial scar formation, increase infarct thinning, and elevate the risk of free wall myocardial rupture.

### 9.4 Management of Left Ventricular Thrombus After MI
Left ventricular (LV) mural thrombus develops in up to 15% of patients with large anterior STEMI and apical akinesis/dyskinesis:
* **Detection:** Echocardiography with ultrasound enhancing agents (contrast) or cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging provides high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity.
* **Anticoagulation Regimen:** Therapeutic anticoagulation with an oral **direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC)** (apixaban, rivaroxaban) or **vitamin K antagonist (warfarin)** (target INR $2.0\text{--}3.0$) is recommended for a minimum duration of **3 to 6 months**.
* **DAPT De-escalation:** Concomitant antiplatelet therapy must be minimized to reduce severe bleeding. In patients requiring DAPT after PCI who develop LV thrombus, transition to single antiplatelet therapy (aspirin or clopidogrel) combined with oral anticoagulation is strongly advised after 1 to 4 weeks.
* **Repeat Imaging:** Repeat echocardiography or CMR is recommended prior to discontinuing anticoagulation to verify complete thrombus resolution and recovery of regional apical wall motion.

### 10.1 Cardiac Intensive Care Unit (CICU)
Admission to a specialized Cardiac Intensive Care Unit (CICU) is reserved for patients requiring advanced hemodynamic monitoring, invasive mechanical support, or intensive nursing care:
* **CICU Admission Criteria:** Patients presenting with ongoing refractory angina, hemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, life-threatening sustained ventricular arrhythmias, high-grade heart block, or acute mechanical complications should be admitted directly to a CICU (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **Step-Down Triage:** Clinically stable, uncomplicated patients with successfully reperfused STEMI or NSTE-ACS without recurrent ischemia or arrhythmias can be safely managed in a telemetry step-down unit, optimizing critical care resource utilization.

### 10.2 Management of Anemia in ACS
Anemia is an independent predictor of adverse ischemic and bleeding outcomes in ACS. Myocardial oxygen delivery is critically dependent on both coronary blood flow and hemoglobin concentration:
* **Transfusion Strategy (MINT Trial):** In patients with ACS and acute or chronic anemia, a **restrictive red blood cell transfusion threshold** (transfusion only when hemoglobin drops below **$7\text{ to }8\text{ g/dL}$**) is reasonable compared to a liberal transfusion strategy (Class 2a, Level of Evidence B-R).
* The multicenter MINT trial ($n=3{,}504$) demonstrated that while a liberal transfusion strategy (transfusing for hemoglobin $< 10\text{ g/dL}$) did not statistically significantly reduce 30-day mortality or recurrent MI, point estimates trended toward lower ischemic events without increasing fluid overload or harm. Clinical decision-making should balance ischemic symptoms, hemodynamic stability, and ongoing bleeding when considering transfusion in patients with hemoglobin levels between $8\text{ and }10\text{ g/dL}$.

## 11. Discharge: Long-Term Management and Secondary Prevention</summary><div class="details-content">

### 11.1 DAPT Strategies in the First 12 Months Postdischarge
Balancing ischemic protection against bleeding complications is the central goal of post-ACS antiplatelet therapy:
* **Standard 12-Month DAPT:** In patients with ACS undergoing PCI who are not at high bleeding risk (HBR), DAPT consisting of aspirin combined with a potent oral P2Y12 inhibitor (**prasugrel** or **ticagrelor**) for at least **12 months** is recommended (Class 1, Level of Evidence A).
* **Extended DAPT ($> 12\text{ months}$):** In patients who have tolerated 12 months of DAPT without bleeding and have high ischemic risk features (e.g., prior MI, multivessel CAD, bifurcation stenting, diabetes) combined with low bleeding risk, extended DAPT with low-dose aspirin plus ticagrelor ($60\text{ mg}$ twice daily) or clopidogrel ($75\text{ mg}$ once daily) is reasonable (Class 2b, Level A; PEGASUS-TIMI 54 and DAPT trials).
* **Gastroprotection:** In patients with increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding (history of GI bleed, age $\ge 65$, concomitant anticoagulants or NSAIDs), routine co-prescription of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) is recommended (Class 1, Level A; COGENT trial).
* **DAPT De-Escalation:** In patients undergoing PCI, de-escalation of DAPT (switching from prasugrel/ticagrelor to clopidogrel after 1 to 3 months, or reducing ticagrelor dose) may be considered (Class 2b, Level B-R) based on platelet function testing, CYP2C19 genotype guidance (POPular Genetics, TAILOR-PCI), or an unguided clinical strategy (TOPIC, TALOS-AMI).
* **Shortened DAPT in High Bleeding Risk (HBR):** In patients undergoing PCI who meet Academic Research Consortium High Bleeding Risk (ARC-HBR) criteria:
  - Discontinuing aspirin after **1 to 3 months** and maintaining **P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy** (ticagrelor or clopidogrel) for up to 12 months is recommended (Class 1, Level A; MASTER DAPT, TWILIGHT, TICO).
  - Alternatively, in extreme HBR patients, discontinuing the P2Y12 inhibitor after **1 month** and continuing aspirin monotherapy is reasonable (Class 2a, Level B-R).

### 11.1.1 Antiplatelet Therapy in Patients Requiring Chronic Oral Anticoagulation
Atrial fibrillation or other indications for oral anticoagulation (OAC) occur in 8% to 12% of ACS patients:
* **Double Therapy (DOAC + Clopidogrel):** In patients with ACS requiring long-term oral anticoagulation who undergo PCI, **double therapy** consisting of a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC at standard stroke prevention dosing) and a single oral P2Y12 inhibitor (**clopidogrel $75\text{ mg}$ once daily**) is recommended in preference to triple therapy (Class 1, Level of Evidence A). Four pivotal randomized trials (PIONEER AF-PCI, RE-DUAL PCI, AUGUSTUS, and ENTRUST-AF PCI) demonstrated that dropping aspirin immediately upon discharge reduces major or clinically relevant bleeding by approximately 50% without a statistically significant increase in stent thrombosis or ischemic events.
* **Duration of Triple Therapy:** If triple therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel + DOAC) is used, it should be limited to the in-hospital period or $\le 1\text{ to }4\text{ weeks}$ post-PCI in patients with exceptionally high ischemic/thrombotic risk and acceptable bleeding risk.
* **Class 3 (Harm):** Potent P2Y12 inhibitors (**prasugrel** or **ticagrelor**) should **not be used** as part of triple therapy with an oral anticoagulant due to prohibitive bleeding risks (Class 3: Harm).
* **Cessation of Antiplatelet Therapy at 12 Months:** After 12 months post-PCI, antiplatelet therapy should generally be discontinued, maintaining DOAC monotherapy lifelong (Class 2a, Level B-R; OAC-ALONE, AFIRE trials).

### 11.2 Reassessment of Lipid Levels and Intensification of Lipid-Lowering Therapy
* **Postdischarge Lipid Panel:** In all patients post-ACS, a fasting or nonfasting lipid panel is recommended **4 to 12 weeks** postdischarge to evaluate therapeutic response and medication adherence (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* **Intensification Goals:** The guideline-directed goal is achieving an **LDL-C $< 55\text{ mg/dL}$ ($< 1.4\text{ mmol/L}$)** and a **$\ge 50\%$ reduction** from baseline.
* **Nonstatin Add-on:** If the LDL-C remains $\ge 55\text{ mg/dL}$ despite maximally tolerated high-intensity statin therapy:
  1. Add **ezetimibe** ($10\text{ mg}$ daily) as first-line nonstatin therapy (Class 1, Level B-R);
  2. Add a **PCSK9 inhibitor** (evolocumab or alirocumab) or **inclisiran** for very high-risk patients failing to achieve LDL targets on statin + ezetimibe (Class 1, Level A);
  3. Consider **bempedoic acid** in statin-intolerant patients (CLEAR Outcomes trial).

### 11.3 SGLT-2 Inhibitors and GLP-1 Receptor Agonists
* **SGLT-2 Inhibitors (Empagliflozin, Dapagliflozin):** In patients with ACS and comorbid type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease (eGFR $20\text{--}60\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2$), or heart failure (with reduced or preserved ejection fraction), an SGLT-2 inhibitor is recommended to reduce cardiovascular mortality, heart failure hospitalizations, and renal disease progression (Class 1, Level A; DAPA-HF, EMPEROR-Reduced, DELIVER, EMPEROR-Preserved).
* **GLP-1 Receptor Agonists (Semaglutide, Dulaglutide):** In patients with ACS and type 2 diabetes mellitus or established obesity without diabetes, a GLP-1 receptor agonist with proven cardiovascular benefit is recommended to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (Class 1, Level A; SUSTAIN-6, LEADER, SELECT trial).



### 11.4 Use of Chronic Colchicine
* **Anti-inflammatory Therapy:** In patients post-ACS on optimal guideline-directed medical therapy, low-dose **colchicine** ($0.5\text{ mg}$ once daily) is reasonable to reduce recurrent ischemic events, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and ischemia-driven coronary revascularization (Class 2b, Level of Evidence B-R).
* The COLCOT trial ($n=4{,}745$) demonstrated that colchicine initiated within 30 days of MI reduced the composite primary ischemic endpoint by 23% ($5.5\%\text{ vs }7.1\%$, hazard ratio 0.77; $P=0.02$). The LoDoCo2 trial confirmed long-term ischemic event reduction in chronic coronary disease. Colchicine should be used with caution in patients with significant renal impairment or hepatic dysfunction.

### 11.5 Immunization
* **Influenza Vaccination:** In patients with ACS without contraindications, annual **influenza vaccination** is recommended prior to hospital discharge or at the earliest ambulatory encounter (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-R).
* The randomized IAMI trial ($n=2{,}571$) and FLUVACS trial demonstrated that early in-hospital influenza vaccination after acute MI or high-risk CAD reduced the composite of all-cause death, MI, or stent thrombosis at 12 months (hazard ratio 0.72; $P=0.014$).
* Routine vaccination against **Streptococcus pneumoniae**, **respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)**, and **COVID-19** is also strongly recommended according to national immunization advisory guidelines.

## 12. Evidence Gaps and Future Directions</summary><div class="details-content">

Despite transformative advancements in early reperfusion, mechanical circulatory support, and secondary prevention pharmacotherapy, several critical clinical questions remain unresolved:
1. **Mechanical Circulatory Support Timing and Selection:** While DanGer Shock demonstrated 180-day survival benefits with routine Impella CP in STEMI cardiogenic shock, vascular and bleeding complications remain substantial. Refined clinical and hemodynamic algorithms (e.g., SCAI shock stages, pulmonary artery pulsatility index, invasive cardiac power output) are needed to identify patients who derive maximal survival advantage while minimizing devicerelated morbidities.
2. **Novel Antithrombotic Strategies:** Next-generation anticoagulants targeting factor XI and XIa (e.g., asundexian, milvexian) hold potential to uncouple antithrombotic efficacy from bleeding risk, potentially revolutionizing combination therapy with antiplatelet agents post-ACS.
3. **Plaque Erosion and Personalized Revascularization:** Intravascular imaging (OCT) has revealed that up to one-third of ACS cases are caused by plaque erosion rather than plaque rupture. Ongoing trials are investigating whether non-stent medical stabilization (effective DAPT and aggressive lipid-lowering) can safely avoid permanent metallic stenting in selected patients with plaque erosion.
4. **Myocardial Infarction With Nonobstructive Coronary Arteries (MINOCA):** High-resolution multimodal imaging (OCT, CMR) is uncovering distinct etiologies (coronary spasm, SCAD, microvascular dysfunction, myocarditis, takotsubo cardiomyopathy). Targeted clinical trials are urgently needed to guide disease-specific pharmacotherapies.
5. **Anti-Inflammatory Therapies Beyond Colchicine:** Interleukin-1$\beta$ and interleukin-6 inhibitors (e.g., ziltivekimab) are currently being evaluated in clinical trials to determine whether targeted cytokine suppression provides superior cardiovascular protection compared to non-targeted anti-inflammatory agents.
6. **Cardioprotection and Myocardial Regeneration:** Novel molecular agents, sodium-glucose cotransporter inhibitors, and cell/gene-based therapeutic platforms aiming to limit reperfusion injury, reduce microvascular obstruction, and promote functional scar healing require further validation.
