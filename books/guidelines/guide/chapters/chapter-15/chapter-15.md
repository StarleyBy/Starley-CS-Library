## Executive Summary: Top 10 Take-Home Messages</summary><div class="details-content">

1. **Multidisciplinary Aortic Teams:** Clinical outcomes for patients with complex thoracic and abdominal aortic disease are significantly enhanced at high-volume specialized aortic centers with multidisciplinary aortic teams (cardiac surgeons, vascular surgeons, interventional cardiologists/radiologists, imaging specialists, anesthesiologists, and geneticists) who determine the timing and modality of intervention.
2. **Shared Decision-Making:** Clinicians and patients should engage in collaborative shared decision-making to determine optimal medical, endovascular, and open surgical strategies, particularly regarding diameter thresholds for elective repair, athletic participation, and maternal-fetal risks before and during pregnancy.
3. **Standardized Aortic Imaging and Reporting:** Contrast-enhanced CT angiography (CTA), MR angiography (MRA), and echocardiography must adhere to rigorous acquisition protocols with orthogonal true-short-axis diameter measurements perpendicular to the blood flow centerline, systematic reporting of landing zones, and protocolized long-term post-repair surveillance.
4. **Lowered Surgical Threshold for Root and Ascending Aneurysms:** At centers with Multidisciplinary Aortic Teams and documented low operative mortality ($< 1\%$), the surgical threshold for elective repair of sporadic aortic root and ascending aortic aneurysms has been lowered from $5.5\text{ cm}$ to **$5.0\text{ cm}$** in selected low-risk patients, and even lower ($4.0\text{ to }4.5\text{ cm}$) in specific heritable thoracic aortic diseases (Loeys-Dietz, Marfan).
5. **Revised Surgical Threshold for Bicuspid Aortic Valve (BAV) Aortopathy:** In patients with BAV and ascending aortic dilation, the threshold for prophylactic surgery without high-risk features has been updated to **$5.0\text{ cm}$** at experienced multidisciplinary aortic centers ($5.5\text{ cm}$ at non-specialized centers).
6. **Routine Genetic Evaluation for Heritable Thoracic Aortic Disease (HTAD):** In patients with thoracic aortic aneurysm or dissection presenting at age $< 60$ years, or with a family history of aortic disease, genetic counseling and comprehensive multi-gene panel testing are recommended to guide tailored surgical thresholds and initiate cascade family screening.
7. **Refined Indications for TEVAR in Uncomplicated Acute Type B Dissection:** In uncomplicated acute Type B aortic dissection (TBAD), thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) should be considered in patients with high-risk clinical or radiological features (refractory pain, recurrent hypertension, false lumen diameter $\ge 22\text{ mm}$, entry tear size $\ge 10\text{ mm}$, or aortic diameter $\ge 40\text{ mm}$) to promote false lumen thrombosis and prevent late aneurysmal degeneration.
8. **Emergency Endovascular Repair for Complicated Acute Type B AAS:** TEVAR is the first-line intervention of choice for acute complicated Type B aortic dissection (malperfusion syndrome, rupture, rapid expansion) and for acute Type B intramural hematoma (IMH) or penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer (PAU) with refractory pain or rupture.
9. **Conservative Management for Low-Grade Blunt Traumatic Injury:** Grade I (intimal tear) blunt traumatic thoracic aortic injuries (BTTAI) should be managed conservatively with serial imaging and blood pressure control; Grade II to IV injuries require prompt TEVAR.
10. **Updated Screening and Surveillance for Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA):** Ultrasound screening is recommended for men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked, and reasonable for first-degree relatives of patients with AAA. Elective repair is indicated at $\ge 5.5\text{ cm}$ in men or $\ge 5.0\text{ cm}$ in women, or for rapid expansion $\ge 0.5\text{ cm}$ in 6 months.

## 2. Normal Anatomy, Landing Zones, and Definitions</summary><div class="details-content">

### 2.1 Normal Aortic Anatomy and Histology
The aorta is the primary conduit delivering oxygenated blood from the left ventricle to the systemic circulation. It is anatomically divided into five segments: aortic root (annulus, sinuses of Valsalva, sinotubular junction), ascending aorta, aortic arch, descending thoracic aorta, and abdominal aorta:

![Figure 1: Anatomy of the Aorta and Its Main Branches](images/fig01_anatomy_aorta_branches.jpg)

![Figure 2: Key Histologic Features of the Aortic Wall](images/fig02_histologic_features_aorta.jpg)

### 2.2 Aortic Landing Zones (Zones 0 Through 10)
Standardized reporting of endovascular repair utilizes an 11-zone classification (Zones 0 to 10) defined by major branch vessels:
* **Zone 0:** Ascending aorta (proximal to brachiocephalic trunk).
* **Zone 1:** Between brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries.
* **Zone 2:** Between left common carotid and left subclavian arteries.
* **Zone 3:** Proximal descending thoracic aorta (first $2\text{ cm}$ distal to left subclavian artery).
* **Zone 4:** Mid-descending thoracic aorta.
* **Zone 5:** Distal descending thoracic aorta (above celiac axis).
* **Zone 6:** Visceral segment (celiac artery to superior mesenteric artery).
* **Zone 7:** Renal artery segment (SMA to lowest renal artery).
* **Zone 8:** Infrarenal aorta (lowest renal artery to aortic bifurcation).
* **Zone 9:** Common iliac arteries.
* **Zone 10:** External iliac arteries.

![Figure 3: Classification of Aortic Anatomic Segments by 11 Landing Zones](images/fig03_eleven_landing_zones.jpg)

### 2.3 Definitions of Aortic Dilation, Ectasia, and Aneurysm
* **Ectasia:** Mild dilation exceeding normal reference limits for age and body size but $< 50\%$ above normal diameter.
* **Aneurysm:** Permanent localized dilation of the aorta with a diameter **$\ge 50\%$ greater** than the expected normal diameter for that anatomic segment (or $> 1.5\times$ normal reference).
* In the ascending aorta, a diameter $\ge 4.0\text{ cm}$ is considered dilated in adults, and $\ge 5.0\text{ cm}$ constitutes an aneurysm requiring specialist evaluation.
* **Normalizing for Body Size:** Aortic cross-sectional area divided by patient height ($\text{cm}^2/\text{m}$) or Aortic Size Index (ASI: diameter/BSA, $\text{cm/m}^2$) improves risk stratification in patients of extreme stature. An **area-to-height ratio $> 10\text{ cm}^2/\text{m}$** indicates significantly increased rupture/dissection risk.

### 2.4 Definitions and Classification of Acute Aortic Syndromes (AAS)
Acute aortic syndrome describes a spectrum of life-threatening conditions involving the aortic wall:
1. **Acute Aortic Dissection (AAD):** Separation of the aortic wall media triggered by an intimal entry tear, creating a false lumen through which blood flows.
   - **Stanford Classification:**
     - **Type A:** Involves the ascending aorta (with or without extension to arch and descending aorta); requires emergency surgical repair.
     - **Type B:** Involves only the aorta distal to the left subclavian artery (Zone 3+); managed medically or with TEVAR.
     - **Non-A Non-B Dissection:** Dissection involving the aortic arch without ascending aortic involvement.
   - **DeBakey Classification:**
     - **Type I:** Arises in ascending aorta and extends downstream into arch and descending aorta.
     - **Type II:** Confined strictly to the ascending aorta.
     - **Type III:** Originates distal to left subclavian artery (IIIa: thoracic only; IIIb: extends below diaphragm).
2. **Intramural Hematoma (IMH):** Blood accumulation within the aortic media without a detectable intimal tear or flow in a false lumen on imaging.
3. **Penetrating Atherosclerotic Ulcer (PAU):** Ulceration of an atheromatous plaque that burrows through the internal elastic lamina into the aortic media, potentially precipitating IMH, pseudoaneurysm, or rupture.

### 2.4.1 Mechanisms of Malperfusion in Aortic Dissection
Malperfusion syndrome occurs in up to 30% of acute dissections and is a primary determinant of in-hospital mortality:
* **Dynamic Malperfusion:** Driven by prolapse of the mobile intimal flap across a branch vessel ostium, fluctuating with blood pressure and cardiac cycle. Corrected by true lumen expansion (stenting or fenestration).
* **Static Malperfusion:** Direct propagation of the dissection flap into the branch vessel wall, thrombosis of the false lumen within the branch, or avulsion of the vessel. Requires dedicated branch vessel stenting or surgical bypass.

![Figure 9: Mechanisms of Dynamic and Static Obstruction in Malperfusion Syndrome](images/fig09_mechanisms_dynamic_static_malperfusion.jpg)

### 2.5 Classification of Thoracoabdominal Aortic Aneurysms (TAAA: Crawford Extents)
Thoracoabdominal aneurysms involve both the descending thoracic and abdominal aorta:
* **Extent I:** From distal to left subclavian artery to above the renal arteries.
* **Extent II:** From distal to left subclavian artery to below the renal arteries (highest risk of paraplegia and renal failure).
* **Extent III:** From mid-descending thoracic aorta (below 6th intercostal space) to below the renal arteries.
* **Extent IV:** Confined to the abdominal aorta from the diaphragm (involving celiac, SMA, renals) to the aortic bifurcation.
* **Extent V (Safi modification):** From mid-descending aorta to above the renal arteries (excluding visceral/renal vessels).

![Figure 10: Classification of Thoracoabdominal Aortic Aneurysms (Crawford Extents I to V)](images/fig10_classification_taaa_crawford.jpg)

### 2.6 Classification of Endoleaks After Endovascular Aortic Repair
Endoleak denotes persistent blood flow within the aneurysm sac outside the stent-graft lumen:
* **Type I:** Inadequate seal at proximal (Ia) or distal (Ib) attachment zone, or iliac occluder (Ic). Requires urgent reintervention due to high rupture risk.
* **Type II:** Retrograde filling of the aneurysm sac from collateral branch vessels (lumbar arteries, inferior mesenteric artery). Often managed conservatively unless sac expands $\ge 5\text{ mm}$.
* **Type III:** Structural graft defect, junctional separation between modular components (IIIa), or fabric tear (IIIb). Requires immediate repair.
* **Type IV:** Graft fabric porosity (typically resolves within 24–48 hours post-implant).
* **Type V (Endotension):** Aneurysm sac pressurization and expansion without visible flow on imaging.

## 3. Imaging Modalities, Acquisition Protocols, and Conventions of Measurement</summary><div class="details-content">

### 3.1 Aortic Imaging Modalities
* **Computed Tomography Angiography (CTA):** Modality of choice for acute aortic syndromes and comprehensive operative planning. Requires non-contrast acquisition (to detect hyperattenuating IMH) followed by ECG-gated contrast-enhanced arterial phase from neck to femoral bifurcations.
* **Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA):** Preferred for serial surveillance in young patients and heritable aortopathies to eliminate ionizing radiation.
* **Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE):** First-line screening for aortic root and valve assessment, LV function, and pericardial effusion.
* **Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE):** Essential intraoperatively and in unstable patients suspected of Type A dissection.
* **Intravascular Ultrasound (IVUS):** Crucial intraprocedural adjunct during TEVAR to confirm true vs false lumen wire position, identify branch vessel takeoff, and guide stent-graft sizing without nephrotoxic contrast.

### 3.2 Conventions of Aortic Measurement
* **Orthogonal Double-Oblique Multiplanar Reconstruction (MPR):** Aortic diameters must be measured on images reformatted strictly **perpendicular to the blood flow centerline** (true short-axis). Axial measurements on unformatted cross-sectional scans overestimate true diameter by up to 20% in tortuous or angled vessels.
* **Edge-to-Edge Conventions:**
  - **CT and MRI:** Measured using **outer-edge-to-outer-edge (OTO)** convention.
  - **Echocardiography:** Measured in end-diastole using **leading-edge-to-leading-edge (LTL)** or inner-edge-to-inner-edge (ITI) depending on validated laboratory standards.
  - Comparison between modalities must account for these systematic differences ($1\text{ to }2\text{ mm}$).

## 4. Multidisciplinary Aortic Teams and Comprehensive Aortic Centers</summary><div class="details-content">

### 4.1 Concept and Clinical Impact of Multidisciplinary Aortic Teams
* Clinical outcomes for complex aortic repair (elective and emergency) demonstrate an inverse volume-outcome relationship: higher institutional and surgeon volumes correlate with significantly reduced perioperative mortality, lower stroke rates, and improved long-term survival.
* **Multidisciplinary Aortic Teams (MAT):** Comprise cardiothoracic surgeons, vascular surgeons, interventional cardiologists, cardiovascular radiologists, cardiac anesthesiologists, and cardiovascular geneticists operating within comprehensive aortic centers (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR).
* The availability of a dedicated MAT justifies lowering elective surgical thresholds (e.g., $5.0\text{ cm}$ for sporadic root/ascending aneurysm) when institutional mortality is demonstrated to be $< 1\%$.

## 6. Aneurysms of the Thoracic and Abdominal Aorta</summary><div class="details-content">

An aortic aneurysm represents a permanent localized dilation $\ge 50\%$ greater than the expected normal diameter for a given anatomic segment. Etiology, natural history, rupture risk, and intervention thresholds vary substantially between thoracic and abdominal aortic segments and between sporadic and heritable disease.

### 6.1 Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm (TAA) Causes and Genetic Aortopathies

#### 6.1.1 Sporadic and Degenerative TAA
Sporadic aneurysms predominantly involve elderly patients with hypertension, atherosclerosis, and smoking history, commonly affecting the ascending or descending aorta.

#### 6.1.2 Heritable Thoracic Aortic Diseases (HTAD)
Up to 20% of patients with thoracic aortic aneurysm or dissection have a genetic basis, either as part of a recognized syndromic disorder or as non-syndromic familial thoracic aortic aneurysm and dissection (FTAAD):
* **Genetic Counseling and Multi-Gene Panel Testing (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR):** In patients with thoracic aortic disease presenting at age $< 60$ years, or with a family history of aortic disease, or with physical features suggestive of a connective tissue disorder, genetic counseling and testing with a comprehensive multi-gene panel (including *FBN1*, *TGFBR1*, *TGFBR2*, *SMAD3*, *TGFB2*, *TGFB3*, *ACTA2*, *MYH11*, *COL3A1*, *PRKG1*, *MYLK*, *LOX*) is recommended.
* **Cascade Clinical and Genetic Screening:** First-degree relatives of affected patients should undergo genetic testing for the identified pathogenic variant or periodic imaging surveillance if genetic testing is negative or uninformative (Class 1, Level B-NR).

#### 6.1.2.1 Marfan Syndrome (MFS)
* Caused by pathogenic variants in *FBN1* encoding fibrillin-1, inherited in an autosomal dominant manner with pleiotropic manifestations (ocular lens dislocation, skeletal overgrowth, dural ectasia, mitral valve prolapse).
* **Imaging Surveillance (Class 1, Level B-NR):** Baseline echocardiography followed by imaging 6 months later to establish stability, then annually if stable. MRA or CTA from head to pelvis at diagnosis to screen for extra-cardiac aneurysms.
* **Medical Therapy (Class 1, Level A):** Maximally tolerated beta-blockers or angiotensin receptor blockers (losartan, irbesartan) are recommended to reduce hemodynamic stress and slow aortic root dilation.
* **Surgical Thresholds (Class 1, Level B-NR):** Prophylactic aortic root replacement is recommended at an external diameter **$\ge 5.0\text{ cm}$**, or **$\ge 4.5\text{ cm}$** in the presence of high-risk features (family history of dissection, rapid growth $\ge 0.3\text{ cm/year}$, severe aortic regurgitation, or desire for pregnancy).
* **Valve-Sparing Root Replacement (VSRR):** Valve-sparing root reimplantation (David procedure) is preferred over composite mechanical valved conduit (Bentall) in experienced centers when aortic valve leaflets are morphologically normal or repairable (Class 1, Level B-NR).

#### 6.1.3 Bicuspid Aortic Valve (BAV) Aortopathy
Bicuspid aortic valve occurs in 1% to 2% of the population and is complicated by ascending aortic aneurysm in up to 50% of individuals due to intrinsic matrix disruption and altered hemodynamics:
* **Imaging Surveillance (Class 1, Level B-NR):** Baseline measurement of aortic root and ascending aorta by echocardiography, CTA, or MRA, with serial follow-up based on diameter.
* **Surgical Intervention Thresholds:**
  - In patients with BAV undergoing surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) for severe stenosis or regurgitation, replacement of the ascending aorta is recommended if diameter **$\ge 4.5\text{ cm}$** (Class 1, Level B-NR).
  - In asymptomatic patients with BAV without severe valve dysfunction, elective aortic surgery is recommended at **$\ge 5.0\text{ cm}$** when performed by a Multidisciplinary Aortic Team with low operative mortality (Class 2a, Level B-NR; otherwise $\ge 5.5\text{ cm}$).
  - In patients with BAV and high-risk features (family history of dissection, rapid expansion $\ge 0.5\text{ cm/year}$, coarctation, or uncontrolled hypertension), elective surgery is reasonable at **$\ge 5.0\text{ cm}$** (Class 2a, Level B-NR).

### 6.2 Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm (AAA): Causes, Risk Factors, and Screening

### 6.3 & 6.4 Medical Therapy and Surveillance for Aortic Aneurysms
* **Blood Pressure Control:** Antihypertensive therapy targeting blood pressure **$< 130/80\text{ mm Hg}$** is recommended in all patients with thoracic or abdominal aortic aneurysms to reduce wall shear stress (Class 1, Level B-NR). Beta-blockers are preferred first-line agents in TAA.
* **Statin Therapy:** Moderate-to-high intensity statins are recommended for all patients with degenerative TAA or AAA to reduce cardiovascular events (Class 1, Level A).
* **Smoking Cessation:** Smoking is the strongest modifiable risk factor for aneurysm growth and rupture; aggressive cessation interventions are mandatory (Class 1, Level A).
* **Antiplatelet Therapy:** Low-dose aspirin ($81\text{ to }100\text{ mg/day}$) is recommended in patients with atherosclerotic aneurysm disease (Class 1, Level A).
* **Surveillance Intervals:**
  - **Thoracic Aorta:** Diameter $4.0\text{ to }4.9\text{ cm}$: annual imaging (CTA or MRA); diameter $\ge 5.0\text{ cm}$: imaging every 6 months.
  - **Abdominal Aorta:** Diameter $3.0\text{ to }3.9\text{ cm}$: ultrasound every 3 years; diameter $4.0\text{ to }4.9\text{ cm}$: ultrasound annually; diameter $5.0\text{ to }5.4\text{ cm}$: ultrasound or CTA every 6 months.

## 7. Acute Aortic Syndromes (AAS)</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute aortic syndromes comprise life-threatening conditions involving disruptions of the aortic wall architecture: acute aortic dissection, intramural hematoma (IMH), penetrating atherosclerotic ulcer (PAU), and blunt traumatic aortic injury (BTAI).

### 7.1 Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Triage
* **Classic Presentation:** Abrupt onset of severe, excruciating chest, back, or abdominal pain described as sharp, tearing, ripping, or stabbing. Pain migratory pattern follows the path of dissection propagation.
* **Atypical & High-Risk Features:** Syncope, pulse deficit, blood pressure differential $> 20\text{ mm Hg}$ between arms, new murmur of aortic regurgitation, focal neurological deficit (stroke, paraplegia), acute limb ischemia, or acute renal/mesenteric ischemia.
* **Risk Stratification Scores:**
  - **Aortic Dissection Detection Risk Score (ADD-RS):** Evaluates high-risk predisposing conditions (Marfan, BAV, recent aortic intervention), high-risk pain features, and high-risk examination signs. Score 0 (low risk), 1 (intermediate risk), $\ge 2$ (high risk).
  - **D-Dimer Biomarker Utility:** In low-to-intermediate pretest probability (ADD-RS $\le 1$), a negative D-dimer ($< 500\text{ ng/mL}$) has a $> 98\%$ negative predictive value for ruling out AAS. D-dimer should not be used to rule out AAS in patients with high clinical pretest probability.

### 7.3 Medical Management of Acute Aortic Syndromes
* **Immediate Anti-Impulse Therapy (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR):**
  - **Target Heart Rate and Blood Pressure:** Initial intravenous beta-blocker therapy (esmolol, labetalol) titrated to **heart rate $< 60\text{ bpm}$**, followed by intravenous vasodilators (nicardipine, nitroprusside) if needed to achieve **systolic blood pressure between $100\text{ and }120\text{ mm Hg}$** (or the lowest tolerated pressure preserving cerebral, renal, and coronary perfusion).
  - **Critical Sequence Principle:** Vasodilators must **never** be administered before beta-blockers to prevent reflex tachycardia and increased ventricular ejection velocity ($dP/dt$), which accelerates dissection propagation.
* **Pain Relief:** Intravenous opioids (fentanyl, morphine) to alleviate pain and blunt sympathetic overdrive.
* **Long-Term Blood Pressure Target (Class 1, Level B-NR):** Maintenance blood pressure **$< 120/80\text{ mm Hg}$** using oral beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, or ARBs.

### 7.4 Surgical and Endovascular Management of Acute Aortic Dissection

#### 7.4.1 Acute Type A Aortic Dissection (ATAAD)
* **Emergency Surgical Repair (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR):** Emergent open surgical repair is indicated for all patients with acute Type A aortic dissection to prevent fatal pericardial tamponade, aortic rupture, coronary malperfusion, and acute severe aortic regurgitation.
* **Operative Principles:**
  - Resection of the primary intimal tear.
  - Replacement of the ascending aorta (hemiarch or total arch replacement).
  - Reconstruction of the aortic root with resuspension of the native aortic valve or root replacement (valve-sparing reimplantation or composite mechanical/biological graft).
  - **Extended Arch Repair With Frozen Elephant Trunk (FET):** Recommended in selected patients with arch entry tears, arch aneurysms, or distal malperfusion to optimize true lumen expansion.
* **Management of Malperfusion in Type A Dissection:**
  - Up to 30% of ATAAD cases present with malperfusion syndrome (coronary, cerebral, mesenteric, renal, or extremity).
  - In mesenteric malperfusion with established bowel ischemia/necrosis, primary percutaneous endovascular fenestration/stenting of visceral arteries before open cardiac repair may be considered by experienced multidisciplinary teams.

#### 7.4.2 Acute Type B Aortic Dissection (ATBAD)
* **Complicated Acute Type B Dissection (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR):** Defined by the presence of rupture, impending rupture, malperfusion syndrome (visceral, renal, lower limb), refractory pain, or uncontrolled hypertension despite maximal medical therapy. **Emergent TEVAR is the first-line treatment of choice**, achieving immediate true lumen restoration and covering the primary intimal tear.
* **High-Risk Uncomplicated Type B Dissection (Class 2a, Level B-R):** In medically stable patients presenting with high-risk clinical or morphological features:
  - False lumen diameter $\ge 22\text{ mm}$
  - Primary intimal entry tear diameter $\ge 10\text{ mm}$
  - Total aortic diameter $\ge 40\text{ mm}$
  - Partial false lumen thrombosis
  - Persistent or recurrent pain
  - Refractory hypertension
  - TEVAR in the subacute phase ($15\text{ to }90$ days) is reasonable to promote false lumen thrombosis, prevent aneurysmal degeneration, and reduce late aortic-related mortality (INSTEAD-XL trial).
* **Low-Risk Uncomplicated Type B Dissection:** Managed with optimal medical therapy (aggressive anti-impulse therapy, BP control, and serial surveillance imaging).

### 7.7 Blunt Traumatic Aortic Injury (BTAI)
* Rapid deceleration injuries in motor vehicle collisions or falls commonly injure the aortic isthmus (distal to left subclavian origin):
* **Classification and Management of BTTAI:**
  - **Grade I (Intimal Tear):** Non-operative conservative management with serial imaging and blood pressure control (Class 1, Level B-NR).
  - **Grade II (Intramural Hematoma):** Urgent TEVAR is reasonable (Class 2a, Level B-NR).
  - **Grade III (Pseudoaneurysm):** Prompt TEVAR is recommended within 24 hours after stabilizing other life-threatening injuries (Class 1, Level B-NR).
  - **Grade IV (Rupture / Free Extravasation):** Emergent TEVAR is recommended immediately (Class 1, Level B-NR).
* **TEVAR as Primary Modality (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR):** TEVAR is strongly preferred over open thoracotomy for blunt aortic trauma due to significantly lower operative mortality, reduced blood loss, and markedly lower paraplegia risk.

## 8. Pregnancy in Patients With Aortopathy</summary><div class="details-content">

Pregnancy carries significant hemodynamic and hormonal stress that markedly increases the risk of aortic dissection or rapid aneurysm enlargement in women with aortopathies. Peak vulnerability occurs during the third trimester and early postpartum period:

### 8.1 Pre-Pregnancy Counseling, Genetic Evaluation, and Medical Management
* **Multidisciplinary Pregnancy Heart Team (Class 1, Level of Evidence C-EO):** Care should be coordinated by a specialized team including maternal-fetal medicine obstetricians, cardiologists, cardiac/vascular surgeons, and anesthesiologists.
* **Preconceptional Imaging and Counseling:** Complete imaging of the thoracic and abdominal aorta by echocardiography and MRA (or CTA) is recommended prior to conception.
* **Teratogenic Medication Discontinuation:** Renin-angiotensin system inhibitors (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNI) are strictly contraindicated and must be discontinued before conception.
* **Beta-Blocker Continuation (Class 1, Level B-NR):** Beta-blockers (metoprolol, labetalol) should be continued throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period in women with Marfan, Loeys-Dietz, or other thoracic aortopathies to mitigate dissection risk.

* **Vaginal Delivery:** Permissible with regional/epidural anesthesia and assisted second stage in women with stable minor aortic dilation ($< 4.0\text{ cm}$ in Marfan, $< 4.5\text{ cm}$ in BAV).
* **Cesarean Delivery (Class 1, Level of Evidence C-LD):** Recommended in women with high-risk features:
  - Aortic root or ascending diameter **$\ge 4.0\text{ to }4.5\text{ cm}$** in Marfan syndrome
  - Aortic diameter **$\ge 4.0\text{ cm}$** or any dilation in Loeys-Dietz syndrome
  - Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (*COL3A1*)
  - Aortic diameter **$\ge 4.5\text{ cm}$** in bicuspid aortic valve or non-syndromic HTAD
  - History of chronic aortic dissection

  - Loeys-Dietz syndrome with diameter **$\ge 4.0\text{ to }4.5\text{ cm}$** (Class 1, Level B-NR).
  - BAV or sporadic TAA with diameter **$\ge 5.0\text{ cm}$** (Class 1, Level B-NR).

  - At gestational age **$\ge 28$ weeks:** Primary emergency cesarean delivery followed immediately by maternal aortic repair during the same operative session.

## 9. Other Aortic Conditions</summary><div class="details-content">

### 9.1 Inflammatory Aortitis (Takayasu Arteritis and Giant Cell Arteritis)
Large-vessel vasculitides cause transmural granulomatous inflammation leading to stenosis, occlusion, aneurysm formation, and dissection:
* **Takayasu Arteritis:** Predominantly affects young women ($< 40$ years), frequently involving the subclavian, carotid, and renal arteries and descending aorta.
* **Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA):** Affects individuals $> 50$ years, involving the cranial branches, thoracic aorta, and arch vessels.
* **Medical Therapy (Class 1, Level of Evidence A):** High-dose systemic glucocorticoids (prednisone $1\text{ mg/kg/day}$) are the cornerstone of induction therapy, combined with steroid-sparing agents (tocilizumab, methotrexate) for maintenance.
* **Revascularization Principles (Class 1, Level B-NR):** Elective surgical or endovascular revascularization should be delayed until complete clinical and serologic remission of vascular inflammation is achieved to prevent restenosis, anastomotic dehiscence, and graft failure.

### 9.2 Infectious Aortitis
* **Native Aorta Infection (Mycotic Aneurysm):** Arises from hematogenous bacteremic seeding of atheromatous plaque or septic embolization (*Staphylococcus aureus*, *Salmonella*, *Streptococcus* species). Rapid aneurysm expansion and rupture risk require prompt radical surgical debridement and reconstruction with cryopreserved arterial allograft, autologous superficial femoral vein graft, or rifampin-soaked polyester graft with prolonged targeted IV antibiotic therapy.
* **Prosthetic Aortic Graft Infection (PAGI):** Life-threatening complication diagnosed by contrast CTA and functional molecular imaging ($^{18}\text{F}$-FDG PET-CT or tagged white blood cell SPECT-CT). Complete graft excision with in situ or extra-anatomic reconstruction is preferred; endovascular stent-grafting is strictly reserved as an emergency bridge in unstable, exsanguinating patients.

### 9.3 Atherosclerotic Disease: Aortic Thrombus, Occlusion, and Porcelain Aorta
* **Mobile / Protruding Aortic Thrombus:** Source of systemic and peripheral atheroembolism; managed with systemic anticoagulation, antiplatelet therapy, and statins, with surgical or endovascular thrombectomy for recurrent emboli.
* **Porcelain Aorta:** Circumferential heavy transmural calcification of the ascending aorta precluding safe cross-clamping or cannulation during cardiac surgery. Requires hypothermic circulatory arrest, "no-touch" techniques, or alternative cannulation sites (femoral, axillary).

## 10. Physical Activity and Quality of Life</summary><div class="details-content">

### 10.1 Exercise Prescription and Lifestyle Recommendations
* **Isometric Straining Avoidance:** Heavy competitive weightlifting, intense isometric resistance exercises, and activities requiring prolonged Valsalva maneuvers (which elevate systolic BP acutely to $> 300\text{ mm Hg}$) are discouraged in patients with aortic aneurysms or prior dissection.
* **Aerobic Exercise Encouraged (Class 1, Level of Evidence B-NR):** Moderate-intensity dynamic aerobic physical activity ($30\text{ to }60\text{ minutes}$, $\ge 3\text{ to }5\text{ days/week}$) is recommended to promote cardiovascular fitness, blood pressure control, and overall well-being.
* **Cardiac Rehabilitation (Class 2a, Level B-NR):** Supervised exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation is reasonable after acute aortic dissection or aortic surgery to facilitate safe functional recovery and psychological reintegration.

## 11. Cost and Value Considerations in Aortic Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

* Ultrasound screening for AAA in 65-year-old men with a smoking history is highly cost-effective, with an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) well below accepted societal willingness-to-pay thresholds ($< \$50,000\text{ per QALY}$ gained).
* Centralization of elective complex aortic surgical and endovascular care in high-volume Multidisciplinary Aortic Centers reduces perioperative failure-to-rescue rates, reoperation costs, and overall health resource consumption.
* Routine multi-gene panel testing in young patients ($< 60$ years) with thoracic aortic disease demonstrates favorable long-term cost-effectiveness by identifying at-risk relatives early through cascade screening, preventing catastrophic unheralded dissections and emergency repairs.

## 12. Evidence Gaps and Future Directions</summary><div class="details-content">

Key priorities for future research in aortic medicine and surgery include:
1. **Biomarker Discovery:** Identification of specific circulating RNA, extracellular matrix degradation products, or proteomic signatures that predict rapid aneurysm expansion or impending rupture before structural failure occurs.
2. **Genomic Architecture:** Unraveling novel genetic variants and polygenic risk scores contributing to non-syndromic familial thoracic and abdominal aortic aneurysms.
3. **Advanced Aortic Biomechanics & CFD:** Transitioning beyond simple maximal diameter to four-dimensional flow MRI and computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modeling of wall shear stress, wall thickness, and cyclic strain to personalize intervention thresholds.
4. **Sex and Racial Disparities:** Investigating biological and structural reasons why women with aortic dissection present later, have smaller absolute diameters at rupture, and suffer higher perioperative mortality than men.
5. **Next-Generation Endovascular Technology:** Development of off-the-shelf branched and fenestrated endografts for the aortic arch and thoracoabdominal aorta with lower spinal cord ischemia and stroke risks.
6. **Cardioprotective and Anti-Degenerative Pharmacotherapies:** Conducting dedicated clinical trials investigating novel molecular targets (e.g., matrix metalloproteinase inhibition, TGF-$\beta$ modulators, SGLT2 inhibitors) to halt or reverse aortic wall degeneration.
