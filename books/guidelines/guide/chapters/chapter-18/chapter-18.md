## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Core Principles of Ultrasound-Guided Vascular Cannulation

Vascular access is one of the most frequently performed invasive procedures in modern clinical medicine. This 2025 update replaces the landmark 2011 ASE guidelines, synthesizing current evidence across diverse medical, surgical, and critical care specialties to establish best practices for ultrasound (US) integration:

1. **The Three Fundamental Roles of Ultrasound:**
   - **Pre-cannulation Assessment:** Identifying vessel anatomy, patency (compressibility and absence of intraluminal thrombus), depth, diameter, respiratory variability, and anatomical anomalies (e.g., vessel transposition, bifurcation variations, or overlap with adjacent arteries).
   - **Dynamic Real-Time Guidance:** Continuous visualization of the needle trajectory and needle tip entering the target vessel lumen, preventing posterior wall puncture, transfixion, and injury to deeper structures (e.g., pleura, nerves, adjacent arteries).
   - **Post-cannulation and Complication Screening:** Unequivocal confirmation of guidewire position within the target vessel lumen in two orthogonal planes prior to dilation, verification of final catheter position, and early detection of immediate mechanical complications (hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, arteriovenous fistula, pneumothorax, or thrombosis).

2. **Technical Modalities & Ergonomics:**
   - **Transducer Alignment:** Use of short-axis (SAX, transverse, out-of-plane) approach for lateral centering and side-to-side orientation, combined with dynamic needle tip positioning (DNTP), or long-axis (LAX, longitudinal, in-plane) approach for complete needle and shaft trajectory visualization.
   - **Ergonomic Configuration:** In-line orientation where the operator, puncture site, ultrasound transducer, and ultrasound screen are situated in a single direct visual axis, eliminating head-turning and disorientation.
   - **Color and Spectral Doppler:** Indispensable adjuncts to distinguish non-pulsatile vs pulsatile waveforms and confirm non-occlusive thrombi when compression alone is inconclusive.

3. **GRADE Evidence Hierarchy:**
   - Recommendations are graded using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework (Grades 1A, 1B, 1C for strong recommendations; 2A, 2B, 2C for weak recommendations).
   - Routine real-time ultrasound guidance is established as the gold standard of care for adult internal jugular vein (IJV) cannulation (**Grade 1A**), radial artery cannulation (**Grade 1B** routinely, **Grade 1A** for weak pulse/failed landmark), femoral vein cannulation (**Grade 1B**), peripheral vein access (**Grade 1B**), subclavian vein access (**Grade 1C**), axillary vein access (**Grade 1B**), and common femoral artery cannulation (**Grade 1B**).

4. **Mandatory Safety Rule:**
   - Under no circumstances should a dilator or large-bore catheter be advanced over a guidewire without prior, direct ultrasound or fluoroscopic confirmation that the wire resides within the intended venous/arterial lumen and has not traversed the posterior wall or entered an unintended vascular structure.

## 1. Introduction</summary><div class="details-content">

Vascular access is an essential component of inpatient and outpatient management across critical care, emergency medicine, cardiology, anesthesiology, nephrology, radiology, and oncology. Indications span intravenous fluid administration, hemodynamic monitoring, continuous vasopressor infusion, parenteral nutrition, hemodialysis, and complex percutaneous cardiovascular interventions (e.g., transcatheter valve replacement, mechanical circulatory support).

Historically, vascular access relied entirely on surface anatomical landmarks and arterial palpation. However, blind puncture is constrained by extensive inter-individual anatomical variations, pathological vessel displacement, thrombosis, vessel spasm, hypovolemia, and obesity. Blind attempts lead to mechanical complications in up to 15% of patients, including unintended arterial puncture, hematoma formation, pneumothorax, hemothorax, pseudoaneurysm, and nerve injury.

The introduction of point-of-care ultrasound has revolutionized vascular access. Real-time ultrasonography visualizes target vessels, defines adjacent anatomical hazard structures, confirms intraluminal needle entry, tracks guidewire trajectory, and verifies proper catheter seating. This 2025 document provides updated, evidence-based recommendations for ultrasound-guided vascular cannulation in both adult and pediatric populations, defining standardized techniques and minimum training requirements.

</div></details>

## 2. Methodology and Evidence Review</summary><div class="details-content">

A multidisciplinary writing committee comprising adult and pediatric echocardiographers, anesthesiologists, cardiologists, vascular surgeons, interventional radiologists, and sonographers conducted a comprehensive literature search across PubMed, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library. The review focused on randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective cohort studies, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses published through 2024 evaluating ultrasound guidance versus landmark-guided or fluoroscopic vascular access.

Recommendations were established using the **Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE)** framework, assessing the balance of benefits versus risks, the quality of supporting evidence, and clinical implications.

## 3. General Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">


<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 3.1. Vascular Cannulation Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

Vascular access techniques fall into two major categories: **landmark-guided (palpation/inspection)** and **ultrasound-guided**. Ultrasound application can be classified into:
- **Static Ultrasound:** Transducer imaging is utilized strictly prior to needle puncture to mark skin projection, ascertain vessel patency, and measure vessel depth and diameter. The transducer is then set aside, and puncture proceeds without real-time visualization. *Static guidance is inferior to real-time guidance and does not protect against needle deviation, patient movement, or vessel transfixion.*
- **Dynamic (Real-Time) Ultrasound Guidance:** The transducer is held continuously throughout needle advancement, visualizing tissue deflection, anterior wall tenting, luminal puncture, blood flash, and guidewire introduction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### Table 2: Ultrasound Roles During Vascular Access</summary><div class="details-content">

| Procedure Phase | Primary Ultrasound Objectives | Specific Clinical Value |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pre-cannulation Assessment** | Identify vascular anatomy, spatial orientation, and vessel size | Determines optimal cannulation site, detects hypoplastic vessels, avoids bifurcations. |
| | Determine vessel patency and compressibility | Excludes complete or partial thrombosis, intraluminal webs, and sclerosis. |
| | Identify adjacent anatomical hazard structures | Prevents accidental puncture of adjacent arteries, nerves, pleura, or mediastinum. |
| **Real-Time Puncture** | Dynamic needle tip guidance and trajectory control | Ensures single-puncture anterior wall entry; prevents posterior wall transfixion. |
| | Visualize luminal entry and needle bevel | Confirms flash corresponds to true intraluminal positioning rather than hematoma. |
| **Guidewire Verification** | Confirm guidewire within vessel lumen in two planes | Mandatory before dilation; completely eliminates large-bore arterial or extravascular cannulation. |
| **Post-cannulation Assessment**| Confirm catheter position and patency | Documents proper placement in central veins; excludes kinking or extravasation. |
| | Immediate complication screening | Detects acute hematoma, perivascular extravasation, pseudoaneurysm, AVF, or pneumothorax. |

The standard modified Seldinger technique employs a small-gauge finder needle or angiocatheter through which a soft-tipped J-wire or straight guidewire is advanced into the lumen. Over this wire, dilators and final catheters or sheaths are introduced.

### 3.2. Ultrasound Imaging of Vessels</summary><div class="details-content">

Optimal vascular imaging depends on transducer selection and acoustic control optimization:
- **Transducer Types:** High-frequency linear array transducers ($7.5	ext{--}15	ext{ MHz}$) provide high spatial resolution for superficial vessels ($< 4	ext{ cm}$ depth). Lower-frequency curvilinear or phased-array probes ($3.5	ext{--}5	ext{ MHz}$) are reserved for deep vessels in obese patients, pediatric transthoracic windows, or deep common femoral anatomy.
- **Optimization Parameters:** Set single transmit focal zone at the target vessel depth. Adjust dynamic range ($50	ext{--}65	ext{ dB}$) and gain to maintain an anechoic vessel lumen while clearly delineating echogenic vessel walls. Center the target vessel horizontally on the screen.

<span id="fig-01"></span>
![Figure 1: Ultrasound probes and image optimization](images/fig01_us_probes_image_optimization.jpg)
*Figure 1: Ultrasound probes and image optimization. (A) Selection of ultrasound probes for vascular access: high-frequency linear array probe (standard for vascular access), small-footprint hockey-stick probe (ideal for pediatric, neck, or wrist anatomy), and curvilinear array (deep vessels). (B) Transducer controls for gain, depth, focal zone placement, and acoustic frequency.*

Vessels can be visualized in three orthogonal imaging planes:
1. **Transverse (Short-Axis, SAX):** Probe perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the vessel. Displays cross-sectional circular anatomy and adjacent structures side-by-side.
2. **Longitudinal (Long-Axis, LAX):** Probe parallel to the vessel path. Displays continuous tubular vessel course, anterior wall, lumen, and posterior wall.
3. **Oblique Plane:** Transducer angled between transverse and longitudinal axes to maximize luminal length while maintaining lateral anatomical context.

<span id="fig-02"></span>
![Figure 2: Vessel orientation and imaging planes](images/fig02_vessel_orientation.jpg)
*Figure 2: Vessel orientation and imaging planes. (A) Transverse (short-axis) view displays circular vessel cross-section and adjacent anatomical relationships. (B) Longitudinal (long-axis) view displays longitudinal extent of vessel with anterior and posterior walls. (C) Oblique orientation provides an elliptical cross-section, balancing longitudinal trajectory with transverse margins.*

A fundamental procedural requirement is reconciling probe orientation with screen display. Each probe features a tactile index marker (notch, light, ridge) that corresponds to an orientation marker on the ultrasound monitor (dot, logo, indicator). Standard vascular access convention places the probe marker towards the patient's anatomical right (in transverse imaging) or cephalad (in longitudinal imaging), so that left-to-right on the screen precisely mirrors left-to-right in the physical operative field.

<span id="fig-03"></span>
![Figure 3: Image display and probe marker orientation](images/fig03_image_display_marker.jpg)
*Figure 3: Image display and probe marker orientation. (A-D) Correlation between physical transducer orientation marker (black triangle/ridge) and on-screen graphical indicator. Aligning operator movement with screen display eliminates paradoxical hand-eye coordination errors during needle advancement.*

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 3.3. Vessel Identification by Ultrasound</summary><div class="details-content">

Differentiating veins from arteries relies on five distinct sonographic criteria:
1. **Compressibility:** Normal veins have thin, low-resistance walls that completely collapse under gentle transducer pressure. Arteries possess thick, muscular, elastic walls that resist compression, maintaining a pulsatile round profile.
2. **Vessel Size and Shape:** Veins are generally larger and exhibit dynamic respiratory caliber variation (collapsing on inspiration during spontaneous ventilation, or dilating during positive-pressure ventilation). Arteries exhibit stable, fixed calibers.
3. **Pulsatility:** Arteries demonstrate prominent systolic expansion and diastolic recoil. Veins exhibit passive, low-velocity, non-pulsatile or gentle cardiac biphasic motion.
4. **Color Flow Doppler:** Arteries display robust, high-velocity, pulsatile flow with systolic frame blooming. Veins display continuous, low-velocity, non-turbulent uniform filling.
5. **Spectral Doppler:** Pulsed-wave Doppler (PWD) provides definitive differentiation: arteries exhibit high-resistance triphasic/biphasic waveforms or low-resistance monophasic systolic peaks; veins demonstrate low-velocity continuous or respiratory-phasic flow.

<span id="fig-04"></span>
![Figure 4: Sonographic differentiation between veins and arteries](images/fig04_vein_artery_differentiation.jpg)
*Figure 4: Sonographic differentiation between veins and arteries in normal circulation. (A) B-mode and compression: veins completely collapse under light transducer pressure, whereas arteries resist compression. (B) Color flow Doppler demonstrates uniform low-velocity venous filling versus pulsatile, aliasing arterial flow. (C) Pulsed-wave Doppler displays continuous venous flow versus characteristic arterial velocity waveforms.*

### 3.4. Ultrasound-Guided Vascular Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

Two dynamic guidance techniques exist for needle advancement:

#### 1. Out-of-Plane (Short-Axis, SAX) Approach
- The transducer images the vessel in transverse cross-section. The needle enters the skin at a $45^\circ	ext{--}60^\circ$ angle, perpendicular to the acoustic beam plane.
- **Advantage:** Provides superior visualization of lateral vessel borders and surrounding structures (e.g., verifying that the needle is dead-center in the vein and away from the adjacent artery).
- **Pitfall & Solution:** The acoustic beam thickness is $< 1	ext{ mm}$. A needle intersecting the beam produces an echogenic bright hyperechoic dot with posterior acoustic shadowing. However, a needle shaft intersecting the beam looks identical to the needle tip. To prevent advancing the unseen tip through the posterior wall, the operator must utilize **Dynamic Needle Tip Positioning (DNTP)**: advance the probe slightly until the bright dot disappears, then advance the needle until the dot reappears; repeat sequentially ("walk the needle") until the tip punctures the anterior vessel wall.

<span id="fig-06"></span>
![Figure 6: Out-of-plane cannulation approach](images/fig06_out_of_plane_approach.jpg)
*Figure 6: Out-of-plane cannulation approach. The transducer is placed in short axis (SAX) perpendicular to the vessel. The needle enters obliquely. Dynamic needle tip positioning is required to follow the hyperechoic needle tip into the anterior vessel wall and avoid posterior wall penetration.*

#### 2. In-Plane (Long-Axis, LAX) Approach
- The transducer images the vessel in longitudinal view. The needle is inserted strictly parallel to and within the plane of the ultrasound beam.
- **Advantage:** The entire length of the needle—including shaft, bevel, and tip—is continuously visualized throughout its path through subcutaneous tissue and anterior wall entry.
- **Pitfall:** Maintaining the thin ($< 1	ext{ mm}$) ultrasound plane aligned with both the needle and the vessel requires advanced manual dexterity. If the probe slides slightly off-center, the vessel or needle may be lost from view.

<span id="fig-07"></span>
![Figure 7: In-plane cannulation approach](images/fig07_in_plane_approach.jpg)
*Figure 7: In-plane cannulation approach. The transducer is aligned in long axis (LAX) along the vessel path. The entire needle length, shaft, and tip are continuously visualized as the needle approaches, tents, and enters the anterior lumen.*

<span id="fig-08"></span>
![Figure 8: Needle tip visualization and anterior wall tenting](images/fig08_needle_tip_visualization.jpg)
*Figure 8: Needle tip visualization and anterior wall tenting. (A) The echogenic needle tip produces a characteristic bright reflection with acoustic shadowing. (B) As the needle tip contacts the anterior wall, mechanical depression (tenting) occurs before luminal snap-through. (C) Free intraluminal positioning without contact with the posterior vessel wall.*

#### Mandatory Guidewire Confirmation
Once blood return is observed, the syringe is removed and a flexible J-tip guidewire is advanced without resistance. Before using any vascular dilator, the operator must obtain sonographic confirmation of the guidewire within the vessel lumen:
1. **Transverse View:** Hyperechoic circular dot centered within the anechoic vein lumen.
2. **Longitudinal View:** Linear echogenic parallel structure ("railroad track" or bright line) extending coaxially within the vascular lumen, confirming absence of posterior wall transfixion, subintimal dissection, or inadvertent arterial placement.

<span id="fig-09"></span>
![Figure 9: Sonographic confirmation of guidewire position](images/fig09_guidewire_identification.jpg)
*Figure 9: Sonographic confirmation of guidewire position. (A) Transverse view demonstrating hyperechoic punctate focus of the guidewire residing safely within the venous lumen. (B) Longitudinal view demonstrating linear echogenic guidewire tracking smoothly within the vessel without penetration of the posterior wall.*

## 4. Venous Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">


<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 4.1. Overview</summary><div class="details-content">

Central venous access is indicated for hemodynamic monitoring (central venous pressure), administration of vasoactive infusions, hypertonic solutions, total parenteral nutrition, rapid fluid resuscitation, hemodialysis, and transvenous cardiac pacing. The primary access sites are the internal jugular vein (IJV), subclavian vein (SCV), axillary vein, and common femoral vein (CFV).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 4.2. Internal Jugular Vein (IJV)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anatomic and Ultrasound Considerations
The right IJV is the preferred central venous cannulation site in adults due to its direct, linear path into the superior vena cava and right atrium, lower dome of the right pleura, and larger average cross-sectional area compared to the left IJV.

However, anatomical relationship between the IJV and the common carotid artery (CA) is highly variable:
- In standard anatomy, the IJV lies anterolateral to the CA.
- In up to 36% of patients, significant anatomical variants exist: the IJV lies directly anterior to the CA in 10%–15%, medial to the CA in 2%, or outside the expected landmark corridor in 5.5%.
- **Margin of Safety:** Defined as the lateral distance between the midpoint of the IJV and the lateral border of the CA. Excessive contralateral neck rotation ($> 30^\circ$) rotates the sternocleidomastoid muscle, causes the IJV to collapse over the CA, and increases the overlap between the two vessels, dramatically elevating the risk of inadvertent through-and-through carotid puncture.
- Patient positioning in modest Trendelenburg ($10^\circ	ext{--}15^\circ$) increases IJV cross-sectional area by $25	ext{--}40\%$ in spontaneously breathing patients, though the effect is blunted in hypovolemic or critically ill individuals.

<span id="fig-10"></span>
![Figure 10: Internal jugular vein anatomy and ultrasound imaging](images/fig10_internal_jugular_anatomy.jpg)
*Figure 10: Internal jugular vein anatomy and ultrasound imaging. (A-D) Anatomical course of bilateral IJVs and common carotid arteries. Transverse ultrasound views demonstrate variable anatomical relationships: anterolateral position, direct anterior overlap, and respiratory caliber changes.*

<span id="fig-11"></span>
![Figure 11: Margin of safety and carotid-IJV overlap](images/fig11_margin_of_safety_ijv.jpg)
*Figure 11: Margin of safety and carotid-IJV overlap. The margin of safety represents the lateral distance from the center of the IJV to the lateral edge of the carotid artery. Excessive contralateral head rotation reduces this margin, bringing the carotid directly posterior to the IJV.*

<span id="fig-12"></span>
![Figure 12: Internal jugular vein landmark technique](images/fig12_ijv_landmark_technique.jpg)
*Figure 12: Internal jugular vein landmark technique. Traditional blind puncture relies on the apex of Sedillot's triangle formed by the sternal and clavicular heads of the sternocleidomastoid muscle and the clavicle. Failure rates of landmark guidance range from 7.0% to 19.4%.*

#### Cannulation Techniques & Evidence
Over 40 randomized controlled trials and multiple meta-analyses demonstrate that real-time ultrasound guidance for IJV cannulation:
- Increases overall cannulation success ($98.7\%$ vs $88.2\%$, $P < 0.001$).
- Increases first-attempt success ($82\%$ vs $53\%$).
- Significantly reduces access time and number of needle passes.
- Reduces accidental carotid artery puncture from $7.0	ext{--}10.0\%$ to $< 1.0\%$.
- Reduces mechanical complications (hematoma, pneumothorax, hemothorax) from $8.4\%$ to $< 1.5\%$.

##### Recommendation for IJV Cannulation
> **Recommendation:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for internal jugular vein cannulation in all adult patients to increase overall success, increase first-pass success, reduce procedural time, and decrease mechanical complications including arterial puncture. (**Grade 1A**)

### 4.3. Subclavian and Axillary Vein</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anatomic and Ultrasound Considerations
The subclavian vein (SCV) originates at the outer border of the first rib as the continuation of the axillary vein and terminates behind the sternoclavicular joint where it unites with the IJV to form the brachiocephalic vein. It is separated from the subclavian artery (SCA) posteriorly by the anterior scalene muscle.

The clavicle poses a significant acoustic barrier to ultrasound imaging of the medial SCV. However, lateral to the clavicle, the **axillary vein** is readily accessible via an infraclavicular approach. Infraclavicular ultrasound provides a wide acoustic window to visualize the axillary vein and distal SCV as they cross over the first and second ribs.

<span id="fig-13"></span>
![Figure 13: Subclavian and axillary vein anatomy](images/fig13_subclavian_axillary_vein.jpg)
*Figure 13: Subclavian and axillary vein anatomy. Anatomical course of the axillary and subclavian vessels crossing anterior to the first rib and deep to the clavicle. (A-C) Sonographic visualization of the axillary vein, subclavian artery, and pleural line.*

#### Cannulation Techniques & Evidence
Cannulation can be performed using:
1. **Infraclavicular In-Plane Approach (LAX):** The transducer is placed inferior and parallel to the clavicle. The axillary vein is imaged longitudinally. The needle is introduced laterally and advanced in-plane under direct visualization, avoiding the first rib, pleura, and underlying artery.
2. **Infraclavicular Out-of-Plane Approach (SAX):** The vessel is imaged in cross-section over the first rib (which acts as a protective acoustic backstop).
3. **Supraclavicular In-Plane Approach:** The transducer is placed in the supraclavicular fossa aligned with the long axis of the SCV/brachiocephalic junction.

<span id="fig-14"></span>
![Figure 14: Subclavian and axillary vein cannulation techniques](images/fig14_subclavian_cannulation_technique.jpg)
*Figure 14: Subclavian and axillary vein cannulation techniques. (A-D) Out-of-plane approach visualizing the vein over the acoustic shadow of the first rib. (E-H) In-plane longitudinal approach demonstrating continuous needle visualization above the pleural reflection.*

RCT evidence confirms that ultrasound guidance for SCV/axillary vein cannulation reduces accidental arterial puncture, virtually eliminates pneumothorax (when using the first rib as a backstop), and improves first-pass success compared to landmark access.

##### Recommendations for Subclavian and Axillary Vein Cannulation
> **Recommendation 1:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for axillary vein cannulation in adult patients to increase success rates and minimize mechanical complications, including inadvertent arterial puncture and pneumothorax. (**Grade 1B**)
>
> **Recommendation 2:** Ultrasound guidance (real-time or static pre-puncture screening) is recommended for subclavian vein cannulation to confirm vessel patency, identify anatomical course, and reduce complication rates. (**Grade 1C**)

### 4.4. Common Femoral Vein (CFV)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anatomic and Ultrasound Considerations
The common femoral vein lies medial to the common femoral artery (CFA) within the femoral sheath in the femoral triangle, immediately inferior to the inguinal ligament. Proximal to the bifurcation, the deep femoral vein (profunda femoris) joins the superficial femoral vein to form the CFV.

Landmark access relies on palpation of the CFA pulse and puncturing $1	ext{ cm}$ medial. However, in $10	ext{--}25\%$ of individuals, the CFV partially or completely overlaps posterior or lateral to the CFA, particularly with external rotation of the hip. In cardiac arrest, shock, or severe peripheral vascular disease, absence of a palpable pulse renders landmark cannulation entirely blind.

<span id="fig-15"></span>
![Figure 15: Femoral vessel anatomy and ultrasound imaging](images/fig15_femoral_vessel_anatomy.jpg)
*Figure 15: Femoral vessel anatomy and ultrasound imaging. Anatomical relationship within the right femoral triangle: femoral nerve (lateral), common femoral artery (middle), and common femoral vein (medial). Sonographic views illustrate the saphenofemoral junction and profunda femoris takeoff.*

#### Cannulation Techniques & Evidence
The patient is positioned supine with the leg in neutral or slight abduction ($15^\circ	ext{--}20^\circ$). Transverse SAX imaging is performed at and just superior to the saphenofemoral junction. Real-time dynamic guidance ensures entry into the anterior wall of the CFV, avoiding entry into the superficial femoral vein or profunda branches.

<span id="fig-16"></span>
![Figure 16: Femoral vessel cannulation technique](images/fig16_femoral_access_technique.jpg)
*Figure 16: Femoral vessel cannulation technique. (A) Transducer positioning in the groin. (B) Real-time needle entry into the common femoral vein lumen, positioned safely medial to the pulsatile femoral artery.*

RCTs in adult emergency and critical care cohorts demonstrate that real-time ultrasound guidance for CFV cannulation increases first-pass success from $58\%$ to $86\%$, reduces total attempts, decreases access time, and significantly lowers accidental femoral artery puncture and hematoma rates.

##### Recommendation for CFV Cannulation
> **Recommendation:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for common femoral vein cannulation in adults to increase first-attempt and overall success rates, shorten cannulation time, and decrease accidental arterial puncture and hematoma formation. (**Grade 1B**)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 4.5. Peripherally Inserted Central Catheter (PICC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anatomic and Ultrasound Considerations
PICC lines provide medium- to long-term central venous access for outpatient chemotherapy, prolonged antibiotic courses, or total parenteral nutrition. Placement is performed in the mid-upper arm (the "green zone" between the antecubital fossa and axilla) targeting the **basilic vein**, **brachial veins**, or **cephalic vein**.
- The **basilic vein** is the preferred target because it is typically the largest, runs medial and superficial to the brachial artery, has no immediately adjacent paired artery in the mid-arm, and exhibits the lowest rate of phlebitis.
- The **paired brachial veins (venae comitantes)** closely flank the brachial artery and median nerve. Puncture carries risks of arterial cannulation and nerve injury.
- The **cephalic vein** has a smaller caliber, higher tortuosity, and forms a sharp right-angle entry into the axillary vein, predisposing to catheter malposition and thrombosis.

#### Evidence & Recommendations
Ultrasound guidance allows measurement of vessel internal diameter to ensure an optimal **catheter-to-vein ratio of $\le 33	ext{--}45\%$** (or catheter occupying $< 45\%$ of luminal area), which is the single most important modifiable factor to prevent catheter-related deep vein thrombosis (DVT).

##### Recommendations for PICC Cannulation
> **Recommendation 1:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for vein selection and dynamic needle insertion during PICC placement in the upper extremity. (**Grade 1C**)
>
> **Recommendation 2:** Pre-procedural ultrasound mapping of vein diameter should be performed to select a catheter size that occupies no more than 45% of the internal vein lumen to minimize the risk of catheter-related venous thrombosis. (**Grade 1C**)

---

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 4.6. Peripheral Intravenous (PIV) Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anatomic and Technical Considerations
Up to 35% of hospitalized patients have "difficult venous access" (DVA), characterized by non-visible, non-palpable veins due to obesity, diabetes, intravenous drug use, chemotherapy, chronic edema, or multiple prior hospitalizations. Traditional blind attempts in DVA patients result in failure rates $> 50\%$, repeated painful punctures, procedural delays, and unnecessary central venous line insertions.

Ultrasound identifies deep peripheral veins in the forearm and upper arm (deep cephalic, basilic, median cubital, and forearm venae comitantes) at depths of $0.5	ext{--}1.5	ext{ cm}$. Catheter length is crucial: standard $1.25	ext{ inch}$ ($32	ext{ mm}$) cannulas frequently dislodge from deep veins because $< 65\%$ of the catheter remains in the lumen; longer cannulas ($1.75	ext{--}2.5	ext{ inches}$, $45	ext{--}64	ext{ mm}$) are required.

<span id="fig-17"></span>
![Figure 17: Peripheral venous anatomy of the upper extremity](images/fig17_peripheral_venous_access.jpg)
*Figure 17: Peripheral venous anatomy of the upper extremity. Venous distribution in the arm and forearm including cephalic, basilic, median cubital, and brachial venae comitantes. Ultrasound cross-sections demonstrate vessel caliber and depth selection criteria.*

#### Evidence & Recommendations
Multiple RCTs and Cochrane reviews demonstrate that in patients with difficult venous access, real-time ultrasound guidance:
- Increases overall success from $70\%$ to $90\%$ ($P < 0.001$).
- Improves first-attempt success ($80\%$ vs $43\%$).
- Reduces median number of attempts from 3 to 1.
- Significantly reduces requirement for central venous line placement.

##### Recommendation for Peripheral Venous Access
> **Recommendation:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for peripheral intravenous cannulation in adult and pediatric patients with difficult or failed landmark access to increase success rates, decrease the number of puncture attempts, and prevent escalation to central venous catheterization. (**Grade 1B**)

## 5. Arterial Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">


<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 5.1. Overview</summary><div class="details-content">

Arterial cannulation is indicated for continuous invasive blood pressure monitoring, frequent arterial blood gas and laboratory sampling, cardiac and peripheral diagnostic angiography, and transcatheter endovascular interventions.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### Table 3: Common Indications for Arterial Access</summary><div class="details-content">

| Category | Specific Clinical Indications |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Hemodynamic Monitoring** | Continuous invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring in shock, major cardiac/thoracic/vascular surgery, and intracranial hypertension. |
| **Blood Sampling** | Frequent arterial blood gas analysis, metabolic monitoring, and coagulation profiles without repeated percutaneous punctures. |
| **Diagnostic Endovascular Procedures** | Coronary angiography, cerebral angiography, aortography, and peripheral visceral/extremity vascular mapping. |
| **Therapeutic Interventions** | Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR/TEVAR). |

| **Vascular Procedures** | Targeted intra-arterial thrombolysis, thrombectomy, embolization for acute hemorrhage, and endovascular AVF creation. |

Site selection prioritizes the **radial artery (RA)** as first-line due to dual palmar arch collateral perfusion, superficial course, and ease of hemostatic compression. Alternative sites include the ulnar artery (UA), brachial artery (BA), axillary artery, common femoral artery (CFA), posterior tibial artery (PTA), and dorsalis pedis artery (DPA).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 5.2. Radial Artery and Ulnar Artery</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anatomy and Access Considerations
The radial artery courses along the lateral aspect of the anterior forearm, overlying the distal radius at the wrist. Collateral blood supply to the hand is provided through the deep and superficial palmar arches fed by both radial and ulnar arteries.

Traditional palpation access is hampered in hypotension, vasoconstriction, peripheral edema, wrist hematoma, or obesity. Ultrasound pre-scanning evaluates radial artery internal diameter (typically $2.0	ext{--}2.8	ext{ mm}$ in adults), depth, tortuosity, atherosclerosis/calcification, and anatomical variations (e.g., high bifurcation or hypoplastic radial artery).

<span id="fig-18"></span>
![Figure 18: Upper limb arterial anatomy](images/fig18_upper_limb_arterial_access.jpg)
*Figure 18: Upper limb arterial anatomy. Course of the radial and ulnar arteries in the forearm terminating in the superficial and deep palmar arches. Sonographic short-axis views demonstrate the relationship of the radial artery to the radial styloid process and flexor tendons.*

#### Cannulation Techniques & Evidence
The wrist is secured in slight dorsiflexion ($30^\circ	ext{--}45^\circ$) on an armboard. Using a high-frequency linear transducer ($10	ext{--}15	ext{ MHz}$) in a sterile sheath, dynamic short-axis guidance with DNTP or long-axis in-plane guidance is employed. A $20	ext{ G}$ or $22	ext{ G}$ catheter-over-needle or micro-puncture Seldinger wire kit is advanced into the lumen.

<span id="fig-19"></span>
![Figure 19: Radial artery cannulation technique](images/fig19_peripheral_artery_cannulation.jpg)
*Figure 19: Radial artery cannulation technique. (A) Arm positioning and wrist stabilization in modest dorsiflexion. (B-D) Real-time ultrasound guidance demonstrating single anterior wall puncture, blood return, and coaxial wire advancement into the radial artery lumen.*

Over 25 RCTs and major meta-analyses demonstrate that real-time ultrasound guidance for radial artery cannulation:
- Increases first-pass success rate from $45	ext{--}55\%$ to $75	ext{--}88\%$ ($P < 0.001$).
- Reduces total number of attempts and time to cannulation.
- Decreases local hematoma formation from $14\%$ to $< 4\%$.
- Is overwhelmingly superior in patients with weak/impalpable pulses, severe hypotension, hypothermia, or prior failed landmark attempts.

The **ulnar artery** represents a safe alternative if the radial artery is occluded, small, or harvested for CABG, provided collateral palmar flow is documented.

##### Recommendations for Radial and Ulnar Artery Cannulation
> **Recommendation 1:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for routine radial artery cannulation in adult patients to increase first-attempt success, reduce attempts, and minimize complications. (**Grade 1B**)
>
> **Recommendation 2:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is strongly recommended for radial artery cannulation in patients with weak, impalpable, or diminished arterial pulses, wrist edema, or after failed palpation attempts. (**Grade 1A**)
>
> **Recommendation 3:** Ultrasound guidance is recommended when cannulating the ulnar artery to ensure adequate vessel caliber and avoid adjacent ulnar nerve injury. (**Grade 1B**)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 5.3. Brachial Artery</summary><div class="details-content">

The brachial artery (BA) runs along the medial aspect of the arm and bifurcates in the cubital fossa into radial and ulnar arteries. Because the brachial artery is an anatomical end-artery with limited collateral pathways across the elbow, complications (thrombosis, dissection, hematoma) can directly precipitate acute upper extremity limb ischemia. Furthermore, the median nerve runs in intimate contact with the medial border of the BA in the antecubital fossa.

Ultrasound guidance is vital to identify the exact position of the median nerve, center the needle within the BA lumen, and achieve a clean single anterior wall puncture.

##### Recommendation for Brachial Artery Cannulation
> **Recommendation:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for brachial artery cannulation to ensure direct intraluminal entry and avoid median nerve injury or posterior wall hematoma. (**Grade 1C**)

---

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 5.4. Axillary Artery and Carotid Artery Access</summary><div class="details-content">

The axillary artery provides large-bore arterial access for hemodynamic support devices (Impella 5.0, intra-aortic balloon pump) or transcatheter interventions when femoral access is precluded by severe peripheral arterial occlusive disease or abdominal aortic aneurysm.
- Anatomically, the axillary artery is surrounded by the cords of the brachial plexus and lies deep to the pectoralis major and minor muscles.
- Direct ultrasound guidance is mandatory to identify brachial plexus cords and guide entry into the second or third portion of the axillary artery above the first rib.

##### Recommendation for Axillary Artery Access
> **Recommendation:** Ultrasound guidance is recommended for axillary artery cannulation to optimize puncture location, confirm luminal wire course, and protect the cords of the brachial plexus. (**Grade 1C**)

---

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 5.5. Common Femoral Artery (CFA)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anatomy and Access Considerations
The common femoral artery (CFA) is the primary access site for cardiac catheterization, PCI, TAVR, endovascular aortic repair (EVAR), and mechanical circulatory support. The ideal puncture zone is the **middle segment of the CFA directly overlying the medial third of the femoral head**, below the inferior margin of the inferior epigastric artery (inguinal ligament) and above the bifurcation into the superficial femoral artery (SFA) and profunda femoris artery.
- Puncture **above the inguinal ligament** (retroperitoneal space) risks catastrophic, non-compressible retroperitoneal hemorrhage.
- Puncture **below the femoral bifurcation** (into SFA or profunda) dramatically increases risks of pseudoaneurysm ($4	ext{--}6	imes$), arteriovenous fistula (AVF), acute thrombosis, and dissection.

#### Evidence & Recommendations
The landmark FAUST (Femoral Arterial Access With Ultrasound Trial) multicenter RCT and subsequent meta-analyses comparing routine ultrasound guidance versus fluoroscopic/palpation guidance demonstrated:
- Significant reduction in accidental venous puncture ($2.4\%$ vs $15.8\%$, $P < 0.0001$).
- Significant improvement in first-pass success ($83\%$ vs $46\%$).
- Significant reduction in total vascular complications (hematoma, pseudoaneurysm, retroperitoneal bleed) from $3.4\%$ to $1.4\%$ ($P = 0.04$).
- Precise avoidance of bifurcations and high retroperitoneal punctures.

##### Recommendation for CFA Cannulation
> **Recommendation:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for common femoral artery cannulation to ensure puncture above the femoral bifurcation and below the inguinal ligament, reduce accidental venous puncture, improve first-pass success, and decrease major vascular complications. (**Grade 1B**)

---

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 5.6. Posterior Tibial and Dorsalis Pedis Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

The dorsalis pedis artery (DPA) and posterior tibial artery (PTA) provide peripheral arterial monitoring options when upper extremity access is impossible (e.g., severe burns, bilateral radial harvest, bilateral arm trauma).
- The DPA lies superficial on the dorsum of the foot between the extensor hallucis longus and extensor digitorum longus tendons.
- The PTA lies posterior to the medial malleolus alongside the posterior tibial nerve.
- Both vessels are prone to severe vasospasm and caliber collapse ($1.2	ext{--}1.8	ext{ mm}$). Real-time ultrasound enables visualization of vessel patency and precise needle entry.

## 6. Vascular Cannulation Complications</summary><div class="details-content">


<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 6.1. Overview</summary><div class="details-content">

Complications associated with vascular cannulation can be classified into: (1) direct vessel injury, (2) adjacent structure injury, (3) technical/equipment failure, and (4) infectious/thrombotic sequelae. Point-of-care ultrasound serves not only to prevent complications during insertion, but also as the primary rapid diagnostic tool for immediate bedside evaluation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 6.2. Common Vascular Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

1. **Hematoma, Seroma, and Abscess:**
   - Subcutaneous hematomas appear as hypoechoic or complex heterogeneous fluid collections adjacent to the punctured vessel. Dynamic compression helps assess expansion.
2. **Thrombosis and Embolization:**
   - Acute vascular thrombosis appears as echogenic intraluminal material with failure of complete vessel compression on B-mode, accompanied by absent or dampened color and spectral Doppler flow.
3. **Vasospasm:**
   - Severe luminal narrowing induced by mechanical stimulation, needle trauma, or repeated passes. Ultrasound documents marked reduction in luminal diameter with increased Doppler flow resistance.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 6.3. Specific Arterial Access Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

1. **Arterial Dissection:**
   - Separation of the intimal layer from the media, presenting on ultrasound as an echogenic intimal flap undulating within the arterial lumen, creating true and false lumens with differential Doppler velocities.
2. **Pseudoaneurysm (False Aneurysm):**
   - Occurs when an arterial puncture site fails to seal, resulting in pulsating blood leaking into periarterial tissues contained by an adventitial/fibrous capsule. Ultrasound reveals an extraluminal pulsating cystic cavity connected to the native artery by a communicating neck.
   - **Pathognomonic Color Doppler Sign:** The classic **"yin-yang sign"** (swirling bidirectional red-and-blue flow within the aneurysm sac) and a characteristic **"to-and-fro" high-velocity waveform** across the narrow neck on spectral Doppler.
3. **Arteriovenous Fistula (AVF):**
   - Results from simultaneous through-and-through puncture of an adjacent artery and vein. Color Doppler demonstrates high-velocity turbulent color mosaic in the perivascular tissue, while spectral Doppler reveals arterialized, high-velocity, low-resistance pulsatile flow within the draining vein.

<span id="fig-21"></span>
![Figure 21: Sonographic features of vascular access complications](images/fig21_vascular_access_complications.jpg)
*Figure 21: Sonographic features of vascular access complications. (A) Acute arterial dissection with intraluminal flap. (B) Pseudoaneurysm demonstrating classic bidirectional swirling color flow ("yin-yang" sign) and narrow neck. (C) Arteriovenous fistula with high-velocity turbulent mosaic connection between artery and vein. (D) Complete occlusive intraluminal venous thrombosis.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 6.4. Site-Specific Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

- **IJV:** Accidental carotid artery puncture, carotid dissection, stroke, neck hematoma compromising airway, thoracic duct laceration (left IJV).
- **SCV / Axillary:** Pneumothorax, hemothorax, subclavian artery pseudoaneurysm, phrenic nerve palsy.
- **Femoral:** Retroperitoneal hematoma (life-threatening occult blood loss), femoral nerve compression, bowel puncture.
- **PICC:** Upper extremity DVT, median/ulnar nerve injury, brachial artery inadvertent puncture.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
### 6.5. Guidewire Safety and Equipment Malposition</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Lost Guidewire:** A preventable catastrophic error occurring when the operator fails to maintain continuous manual control of the distal wire tip during catheter introduction.
- **Guidewire Looping or Kinking:** Direct ultrasound confirms whether the wire is smoothly tracking central vessels or has retroflexed into collateral branches (e.g., IJV wire looping into the subclavian vein or internal mammary vein).

</div></details>

</div></details>

### 7.3. Pediatric Arterial Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

RCT evidence confirms that ultrasound guidance for pediatric radial artery cannulation increases first-attempt success ($65	ext{--}78\%$ vs $30	ext{--}35\%$), shortens cannulation time, and prevents vessel occlusion.

##### Recommendations for Pediatric Arterial Access
> **Recommendation 1:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for radial artery cannulation in pediatric patients to increase first-attempt success and reduce procedural time and attempts. (**Grade 1B**)
>
> **Recommendation 2:** Real-time ultrasound guidance is recommended for femoral artery cannulation in infants and children. (**Grade 1B**)

## 10. Conclusions</summary><div class="details-content">

The use of real-time point-of-care ultrasound for vascular cannulation represents a fundamental patient safety advancement. Integrating ultrasound before, during, and after vessel puncture significantly improves overall and first-pass success, reduces procedural time, and drastically curtails serious mechanical complications across diverse venous and arterial access sites in both adult and pediatric populations.

Clinicians must adhere to rigorous procedural standards: performing comprehensive pre-access scans, employing real-time needle visualization with dynamic tip control, and mandating definitive sonographic confirmation of the guidewire within the vascular lumen in two orthogonal planes prior to dilation. Institutional investment in simulation-based education and standardized credentialing is essential to translate these recommendations into uniform clinical excellence.

</div></details>
