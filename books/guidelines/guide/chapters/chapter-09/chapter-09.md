## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Key Concepts and Paradigm Shifts in the 2019 Guidelines
* **Diagnostic Strategy:** D-dimer cut-offs adjusted for age ($age \times 10\ \mu\text{g/L}$ in patients $> 50$ years) or adapted to clinical pre-test probability (YEARS algorithm: $1000\ \text{ng/mL}$ if 0 items, $500\ \text{ng/mL}$ if $\ge 1$ item) are established alternatives to fixed cut-offs, significantly reducing unnecessary CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA).
* **Hemodynamic Instability Definition:** Delineates high-risk PE by three overt clinical presentations: cardiac arrest (CPR required), obstructive shock (systolic BP $< 90\ \text{mmHg}$ or vasopressors required with end-organ hypoperfusion), or persistent hypotension (systolic BP $< 90\ \text{mmHg}$ or drop $\ge 40\ \text{mmHg}$ for $> 15\ \text{min}$ not due to sepsis, arrhythmia, or hypovolemia).
* **Prognostic Stratification:** Stratifies acute PE into high-, intermediate-high-, intermediate-low-, and low-risk categories based on hemodynamic stability, clinical scores (PESI class III–V or sPESI $\ge 1$), right ventricular (RV) dysfunction on imaging (echocardiography or CTPA), and elevated cardiac biomarkers (cardiac troponins, natriuretic peptides).
* **RV Dysfunction Caution:** RV pressure overload and dysfunction may be present and adversely affect early outcome even in patients classified as 'low risk' by clinical scores alone.
* **Acute Reperfusion:** Systemic thrombolysis is Class I for high-risk PE. Surgical pulmonary embolectomy or percutaneous catheter-directed therapy are Class IIa alternatives when thrombolysis is contraindicated or has failed. Routine primary thrombolysis is Class III (not recommended) for intermediate- or low-risk PE.
* **Oral Anticoagulation:** Non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs: apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) are recommended as first-choice therapy in eligible patients (Class I A over VKAs).
* **Duration of Anticoagulation:** All patients require $\ge 3$ months of therapeutic anticoagulation. Risk factors for recurrence are categorized as major transient ($< 3\%/\text{year}$ after withdrawal; stop at 3 months), minor transient / persistent non-malignant ($3\text{--}8\%/\text{year}$; consider extension), and high persistent risk ($> 8\%/\text{year}$; indefinite anticoagulation indicated, including antiphospholipid syndrome and active cancer). The obsolete terms "provoked" vs "unprovoked" are abandoned.
* **Reduced-Dose NOACs:** Reduced doses of apixaban ($2.5\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d.) or rivaroxaban ($10\ \text{mg}$ o.d.) should be considered for extended secondary prophylaxis after the first 6 months.
* **Cancer-Associated Thrombosis:** Edoxaban or rivaroxaban should be considered as alternatives to LMWH in patients without gastrointestinal malignancies.
* **Pregnancy:** Dedicated diagnostic pathway combining clinical rules, D-dimer, venous compression ultrasound, and low-dose imaging (perfusion scan or CTPA). Therapeutic weight-adjusted LMWH is the treatment of choice; NOACs are strictly contraindicated.
* **Post-PE Care & CTEPH:** Systematic multidisciplinary outpatient follow-up at 3–6 months to assess persistent dyspnea, functional limitation, and screen for chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH).

## 1. Preamble and 2. Introduction</summary><div class="details-content">

Guidelines summarize and evaluate available evidence with the aim of assisting health professionals in proposing the best management strategies for an individual patient with a given condition. Guidelines and their recommendations facilitate decision making in daily clinical practice. However, the final decisions concerning an individual patient must be made by the responsible health professional in consultation with the patient and caregiver.

The 2019 Guidelines follow previous ESC Guidelines published in 2000, 2008, and 2014. These Guidelines focus on the diagnosis and management of acute PE in adult patients.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### What is New in the 2019 Guidelines?</summary><div class="details-content">

#### New and Revised Concepts in 2019
* **Diagnosis:** D-dimer cut-offs adjusted for age or clinical probability can be used as alternatives to fixed cut-offs. Updated radiation dose estimates for CTPA and V/Q scans are provided.
* **Risk Assessment:** Clear definition of hemodynamic instability and high-risk PE. Assessment of PE severity and early PE-related risk is recommended in addition to comorbidity. RV dysfunction can affect early outcomes even in patients classified as 'low risk' by clinical scores.
* **Acute Phase Treatment:** Thoroughly revised section on hemodynamic and respiratory support for high-risk PE. Dedicated algorithm for high-risk PE. NOACs are recommended as first choice over VKAs. Risk-adjusted management incorporates clinical severity, comorbidities, and RV dysfunction.
* **Chronic Treatment after First 3 Months:** VTE recurrence risk factors categorized as high ($> 8\%/\text{year}$), intermediate ($3\text{--}8\%/\text{year}$), or low ($< 3\%/\text{year}$). The terms "provoked" vs "unprovoked" are no longer supported. Reduced-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban is considered after 6 months.
* **Cancer:** Edoxaban or rivaroxaban considered alternatives to LMWH, with caution in gastrointestinal cancer due to bleeding risk.
* **Pregnancy:** Dedicated diagnostic algorithm; updated radiation absorption data.
* **Long-Term Sequelae:** Integrated care model proposed; comprehensive follow-up algorithm for post-PE functional limitation and CTEPH.

#### Changes in Recommendations (2014 vs 2019)
* **Rescue thrombolytic therapy** for patients who deteriorate hemodynamically on anticoagulation upgraded from **IIa** to **I**.
* **Surgical embolectomy or catheter-directed treatment** as alternatives to rescue thrombolysis upgraded from **IIb** to **IIa**.
* **D-dimer measurement and clinical prediction rules** in pregnancy or post-partum upgraded from **IIb** to **IIa**.
* **Further evaluation for asymptomatic PE survivors** at increased risk for CTEPH upgraded from **III** to **IIb**.

## 3. General Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 3.1 Epidemiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), clinically presenting as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE), is globally the third most frequent acute cardiovascular syndrome behind myocardial infarction and stroke. Annual incidence rates range from 39 to 115 per 100,000 population for PE, and from 53 to 162 per 100,000 for DVT. Cross-sectional data show that the incidence of VTE is almost eight times higher in individuals aged $\ge 80$ years than in the fifth decade of life. Longitudinal studies reveal a rising annual PE incidence rate over time, posing an escalating burden on healthcare systems with annual expenditures of up to 8.5 billion euros in the European Union.

PE causes up to 300,000 deaths annually in the United States. In six European countries (population 454.4 million), over 370,000 deaths were VTE-related in 2004: 34% presented with sudden fatal collapse before therapy could take effect, and in 59% PE was diagnosed only post-mortem. Time-trend analyses indicate decreasing case fatality rates over the past 15 years, reflecting earlier diagnosis, more effective interventions, and guideline adherence ([Fig. 1](#fig-1)), although modern overdiagnosis of subsegmental defects may artificially inflate the denominator.

### 3.2 Predisposing Factors</summary><div class="details-content">

VTE results from the convergence between patient-related (usually permanent) and setting-related (usually temporary) risk factors ([Table 3](#table-3)). Major trauma, surgery, lower-limb fractures, joint arthroplasty, and spinal cord injury represent strong provoking triggers ($	ext{odds ratio } > 10$). Active malignancy carries high thrombotic risk (highest in hematological, lung, gastrointestinal, pancreatic, and brain cancers).

Estrogen-containing combined oral contraceptives confer a 2- to 6-fold increase in VTE risk, particularly third-generation formulations containing desogestrel or gestodene. Progesterone-only pills and levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine devices do not significantly increase VTE risk and are preferred in women with personal or family history of thrombosis. Infection, blood transfusions, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents, and shared cardiovascular risk factors (smoking, obesity, hypertension, diabetes) further contribute to thrombotic risk.

### 3.3 Pathophysiology and Determinants of Outcome</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute PE impairs both pulmonary circulation and gas exchange. Right ventricular (RV) failure secondary to acute pressure overload is the primary cause of death in severe PE ([Fig. 2](#fig-2)). Mean pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) increases acutely when $> 30\text{--}50\%$ of the total cross-sectional area of the pulmonary arterial bed is occluded. Thromboxane A2 and serotonin release induce reflex vasoconstriction, compounding mechanical obstruction. A non-preconditioned, thin-walled RV is incapable of generating a mean PAP $> 40\ \text{mmHg}$.

<span id="fig-2"></span>![](fig2_hemodynamic_collapse_pe.jpg)

**FIGURE 2.** Key factors contributing to hemodynamic collapse and death in acute pulmonary embolism. Acute RV afterload increase triggers RV dilation, tricuspid regurgitation, neurohormonal activation, wall tension elevation, and myocardial ischemia, culminating in obstructive shock.

The abrupt increase in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) leads to RV dilation, shifting the interventricular septum toward the left ventricle (interventricular interdependence). Leftward septal bowing impedes early diastolic LV filling, reducing LV preload, stroke volume, and systemic cardiac output. Systemic hypotension diminishes coronary perfusion pressure to the hypertrophied, hypertensive RV myocardium, producing an oxygen supply-demand mismatch, RV ischemia, cardiogenic shock, and cardiovascular collapse ([Fig. 2](#fig-2)).

Respiratory failure results from ventilation-perfusion mismatch, intrapulmonary arteriovenous shunting, low mixed venous oxygen saturation ($S\bar{v}O_2$), and right-to-left shunting through a patent foramen ovale (present in ~ 30% of patients), which also creates an avenue for paradoxical systemic arterial embolization.

Hemodynamic instability defines acute high-risk PE through three distinct clinical manifestations ([Table 4](#table-4)).

## 4. Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinical signs and symptoms of acute PE are non-specific: dyspnea (acute or worsening), pleuritic chest pain, substernal angina-like pain, cough, hemoptysis, syncope, and presyncope. Syncope occurs in up to 17% of patients and correlates with central emboli, hemodynamic instability, and RV dysfunction. Arterial hypoxemia and hypocapnia are frequent, but up to 40% of patients exhibit normal oxygen saturation ($SaO_2$). ECG signs of RV strain ($S_1Q_3T_3$ pattern, T-wave inversions in $V_1\text{--}V_4$, right bundle branch block) are observed in severe cases; sinus tachycardia is present in ~ 40%.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Assessment of Clinical Probability and Diagnostic Algorithms</summary><div class="details-content">

The pre-test clinical probability of PE must be assessed using validated clinical prediction rules (revised Geneva score, Wells score) ([Table 5](#table-5)) or structured empirical gestalt. Pre-test probability stratifies patients into three levels (low: ~ 10% prevalence, intermediate: ~ 30%, high: ~ 65%) or two levels (PE-unlikely: ~ 12%, PE-likely: ~ 30%).

#### Pulmonary Embolism Rule-Out Criteria (PERC)
To avoid testing overuse in emergency settings with very low prevalence, the PERC rule encompasses eight criteria: age $< 50$ years, pulse $< 100\ \text{bpm}$, $SaO_2 > 94\%$, no unilateral leg swelling, no hemoptysis, no recent trauma/surgery, no prior VTE, and no oral hormone use. If all 8 criteria are fulfilled in a patient with low clinical gestalt, PE can be safely ruled out without laboratory testing.

#### D-Dimer Testing
Plasma D-dimer has high sensitivity ($> 95\%$) and high negative predictive value. A normal D-dimer safely excludes PE in outpatients with low/intermediate clinical probability. Fixed cut-off ($500\ \mu\text{g/L}$) specificity declines to ~ 10% in patients $> 80$ years. Two validated strategies optimize diagnostic yield:
* **Age-Adjusted Cut-off:** For patients $> 50$ years, cut-off is calculated as:
  $$\text{D-dimer cut-off} = \text{Age} \times 10\ \mu\text{g/L}$$
  Increases exclusion rate from 6.4% to 30% without increasing false-negative events.
* **YEARS Clinical Probability-Adapted Algorithm:** Uses 3 Wells items (clinical signs of DVT, hemoptysis, PE most likely diagnosis). PE excluded if 0 items and D-dimer $< 1000\ \text{ng/mL}$, or $\ge 1$ item and D-dimer $< 500\ \text{ng/mL}$. Reduces CTPA utilization by 48%.

#### Echocardiographic Assessment of Right Ventricular Pressure Overload
Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) plays a central diagnostic and risk-stratification role ([Fig. 3](#fig-3)). Key parameters indicating acute RV strain include:
* RV dilation with basal RV/LV diameter ratio $> 1.0$ in the apical 4-chamber view;
* McConnell sign (hypokinesia of the RV free wall with normal or hyperdynamic RV apex);
* 60/60 sign (pulmonary ejection acceleration time $< 60\ \text{ms}$ combined with peak systolic tricuspid gradient $< 60\ \text{mmHg}$);
* Decreased tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE $< 16\ \text{mm}$);
* Decreased peak systolic velocity of the tricuspid annulus ($S' < 9.5\ \text{cm/s}$);
* Distended inferior vena cava with diminished inspiratory collapsibility ($< 50\%$);
* Direct visualization of mobile right heart thrombi (RiHTh).

## 5. Assessment of Pulmonary Embolism Severity and the Risk of Early Death</summary><div class="details-content">

Risk stratification of patients with acute PE is mandatory for determining the appropriate monitoring and therapeutic strategy. Initial risk stratification is based on clinical signs of hemodynamic instability ([Table 4](#table-4)), which delineate high-risk PE associated with early mortality $> 15\%$. In hemodynamically stable patients, advanced risk stratification requires assessing two prognostic dimensions:
1. Indicators of PE severity and RV dysfunction (clinical, imaging, and laboratory biomarkers);
2. Pre-existing comorbidity and aggravating conditions affecting 30-day all-cause mortality.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clinical, Imaging, and Biochemical Markers of Severity</summary><div class="details-content">

#### 5.1 Clinical Parameters
Acute RV failure with low systemic cardiac output is the primary determinant of early death. Clinical markers of adverse short-term prognosis include tachycardia ($> 110\ \text{bpm}$), borderline systolic BP ($90\text{--}100\ \text{mmHg}$), tachypnea ($> 30\ \text{breaths/min}$), low oxygen saturation ($< 90\%$) despite oxygen, and syncope.

#### 5.2 Imaging of Right Ventricular Size and Function
* **Echocardiography (TTE):** Evidence of RV pressure overload and dysfunction is present in $> 25\%$ of unselected acute PE patients. Key prognostic findings include basal $RV/LV \text{ diameter ratio} \ge 1.0$, TAPSE $< 16\ \text{mm}$, McConnell sign, and the 60/60 sign ([Fig. 3](#fig-3)). RV dysfunction on TTE is an independent predictor of early mortality even in normotensive patients.
* **CT Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA):** An enlarged RV defined as an axial four-chamber $RV/LV \text{ ratio} \ge 1.0$ is associated with a 2.5-fold increased risk of all-cause mortality and a 5-fold increased risk of PE-related death. Contrast reflux into the inferior vena cava and hepatic veins further indicates severe RV volume and pressure overload.

#### 5.3 Laboratory Biomarkers
* **Markers of Myocardial Injury (Troponins & H-FABP):** Elevated cardiac troponin I or T reflects acute RV transmural ischemia and microinfarction due to severe wall tension and compromised coronary driving pressure. Troponin elevation occurs in 30% to 60% of acute PE patients and carries an OR of 5.9 for mortality in normotensive patients. High-sensitivity troponin assays possess high negative predictive value ($98\%$ at levels $< 14\ \text{pg/mL}$). Heart-type fatty acid-binding protein (H-FABP $\ge 6\ \text{ng/mL}$) is an early, highly sensitive marker of RV myocardial damage.
* **Markers of RV Dysfunction (BNP & NT-proBNP):** Excessive myocardial stretch triggers the release of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and N-terminal pro-BNP (NT-proBNP). Levels reflect RV hemodynamic compromise. An NT-proBNP cut-off $< 500\ \text{pg/mL}$ reliably rules out adverse early clinical outcomes, whereas levels $\ge 600\ \text{pg/mL}$ indicate high risk of clinical decompensation.
* **Other Biomarkers:** Plasma lactate $\ge 2\ \text{mmol/L}$ signals systemic hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism. Renal impairment (elevated creatinine, reduced eGFR, cystatin C), hyponatremia (OR 5.6 for in-hospital death), and copeptin $\ge 24\ \text{pmol/L}$ (OR 5.4) correlate with neurohormonal activation and early shock.

#### 5.4 Combined Prognostic Scores
Validated combined scores (Bova score, FAST score) integrate clinical variables, imaging findings, and cardiac biomarkers to refine stratification. A positive troponin combined with RV dysfunction on TTE/CTPA identifies normotensive patients at high risk of decompensation.

#### 5.6 Classification of PE Severity and Early Death Risk
Patients with acute PE are stratified into four distinct risk categories ([Table 8](#table-8)):
* **High-Risk:** Hemodynamic instability present ([Table 4](#table-4)). Early mortality $> 15\%$. Immediate reperfusion indicated.
* **Intermediate-High-Risk:** Hemodynamically stable, PESI class III–V or sPESI $\ge 1$, with **both** RV dysfunction (on TTE/CTPA) **and** elevated cardiac biomarkers (positive troponin). Early mortality 3%–10%. Close inpatient monitoring required due to risk of hemodynamic collapse.
* **Intermediate-Low-Risk:** Hemodynamically stable, PESI class III–V or sPESI $\ge 1$, with only one (or neither) positive finding between RV dysfunction and elevated cardiac troponin. Hospitalization indicated.
* **Low-Risk:** Hemodynamically stable, PESI class I–II or sPESI = 0, no RV dysfunction, and normal biomarkers. Candidates for early discharge and home outpatient treatment.

## 6. Treatment in the Acute Phase</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 6.1 Hemodynamic and Respiratory Support</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute RV failure with low systemic output is the leading cause of death in high-risk PE. Management comprises:
* **Oxygen and Ventilation:** Supplemental $O_2$ indicated if $SaO_2 < 90\%$. High-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) or non-invasive positive-pressure ventilation should be prioritized over endotracheal intubation. If mechanical ventilation is unavoidable, apply lung-protective ventilation: tidal volumes $\sim 6\ \text{mL/kg}$ lean body weight to maintain end-inspiratory plateau pressure $< 30\ \text{cm H}_2\text{O}$, with cautious positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) to avoid impeding venous return.
* **Volume Optimization:** Aggressive volume resuscitation is harmful, provoking RV overdistension, tricuspid regurgitation, and reduced LV output ([Table 9](#table-9)). Judicious fluid challenge ($\le 500\ \text{mL}$ saline or Ringer's lactate over 15–30 min) is restricted strictly to patients with normal-to-low central venous pressure (small, collapsible IVC).
* **Inotropes and Vasopressors:**
  - **Norepinephrine ($0.2	ext{--}1.0\ \mu\text{g/kg/min}$):** First-line agent in cardiogenic shock. Restores systemic blood pressure, improves ventricular interaction, and enhances RV coronary driving pressure without raising PVR.
  - **Dobutamine ($2	ext{--}20\ \mu\text{g/kg/min}$):** May be added to improve RV inotropy in low cardiac output with maintained blood pressure; caution regarding tachycardia and peripheral vasodilation.
  - **Levosimendan & Systemic Vasodilators:** Systemic vasodilators (nitrates, PDE-5 inhibitors) worsen systemic hypotension and are not recommended.
* **Mechanical Circulatory Support:** Temporary venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) provides rapid cardiopulmonary stabilization in refractory circulatory collapse or cardiac arrest, ideally as a bridge to surgical embolectomy or catheter-directed reperfusion. Impella RP right ventricular assist device may be considered in selected centers.
* **Advanced Life Support in Cardiac Arrest:** Suspected PE causing cardiac arrest with non-shockable rhythm warrants immediate bolus thrombolysis; CPR should be continued for $\ge 60\text{--}90\ \text{min}$ post-administration.

### 6.2 Initial Anticoagulation</summary><div class="details-content">

In patients with intermediate or high pre-test probability, parenteral anticoagulation must be initiated immediately while diagnostic investigations are underway.
* **Parenteral Anticoagulation:** Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH, e.g. enoxaparin $1\ \text{mg/kg}$ b.i.d. or $1.5\ \text{mg/kg}$ o.d.) or fondaparinux are preferred over unfractionated heparin (UFH) due to lower risks of major bleeding and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). UFH (bolus $80\ \text{IU/kg}$ followed by $18\ \text{IU/kg/h}$, titrated to aPTT 1.5–2.5 times control) is reserved for hemodynamically unstable high-risk PE, imminent surgical/catheter reperfusion, and severe renal failure (creatinine clearance $< 30\ \text{mL/min}$).
* **Non-Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulants (NOACs):** Direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) are recommended as first-choice therapy in eligible patients (Class I A over VKAs). Rivaroxaban ($15\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d. for 3 weeks followed by $20\ \text{mg}$ o.d.) and apixaban ($10\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d. for 7 days followed by $5\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d.) are initiated as single-oral drug regimens without heparin lead-in. Dabigatran ($150\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d.) and edoxaban ($60\ \text{mg}$ o.d.) require $\ge 5$ days of initial parenteral anticoagulation. In meta-analyses, NOACs demonstrate equivalent efficacy in preventing recurrent VTE with a 40% reduction in major bleeding, a 63% reduction in intracranial hemorrhage, and a 64% reduction in fatal bleeding compared with VKAs.
* **Vitamin K Antagonists (VKAs):** Warfarin is initiated concomitantly with parenteral heparin and continued until INR reaches 2.0–3.0 on two consecutive days, with minimum 5 days of overlap. VKAs remain mandatory in antiphospholipid syndrome and severe renal impairment.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 6.3 Reperfusion Treatment and Specialized Interventions</summary><div class="details-content">

#### 6.3.1 Systemic Thrombolysis
Thrombolytic therapy rapidly restores pulmonary perfusion, decreases PAP and PVR, and reduces RV dilation. Greatest benefit is observed within 48 hours of symptom onset. Approved regimens and contraindications are detailed in [Table 10](#table-10). Accelerated infusion of recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA, $100\ \text{mg}$ over 2 hours) is standard. In the PEITHO trial (normotensive intermediate-high risk), tenecteplase significantly prevented hemodynamic collapse compared with heparin alone, but caused a significant increase in major extracranial bleeding ($6.3\%$ vs $1.2\%$) and hemorrhagic stroke ($2.0\%$ vs $0.2\%$). Thus, primary systemic thrombolysis is restricted to high-risk PE; in intermediate-risk PE, it is reserved as rescue therapy upon hemodynamic deterioration.

#### 6.3.2 Percutaneous Catheter-Directed Treatment & 6.3.3 Surgical Embolectomy
* **Catheter-Directed Treatment (CDT):** Employs mechanical thrombus fragmentation, aspiration, or low-dose ultrasound-assisted in situ thrombolysis (e.g. EKOS system). Indicated in high-risk PE when systemic thrombolysis is contraindicated or has failed, and in selected intermediate-high risk cases failing anticoagulation.
* **Surgical Pulmonary Embolectomy:** Performed on cardiopulmonary bypass without aortic cross-clamping and cardioplegic arrest via bilateral pulmonary arteriotomy. Indicated in high-risk PE with contraindications to thrombolysis, failed thrombolysis, or right heart thrombi straddling a patent foramen ovale. Hospital mortality is $\sim 5	ext{--}12\%$, with 1-year survival $> 90\%$.
* **Multidisciplinary PE Response Teams (PERT):** Real-time activation of cardiology, pulmonology, critical care, cardiothoracic surgery, and interventional radiology to expedite decision-making.
* **Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) Filters:** Indicated only in acute PE with absolute contraindications to anticoagulation, or confirmed recurrence despite therapeutic anticoagulation. Routine placement is Class III (not recommended), as trials (PREPIC, PREPIC-2) showed no mortality benefit and doubled the long-term risk of DVT.

## 7. Integrated Risk-Adapted Diagnosis and Management</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 7.1 Diagnostic Strategies by Hemodynamic Status</summary><div class="details-content">

#### 7.1.1 Suspected Pulmonary Embolism with Hemodynamic Instability
In patients presenting with shock or persistent hypotension, clinical probability of massive PE is extremely high. The immediate bedside test of choice is **transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)** ([Fig. 4](#fig-4)).
* If signs of acute RV overload/dysfunction are **absent**, PE is virtually excluded as the cause of hemodynamic collapse, and immediate search for alternative etiologies (cardiac tamponade, aortic dissection, acute myocardial infarction, hypovolemic shock) must be pursued.
* If signs of RV dysfunction are **present** and the patient is too critically unstable for transfer, emergency reperfusion therapy (systemic thrombolysis or bedside surgical/catheter intervention) should be initiated immediately based on echocardiographic findings alone ([Fig. 4](#fig-4)). Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or bilateral venous CUS can provide rapid confirmatory evidence at the bedside.
* If the patient can be stabilized sufficiently, immediate emergency **CTPA** confirms pulmonary arterial occlusion before definitive reperfusion ([Fig. 4](#fig-4)).

<span id="fig-4"></span>![](fig4_diag_high_risk_pe.jpg)

**FIGURE 4.** Diagnostic algorithm for patients with suspected high-risk pulmonary embolism presenting with hemodynamic instability.

#### 7.1.2 Suspected Pulmonary Embolism without Hemodynamic Instability
The diagnostic pathway is guided by pre-test clinical probability (three-level: low, intermediate, high; or two-level: PE-unlikely, PE-likely) ([Fig. 5](#fig-5)).
* **Low or Intermediate Probability / PE-Unlikely:** Measure plasma D-dimer using a high-sensitivity assay or an age-adjusted / probability-adapted cut-off (YEARS algorithm). A negative D-dimer safely excludes PE without imaging (failure rate $< 1.0\%$). If D-dimer is positive, proceed to CTPA.
* **High Clinical Probability / PE-Likely:** Proceed directly to multidetector CTPA without D-dimer testing, as a negative D-dimer does not reliably rule out PE in this cohort. A segmental or more proximal filling defect establishes the diagnosis; normal CTPA reliably excludes PE.
* **Ventilation/Perfusion (V/Q) Scintigraphy:** An established alternative to CTPA in patients with elevated D-dimer and contraindications to iodinated contrast (severe renal impairment, severe iodine allergy) or in young female patients to limit radiation exposure to breast tissue. A normal perfusion scan excludes PE; a high-probability scan confirms PE when clinical probability is non-low.

### 7.2 Risk-Adjusted Management Strategy and Early Discharge</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-6"></span>![](fig6_central_management_strategy.jpg)

**FIGURE 6 (Central Illustration).** Risk-adjusted management strategy for acute pulmonary embolism. Therapeutic anticoagulation is initiated promptly. High-risk patients undergo immediate primary reperfusion and hemodynamic support. Normotensive patients are stratified by clinical severity (PESI / sPESI / Hestia criteria) and RV dysfunction: intermediate-high-risk patients require hospital monitoring for rescue reperfusion upon deterioration; intermediate-low-risk patients are hospitalized; selected low-risk patients without serious comorbidity or social barriers are eligible for early discharge and outpatient home treatment.

#### 7.2.1 Emergency Treatment of High-Risk PE
High-risk PE mandates immediate hemodynamic stabilization, respiratory optimization, intravenous UFH bolus, and primary reperfusion—primarily full-dose systemic thrombolysis ([Table 10](#table-10)). In the event of thrombolysis failure or absolute contraindication, surgical pulmonary embolectomy or catheter-directed therapy is indicated. Once hemodynamically stable, patients are transitioned from parenteral heparin to oral anticoagulation based on clinical judgment.

#### 7.2.2 Treatment of Intermediate-Risk PE
Anticoagulation is the cornerstone of therapy. In **intermediate-high-risk PE** (both RV dysfunction on TTE/CTPA and positive troponin present), the risk of early hemodynamic decompensation peaks within the first 48–72 hours (mean time to collapse $1.79 \pm 1.60\ \text{days}$ in the PEITHO trial). These patients should be hospitalized in an intermediate care unit with continuous telemetry monitoring. Routine primary thrombolysis is contraindicated due to unacceptable intracranial hemorrhage risk; rescue thrombolysis or embolectomy is reserved strictly for patients who develop clinical signs of hemodynamic collapse. In **intermediate-low-risk PE**, standard inpatient ward care with oral anticoagulation is appropriate.

#### 7.2.3 Management of Low-Risk PE: Triage for Early Discharge and Home Treatment
Early discharge (within 24–48 hours) and outpatient home anticoagulation can be safely considered if three conditions are fulfilled:
1. Low early PE-related mortality risk (PESI class I–II, sPESI = 0, or Hestia criteria negative);
2. Absence of severe comorbid conditions (cancer requiring hospitalization, severe renal/hepatic impairment, high bleeding risk);
3. Reliable outpatient infrastructure and patient adherence (adequate home social support, access to emergency care).

The **Hestia exclusion criteria** comprise a clinical checklist (hemodynamic instability, thrombolysis/embolectomy indicated, active bleeding/high bleeding risk, $> 24\ \text{h}$ oxygen requirement, IV pain medication, severe renal/hepatic disease, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, pregnancy, social barriers). If all criteria are absent, home treatment achieves a 3-month recurrent VTE rate of $2.0\%$ and $< 1.0\%$ mortality. Excluding RV dysfunction on TTE or CTPA before early discharge is strongly advised to prevent discharging patients with occult intermediate-risk PE.

## 8. Chronic Treatment and Prevention of Recurrence</summary><div class="details-content">

The objective of chronic anticoagulation is to complete treatment of the acute index event and prevent recurrent venous thromboembolism. All patients with acute PE require a minimum of **3 months** of therapeutic anticoagulation. Beyond 3 months, the decision to extend anticoagulation indefinitely is governed by balancing the individual risk of VTE recurrence against the long-term risk of major bleeding.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 8.1 Categorization of VTE Recurrence Risk</summary><div class="details-content">

Recurrence rates after stopping anticoagulation are strongly dictated by the characteristics of the index event ([Table 11](#table-11)). The 2019 Guidelines discard the traditional dichotomy of "provoked" vs "unprovoked" VTE in favor of risk categorization based on long-term recurrence rates after treatment withdrawal:
* **Low Recurrence Risk ($< 3\%/\text{year}$):** Major transient or reversible provoking risk factors associated with $> 10$-fold increased initial risk (e.g., major surgery under general anesthesia $> 30\ \text{min}$, bed rest in hospital $\ge 3\ \text{days}$ for acute medical illness, major trauma with fractures). Anticoagulation is discontinued at 3 months.
* **Intermediate Recurrence Risk ($3\text{--}8\%/\text{year}$):** Minor transient or reversible risk factors (minor surgery $< 30\ \text{min}$, hospital admission $< 3\ \text{days}$, estrogen therapy/contraception, pregnancy/puerperium, long-haul travel, immobilization at home) or non-malignant persistent risk factors (active inflammatory bowel disease, active autoimmune disease). Extended anticoagulation should be considered.
* **High Recurrence Risk ($> 8\%/\text{year}$):** Active cancer, recurrent VTE in the absence of major transient triggers, and major persistent thrombophilias (antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, antithrombin, protein C or S deficiency, homozygous factor V Leiden or prothrombin G20210A). Indefinite anticoagulation is recommended.

### 8.2 Bleeding Risk Assessment and 8.3 Extended Anticoagulation Regimens</summary><div class="details-content">

Bleeding risk must be assessed at the initiation of anticoagulation and reassessed periodically (annually in low-risk, every 3–6 months in high-risk patients). Risk factors include advanced age ($> 75$ years), prior bleeding, anemia, active cancer, renal or hepatic impairment, concomitant antiplatelet or NSAID therapy, and labile INRs on VKAs.

#### Extended Anticoagulation Regimens with NOACs
Phase III extension trials demonstrate that extended NOAC therapy reduces recurrent VTE by $\sim 80\text{--}90\%$ with major bleeding rates $\sim 1\%$.
* **Apixaban:** $2.5\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d. or $5\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d. In AMPLIFY-EXT, apixaban $2.5\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d. reduced recurrent VTE by 81% compared with placebo, with major bleeding rates ($0.2\%$) identical to placebo.
* **Rivaroxaban:** $10\ \text{mg}$ o.d. or $20\ \text{mg}$ o.d. In EINSTEIN-CHOICE, rivaroxaban $10\ \text{mg}$ o.d. reduced recurrent VTE by 74% compared with aspirin ($100\ \text{mg}$), with no increase in major bleeding ($0.4\%$).
* **Dabigatran:** $150\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d. (RE-SONATE, RE-MEDY).
* **Edoxaban:** $60\ \text{mg}$ o.d. (reduced to $30\ \text{mg}$ in moderate renal impairment).
* **Alternative Agents:** In patients refusing or unable to tolerate oral anticoagulants, aspirin ($100\ \text{mg}$ o.d.) or sulodexide ($2 \times 250\ \text{LSU}$ b.i.d.) may be considered, although both are significantly less effective than NOACs.

### 8.5 Management of Pulmonary Embolism in Patients with Cancer</summary><div class="details-content">

Active cancer is a potent trigger for VTE recurrence (risk $> 8\%/\text{year}$) and major bleeding complications while anticoagulated. Low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH, dalteparin $200\ \text{IU/kg}$ o.d. for 1 month then $150\ \text{IU/kg}$ o.d.) has historically been the standard of care based on trials (CLOT) demonstrating a 40% reduction in recurrent VTE compared with VKAs without increased major bleeding.

Recent trials comparing oral factor Xa inhibitors with LMWH in cancer-associated thrombosis:
* **Edoxaban (Hokusai VTE Cancer):** Edoxaban ($60\ \text{mg}$ o.d. following $\ge 5$ days LMWH) was non-inferior to dalteparin in preventing recurrent VTE or major bleeding over 12 months. However, major bleeding was higher with edoxaban ($6.9\%$ vs $4.0\%$), driven entirely by bleeding in patients with gastrointestinal cancers.
* **Rivaroxaban (SELECT-D):** Rivaroxaban ($15\ \text{mg}$ b.i.d. for 3 weeks then $20\ \text{mg}$ o.d.) showed significantly lower recurrent VTE ($4\%$ vs $11\%$, HR 0.43) compared with dalteparin, but higher clinically relevant non-major bleeding ($13\%$ vs $4\%$), particularly in gastrointestinal and urothelial malignancies.

Therefore, LMWH remains preferred in patients with gastrointestinal luminal tumors, active mucosal bleeding, severe renal impairment, or severe chemotherapy-induced nausea/malabsorption. Edoxaban or rivaroxaban are valid alternatives in patients without gastrointestinal cancer. Anticoagulation should be continued indefinitely or until the malignancy is actively cured. Incidental PE detected on oncological staging CT scans should be treated identically to symptomatic PE.

## 9. Pulmonary Embolism and Pregnancy</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute PE remains one of the leading causes of direct maternal mortality in high-income countries. The risk of VTE is increased throughout pregnancy, reaching a peak during the post-partum period. Risk is amplified by assisted reproduction techniques (in vitro fertilization carries an HR of 1.77 overall and 4.22 in the first trimester), prior VTE, obesity, pre-eclampsia, cesarean section, and post-partum hemorrhage.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 9.1–9.2 Diagnostic Strategy in Pregnancy</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinical signs of PE overlap with physiologic symptoms of normal pregnancy. D-dimer levels rise progressively with gestational age, limiting the specificity of standard cut-offs, though a negative high-sensitivity D-dimer retains high negative predictive value.

The **pregnancy-adapted YEARS algorithm** combines three clinical criteria (signs of DVT, hemoptysis, PE most likely diagnosis) with D-dimer levels:
* If 0 YEARS items and D-dimer $< 1000\ \text{ng/mL}$, or $\ge 1$ item and D-dimer $< 500\ \text{ng/mL}$, PE is ruled out without imaging.
* In prospective management trials, this algorithm safely ruled out PE without CTPA in up to 32% to 48% of pregnant women, with a 3-month failure rate of $0.21\%$.

#### Diagnostic Imaging in Pregnancy
Radiation exposure to the fetus with modern imaging modalities is minimal ($< 0.5\ \text{mGy}$), well below the threshold for fetal damage or oncogenesis ($50\text{--}100\ \text{mGy}$) ([Table 12](#table-12)). A normal chest X-ray followed by a low-dose perfusion lung scan or low-radiation protocol CTPA safely excludes PE without compromising maternal breast or fetal safety. Compression ultrasonography (CUS) should be performed first if signs of DVT are present, as finding proximal DVT justifies therapeutic anticoagulation and obviates ionizing thoracic radiation.

### 9.3 Treatment of PE in Pregnancy, Delivery, and Amniotic Fluid Embolism</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anticoagulation Regimens and Delivery Management
* **LMWH:** Treatment of choice. Does not cross the placenta, does not confer fetal hemorrhage or teratogenicity, and has superior pharmacokinetics compared to UFH. Dose is adjusted to early pregnancy body weight ($100\ \text{IU/kg}$ b.i.d. or $150\ \text{IU/kg}$ o.d.). Routine anti-Xa monitoring is reserved for extremes of body weight or renal failure.
* **VKAs & NOACs:** VKAs cross the placenta, causing warfarin embryopathy in the first trimester and intracranial hemorrhage or placental abruption in the third trimester. NOACs are strictly contraindicated throughout pregnancy and lactation.
* **Labor and Delivery Management:** Planned delivery is coordinated by a multidisciplinary Pregnancy Heart Team. Therapeutic LMWH must be stopped $\ge 24\ \text{hours}$ prior to planned induction of labor or regional (spinal/epidural) anesthesia to avoid spinal hematoma. LMWH should not be administered within 4 hours of epidural catheter removal. In high-risk situations, convert LMWH to IV UFH $\ge 36\ \text{hours}$ before delivery, discontinuing infusion 4–6 hours prior to anticipated anesthesia.
* **Post-partum Anticoagulation:** Administered for $\ge 6\ \text{weeks}$ post-partum with total duration $\ge 3\ \text{months}$. LMWH and warfarin are safe during breastfeeding; NOACs are not.

#### 9.4 Amniotic Fluid Embolism (AFE)
A rare, catastrophic pregnancy-specific emergency characterized by sudden cardiovascular collapse, profound hypoxemia, and severe disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Occurs during labor or within minutes of delivery. Treatment is immediate, aggressive hemodynamic and respiratory resuscitation with blood product and clotting factor replacement.

## 10. Long-Term Sequelae of Pulmonary Embolism and Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension</summary><div class="details-content">

Following acute PE, pulmonary arterial patency is restored in the majority of survivors within months; routine follow-up CTPA is not indicated in asymptomatic patients. However, persistent dyspnea, exercise intolerance, and reduced physical performance occur in 20% to 75% of patients (post-PE syndrome), frequently related to physical deconditioning, obesity, or coexisting cardiopulmonary comorbidities rather than large residual thrombi.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 10.2 Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension (CTEPH)</summary><div class="details-content">

CTEPH is a progressive, life-threatening pulmonary vascular disorder ([Table 13](#table-13)) caused by fibrotic organization of persistent thromboemboli in the pulmonary arterial tree, accompanied by secondary microvascular remodeling. The cumulative incidence is estimated between 0.1% and 9.1% within 2 years of acute PE.

#### Diagnosis of CTEPH
Diagnostic confirmation requires findings documented after at least **3 months** of therapeutic anticoagulation:
1. Mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) $\ge 25\ \text{mmHg}$ with pulmonary arterial wedge pressure (PAWP) $\le 15\ \text{mmHg}$ on resting right heart catheterization;
2. Multiple mismatched segmental perfusion defects on planar V/Q scan (sensitivity 96–97%, superior to CTPA for screening);
3. Specific chronic thromboembolic vascular lesions on CTPA, dual-energy CT, or pulmonary cineangiography (ring-like stenoses, webs, slits, pouches, and chronic total occlusions).

#### Treatment Modalities for CTEPH
1. **Surgical Pulmonary Endarterectomy (PEA):** Treatment of choice for operable CTEPH. Involves bilateral true endarterectomy through the medial layer under cardiopulmonary bypass with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA). Operative mortality in high-volume expert centers is $< 4.7\%$, leading to near-normalization of hemodynamics and substantial functional recovery.
2. **Balloon Pulmonary Angioplasty (BPA):** Stepwise transcatheter dilation of inoperable subsegmental lesions or persistent/recurrent PH post-PEA. Registry data show significant mPAP reduction and 1-year survival $> 95\%$.
3. **Medical Therapy:** Lifelong oral anticoagulation with VKAs is mandatory in all patients. **Riociguat** (oral soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator) is approved for inoperable CTEPH or persistent/recurrent PH after PEA, significantly improving 6-minute walk distance and lowering PVR.

## 11. Non-Thrombotic PE, 12. Key Messages, and 14. 'What to Do' and 'What Not to Do' Synthesis</summary><div class="details-content">

### 11. Non-Thrombotic Pulmonary Embolism
Non-thrombotic pulmonary embolism encompasses occlusion of the pulmonary vasculature by non-thrombotic cellular or exogenous matter: septic emboli (tricuspid endocarditis, infected lines), fat embolism (long-bone orthopedic fractures), air embolism (central venous cannulation, barotrauma), amniotic fluid embolism, tumor embolism (renal cell, hepatic carcinoma), and foreign material (polymethylmethacrylate bone cement, intravenous drug abuse talc). Management focuses on organ support, surgical/endovascular extraction when indicated, and addressing the specific underlying pathology.

### 12. Key Messages (Ten Core Principles of PE Management)
1. **Hemodynamic Instability:** Perform immediate bedside TTE to differentiate acute RV failure from other life-threatening conditions (tamponade, dissection, acute valve failure).
2. **Immediate Anticoagulation:** Institute parenteral anticoagulation without delay when PE is suspected while diagnostic workup is underway.
3. **Validated Diagnostic Algorithms:** Use standardized pre-test probability assessment and D-dimer testing to avoid unnecessary radiation and imaging costs.
4. **Subsegmental PE Caution:** Scrutinize isolated subsegmental CTPA defects to prevent false-positive overdiagnosis and inappropriate anticoagulation.
5. **Stratification in Normotension:** Evaluate RV size/function (TTE or CTPA) and cardiac biomarkers (troponins) in all normotensive PE patients.
6. **High-Risk Reperfusion:** Select the optimal reperfusion strategy (systemic thrombolysis, surgical embolectomy, catheter-directed therapy) immediately for high-risk PE.
7. **NOAC Preference:** Prioritize oral factor Xa or direct thrombin inhibitors (NOACs) over traditional LMWH-VKA regimens unless contraindicated.
8. **Lifelong Recurrence Awareness:** Re-evaluate patients at 3–6 months to balance recurrence vs bleeding risks and determine anticoagulation duration.
9. **Pregnancy Safety:** Utilize validated diagnostic algorithms (YEARS) and low-dose imaging protocols (perfusion scan or CTPA); LMWH is drug of choice.
10. **Post-PE Follow-up:** Implement structured follow-up at 3–6 months to assess persistent functional limitation and screen for CTEPH.
