# Improving Oxygenation and Management of Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Oxygenation Basics and F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> Adjustment
* **Hypoxia vs. Hypoxemia:** Hypoxia = reduced O<sub>2</sub> in tissues (four types: hypoxemic, anemic, circulatory, histotoxic). Hypoxemia = reduced PaO<sub>2</sub> (<80 mm Hg) and SaO<sub>2</sub> (<95%).
* **Linear relationship between PaO<sub>2</sub> and F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>:** Known PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> = Desired PaO<sub>2</sub>/Desired F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>. Allows precise F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> titration.
* **P/F ratio (PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>):** Normally ~500 (100/0.21). Used to set F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> and classify ARDS severity.
* **Mean airway pressure (\(\overline{P}_{aw}\)):** Major determinant of oxygenation in ARDS. Increases with higher PEEP, prolonged inspiratory time, inverse ratio ventilation. Calculated as (PIP – PEEP) × (inspiratory time/total cycle time) + PEEP.
* **O<sub>2</sub> toxicity:** Avoid F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> >0.6 if possible. Target PaO<sub>2</sub> 60–90 mm Hg, SpO<sub>2</sub> ≥90%.

### PEEP and CPAP Fundamentals
* **Atelectasis pathophysiology:** Collapse of alveoli from airway obstruction, shallow breathing, or surfactant deficiency. Leads to shunt (perfusion without ventilation).
* **Goals of PEEP/CPAP:** Maintain PaO<sub>2</sub> ≥60 mm Hg (SpO<sub>2</sub> ≥90%), recruit alveoli, restore FRC, enhance tissue oxygenation.
* **PEEP** = positive end-expiratory pressure during mechanical ventilation. **CPAP** = positive pressure throughout spontaneous breathing (no mandatory breaths).
* **Low (physiological) PEEP:** 3–5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O – prevents loss of FRC from supine position/intubation.
* **Therapeutic PEEP:** ≥5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O, up to >20 cm H<sub>2</sub>O in ARDS. Requires careful monitoring.
* **Optimal PEEP:** Level at which static compliance is highest after recruitment maneuver (decremental PEEP study). Originally defined by Suter (1975) as maximum beneficial effects (↑DO<sub>2</sub>, ↑FRC, ↑C<sub>S</sub>, ↓shunt) without cardiovascular compromise.

### Selecting Optimal PEEP – Practical Approach
* **Increments:** 3–5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O in adults, hold 15 minutes, measure parameters.
* **Key monitored parameters (Box 13.7):** BP, breath sounds, PIP, P<sub>plat</sub>, C<sub>S</sub>, PaO<sub>2</sub>, PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, P<sub>(A-a)</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, P<sub>(a-et)</sub>CO<sub>2</sub>, hemodynamics (C.O., PvO<sub>2</sub>, PAOP).
* **Static compliance (C<sub>S</sub>):** Increases as FRC is restored, decreases when overdistention occurs (duck-billed appearance on pressure-volume loop, Fig. 13.6).
* **P<sub>(a-et)</sub>CO<sub>2</sub> gradient:** Normal 4.5±2.5 mm Hg. Widening indicates excessive PEEP → ↓C.O., ↑V<sub>D</sub>/V<sub>T</sub>.
* **Mixed venous O<sub>2</sub> (PvO<sub>2</sub>):** Normally 35–40 mm Hg (SvO<sub>2</sub> 75%). Minimal acceptable PvO<sub>2</sub> ~28 mm Hg (SvO<sub>2</sub> ~50%).
* **O<sub>2</sub> delivery (DO<sub>2</sub>):** Cardiac output × CaO<sub>2</sub>. Normal ~1000 mL/min. Optimal PEEP maximizes DO<sub>2</sub>.

### Hemodynamic Monitoring with High PEEP
* PEEP >15 cm H<sub>2</sub>O often requires pulmonary artery catheter.
* Catheter tip must be in Zone 3 (dependent lung) for accurate PAOP (Fig. 13.8).
* PEEP can falsely elevate PAOP (overinflation) or lower it (reduced venous return → relative hypovolemia).
* Table 13.6: Example – PEEP 15 cm H<sub>2</sub>O increased PaO<sub>2</sub> from 90 to 450 mm Hg without significant change in C.O.

### Contraindications and Physiological Effects
* **Absolute contraindication:** Untreated tension pneumothorax.
* **Relative contraindications:** Hypovolemia, bronchopleural fistula, unilateral lung disease (may require independent lung ventilation).
* **Pulmonary effects:** Recruits collapsed alveoli but can overdistend already open units (ventral regions). Maintain P<sub>plat</sub> <30 cm H<sub>2</sub>O.
* **Cardiovascular effects:** Decreased venous return, decreased C.O., hypotension. May require fluid loading or inotropes.
* **Non-ARDS uses of PEEP/CPAP:** Cardiogenic pulmonary edema (reduces preload, improves oxygenation), postoperative atelectasis, sleep apnea, cystic fibrosis (PEP therapy).

### Weaning from PEEP
* Premature withdrawal causes rapid derecruitment and hypoxemia (Fig. Box 13.10).
* Criteria for weaning trial: PaO<sub>2</sub> acceptable on F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> <0.5, hemodynamically stable, nonseptic, C<sub>S</sub> >25 mL/cm H<sub>2</sub>O, P/F ratio >250–300.
* Procedure (Table 13.8): Reduce PEEP by 5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O, monitor SpO<sub>2</sub>. If fall <20% of previous value – tolerate lower PEEP. If >20% – not ready.

### Basics of Oxygen Delivery to the Tissues

The most common parameters used to assess the oxygenation status of patients are the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, SpO<sub>2</sub>, ABGs, hemoglobin (Hb), presence of abnormal Hb species, PaO<sub>2</sub>, PaO<sub>2</sub>/PAO<sub>2</sub>, PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, shunt, cardiac output, mixed venous O<sub>2</sub> saturation (SvO<sub>2</sub>), and O<sub>2</sub> content of mixed venous blood (CvO<sub>2</sub>). Measuring DO<sub>2</sub> to the tissues provides valuable information about O<sub>2</sub> availability to the tissues. O<sub>2</sub> utilization by the tissues can be determined by measuring arterial-to-mixed venous O<sub>2</sub> content difference (C[a-v]O<sub>2</sub>), O<sub>2</sub> consumption (VO<sub>2</sub>), cardiac output, and SvO<sub>2</sub>. **Alveolar Air Equation (Calculation of Alveolar PO<sub>2</sub>, P<sub>A</sub>O<sub>2</sub>)**

$$P_AO_2 = F_IO_2(P_B - P_{H_2O}) - \left[PaCO_2\left\{F_IO_2\left(1 - \frac{F_IO_2}{R}\right)\right\}\right]$$

where  
\(P_AO_2\) = alveolar partial pressure of O<sub>2</sub> (mm Hg)  
\(F_IO_2\) = inspired O<sub>2</sub> fraction  
\(P_B\) = barometric pressure (mm Hg)  
\(P_{H_2O}\) = water vapor pressure (at 37°C = 47 mm Hg)  
R = respiratory quotient (VO<sub>2</sub>; R of 0.8 is commonly used)  

With an \(F_IO_2 \le 0.6\) (low value), the effect of R on \(P_AO_2\) is small.  
To estimate the \(P_AO_2\) for \(F_IO_2\) values <0.6:

\(P_IO_2 = F_IO_2(P_B - P_{H_2O})\)

**O<sub>2</sub> consumption (VO<sub>2</sub>):** V

### Evaluating PaO<sub>2</sub>, SpO<sub>2</sub>, and F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> in Ventilator Patients

The F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> should be measured at regular intervals or continuously, if possible, to ensure that the patient is receiving the appropriate concentration of inspired O<sub>2</sub>. When changes in the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> are initially made for adult patients, ABGs should be measured within 15 minutes, although some clinicians choose to obtain a sample after 30 minutes. Many clinicians often rely on pulse oximetry measurements to assess the oxygenation of neonatal and pediatric patients, thus reducing the frequency of ABGs ordered for these patients.

Every attempt should be made to prevent complications associated with O<sub>2</sub> toxicity by administering an F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> below 0.6 while maintaining the PaO<sub>2</sub> between 60 and 90 mm Hg and the CaO<sub>2</sub> near normal (20 mL/dL). This goal is not always possible, and sometimes a higher F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> is required. (See section on selection of F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> or adjusting mean airway pressure.)

The SpO<sub>2</sub> can be used to titrate F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> once the relationship between PaO<sub>2</sub> and SpO<sub>2</sub> has been established. (After mechanical ventilation is initiated, an arterial blood gas [ABG] sample is obtained and the PaO<sub>2</sub> is compared with the patient’s current SpO<sub>2</sub> to establish this relationship.) A goal for maintaining SpO<sub>2</sub> at greater than 90% is appropriate. It is important to understand, however, that the SpO<sub>2</sub> will not always correlate perfectly with PaO<sub>2</sub>. Some patients will have a large discrepancy between SpO<sub>2</sub> and PaO<sub>2</sub> and need to be monitored more carefully (see Chapter 10). For example, in patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), their normal PaO<sub>2</sub> may be near 55 mm Hg (SaO<sub>2</sub> ∼80%) and the SpO<sub>2</sub> values may be closer to 88% to 90% on room air.

Although the inspired O<sub>2</sub> percentage can be determined using multiuse O<sub>2</sub> analyzers, most intensive care unit (ICU) ventilators have built-in O<sub>2</sub> analyzers that can provide continuous measurements of F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>. Examples include the Dräger Infinity Evita V500 Critical Care Ventilator (Drägerwerk AG & Co. KGaA, Lubeck, Germany), the Hamilton-C3 and G5 ventilators (Hamilton Medical, Bonaduz, Switzerland), the Medtronics PB 980 (Medtronic Minimally Invasive Therapies, Medtronics, Minneapolis, MN), and the Servo-i, Servo-s, and Servo-U ventilators (Getinge, Wayne, NJ).

#### Adjusting F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub></summary><div class="details-content">

The ABGs obtained after mechanical ventilation is initiated are compared with the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> being delivered. A linear relationship exists between PaO<sub>2</sub> and F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> for any patient as long as the person’s cardiopulmonary status remains fairly stable. In other words, the minute ventilation, cardiac output, shunt, and ratio of volume of dead space to tidal volume (V<sub>D</sub>/V<sub>T</sub>) must not change significantly between the time the ABG comparison is made and the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> is changed. Most of the time, this is the case because ventilator changes are made quickly after blood gas results are obtained.

Because of this linear correlation, the known PaO<sub>2</sub> and the known F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> can be used to select the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> necessary to achieve a desired PaO<sub>2</sub>:

$$\frac{P_aO_2(\text{Known})}{F_IO_2(\text{Known})} = \frac{P_aO_2(\text{Desired})}{F_IO_2(\text{Desired})}$$

This equation provides a reliable method for making appropriate changes in the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> to achieve a desired PaO<sub>2</sub>.

Some institutions use the ratio of PaO<sub>2</sub>/PAO<sub>2</sub> for evaluation of oxygenation and for predicting the inspired O<sub>2</sub> concentration. The ratio of PaO<sub>2</sub> to F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (commonly called the *P-to-F ratio* [PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>]) also has become a popular way to set F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> because of its simplicity. To estimate the change in F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, for example, if the PaO<sub>2</sub> is 60 mm Hg with an F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> of 0.3 and the target PaO<sub>2</sub> is 80 mm Hg, the calculation of F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> is as follows:

$$\frac{\text{Known}\,P_aO_2}{F_IO_2(\text{Known})} = \frac{\text{Desired}\,P_aO_2}{F_IO_2(\text{Desired})}$$

and 60/0.3 = 80/X, where X would be the new F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> setting. In this example, the new setting for F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> would be 0.4 (Case Study 13.2). Making adjustments in F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> has a greater effect on patients with hypoventilation and reduced ventilation-perfusion (\(\dot{V}/\dot{Q}\)), in which higher alveolar O<sub>2</sub> has better access to pulmonary blood flow than situations of pulmonary shunt. Using the P-to-F ratio (PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>) to adjust the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> is not as accurate as using the PaO<sub>2</sub>/PAO<sub>2</sub> because the alveolar PO<sub>2</sub> determination takes PaCO<sub>2</sub> into account.

</div></details>

### Selection of F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> or Adjustment of Mean Airway Pressures

Although the exact safe level of F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> in mechanically ventilated patients is not known at this time, it is generally agreed that maintaining a high F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (>0.6) can result in O<sub>2</sub> toxicity. Besides the tissue damage associated with long-term use of 100% O<sub>2</sub>, it also has an additional complication. Breathing 100% O<sub>2</sub> can lead to **absorption atelectasis** and increase intrapulmonary shunting (i.e., shunt fraction), which further contributes to hypoxemia (Box 13.3). Thus F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> should be kept as low as possible. Although the lower limits of permissive hypoxemia remain controversial, most practitioners agree that a target PaO<sub>2</sub> of 60 mm Hg and an SpO<sub>2</sub> of 90% are acceptable lower limits for most adult patients. If the PaO<sub>2</sub> remains very low while the patient is breathing an enriched O<sub>2</sub> mixture, significant shunting, \(\dot{V}/\dot{Q}\) abnormalities, and/or diffusion defects are present. In these cases, other methods to improve oxygenation, besides increasing F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, must be considered. One approach that can be used to increase the PaO<sub>2</sub> involves increasing the \(\overline{P}_{aw}\). The \(\overline{P}_{aw}\) is the average pressure above baseline during a total respiratory cycle (I + E) (Fig. 13.1).

##### BOX 13.4: Simple Method for Calculating \(\overline{P}_{aw}\)</summary><div class="details-content">

$$\overline{P}_{aw} = \frac{1}{2} \left[ \text{PIP} \times \left( \frac{\text{inspiratory time}}{\text{total respiratory cycle}} \right) \right]$$

When PEEP is used, the equation is as follows:

$$\overline{P}_{aw} = \frac{1}{2}(PIP - PEEP) \times \left(\frac{inspiratory\ time}{total\ cycle\ time}\right) + PEEP$$

Fortunately, most ICU ventilators measure pressures and time and perform this calculation automatically.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### BOX 13.5: Inverse Ratio Ventilation (IRV)</summary><div class="details-content">

IRV is one method for increasing \(\overline{P}_{aw}\), in which inspiratory time (T<sub>I</sub>) is longer than expiratory time (T<sub>E</sub>). IRV can be used with either pressure-controlled or volume-controlled ventilation. The rationale behind increasing T<sub>I</sub> is to recruit lung units and avoid overinflating normal units. Keeping alveoli open for extended periods may reduce shunt and \(\dot{V}/\dot{Q}\) mismatch.

When an inverse I/E ratio is used with pressure ventilation (PC-IRV), a longer T<sub>I</sub> will increase \(\overline{P}_{aw}\). With PC-IRV, the PIP and PEEP do not change because these are set values. However, volume delivery varies with changes in compliance and resistance. Exhaled V<sub>T</sub> and \(\dot{V}_E\) must be closely monitored. (NOTE: Increase in auto-PEEP reduces V<sub>T</sub> delivery in PC-IRV.) In addition, using IRV may require sedation and paralysis in a number of patients because an increased I/E breathing pattern is uncomfortable.

Volume control inverse ratio ventilation (VC-IRV) is an alternative to PC-IRV, but it is seldom used. With VC-IRV, V<sub>T</sub> is ensured, if this is a desired goal. VC-IRV can be accomplished by selecting VC-CMV, using a descending waveform to lengthen T<sub>I</sub> or setting a longer T<sub>I</sub> if the ventilator is time cycled. T<sub>I</sub> can be further lengthened by adding inspiratory pause and slowing inspiratory flows.

It is important to recognize that IRV can create several risks for patients. Both dynamic hyperinflation (auto-PEEP) and increased \(\overline{P}_{aw}\) can increase the risk for lung damage. Furthermore, cardiac output may decrease with increased \(\overline{P}_{aw}\). An I/E ratio of 2:1 is therefore rarely exceeded because of adverse hemodynamic consequences.

Manipulating pressures to increase \(\overline{P}_{aw}\) may result in injury to the lung from trapped air and overdistention and possibly barotrauma (e.g., pneumothorax). (See Chapter 17 for pulmonary complications of positive pressure ventilation.) High thoracic pressures can reduce venous return and cardiac output. (See Chapter 16 for cardiovascular complications.) Therefore it is important to monitor the \(\overline{P}_{aw}\) and assess the patient’s response.

## INTRODUCTION TO POSITIVE END-EXPIRATORY PRESSURE AND CONTINUOUS POSITIVE AIRWAY PRESSURE</summary><div class="details-content">

Because PEEP is so frequently used to increase \(\overline{P}_{aw}\) and improve and maintain oxygenation, it occupies a key role as a technique for treating acute parenchymal lung injury, such as severe atelectasis associated with various pulmonary pathological processes. The goal of using PEEP is to recruit collapsed alveoli while avoiding overdistention of already open alveoli. It is important to set an appropriate level of PEEP that avoids overdistention while maintaining alveolar patency and preventing alveoli from collapsing during exhalation of a tidal volume. Both overdistention and repeated collapse and reexpansion of alveoli are associated with ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) (see Chapter 17 for more details about the effects of PEEP and VILI). The following section reviews the pathophysiology of atelectasis, indications for using PEEP and CPAP, and techniques used to establish an appropriate level of PEEP for a patient.

### Pathophysiology of Atelectasis

Atelectasis is defined as the partial or complete collapse of previously expanded areas of the lung producing a shrunken, airless state. It can result from blockage of air passages, shallow breathing (e.g., postoperative atelectasis), or surfactant deficiency. The loss of surfactant can be a result of damage to surfactant-producing cells (type II pneumocytes), leakage of plasma proteins that inhibit surfactant production, or the presence of inflammatory mediators (e.g., cytokines). These factors tend to promote atelectasis, particularly in the presence of high O<sub>2</sub> concentrations, pulmonary edema, general anesthesia, mechanical ventilation, chemical toxicity, and ARDS.

The treatment of acute atelectasis involves identifying the cause and then initiating an appropriate corrective action. For mechanical obstruction of the airways, coughing, suctioning, percussion, and therapeutic fiberoptic bronchoscopy may be indicated to clear the obstruction. Therapy with PEEP or CPAP may also be used to help inflate (i.e., recruit) collapsed alveoli.

### Goals of PEEP and CPAP

The goals of PEEP/CPAP therapy are:

1. Maintain a PaO<sub>2</sub> 60 mm Hg or greater and SpO<sub>2</sub> at 90% or greater, at an acceptable pH
2. Recruit alveoli and maintain them in an aerated state
3. Restore functional residual capacity
4. Enhance tissue oxygenation

Achieving these goals may provide opportunities to reduce the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> to safer levels (<0.6). Note that sustaining cardiovascular function and avoiding lung injury are critical requirements for effective PEEP/CPAP therapy.

### Terminology

The term *PEEP* as it is commonly used implies that the patient is receiving mechanical ventilatory support and the baseline (i.e., end-expiratory) pressure is above zero cm H<sub>2</sub>O. CPAP is pressure above the ambient pressure maintained during spontaneous ventilation. With CPAP, expiratory positive airway pressure and inspiratory positive airway pressure are both positive and equal, albeit the ventilator does not provide mandatory breaths.

### Technical Aspects of PEEP and CPAP Devices

Generally, PEEP is used when a problem in the lungs results in collapse of alveoli and small airways (atelectasis). If a significant number of alveoli collapse, more areas of the lung are perfused but not ventilated, resulting in a shuntlike situation.

When PEEP is set on a ventilator, the expiratory valve of the ventilator closes when the expiratory pressure drops to the set PEEP level. This traps a certain amount of pressure and volume in the lungs that can prevent or reverse alveolar collapse and reduce the amount of pulmonary shunting.

Although CPAP can be used to reduce shunting, as mentioned earlier, its application is for patients who are spontaneously breathing. CPAP can be achieved with a mechanical ventilator by setting the mode selection switch to the spontaneous/CPAP mode and then setting the desired level of CPAP (PEEP control). CPAP can be delivered to spontaneously breathing patients using a freestanding setup (i.e., one without a mechanical ventilator). It is important to recognize that using freestanding CPAP devices carries a potential liability because these devices are usually “homemade” and have not undergone a formal evaluation for approval by an authorized independent agency. (Freestanding CPAP systems are discussed in more detail later in this chapter.)

### Application of CPAP and PEEP to the Patient’s Airway

The positive pressure employed with CPAP or PEEP is commonly applied to the airway noninvasively with a mask or nasal prongs or invasively through an endotracheal tube (ET) or a tracheostomy tube. Noninvasive CPAP administered with a soft silicon mask eliminates the need for endotracheal intubation in specified groups of patients (see Chapter 19). A variety of tight-fitting masks can be applied to the face or nose, with the pressure adjusted to as high as 15 cm H<sub>2</sub>O (see Figs. 19.4, 19.6, and 19.7). Excessive leakage around the mask, however, can create a problem when trying to maintain a desired pressure.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Mask CPAP</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients receiving mask CPAP are usually alert, awake, and oriented. These patients can protect their lower airways, support work of breathing (WOB), and maintain a normal PaCO<sub>2</sub> without excessive ventilatory effort. Patients receiving noninvasive mask CPAP should have a PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> ratio greater than 200 mm Hg and stable cardiovascular status. The hazards and complications of mask CPAP can include vomiting and aspiration, skin necrosis or discomfort from the mask, CO<sub>2</sub> retention, increased WOB, and cerebral hemorrhage at high CPAP levels (infants).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Nasal CPAP</summary><div class="details-content">

By taking advantage of the fact that neonates are obligate nose breathers, plastic or Silastic nasal prongs can be fitted into an infant’s nares. CPAP pressures up to about 15 cm H<sub>2</sub>O can be administered with these devices (see Fig. 22.2). Loss of pressure from the system can occur through the mouth at high pressures (>15 cm H<sub>2</sub>O) (Case Study 13.3). Problems of nasal CPAP include gastric distention, pressure necrosis, swelling of nasal mucosa, and abrasion of the posterior pharynx. (See Chapter 22 for additional information on nasal CPAP in infants.)

#### Flow and Threshold Resistors</summary><div class="details-content">

When PEEP/CPAP is used, the flow or pressure the patient must generate to obtain inspiratory flow for a spontaneous breath or to trigger a mandatory breath depends on the type of system used. High-gas flow systems, pressurized reservoirs, demand valves, and demand flow systems respond to patient flow demand. The more rapidly and easily these devices respond to patient effort, the less WOB is required. Expiratory pressure, on the other hand, is maintained above ambient pressure with PEEP/CPAP and can be accomplished using a variety of devices that are classified as either flow or threshold resistors.

A *flow resistor* achieves expiratory pressure by creating a resistance to gas flow through an orifice. As the diameter of the orifice decreases in size, the pressure level applied increases; conversely, as the diameter of the orifice increases, the applied pressure level decreases. Changes in expiratory gas flow also affect the expiratory pressure applied with a flow resistor; that is, the pressure is flow dependent. The higher the expired gas flow, the higher is the expiratory pressure generated; the lower the expired gas flow, the lower is the pressure. (Positive expiratory pressure [PEP] mask therapy is based on this principle.) An ideal flow resistor is one in which pressure increases linearly with flow.

With threshold resistors, a constant pressure is provided throughout expiration regardless of the rate of gas flow (i.e., flow independent). When a threshold resistor is used in the expiratory limb of a ventilator circuit, the exhaled air passes unimpeded until pressure falls to the preset PEEP value. At that time, the expiratory gas flow stops and the system pressure is maintained at the preset PEEP level. The expiratory valves on most ventilators behave as threshold resistors. Note that these threshold resistors are free-floating and provide minimal resistance to exhalation (Key Point 13.2).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Circuitry for Spontaneous CPAP With Freestanding Systems and Mechanical Ventilators</summary><div class="details-content">

Ventilators can provide CPAP for a spontaneously breathing patient by simply eliminating the mandatory breaths (CPAP/spontaneous mode) and adjusting the PEEP level to the desired pressure. Current ICU ventilators can be used for CPAP because they incorporate inspiratory flow systems that typically respond quickly to a patient’s breathing effort and do not increase WOB.

CPAP can also be provided by a freestanding system without using a ventilator. There are two types of freestanding or standalone CPAP or expiratory positive airway pressure systems: continuous flow CPAP, which is a closed system; and demand-flow spontaneous CPAP, which is an open system. Both systems can be used only for patients who do not require mechanical ventilation but who might benefit from the effects of CPAP on oxygenation. Patients must be able to comfortably maintain a near-normal PaCO<sub>2</sub>. As mentioned previously, these systems are not used often in the clinical setting. Most institutions simply use a ventilator in the spontaneous/CPAP mode.

</div></details>

## PEEP RANGES</summary><div class="details-content">

Two levels or ranges of PEEP can be employed: minimum or low PEEP, also called *physiological PEEP*, and therapeutic PEEP.

### Minimum or Low PEEP

In most situations it is appropriate to use a minimum level of PEEP (3–5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O) to help preserve a patient’s normal functional residual capacity (FRC). FRC usually decreases when a patient is intubated or placed in a supine position. The reduction in FRC is primarily due to the abdominal contents moving upward and exerting pressure on the diaphragm. Because minimum PEEP involves the application of only 3 to 5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O, it is usually not considered a problem in terms of causing complications.

### Therapeutic PEEP

Therapeutic PEEP involves using 5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O or greater. It is used in the treatment of refractory hypoxemia caused by increased intrapulmonary shunting and \(\dot{V}/\dot{Q}\) mismatching accompanied by a decreased FRC and pulmonary compliance. High levels of therapeutic PEEP (e.g., ≥15 cm H<sub>2</sub>O) are beneficial for a small percentage of the patients with ARDS. Because high levels of PEEP are often associated with cardiopulmonary complications, physiological response to therapy must be monitored carefully.

### Optimal PEEP

The term *optimum* (or best) *PEEP* was coined by Suter and colleagues in 1975. Optimal PEEP is considered the level at which the maximum beneficial effects of PEEP occur (i.e., increased DO<sub>2</sub>, FRC, and C<sub>S</sub> and decreased \(\dot{Q}_S/\dot{Q}_T\)). This level of PEEP is also considered optimal because it is not associated with profound cardiopulmonary side effects, such as decreased venous return, decreased cardiac output, decreased blood pressure (BP), increased shunting, increased V<sub>D</sub>/V<sub>T</sub>, barotrauma, and volutrauma, and it is accomplished at safe levels of inspired O<sub>2</sub> (F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> <0.40). It is important to note that optimal PEEP should be correlated with criteria other than arterial PO<sub>2</sub> alone. Thus optimal PEEP has more recently been defined as the level of PEEP at which static compliance is highest as PEEP is decreased after a **recruitment maneuver** (RM). (RMs are discussed later in this chapter.)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## INDICATIONS FOR PEEP AND CPAP</summary><div class="details-content">

ARDS remains a prime example of a pathophysiological state in which PEEP is used as an effective means of improving oxygenation. Patients with ARDS do not benefit from mechanical ventilatory support without PEEP. Although collapsed alveoli may open during a positive pressure inspiration, unstable alveoli and airways tend to collapse if the airway pressure returns to ambient pressure during expiration. Because approximately two-thirds of the respiratory cycle is spent in expiration, blood passing through these areas of collapsed alveoli during expiration creates a shuntlike state, which perpetuates hypoxemia.

Patients with ARDS benefit from PEEP because it helps prevent collapse of the small airways and alveoli, and thus it aids in recruiting closed lung units. The edema-filled alveoli may have their volumes partially air-filled with this technique as well, leading to the restoration of FRC. Lung compliance (C<sub>L</sub>) and gas distribution are enhanced, thereby reducing the shunt effect of venous admixture and improving oxygenation. Box 13.6 lists the indications for PEEP therapy.

The indications for initiating CPAP are similar to the criteria used for initiating PEEP; the primary difference between using CPAP versus PEEP is that the patient provides the WOB at all times during CPAP. Thus CPAP is appropriate if a patient is capable of breathing spontaneously without difficulty and is able to maintain an acceptable PaCO<sub>2</sub>.

Ultimately, PEEP allows for the reduction of F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> because it improves oxygenation and helps avoid the complications associated with a high F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> in certain disorders. Examples of disorders that may benefit from the use of PEEP include the following:

- ARDS
- Cardiogenic pulmonary edema in adults and children
- Bilateral, diffuse pneumonia

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## INITIATING PEEP THERAPY</summary><div class="details-content">

Once a patient’s clinical condition indicates that PEEP or CPAP therapy is warranted, it is prudent to initiate this form of therapy as soon as possible. High plateau pressures (P<sub>plat</sub> > 30 cm H<sub>2</sub>O) along with F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> values of 0.5 or greater may damage alveolar cells (type I and II) in less than 24 hours; therefore PEEP or CPAP should be initiated early in the course of therapy to avoid lung damage from high pressures, volumes, and F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (see Chapter 17 for more information on VILI).

</div></details>

## SELECTING THE APPROPRIATE PEEP/CPAP LEVEL (OPTIMAL PEEP)</summary><div class="details-content">

The goal when selecting an appropriate PEEP/CPAP setting for a patient should be to achieve a PEEP/CPAP level that produces maximum beneficial effects and is not associated with profound cardiopulmonary side effects. The medical literature describes several techniques that can be used to determine when the optimum level of PEEP/CPAP for a patient. The consensus of these different approaches is reviewed in the following pages. Specific techniques used in the management of patients with ARDS are discussed later in this chapter.

### Application of PEEP Above 5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O

For adults, PEEP is usually increased in increments of 3 to 5 cm H<sub>2</sub>O. In infants, this range is generally 2 to 3 cm H<sub>2</sub>O. Some clinicians follow specific step increases in F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> and PEEP when performing a PEEP study using the procedure outlined in an ARDSnet study comparing the use of higher and lower tidal volumes (Table 13.2). Other clinicians follow a more rapidly increasing PEEP-F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> table from a follow-up study (Table 13.3). Both the low and high PEEP titration techniques for establishing the appropriate PEEP level appear to have similar morbidity and mortality rates.

In addition to using either a moderate (low PEEP increments) or more aggressive (high PEEP incremental changes) technique, other practitioners will use techniques such as slow or static pressure-volume loops to determine optimum the PEEP level. Still other clinicians use an RM, which may be accompanied by a decremental PEEP study. Pressure-volume loops and RMs are reviewed later in this chapter.

Regardless of the procedure, an optimal oxygenation point should be targeted that allows adequate tissue oxygenation (optimum O<sub>2</sub> transport) at a safe F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> and safe ventilating pressures with an acceptable PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> ratio (Key Point 13.3). Along with improving oxygenation, cardiovascular status should be monitored to identify adverse side effects of PEEP so that they can be managed appropriately by the attending physician. Excessive PEEP must also be avoided because it can result in lung overinflation, stressing pulmonary cells and potentially resulting in an inflammatory reaction and possible barotraumas (see Chapter 17).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Optimal PEEP Study</summary><div class="details-content">

Performing an optimal PEEP study is a function most often reserved for patients requiring 10 cm H<sub>2</sub>O or more of PEEP. For example, in patients with ARDS, PEEP levels greater than 20 cm H<sub>2</sub>O of PEEP may be required. Box 13.7 lists the parameters most often monitored during a PEEP or CPAP study. Boxes 13.3 and 13.8 provide information for shunt calculations, which helps monitor PEEP/CPAP when a pulmonary artery catheter is being used (see Chapter 11).

The following common target goals can be used to assess a patient’s response to PEEP:

- A PaO<sub>2</sub> of 60 mm Hg to 100 mm Hg while receiving an F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> of 0.4 or less, which represents a SaO<sub>2</sub> of 90% to 97% at a normal pH. (NOTE: The range of PaO<sub>2</sub> from the ARDSnet study targeted PaO<sub>2</sub> from 55–80 mm Hg and SpO<sub>2</sub> from 88%–95%.)
- Optimum O<sub>2</sub> transport is present; normal O<sub>2</sub> transport is about 1000 mL/min of O<sub>2</sub> (5 L/min × 20 vol% × 10). A pulmonary shunt fraction less than 15%, when this parameter is monitored.
- A minimal amount of cardiovascular compromise, which includes adequate systemic BP, a decrease of less than 20% in cardiac output, and stable pulmonary vascular pressures (i.e., pulmonary artery occlusion pressure [PAOP], pulmonary vascular resistance [PVR]).
- Improving C<sub>L</sub> and improved lung aeration.
- A PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> ratio greater than 300.
- The point of minimum arterial to end-tidal PCO<sub>2</sub> gradient.

##### BOX 13.8: Clinical Shunt Calculation</summary><div class="details-content">

When a patient is breathing 100% O<sub>2</sub>, \(\dot{Q}_S/\dot{Q}_T\) can be *estimated* with the following equation:

$$\dot{Q}_S/\dot{Q}_T = (P_{(A-a)}O_2 \times 0.003) / [(P_{(A-a)}O_2 \times 0.003) + (Ca - C\overline{v}O_2)]$$

\(\dot{Q}_S/\dot{Q}_T\) is affected by variations in \(\dot{V}/\dot{Q}\) mismatching and by fluctuations in mixed venous O<sub>2</sub> saturations (S\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2\)) and F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2\). Using the factor 0.003 in the calculation above converts the reading from mm Hg to vol% (mL/100 mL) so that it becomes C<sub>(A-a)</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, or the alveolar-to-arterial content difference.

The C[a-\(\overline{v}\)]O<sub>2</sub> portion of the equation is the arterial-to-venous content difference. C[a-\(\overline{v}\)]O<sub>2</sub> is often assigned the value of 3.5 vol%, the approximate arterial-to-venous O<sub>2</sub> content difference in a critically ill patient. ##### Ventilator Parameters</summary><div class="details-content">

Measurement of ventilator parameters, such as \(\dot{V}_E\), V<sub>T</sub>, f, flow, PIP, and P<sub>plat</sub>, can provide valuable information about changes in the patient’s C<sub>L</sub> and airway resistance (R<sub>aw</sub>).

Measuring \(\dot{V}_E\) and V<sub>T</sub> is relatively easy. One does not expect \(\dot{V}_E\) to change as a direct result of using CPAP or PEEP; PaCO<sub>2</sub> is not directly affected. If a patient has been hyperventilating (low PaCO<sub>2\)) because of hypoxemia before the use of CPAP or PEEP, the relief of the hypoxemia with PEEP/CPAP should be accompanied by a decrease in \(\dot{V}_E\) and a rise in PaCO<sub>2</sub>. PaCO<sub>2</sub> may decrease if PEEP improves ventilation to previously perfused alveoli. High levels of PEEP expand the conductive airways, in part because of dilation of terminal and respiratory bronchioles, which increases dead space and leads to an increased PaCO<sub>2</sub>.

</div></details>

##### Static Compliance</summary><div class="details-content">

Compliance is considered a good indicator of the effects of PEEP on the lung. As PEEP progressively restores FRC, compliance should increase (Fig. 13.5). Most ICU ventilators allow for the measurement of C<sub>D</sub> and calculation of C<sub>S</sub>. (NOTE: The GE Healthcare Engstrom Carescape R860 [GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL] has an option that allows for the measurement of FRC [FRC INview, GE Healthcare]). It is important to recognize, however, that when PEEP reaches a point at which it causes overdistention of the lung, compliance will decrease. Evidence of this reduction in compliance because of overdistention of the alveoli can be seen in the pressure-volume graphic. A characteristic duck-billed appearance occurs at the top of the pressure-volume loop (Fig. 13.6). (NOTE: When calculating static compliance [C<sub>S\)], calculations of tubing compliance [C<sub>T\)] must be taken into account and include the PEEP value. If auto-PEEP is present, it must be measured and considered part of the end-expiratory pressure when calculating compliance.)

In patients with chest wall injuries or hypovolemia, the use of C<sub>S</sub> alone is not a good indicator of optimal PEEP or cardiovascular changes with PEEP, even at low levels of PEEP. More invasive monitoring techniques, such as the placement of a balloon-tipped pulmonary artery catheter, may be needed. For example, if PEEP levels above 15 to 20 cm H<sub>2</sub>O are used, compliance is not a good indication of cardiovascular function and monitoring pulmonary artery pressures may be indicated. Notice that this type of invasive monitoring increases the risk for complications, so implementation must be considered carefully (see Chapter 11).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

###### Arterial-to-Venous Oxygen Content Difference</summary><div class="details-content">

The arterial-to-mixed venous O<sub>2</sub> content difference (C[a-\(\overline{v}\)]O<sub>2</sub>), which at rest is normally 5 vol%, reflects O<sub>2</sub> utilization by the tissues. An increase in C[a-\(\overline{v}\)]O<sub>2</sub> with an increase in PEEP may indicate hypovolemia, cardiac malfunction, decreased venous return to the heart, and decreased cardiac output, or increased VO<sub>2</sub>.

A decrease in C[a-\(\overline{v}\)]O<sub>2</sub> with an increase in PEEP may also be associated with an increase in cardiac output resulting from improved or augmented cardiac function. Reductions in O<sub>2</sub> extraction by the tissues can result from reduced metabolic rate or histotoxic hypoxia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

###### Mixed Venous Oxygen Tension or Saturation</summary><div class="details-content">

P\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2</sub> is normally about 35 to 40 mm Hg (normal S\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2\)=75%). A P\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2</sub> of 28 mm Hg is probably the minimal acceptable level for P\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2</sub> and represents an S\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2</sub> of about 50%.

As PEEP is increased, it often leads to an improvement in PaO<sub>2</sub> and P\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2</sub> with no net change in . This indicates that O<sub>2</sub> transport is improving, with no apparent change in C[a-\(\overline{v}\)]O<sub>2</sub>, cardiac output, and the level of shunting is also decreasing.

PEEP may increase PaO<sub>2</sub> and P\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2</sub>, decrease net C[a-\(\overline{v}\)]O<sub>2</sub>, and improve O<sub>2</sub> delivery. If VO<sub>2</sub> is constant, these changes suggest a rise in cardiac output. On the other hand, in patients in whom P\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2</sub> decreases with PEEP and C[a-\(\overline{v}\)]O<sub>2</sub> increases, cardiac output and O<sub>2</sub> delivery may decrease. However, if P\(\overline{v}\)O<sub>2</sub> was high to start and then drops to a normal value, no harmful effects may be present (Case Study 13.4).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

###### Cardiac Output</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac output provides key information about a patient’s cardiovascular response to PEEP. Thus as PEEP improves \(\dot{V}/\dot{Q}\) relationships, oxygenation also improves, which may enhance cardiac performance. As intrapleural pressures increase or as the gas exchange units become overdistended, however, venous return decreases and cardiac function is altered; cardiac output then declines, and the point of optimal PEEP is no longer present (Fig. 13.7 and Table 13.5). (NOTE: Cardiac output can be estimated noninvasively using the Respironics NM3 monitor from Philips, Andover, MA)

Some clinicians increase vascular volumes with fluid administration (fluid challenge) and give inotropic agents to maintain cardiac function as PEEP is increased. This approach provides a way to ensure that O<sub>2</sub> transport or delivery, or both, are sufficient to meet the O<sub>2</sub> demand by the tissues.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SUMMARY</summary><div class="details-content">

- The most common parameters used to assess oxygenation status of patients are the F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub>, arterial blood gases (ABGs), CO-oximetry, and hemodynamic measurements.
- A linear relationship exists between PaO<sub>2</sub> and F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> for any patient as long as the cardiopulmonary status remains fairly constant.
- Although the exact safe level of F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> that is safe for mechanically ventilated patients is not known at this time, it is generally agreed that maintaining a high F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> (>0.6) can result in O<sub>2</sub> toxicity.
- Mean airway pressure is a major determinant of oxygenation in patients with ARDS because it affects mean alveolar pressure (P<sub>alv</sub>) and alveolar recruitment.
- The treatment of acute atelectasis involves identifying the cause and then initiating an appropriate corrective action.
- The goals of PEEP/CPAP therapy are to enhance tissue oxygenation; maintain a PaO<sub>2</sub> above 60 mm Hg and SpO<sub>2</sub> ≥90%, at an acceptable pH; recruit alveoli and maintain them in an aerated state; and restore functional residual capacity.
- PEEP as it is commonly used implies that the patient is receiving ventilatory support and the baseline pressure is above zero; CPAP is pressure above the ambient pressure maintained during spontaneous ventilation.
- Two levels or ranges of PEEP can be employed: minimum or low PEEP, also called “physiological PEEP,” and therapeutic PEEP.
- Therapeutic PEEP is used in the treatment of refractory hypoxemia caused by increased intrapulmonary shunting and ventilation-perfusion mismatching accompanied by a decreased FRC and pulmonary compliance.
- PEEP is beneficial for the treatment of patients with ARDS because it helps prevent the collapse of the small airways and alveoli and thus aids in recruiting closed lung units.
- C<sub>S</sub> is considered a good indicator of the effects of PEEP on the lung.
- When PEEP greater than 15 cm H<sub>2</sub>O is used, it is important to evaluate the patient’s hemodynamic status closely, which may require the placement of a balloon-flotation pulmonary artery catheter.
- Static pressure-volume (SPV) loops have been used to select an optimal PEEP level and V<sub>T</sub> in ALI. Current theory suggests that it is more important to set PEEP above the upper inflection point detected during deflation of the lung (UIPd).
- PEEP can be detrimental to a patient’s cardiovascular status because it can reduce cardiac output and compromise circulation, leading to a reduction in BP. A relative contraindication for PEEP is hypovolemia.
- Classification of the severity of ARDS is based on the PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> ratio. Mild ARDS is defined as a PaO<sub>2</sub>/F<sub>I</sub>O<sub>2</sub> ratio of between 300 and 200 mm Hg, moderate ARDS is defined as between 200 and 100 mm Hg, and severe ARDS is defined as a ratio of 100 mm Hg or less.
- ARDS can be divided into two phases: an acute exudative phase characterized by inflammation and alveolar filling and a subacute phase, in which fibrosing alveolitis occurs.
- Clinicians sometimes divide ARDS into two categories: *direct* and *indirect*. *Direct* ARDS includes disorders of the lung that directly affect lung tissue; *indirect* ARDS is referred to as *secondary* or *nonpulmonary* ARDS, because injury to the lung is not the result of a direct lung insult.
- The ARDS Network trial and other studies provide strong evidence in support of using V<sub>T</sub>s of 6 mL/kg or lower and a P<sub>plat</sub> less than 30 cm H<sub>2</sub>O when providing ventilation for patients with ARDS.
- A recruitment maneuver (RM) is a sustained increase in pressure in the lungs with the goal of opening as many collapsed lung units as possible.
- Recruitment occurs across the entire range of lung volume from residual volume to TLC. Once the lungs are recruited, they are kept open by maintaining an adequate PEEP above the LIP of an inspiratory maneuver or, preferably, above the UIP of a deflation (expiratory) maneuver.
- Lung recruitment maneuvers are more likely to improve oxygenation and lung compliance in patients with secondary ARDS.
- Placing a patient with ARDS in a prone position may improve oxygenation and decrease the degree of shunt in the patient’s lungs.
