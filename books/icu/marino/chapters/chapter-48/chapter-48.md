# Nutritional Requirements

> *The more impure bodies are fed, the more diseased they will become.*
> 
> — Hippocrates, *Aphorisms*

The fundamental objective of nutritional support in the intensive care unit (ICU) is to provide the daily energy and nutrient substrates required to sustain organ function, preserve lean body mass, and facilitate tissue repair (1–3). However, achieving this objective in critically ill patients is fraught with physiological complexity (4,5). Normal regulatory mechanisms governing substrate mobilization, hormone responsiveness, and intermediary metabolism are profoundly altered during severe systemic inflammation, sepsis, and neuroendocrine stress (1–5).

This chapter examines the physiological principles of energy expenditure and fuel oxidation, current methodologies for measuring and predicting caloric and protein requirements, the essential roles of vitamins and trace elements, and the evolving paradigm regarding whether aggressive nutritional feeding during acute critical illness can paradoxically induce metabolic and tissue harm (1–5).

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Key Principles and Clinical Directives</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Daily Energy Expenditure and Calorimetry:** Indirect calorimetry represents the gold standard for measuring resting energy expenditure (REE) in mechanically ventilated and spontaneously breathing ICU patients (1–3). When indirect calorimetry is unavailable, a simple weight-based target of $25\text{ kcal/kg/day}$ based on actual body weight (or adjusted body weight for patients exceeding 125% of ideal body weight) is clinically recommended (2,3).
- **Hypocaloric Initial Nutrition:** Early aggressive feeding during acute critical illness causes overfeeding, hyperglycemia, hypercapnia, and hepatic steatosis because endogenous glucose production is nonsuppressible (1,3,12). Clinical practice guidelines recommend providing no more than 70% of measured REE for the first 3 days (or no more than 70% of estimated REE for the first 7 days), advancing gradually toward 100% of caloric goals as acute inflammation subsides (3).
- **Carbohydrate Substrate Targets:** Obligate central nervous system glucose requirements total approximately 100–120 g/day, mandating a minimum daily intake of at least $130\text{ g/day}$ (3). To avoid excessive $\text{CO}_2$ production and de novo lipogenesis, carbohydrate infusion rates should never exceed $5\text{ mg/kg/min}$ ($7.2\text{ g/kg/day}$) (3). Target blood glucose concentrations should be maintained between 140 and $180\text{ mg/dL}$ ($7.8\text{–}10.0\text{ mmol/L}$) (2,3).
- **Lipid Emulsions and Propofol Safety:** Lipids provide approximately 30% of total daily nonprotein calories. Linoleic acid (a polyunsaturated omega-6 fatty acid) is the sole essential dietary fatty acid; providing 0.5% of total caloric intake as linoleic acid prevents scaly dermatitis, cardiomyopathy, and immune dysfunction (15). Total lipid intake should not exceed $1.5\text{ g/kg/day}$ (3). Because propofol is formulated in a 10% lipid emulsion delivering $1.1\text{ kcal/mL}$, its caloric contribution must be tracked daily to prevent hypertriglyceridemia, acute pancreatitis, and lipid overload (2,3,16).
- **Protein Intake and Nitrogen Balance:** Daily protein requirements are elevated to $1.2\text{–}2.0\text{ g/kg/day}$ during critical illness to counter profound hypercatabolism (1,2). Nitrogen balance can be calculated as:
  $$\text{Nitrogen Balance (g/24 h)} = [\text{Protein Intake (g/24 h)} / 6.25] - [\text{UUN (g/24 h)} + (4\text{–}6)]$$
  Attaining positive or neutral nitrogen balance requires both adequate amino acid delivery and sufficient nonprotein calories (at or slightly above REE) to spare amino acids from oxidative deamination (18).
- **Thiamine Deficiency (Vitamin $\text{B}_1$):** Thiamine pyrophosphate is the obligate coenzyme for pyruvate dehydrogenase, which bridges glycolysis to the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid cycle (19). Thiamine stores deplete within 2 to 3 weeks of malnutrition, alcoholism, septic shock, or prolonged furosemide therapy (19–22). Deficiency manifests as high-output heart failure ("wet beriberi"), Wernicke's encephalopathy (mental confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia), or refractory lactic acidosis (27–32). Suspected thiamine deficiency warrants high-dose parenteral thiamine: $500\text{ mg}$ IV every 8 hours for 2–3 days, followed by $250\text{ mg}$ IV daily for 3–5 days, alongside aggressive magnesium repletion (33,34).
- **Trace Elements and Antioxidant Protection:** Unbound, free iron ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) catalyzes the generation of cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals via Fenton chemistry and triggers ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of non-apoptotic regulated cell death (47–49). Parenteral iron is contraindicated during acute systemic inflammation unless severe iron deficiency anemia is proven with a serum ferritin $\lt 18\ \mu\text{g/L}$ in the absence of inflammation (51). Selenium is the catalytic center of glutathione peroxidase; supplementation up to $225\ \mu\text{g/day}$ supports endogenous antioxidant defenses during intense oxidative stress (52–54).
- **The Paradox of Nutrient Harm in Acute Critical Illness:** In acutely ill patients, exogenous glucose is largely shunted into lactate (up to 85% conversion vs $\lt 5\%$ in healthy adults) due to pyruvate dehydrogenase suppression (56,57). Forced full feeding during the early acute phase fails to halt muscle catabolism and can exacerbate organ failure, validating Hippocrates' ancient aphorism that feeding an impure, inflamed body fuels systemic illness (55).

## Daily Energy Expenditure</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Oxidation of Nutritional Fuels</summary><div class="details-content">

All biological energy utilized by human cells originates as solar energy stored within the covalent chemical bonds of organic molecules. Molecules possessing abundant high-energy carbon-carbon and carbon-hydrogen bonds serve as metabolic fuels. During oxidative metabolism, nutritional fuels—lipids, carbohydrates, and proteins—react with molecular oxygen ($\text{O}_2$), cleaving covalent bonds and liberating enthalpy in the form of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and heat.

This oxidative combustion consumes oxygen and produces carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$), water, and metabolic heat. The stoichiometric relationships, oxygen requirements, carbon dioxide production rates, respiratory quotients, and energetic yields per gram of each primary fuel class are outlined in Table 48.1.

| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Lipids** | $2.00\text{ L/g}$ | $1.40\text{ L/g}$ | 0.70 | $9.1\text{ kcal/g}$ |
| **Proteins** | $0.96\text{ L/g}$ | $0.78\text{ L/g}$ | 0.80 | $4.0\text{ kcal/g}$ |

Several fundamental physiological concepts arise from these oxidative characteristics:

- **Energetic Density:** Lipids yield more than twice as much energy per unit weight ($9.1\text{ kcal/g}$) as carbohydrates ($3.7\text{ kcal/g}$) or proteins ($4.0\text{ kcal/g}$) because the fatty acyl chains exist in a highly reduced chemical state.
- **Respiratory Quotient ($\text{RQ}$):** The respiratory quotient represents the molar ratio of $\text{CO}_2$ produced to $\text{O}_2$ consumed ($\text{RQ} = \text{VCO}_2 / \text{VO}_2$). Carbohydrate oxidation generates exactly one mole of $\text{CO}_2$ for each mole of $\text{O}_2$ consumed ($\text{RQ} = 1.00$). In contrast, lipid oxidation requires significant oxygen consumption to oxidize hydrogen atoms into water, yielding an $\text{RQ}$ of 0.70. Protein oxidation produces an intermediate $\text{RQ}$ of 0.80.
- **Ventilatory Burden:** Because carbohydrate oxidation produces $43\%$ more $\text{CO}_2$ per liter of oxygen consumed than lipid oxidation, excessive carbohydrate intake substantially increases whole-body carbon dioxide production ($\text{VCO}_2$), placing a major ventilatory workload on patients with compromised pulmonary reserve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Indirect Calorimetry</summary><div class="details-content">

Direct measurement of whole-body heat production (direct calorimetry) is technically impossible in hospitalized intensive care patients. However, resting energy expenditure (REE) can be determined with high precision from measurements of whole-body oxygen consumption ($\text{VO}_2$) and carbon dioxide production ($\text{VCO}_2$) using the Weir equation (6):

$$\text{REE (kcal/min)} = (3.6 \times \text{VO}_2) + (1.1 \times \text{VCO}_2) - 61 \qquad (48.1)$$

When oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production are expressed in liters per minute at standard temperature and pressure dry (STPD), multiplying the calculated minute energy expenditure by 1,440 minutes yields the total 24-hour resting caloric requirement (6,7). This physiological measurement constitutes the foundation of indirect calorimetry.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Clinical Methodology and Technical Limitations</summary><div class="details-content">

Indirect calorimetry is executed at the bedside utilizing dedicated metabolic carts. In spontaneously breathing patients, expired gases are collected via a ventilated canopy hood placed over the patient's head. In mechanically ventilated patients, high-precision flow sensors and gas analyzers for paramagnetic oxygen and infrared carbon dioxide are integrated directly into the inspiratory and expiratory limbs of the ventilator breathing circuit (7,8).

Measurements are recorded under resting, steady-state conditions for 15 to 30 minutes. Clinical investigations have firmly demonstrated that a 30-minute steady-state evaluation accurately mirrors energy expenditure measured continuously across 24 hours (8). Modern metabolic monitors utilize rapid-response fuel-cell or optical oxygen sensors that maintain measurement accuracy up to an inspired oxygen fraction ($\text{FiO}_2$) of $0.70$ (70%) (9), a substantial technical advance over older instruments that became unreliable at an $\text{FiO}_2 \gt 0.60$ (10).

Nevertheless, indirect calorimetry possesses key clinical and logistical constraints:

- **Circuit Leaks:** The presence of an uncuffed endotracheal tube, active bronchopleural fistula, or chest tube air leak invalidates volumetric expired gas analysis.
- **Unstable Ventilatory Parameters:** Frequent changes in minute ventilation, positive end-expiratory pressure, or inspired oxygen concentration preclude steady-state equilibrium.
- **Extracorporeal Support:** Patients receiving venovenous or venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) or continuous renal replacement therapy with regional citrate clearance experience non-pulmonary gas and caloric exchanges that distort pulmonary measurements.
- **Cost and Operational Complexity:** The high financial cost of equipment and the necessity for specialized respiratory therapy personnel restrict universal bedside availability.

### Simple Predictive Formulas for Energy Needs</summary><div class="details-content">

While more than 200 mathematical predictive equations exist (including the Harris-Benedict, Schofield, and Mifflin-St Jeor formulas), none has demonstrated superior predictive accuracy compared to simple weight-based calculations in critically ill adults (2,3,11).

When indirect calorimetry is unavailable, international critical care societies recommend estimating baseline daily energy requirements using a pragmatic weight-based formula (2,3):

$$\text{REE (kcal/day)} = 25 \times \text{body weight (kg)} \qquad (48.2)$$

##### Adjustments for Obesity

In non-obese patients, actual admission body weight is utilized. However, in patients whose actual body weight exceeds 125% of ideal body weight (IBW), using actual weight leads to significant caloric overestimation due to the lower metabolic activity of adipose tissue. In such cases, an adjusted body weight is recommended (11):

$$\text{Adjusted weight (kg)} = [(\text{actual weight} - \text{ideal weight}) \times 0.25] + \text{ideal weight} \qquad (48.3)$$

Ideal body weight is derived from standard actuarial tables or calculated via the Devine formula ($\text{IBW}_{\text{male}} = 50\text{ kg} + 2.3\text{ kg}$ per inch over 5 feet; $\text{IBW}_{\text{female}} = 45.5\text{ kg} + 2.3\text{ kg}$ per inch over 5 feet).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Clinical Recommendations for Caloric Intake</summary><div class="details-content">

Contemporary clinical guidelines recommend initiating enteral nutrition within 24 to 48 hours of ICU admission once hemodynamic resuscitation is complete (1–3). However, modern paradigms advise against full caloric delivery during the acute phase of critical illness (1,3).

During early acute illness, severe neuroendocrine activation unleashes massive gluconeogenesis and lipolysis that cannot be suppressed by exogenous nutrient administration (12). Infusing full caloric targets on top of active endogenous fuel mobilization produces severe overfeeding, exacerbating hyperglycemia, hyperinsulinemia, hypercapnia, and hepatic steatosis (12,13). Furthermore, in chronically malnourished individuals, rapid caloric repletion provokes life-threatening refeeding syndrome characterized by profound hypophosphatemia, hypokalemia, and hypomagnesemia (13).

The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guidelines outline the following phased feeding strategy (3):

- **Measured REE (via Indirect Calorimetry):** Caloric delivery should not exceed 70% of measured REE during the first 3 days of ICU admission. From day 4 onward, caloric intake is stepped up incrementally toward 100% of measured REE across the remainder of the first week.
- **Estimated REE (via Predictive Formulas):** Caloric delivery should be restricted to no more than 70% of estimated energy requirements (approximately $18\text{–}20\text{ kcal/kg/day}$) for the entire first 7 days, preventing cumulative caloric overfeeding.

#### Obligate Glucose Needs and Stress Hyperglycemia</summary><div class="details-content">

The adult human central nervous system relies almost exclusively on glucose as its obligate metabolic fuel under acute conditions, consuming approximately 100 to $120\text{ g}$ of glucose each day (3). In addition, erythrocytes, renal medullary cells, and healing wound fibroblasts depend strictly on glycolysis. Consequently, a baseline dietary carbohydrate intake of at least $130\text{ g/day}$ is necessary to sustain cerebral function and reduce obligate hepatic gluconeogenesis from protein catabolism (3).

However, counter-regulatory neuroendocrine hormones (epinephrine, cortisol, glucagon, and growth hormone) combined with inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-1, and interleukin-6) induce profound peripheral insulin resistance and accelerate uninhibited hepatic glucose output (12,14).

When exogenous carbohydrate delivery exceeds oxidative disposal capacity, excess glucose is shunted into de novo hepatic lipogenesis, precipitating hepatic steatosis, immune suppression, and excessive carbon dioxide generation (12). To prevent these metabolic derangements, glucose infusion rates must be limited to a maximum of $5\text{ mg/kg/min}$ ($7.2\text{ g/kg/day}$), equating to approximately $500\text{ g/day}$ in a $70\text{-kg}$ adult (3). Blood glucose concentrations should be titrated to a target range of 140 to $180\text{ mg/dL}$ ($7.8\text{–}10.0\text{ mmol/L}$) using intravenous insulin infusions, strictly avoiding hypoglycemia ($\lt 70\text{ mg/dL}$) (2,3).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Lipid Requirements and Fuel Stores</summary><div class="details-content">

Lipids provide approximately 30% of total daily energy requirements in standard enteral and parenteral formulations. Adipose tissue triglyceride stores represent the overwhelming majority of endogenous fuel reserves in the human body ($141{,}000\text{ kcal}$, Table 48.2).

Dietary lipids consist of triglycerides—a glycerol backbone esterified to three fatty acids. Fatty acids are classified by aliphatic chain length (short, medium, long) and the degree of saturation.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Essential Fatty Acids and Propofol Sedation</summary><div class="details-content">

Linoleic acid, an 18-carbon omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA, 18:2n-6), is the sole fatty acid that humans cannot synthesize de novo due to the absence of delta-12 desaturase enzymes (15). Linoleic acid is therefore an obligate dietary nutrient.

Deficiency of linoleic acid manifests clinically as a severe scaling, erythematous dermopathy, uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation, dilated cardiomyopathy, impaired platelet aggregation, and increased susceptibility to severe bacterial infection (15). This deficiency syndrome is entirely prevented by providing at least 0.5% of total dietary energy intake as linoleic acid. In commercial enteral and parenteral formulations, soybean and safflower oils serve as the primary sources of linoleic acid (15).

##### Propofol-Derived Lipid Burden

Propofol (2,6-diisopropylphenol), an intravenous hypnotic widely utilized for sedation in mechanically ventilated patients, is formulated as a 10% lipid emulsion containing soybean oil, glycerol, and egg yolk phospholipids (2,3,16). This vehicle provides $1.1\text{ kcal/mL}$ of nonprotein lipid calories.

In patients receiving high-dose or prolonged propofol infusions, the lipid vehicle contributes a substantial caloric load that can easily cause severe hypertriglyceridemia and overfeeding if not subtracted from the patient's nutritional regimen (16). Daily monitoring of serum triglycerides is mandatory, and nutritional lipid infusions should be reduced or discontinued if propofol provides significant caloric delivery (2,3,16).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Complications of Excessive Lipid Infusion</summary><div class="details-content">

Clearance of circulating triglycerides is mediated by endothelial lipoprotein lipase. During severe systemic inflammation and sepsis, lipoprotein lipase activity is substantially suppressed, resulting in impaired clearance of intravenous lipid emulsions (16).

Excessive lipid administration promotes fat overload syndrome, characterized by hypertriglyceridemia, hepatosplenomegaly, hepatic dysfunction, fever, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, and coagulopathy (16). Furthermore, intravenous free fatty acids can induce pulmonary vasoconstriction, worsen ventilation-perfusion mismatching, impair reticuloendothelial clearance of bacteremia, and provoke acute pulmonary microvascular injury through free radical lipid peroxidation (17).

Consequently, clinical guidelines recommend a maximum lipid intake of $1.5\text{ g/kg/day}$ (3). Serum triglyceride levels should be monitored every 48 to 72 hours, with lipid reduction recommended if triglycerides exceed $300\text{–}400\text{ mg/dL}$ ($3.4\text{–}4.5\text{ mmol/L}$) (2,3).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Protein Requirements and Nitrogen Balance</summary><div class="details-content">

In healthy resting adults, baseline protein turnover requires a daily intake of 0.8 to $1.0\text{ g/kg/day}$ to replace obligate losses. However, critical illness triggers a catabolic stress state driven by cortisol, catecholamines, and inflammatory cytokines, accelerating skeletal muscle proteolysis up to threefold (1,2,18).

To attenuate progressive lean muscle wasting, current guidelines recommend a daily protein intake of 1.2 to $2.0\text{ g/kg/day}$ in critically ill adult patients (1,2). In patients with major thermal injuries, multitrauma, or open abdomen, requirements may reach $2.5\text{ g/kg/day}$ (2).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Evaluation of Nitrogen Balance</summary><div class="details-content">

The adequacy of protein intake can be quantitatively assessed via nitrogen balance—the mathematical difference between 24-hour nitrogen intake and 24-hour total nitrogen excretion. Nitrogen balance calculations should only be performed in hemodynamically stable patients with steady renal function (18).

##### Nitrogen Excretion

Approximately two-thirds of nitrogen derived from endogenous protein catabolism is excreted through the kidneys, with roughly 85% of urinary nitrogen contained in urea (the remainder comprising creatinine, uric acid, and ammonia) (18). The remaining protein-derived nitrogen is excreted through stool, sweat, shed skin, and normal respiratory secretions, amounting to approximately 4 to $6\text{ g/day}$ in the absence of severe gastrointestinal pathology. Total 24-hour nitrogen excretion is expressed as (18):

$$\text{Nitrogen Excretion (g/24 h)} = \text{UUN (g/24 h)} + (4\text{–}6) \qquad (48.4)$$

where $\text{UUN}$ is the measured 24-hour urinary urea nitrogen in grams. In patients with active diarrhea, fistulous drainage, or massive burn exudate, non-urinary nitrogen losses cannot be estimated accurately, rendering standard balance calculations invalid.

##### Nitrogen Intake

Natural dietary proteins contain an average of 16% elemental nitrogen by weight, which establishes the conversion factor of $1\text{ g nitrogen}$ per $6.25\text{ g protein}$ (18):

$$\text{Nitrogen Intake (g/24 h)} = \text{Protein Intake (g/24 h)} / 6.25 \qquad (48.5)$$

##### Net Nitrogen Balance

Net nitrogen balance across a 24-hour period is derived by subtracting excretion from intake:

$$\text{Nitrogen Balance (g/24 h)} = [\text{Protein Intake (g/24 h)} / 6.25] - [\text{UUN (g/24 h)} + (4\text{–}6)] \qquad (48.6)$$

A negative nitrogen balance reflects net muscle proteolysis and loss of structural protein, whereas a neutral or positive balance denotes preservation or rebuilding of lean body mass.

#### Nonprotein Calories and Nitrogen Retention</summary><div class="details-content">

Providing abundant dietary protein cannot restore a positive nitrogen balance if nonprotein energy intake is insufficient. When caloric intake fails to cover daily resting energy expenditure, administered amino acids are deaminated and funneled into hepatic gluconeogenesis to satisfy cellular ATP demands, rather than being incorporated into protein synthesis.

#### Recommendations for Protein Intake in Critical Illness</summary><div class="details-content">

Achieving the targeted protein intake of 1.2 to $2.0\text{ g/kg/day}$ is frequently challenging when using standard enteral formulations, because typical commercial tube feeds have a nonprotein calorie-to-nitrogen ratio ($\text{NPC:N}$) between $130:1$ and $150:1$. In an enterally fed patient whose caloric goal is restricted to $20\text{ kcal/kg/day}$, standard formulas provide only $0.8\text{–}1.0\text{ g protein/kg/day}$.

To achieve recommended protein targets without inducing caloric overfeeding, modular protein supplements (liquid or powdered whey protein, intact caseinates, or free amino acid packets) should be added to the feeding regimen (1–3).

In patients with acute kidney injury receiving renal replacement therapy (continuous venovenous hemodiafiltration or intermittent hemodialysis), dialytic amino acid clearance can exceed 10 to $15\text{ g/day}$, justifying higher protein targets up to $2.0\text{–}2.5\text{ g/kg/day}$ to sustain nitrogen equilibrium (2,3).

In critically ill patients, systemic inflammation, hypermetabolism, altered gastrointestinal absorption, increased renal clearance, and continuous dialytic losses dramatically elevate daily vitamin requirements. Several specific vitamin deficiency states carry critical clinical implications.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Thiamine Deficiency</summary><div class="details-content">

Thiamine (vitamin $\text{B}_1$) is an obligate cofactor for carbohydrate metabolism. Once absorbed, thiamine is phosphorylated into its active coenzyme form, thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP). TPP functions as the prosthetic group for three key multi-enzyme complexes (19,20):

- **Pyruvate Dehydrogenase:** Catalyzes the oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, linking cytosolic glycolysis to the mitochondrial tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle.
- **$\alpha$-Ketoglutarate Dehydrogenase:** Drives the oxidative conversion of $\alpha$-ketoglutarate to succinyl-CoA within the TCA cycle.
- **Transketolase:** Governs non-oxidative reactions within the pentose phosphate pathway, producing ribose-5-phosphate for nucleotide synthesis and NADPH for cellular glutathione reduction.

Total body thiamine stores in healthy adults are remarkably modest (approximately $30\text{ mg}$), and complete dietary deprivation leads to total thiamine exhaustion within 2 to 3 weeks (19).

#### Pathophysiology and Predisposing Conditions</summary><div class="details-content">

Thiamine deficiency is frequently underrecognized in intensive care units. Chronic alcohol abuse is the single leading predisposing factor; ethanol directly impairs intestinal brush-border thiamine transport, reduces hepatic storage capacity, and inhibits phosphorylation into TPP (20).

However, thiamine deficiency occurs commonly in non-alcoholic ICU patients:

- **Septic Shock:** Prevalence surveys have documented biochemical thiamine deficiency in up to 70% of septic shock patients on ICU admission, resulting from intense metabolic consumption and increased capillary leak (21).
- **Loop Diuretics:** Chronic furosemide administration significantly augments urinary thiamine excretion via diuresis-induced kaliuresis and natriuresis, contributing to severe thiamine depletion in patients with congestive heart failure (22).
- **Magnesium Depletion:** The enzyme thiamine pyrophosphokinase, which converts free thiamine into active TPP, is strictly magnesium-dependent. In patients with uncorrected hypomagnesemia, administered thiamine cannot be utilized by tissues (23,24).
- **Carbohydrate-Induced Refeeding Deficit:** Infusing large glucose loads (e.g., $5\%$ or $10\%$ dextrose maintenance fluids or high-carbohydrate parenteral nutrition) into patients with depleted thiamine reserves forces glycolytic flux through pyruvate dehydrogenase, consuming residual TPP and triggering acute metabolic collapse (25,26).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Clinical Syndromes and Neuroimaging</summary><div class="details-content">

The classical clinical manifestations of thiamine deficiency comprise distinct cardiovascular and neurological syndromes (Table 48.4):

- **High-Output Heart Failure ("Wet Beriberi"):** Characterized by severe peripheral arteriolar vasodilation, decreased systemic vascular resistance, compensatory high-output biventricular failure, pulmonary congestion, and peripheral edema (27,28). Although chronic furosemide therapy promotes thiamine deficiency, routine thiamine supplementation in ambulatory heart failure has not consistently demonstrated hemodynamic improvement (29).
- **Peripheral Neuropathy ("Dry Beriberi"):** Symmetrical motor and sensory axonal polyneuropathy, characterized by distal paresthesias, foot drop, hyporeflexia, and muscle tenderness (30).
- **Type B Lactic Acidosis:** When pyruvate dehydrogenase is paralyzed by TPP depletion, pyruvate cannot enter the TCA cycle and is instead shunted to lactate via cytosolic lactate dehydrogenase (19,31). Thiamine deficiency should always be suspected in ICU patients presenting with unexplained or worsening lactic acidosis without tissue hypoperfusion (31,32).
- **Wernicke's Encephalopathy:** The most catastrophic consequence of thiamine deficiency. The classical triad consists of acute encephalopathy (confusion, lethargy, coma), oculomotor dysfunction (nystagmus, bilateral lateral rectus palsy, conjugate gaze paralysis), and gait ataxia (30). However, the complete classic triad is present in only 20% of cases (30). Approximately 80% of patients present solely with altered mental status, and 30% exhibit isolated ocular abnormalities (30).

#### Diagnostic Evaluation and Multistep Treatment Protocol</summary><div class="details-content">

Serum or plasma free thiamine levels correlate poorly with intracellular stores and are unreliable. Direct measurement of whole-blood thiamine pyrophosphate (normal reference range: 63 to $229\text{ nmol/L}$) via high-performance liquid chromatography represents the definitive laboratory test (30). However, because test results often require several days, thiamine deficiency remains a clinical diagnosis.

Empiric therapy must be instituted immediately upon clinical suspicion. Conventional doses of $100\text{ mg}$ thiamine daily are completely inadequate for treating or reversing acute thiamine-deficient neurovascular syndromes (33). The validated high-dose parenteral repletion protocol is structured as follows (33,34):

##### Four-Step Thiamine Repletion Regimen

1. **Step 1 (Acute Loading):** Thiamine $500\text{ mg}$ IV infused over 30 minutes every 8 hours for 2 to 3 days.
2. **Step 2 (Step-Down IV):** Thiamine $250\text{ mg}$ IV once daily for 3 to 5 days.
3. **Step 3 (Oral Consolidation):** Thiamine $100\text{ mg}$ orally three times daily for 1 to 2 weeks.
4. **Step 4 (Maintenance):** Thiamine $100\text{ mg}$ orally once daily until full nutritional recovery.

Intravenous administration is mandatory during the initial phase because intestinal thiamine absorption is severely deranged in critically ill patients (33). Furthermore, concurrent hypomagnesemia must be aggressively identified and treated with intravenous magnesium sulfate (targeting serum magnesium $\ge 2.0\text{ mg/dL}$ or $\ge 0.8\text{ mmol/L}$) to enable enzymatic synthesis of active TPP (23,24).

### Vitamin D Deficiency in Critical Illness</summary><div class="details-content">

Biochemical vitamin D deficiency is documented in 40% to 100% of intensive care patients on admission (35). This staggering prevalence reflects baseline community-acquired deficiency compounded by acute critical illness, hemodilution, decreased hepatic synthesis of vitamin D-binding protein, and inadequate micronutrient content in standard enteral formulas (which supply only 200–$400\text{ IU/day}$, well below the standard baseline requirement of $600\text{ IU/day}$) (35).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Immune Regulation and Consequences</summary><div class="details-content">

Beyond its classical role in skeletal calcium-phosphate homeostasis, the active hormonal metabolite of vitamin D, 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D ($1{,}25\text{-(OH)}_2\text{D}$ or calcitriol), exerts profound pleiotropic immunomodulatory actions (36). Calcitriol binds nuclear vitamin D receptors on macrophages, monocytes, and dendritic cells, stimulating the transcription of antimicrobial peptides (cathelicidin and $\beta$-defensins), suppressing excessive pro-inflammatory cytokine expression (interleukin-6, TNF-$\alpha$), and modulating endothelial barrier permeability (36). Severe vitamin D deficiency in the ICU is strongly associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, increased susceptibility to nosocomial sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and higher 30-day mortality (35,36).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Laboratory Diagnosis and Supplementation</summary><div class="details-content">

Vitamin D status is established by measuring the stable circulating storage metabolite, 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25-OHD] (37):

- **Deficiency State:** Plasma 25-OHD $\lt 50\text{ nmol/L}$ ($\lt 20\text{ ng/mL}$).
- **Severe Deficiency:** Plasma 25-OHD $\lt 30\text{ nmol/L}$ ($\lt 12\text{ ng/mL}$).

Oral and enteral absorption of vitamin D is unreliable in critically ill patients due to gastroparesis, ileus, mucosal edema, and impaired bile acid secretion (37). Clinical trials have demonstrated that a single intramuscular injection of $150{,}000\text{ IU}$ of cholecalciferol safely restores plasma 25-OHD concentrations to normal physiological levels in 90% of critically ill adults within 72 hours (37). High-dose oral loading regimens ($540{,}000\text{ IU}$) evaluated in randomized trials (e.g., the VIOLET trial) have normalized serum levels but did not confer an overall 90-day survival benefit across unselected ICU populations.

</div></details>
</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Antioxidant Vitamins (Vitamins C and E)</summary><div class="details-content">

Intense systemic inflammation and ischemia-reperfusion generate massive quantities of reactive oxygen species (ROS)—including superoxide anions ($\text{O}_2^{\bullet-}$), hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$), and hydroxyl radicals ($^{\bullet}\text{OH}$)—that overwhelm endogenous antioxidant defenses, inflicting severe lipid peroxidation, protein carbonylation, and DNA fragmentation (38,44).

Two essential vitamins constitute the frontline non-enzymatic antioxidant barrier (38–40):

- **Vitamin E ($\alpha$-Tocopherol):** The preeminent hydrophobic, chain-breaking antioxidant in human biology. Lipophilic $\alpha$-tocopherol intercalates within phospholipid bilayers, intercepting peroxyl radicals and halting the propagation of autocatalytic membrane lipid peroxidation (39).
- **Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid):** The paramount hydrophilic, water-soluble antioxidant. Ascorbate scavenges aqueous superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, inhibits leukocyte-endothelial adhesion, and acts as an obligate electron donor to reduce oxidized $\alpha$-tocopheroxyl radicals back into functional $\alpha$-tocopherol at the membrane interface (40).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Protective Mechanisms and Clinical Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Circulating concentrations of both ascorbic acid and $\alpha$-tocopherol plummet rapidly following major trauma, sepsis, burns, and cardiac arrest (41,42). While early phase II investigations of high-dose intravenous ascorbic acid (often combined with thiamine and hydrocortisone) reported dramatic improvements in vasopressor requirements and organ clearance, large multicenter randomized clinical trials (including the LOVIT and CITRIS-ALI trials) failed to demonstrate mortality reductions or improvements in organ dysfunction, and high-dose vitamin C was associated with a higher risk of death or organ dysfunction in sepsis in some cohorts (38,43).

Consequently, current international guidelines do not endorse supraphysiological intravenous "antioxidant megadosing" (3). Instead, standard nutritional repletion ensuring the daily baseline requirements of vitamin C ($90\text{ mg/day}$) and vitamin E ($15\text{ mg/day}$) is recommended to prevent overt tissue deficiency (1–3).

Two essential trace elements—iron and selenium—command extraordinary relevance in critical care due to their profound interplay with cellular oxidative injury and tissue inflammation.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Iron and Oxidative Cytotoxicity</summary><div class="details-content">

The total body iron content in healthy adults approximates $4.5\text{ g}$. Virtually all physiological iron is tightly chelated within functional complexes (hemoglobin, myoglobin, cytochromes) or intracellular storage proteins (ferritin, hemosiderin) (46). In the circulation, ionic iron is bound to transferrin, which normally exhibits a saturation of only 30% (46). This abundant unbound transferrin capacity ensures that free, unchelated iron is kept at virtually undetectable concentrations in plasma.

The strict biological sequestering of iron represents a vital evolutionary defense mechanism: unchelated iron is extraordinarily cytotoxic (46,47).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Hydroxyl Radical Generation and Ferroptosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Free iron promotes catastrophic cellular and tissue injury through two primary mechanisms:

- **Fenton and Haber-Weiss Chemistry:** In its reduced ferrous state ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$), free iron reacts directly with hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$), generating the hydroxyl radical ($^{\bullet}\text{OH}$):
  $$\text{Fe}^{2+} + \text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{Fe}^{3+} + ^{\bullet}\text{OH} + \text{OH}^-$$
  The hydroxyl radical is the most reactive and biologically damaging free radical known, possessing a nanosecond half-life and instantly damaging nucleic acids, peroxidizing membrane lipids, and denaturing functional proteins (47).
- **Induction of Ferroptosis:** Iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation triggers ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of non-apoptotic regulated cell death characterized by the loss of plasma membrane integrity, mitochondrial shrinkage, and rupture of outer mitochondrial membranes (48). Ferroptosis is driven by acute inflammatory cascades and plays a central role in acute kidney injury, myocardial infarction, and sepsis-induced multiorgan failure (48,49).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Iron Restriction and Evaluation of Stores</summary><div class="details-content">

During acute critical illness and systemic inflammation, hepatic synthesis of hepcidin rises dramatically in response to interleukin-6. Hepcidin binds and degrades the cellular iron exporter ferroportin on enterocytes and macrophages, trapping iron intracellularly and inducing profound hypoferremia (50). This hypoferremia is an adaptive evolutionary antimicrobial response ("nutritional immunity") that deprives invading pathogens of the iron required for replication (50).

Administering parenteral iron during active critical illness bypasses this protective mechanism, floods the circulation with non-transferrin-bound iron, triggers oxidative bursts, and exacerbates bacteremia and organ damage (47,50). Consequently, iron administration is contraindicated during acute systemic inflammation. Iron therapy should only be considered during convalescence when tissue iron depletion is unequivocally established (serum ferritin $\lt 18\ \mu\text{g/L}$ in the absence of inflammation; ferritin values exceeding $100\ \mu\text{g/L}$ exclude true iron deficiency) (51).

</div></details>
</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Selenium and Antioxidant Defenses</summary><div class="details-content">

Selenium is an essential micronutrient incorporated into twenty-five selenoproteins as the unique 21st amino acid, selenocysteine. The preeminent selenoprotein is glutathione peroxidase, an intracellular enzymatic antioxidant that reduces toxic hydrogen peroxide and organic lipid hydroperoxides into water and harmless alcohols, utilizing reduced glutathione (GSH) as an electron donor (52,53).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Glutathione Peroxidase Activity and Supplementation</summary><div class="details-content">

Baseline selenium requirements are $55\ \mu\text{g/day}$ in healthy adults (Table 48.5). During critical illness, systemic selenium utilization accelerates, and circulating levels drop precipitously due to urinary losses, capillary leak, and inflammatory consumption (45,52). Plasma selenium concentrations below $100\ \mu\text{g/L}$ correlate directly with suppressed glutathione peroxidase activity and severe oxidative organ damage (53).

Intravenous selenium supplementation (administered as sodium selenite) can support cellular antioxidant enzymatic capacity. However, clinical trials examining supraphysiological selenium boluses have not demonstrated consistent survival improvements (54). International guidelines recommend providing physiological trace element supplementation without exceeding a maximum daily selenium dose of $225\ \mu\text{g/day}$ to avoid selenium toxicity (54).

## A Final Word: A Fly in the Ointment</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### The Paradox of Feeding in Critical Illness</summary><div class="details-content">

The classical historical rationale for providing specialized nutrition support to critically ill patients has rested upon a straightforward hypothesis: acute severe illness induces muscle wasting and malnutrition, and infusing calories and protein will correct or alleviate that malnutrition.

However, this rationale conflates starvation with stress-induced hypermetabolism. Simple starvation represents pure nutritional deprivation: fuel stores are depleted, basal metabolic rate decreases, insulin levels fall, lipolysis predominates, and exogenous nutrient administration immediately halts catabolism and restores cellular equilibrium (55). In sharp contrast, the catabolic state of acute critical illness is not caused by starvation; it is the direct consequence of severe neuroendocrine and inflammatory activation that fundamentally deranges cellular nutrient processing (55).

Providing exogenous nutrients cannot correct this metabolic injury until the underlying critical illness is resolved. In fact, forced nutritional support during the acute phase can induce substantial physiological harm (55).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Abnormal Substrate Processing and Glycolytic Shunting</summary><div class="details-content">

A striking manifestation of abnormal nutrient processing during acute critical illness is the cellular fate of infused glucose. In healthy individuals, fewer than 5% of an intravenous glucose load is converted into lactate, with the remaining 95% oxidized to carbon dioxide or stored as glycogen. In critically ill and surgical patients, however, as much as 85% of an administered glucose load is shunted into lactate production (56).

#### Lipid Peroxidation and Tissue Harm</summary><div class="details-content">

A parallel danger attends the administration of intravenous lipid emulsions during acute inflammation. Infusing large quantities of polyunsaturated fatty acids into an environment saturated with reactive oxygen species accelerates lipid peroxidation, producing cytotoxic lipid hydroperoxides, impairing pulmonary endothelial gas exchange, and suppressing reticuloendothelial clearance of bacteremia (17).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Evolutionary Adaptation: Anorexia of Acute Infection</summary><div class="details-content">

These metabolic realities explain why large landmark multicenter randomized clinical trials—including EPaNIC (early vs late parenteral nutrition), EDEN (initial trophic vs full enteral feeding), and PERMIT (permissive underfeeding vs standard enteral feeding)—have consistently demonstrated that aggressive early caloric administration does not reduce mortality or infection rates, but instead prolongs mechanical ventilation, increases ICU length of stay, and elevates organ failure scores.

The acute loss of appetite (anorexia) observed across all mammalian species during acute infection and trauma is not an evolutionary defect requiring aggressive reversal with feeding tubes. It represents a highly conserved, adaptive physiological response that limits substrate availability to invading microorganisms, stimulates cytoprotective macroautophagy, clears damaged organelles, and reduces cellular metabolic workload (55).

Hippocrates recognized this fundamental clinical truth nearly twenty-five centuries ago:

> *The more impure bodies are fed, the more diseased they will become.*
