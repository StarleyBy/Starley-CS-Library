# Platelets and Plasma

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

Hemostatic competence is critical for surviving vascular injury and major surgical interventions in intensive care. Abnormalities in platelet count, platelet function, and circulating coagulation factors are exceptionally common, but their clinical management is frequently guided by misperceptions:
* **The Clinical Spectrum of Thrombocytopenia:** While thrombocytopenia occurs in up to 60% of ICU patients, primary hemostatic plug formation remains intact until platelets fall below $100,000/\mu\text{L}$. In the absence of an anatomical bleeding source, spontaneous hemorrhage is exceedingly rare until counts drop below $10,000/\mu\text{L}$.
* **The Paradox of Thrombosis over Bleeding:** In critically ill patients, thrombocytopenia is most often an indicator of catastrophic thrombotic consumption rather than a bleeding diathesis. Conditions such as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), and hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) present with low platelets but manifest primarily with life-threatening arterial and venous thrombosis or microvascular occlusion.
* **Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia:** An immune-mediated catastrophe triggered by IgG antibodies against platelet factor 4 (PF4)-heparin complexes, carrying a 25–70% incidence of thrombosis and a 30% mortality if unrecognized. Diagnosis rests on pre-test probability scoring (the 4Ts score). Immediate cessation of all heparin and substitution with non-heparin anticoagulants (argatroban, bivalirudin, or DOACs) is mandatory, while warfarin is strictly contraindicated during the acute phase.
* **Platelet Transfusion Standards:** Prophylactic platelet transfusions are indicated only for severe hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia ($\le 10,000/\mu\text{L}$) or prior to invasive procedures ($< 50,000/\mu\text{L}$ for surgery/lumbar puncture, $< 20,000/\mu\text{L}$ for central venous catheterization). Platelet transfusions are contraindicated in thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP/HUS) and HIT, where they fuel microvascular thrombosis.
* **Plasma and the Fallacy of the INR:** Prophylactic transfusion of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) to "correct" mild-to-moderate INR elevations in non-bleeding patients is ineffective and hazardous. FFP transfusions lower the INR while simultaneously replenishing endogenous anticoagulant proteins (antithrombin, protein C, protein S), producing no net gain in thrombin generation while exposing patients to volume overload (TACO) and lung injury (TRALI). Acute warfarin reversal is achieved rapidly and safely with 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (4F-PCC) rather than plasma.

## Overview of Hemostasis and Response to Injury</summary><div class="details-content">

Vascular endothelium maintains a thromboresistant barrier that prevents intravascular clot formation under basal flow conditions.

### The Endothelial Glycocalyx
The luminal surface of healthy vascular endothelium is lined by the glycocalyx, a carbohydrate-rich meshwork of proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans, and adsorbed plasma proteins:
* **Macromolecular Barrier:** Restricts transvascular flux of large plasma proteins (albumin), sustaining intravascular oncotic pressure.
* **Antithrombotic Fluidity:** Prevents platelet and leukocyte adherence to quiescent endothelium.
* **Shear Sensing:** Serves as a mechanotransducer for fluid shear stress, stimulating endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) to release nitric oxide (NO) and prostacyclin ($\text{PGI}_2$), promoting tonic vasodilation and inhibiting platelet activation.

### The Biphasic Hemostatic Response
Disruption of the vascular endothelial barrier triggers a coordinated biphasic hemostatic cascade:
1. **Primary Hemostasis (Platelet Plug Formation):**
   * Platelets adhere to exposed subendothelial collagen via von Willebrand factor (vWF) binding to glycoprotein Ib/IX receptors.
   * Adherent platelets degranulate, releasing calcium, adenosine diphosphate (ADP), and thromboxane $\text{A}_2$.
   * Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa surface receptor complexes undergo conformational activation, binding plasma fibrinogen to form cross-linked platelet aggregates that plug the vascular breach.
2. **Secondary Hemostasis (Fibrin Generation):**
   * **Tissue Factor Pathway:** Subendothelial tissue factor binds activated factor VII (FVIIa), initiating the extrinsic tenase complex.
   * **Contact Activation Pathway:** Kininogens and factor XII initiate the intrinsic pathway upon contact with charged surfaces.
   * Both pathways converge on the prothrombinase complex, converting prothrombin (Factor II) into active thrombin ($\text{FIIa}$). Thrombin cleaves soluble fibrinogen (Factor I) into insoluble fibrin monomers that polymerize and are cross-linked by factor XIIIa, transforming the fragile platelet plug into a stable, contracted thrombus.

## Thrombocytopenia in the Critically Ill</summary><div class="details-content">

Thrombocytopenia occurs in 30% to 60% of intensive care patients, serving as an independent predictor of prolonged ICU stay and mortality.

### Definition and Hemostatic Thresholds
* **Reference Parameters:** Normal circulating platelet count ranges between $150,000\text{ and } 400,000/\mu\text{L}$.
* **Functional Integrity:** Primary hemostatic capacity is fully maintained until the platelet count drops below $100,000/\mu\text{L}$.
* **Spontaneous Bleeding Threshold:** In the absence of an anatomical lesion, trauma, or platelet dysfunction, spontaneous microvascular bleeding does not occur until counts decline to $\le 5,000\text{--}10,000/\mu\text{L}$.

### Pseudothrombocytopenia
Spurious laboratory thrombocytopenia occurs in approximately 2% of hospitalized patients due to EDTA-dependent agglutinins:
* In vitro exposure to ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) induces conformational changes in glycoprotein IIb/IIIa, causing cold-dependent platelet clumping. Automated hematology analyzers misclassify platelet clumps as leukocytes, reporting artifactually depressed platelet counts.
* **Diagnostic Verification:** Examination of a peripheral blood smear reveals prominent platelet clumps. The artifact is eliminated by repeating the platelet count in a collection tube containing sodium citrate or lithium heparin.

## Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia</summary><div class="details-content">

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT, Type II) is a life-threatening, immune-mediated prothrombotic drug reaction occurring in 0.5% to 5% of patients exposed to heparin.

### Immunopathogenesis
Heparin binds electrostatically to positively charged platelet factor 4 (PF4) tetramers released from platelet $\alpha$-granules:
* **Antigenic Complex:** Stoichiometric binding forms multimolecular PF4-heparin complexes that provoke the synthesis of high-affinity IgG antibodies.
* **Platelet Activation:** The IgG-PF4-heparin immune complex binds to platelet $\text{Fc}\gamma\text{RIIa}$ receptors, inducing intense platelet activation, microparticle shedding, and systemic thrombin generation.
* **Non-Dose-Dependent Nature:** Sensitization can occur following trivial heparin exposure, including intravascular catheter flushes or heparin-bonded arterial lines.
* **Incidence Variance:** Risk is 2- to 10-fold higher with unfractionated heparin (UFH) than with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH). Incidence ranges from $< 1\%$ in medical ICU patients to $1\text{--}3\%$ following cardiothoracic or major orthopedic surgery.

### Clinical Presentation and Thrombotic Hazards
* **Timing of Onset:** Classic HIT manifests 5 to 10 days after starting heparin. Rapid-onset HIT occurs within 24 hours in patients possessing circulating antibodies from heparin exposure within the preceding 3 months (90 days). Delayed-onset HIT manifests up to 3 weeks after heparin has been discontinued.
* **Thrombocytopenia Profile:** Platelet counts typically drop by $\ge 50\%$ from baseline; absolute nadirs rarely fall below $20,000/\mu\text{L}$.
* **Thrombosis, Not Bleeding:** Bleeding is exceptionally rare in isolated HIT. Untreated HIT carries a 17% to 55% incidence of venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, venous limb gangrene) and a 1% to 3% incidence of catastrophic arterial thrombosis (ischemic stroke, acute limb ischemia, myocardial infarction). In 25% of cases, thrombosis precedes the onset of thrombocytopenia.

### Diagnostic Testing and Clinical Algorithm
* **PF4-Heparin Immunoassay (ELISA):** Highly sensitive ($> 98\%$), but low specificity ($50\text{--}70\%$) due to non-pathogenic antibodies. A negative ELISA reliably excludes HIT; a positive result must be interpreted in clinical context.
* **Serotonin Release Assay (SRA):** The definitive gold-standard functional assay. Measures radiolabeled serotonin release from healthy donor platelets in the presence of patient serum and therapeutic heparin concentrations.
* **Clinical Management Protocol:**
  1. For intermediate or high 4Ts scores, immediately discontinue all heparin formulations (including flushes and catheter lock solutions).
  2. Initiate therapeutic non-heparin anticoagulation promptly without awaiting confirmatory laboratory results (delaying therapy confers a tenfold increase in thrombosis).
  3. Perform bilateral lower-extremity compression ultrasonography to screen for subclinical deep vein thrombosis.

### Alternative Anticoagulants in Acute HIT
* **Warfarin is Strictly Contraindicated:** Administering vitamin K antagonists in acute HIT precipitates microvascular thrombosis and venous limb gangrene by rapidly depleting protein C before prothrombin is suppressed. If warfarin was recently administered, reverse it with intravenous vitamin K.
* **Argatroban:** A synthetic direct thrombin inhibitor with hepatic elimination, approved for HIT:
  * Initiate continuous infusion at $1\text{--}2\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$ (reduce to $0.5\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$ in hepatic dysfunction or multi-organ failure).
  * Titrate to achieve an activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) 1.5 to 3.0 times baseline.
  * No dosage adjustment required for renal insufficiency. Continue until platelets normalize ($> 150,000/\mu\text{L}$).
* **Bivalirudin:** Preferred in patients with combined renal and hepatic failure or those undergoing urgent cardiac surgery or percutaneous coronary intervention.
* **DOACs (Rivaroxaban):** Recommended for clinically stable patients and for post-discharge oral anticoagulation for 1 to 3 months. Patients must wear an emergency medical alert and avoid heparin for life.

## Thrombotic Microangiopathies</summary><div class="details-content">

The thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs) are life-threatening syndromes characterized by consumptive thrombocytopenia, microvascular platelet-fibrin thrombosis, and microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA).

### Disseminated Intravascular Coagulation (DIC)
A secondary systemic thrombo-hemorrhagic syndrome provoked by massive tissue factor exposure in severe trauma, septic shock, acute promyelocytic leukemia, or obstetric catastrophes (amniotic fluid embolism, abruptio placentae):
* **Pathophysiology:** Uncontrolled thrombin generation leads to widespread microvascular thrombosis, tissue ischemia, and consumptive exhaustion of platelets and clotting factors ([Table 13.3](#tab-13-3)). Secondary hyperfibrinolysis generates high concentrations of D-dimers.
* **Purpura Fulminans:** A malignant form seen in meningococcemia and severe sepsis, manifested by peripheral acral gangrene and cutaneous infarction.
* **Therapy:** Management centers on aggressive treatment of the underlying cause. Platelets, cryoprecipitate (target fibrinogen $> 150\text{ mg/dL}$), and plasma are restricted strictly to patients with active hemorrhage or prior to urgent surgery. Mortality exceeds 80% when accompanied by multi-organ failure.

### Thrombotic Thrombocytopenic Purpura (TTP)
A medical emergency that is fatal within 24–48 hours if untreated, occurring predominantly in adult females (female-to-male ratio 3:1):
* **Pathophysiology:** Caused by autoantibodies neutralizing the metalloprotease ADAMTS-13 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motifs, member 13). ADAMTS-13 deficiency ($< 10\%$ activity) prevents cleavage of ultra-large vWF multimers, provoking spontaneous microvascular platelet aggregation.
* **The Classic Pentad:** Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, severe thrombocytopenia, fluctuating neurological deficits, fever, and mild renal impairment (serum creatinine typically $< 2.0\text{ mg/dL}$). The complete pentad is present in $< 10\%$ of cases; the dyad of thrombocytopenia and MAHA without another clear cause mandates emergency therapy.
* **Emergency Treatment:** Immediate therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) replacing 1.5 plasma volumes daily with donor plasma, combined with systemic corticosteroids and caplacizumab (an anti-vWF nanobody). Early initiation within 48 hours converts a 90% mortality rate into a 90% survival rate. Platelet transfusions are contraindicated.

### Hemolytic Uremic Syndrome (HUS)
Characterized by the triad of microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and dominant acute kidney injury:
* **Typical (Shiga-Toxin) HUS:** Triggered by enterohemorrhagic *Escherichia coli* (STEC serotype O157:H7) producing Shiga-like toxins that injure renal glomerular capillary endothelial cells. Treatment is supportive; antibiotics and plasma exchange are avoided.
* **Atypical HUS (aHUS):** An unregulated alternative complement pathway disorder caused by genetic mutations in complement regulatory proteins (Factor H, Factor I, CD46). It manifests with severe hypertension, acute renal failure, and extrarenal involvement in 20% of cases. Treatment of choice is eculizumab or ravulizumab (monoclonal antibodies inhibiting complement protein C5).

## Platelet Transfusions and Administration Guidelines</summary><div class="details-content">

Platelet transfusions should be reserved for patients with active hemorrhage or documented high bleeding risk.

### Clinical Indications
* **Active Hemorrhage:** Maintain platelet count $> 50,000/\mu\text{L}$ in major trauma, surgery, or gastrointestinal bleeding. In intracranial or central nervous system hemorrhage, maintain platelets $> 100,000/\mu\text{L}$.
* **Prophylactic Transfusion Thresholds (AABB Guidelines):**
  * Clinically stable ICU patients without bleeding: transfuse at $\le 10,000/\mu\text{L}$.
  * Elective major surgery or lumbar puncture: transfuse at $< 50,000/\mu\text{L}$.
  * Central venous line placement: transfuse at $< 20,000/\mu\text{L}$.
* **Absolute Contraindications:** Thrombotic microangiopathies (TTP and HUS) and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), where transfused platelets fuel microvascular thrombosis.

### Platelet Formulations
1. **Whole Blood-Derived (Pooled) Platelets:** Prepared from 4 to 6 donors by differential centrifugation. A standard pooled dose contains $\approx 38 \times 10^{10}$ platelets in $260\text{ mL}$ of plasma, yielding an equivalent concentration of $130 \times 10^9/\mu\text{L}$.
2. **Apheresis (Single-Donor) Platelets:** Harvested from a single donor using automated cell separators, yielding an equivalent therapeutic dose ($3 \times 10^{11}$ platelets).
3. **Storage and Leukoreduction:** Platelets are stored at room temperature ($20\text{--}24\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) under continuous agitation, limiting shelf-life to 5 days. Universal leukocyte reduction diminishes febrile nonhemolytic reactions, prevents HLA alloimmunization, and eliminates CMV transmission.

### Post-Transfusion Yield and Refractoriness
* **Expected Increment:** In an average adult, one therapeutic unit of platelets raises the circulating count by $35,000\text{ to } 50,000/\mu\text{L}$ measured at 1 hour post-infusion ([Fig. 13.2](#fig-13-2)). The count declines by roughly 40% at 24 hours due to normal consumption.
* **Platelet Refractoriness:** Repeated transfusions diminish subsequent yields (a 25% drop after 5 transfusions) secondary to recipient alloantibodies against donor HLA class I or ABO antigens. Managed by providing leukocyte-reduced, ABO-identical, or HLA-matched platelets.

## Plasma Products and Coagulation Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Plasma products provide exogenous coagulation factors and natural anticoagulant proteins to treat complex coagulopathies.

### Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP)
Prepared by centrifuging donor blood and freezing plasma at $\le -18\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ within 8 hours of phlebotomy:
* **Specifications:** Unit volume $\approx 230\text{ mL}$; stored for up to 12 months. Thawed at 37 °C and stored at 1–6 °C for up to 5 days.
* **Massive Transfusion:** Administered in balanced $1:1$ or $1:2$ ratios with PRBCs during massive hemorrhage protocols to prevent dilutional coagulopathy.
* **Liquid Plasma:** Plasma that is never frozen and maintained at 1–6 °C for up to 26 days, eliminating thawing delays during emergency resuscitation.

### The Fallacy of the INR and Prophylactic FFP
* **Ineffective Practice:** Up to 70% of FFP is prescribed to non-bleeding ICU patients (e.g., cirrhotics) to normalize mildly elevated INRs ($1.5\text{--}2.0$) before minor procedures. Randomized trials confirm that prophylactic FFP does not reduce bleeding risks.
* **Biochemical Equilibrium:** The INR measures only procoagulant factors (II, VII, X). FFP replenishes both procoagulants and natural anticoagulant proteins (antithrombin, protein C, protein S) in parallel ([Fig. 13.3](#fig-13-3)). Consequently, the net capacity to generate thrombin remains unchanged.
* **Hazards of FFP:** High-volume plasma administration exposes patients to circulatory overload (TACO, 1 in 70 to 1,500 units), allergic reactions (1 in 600 to 2,000), and TRALI (1 in 250,000).

### Reversal of Warfarin Anticoagulation
* **4-Factor Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (4F-PCC):** The treatment of choice for life-threatening bleeding or urgent reversal of vitamin K antagonists.
* **Superiority over FFP:** 4F-PCC contains concentrated factors II, VII, IX, and X plus protein C and S in a small volume ($100\text{ mL}$). Combined with intravenous vitamin K ($10\text{ mg}$), 4F-PCC normalizes INR within 30 minutes without thawing delays or risk of fluid overload, rendering FFP obsolete for this indication.

### Cryoprecipitate
Formed by collecting the cold-insoluble precipitate that forms when FFP is thawed at 4 °C:
* **Constituents and Yield:** A single unit of cryoprecipitate ($10\text{--}15\text{ mL}$) contains $150\text{--}250\text{ mg}$ of fibrinogen (concentration $10\text{--}20\text{ g/L}$), along with factor VIII, von Willebrand factor, factor XIII, and fibronectin. Stored frozen at $\le -18\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ for 12 months; once thawed, it must be administered within 4 hours.
* **Indications:** Indicated when plasma fibrinogen drops below $150\text{--}200\text{ mg/dL}$ during massive trauma resuscitation or severe obstetric hemorrhage. Dosing at 1 unit per 10 kg body weight raises plasma fibrinogen by approximately $50\text{ mg/dL}$.
* **Fibrinogen Concentrates:** Highly purified, viral-inactivated human fibrinogen concentrates ($20\text{ g/L}$) provide standardized dosing without thawing delays or pathogen risks, increasingly replacing cryoprecipitate in modern critical care.

## A Final Word: Changing Perceptions</summary><div class="details-content">

Contemporary clinical science mandates a fundamental revision of traditional concepts in transfusion medicine:
1. **Thrombocytopenia Signifies Underlying Disease:** In non-bleeding patients, an isolated low platelet count is not a mandate for transfusion, but a diagnostic indicator of underlying sepsis, drug-induced clearance, or microangiopathy.
2. **Thrombosis Exceeds Hemorrhage as a Threat:** The primary clinical danger in syndromes featuring severe thrombocytopenia (HIT, DIC, TTP, HUS) is widespread macro- and microvascular thrombosis, not bleeding. Prophylactic platelet administration in these settings is dangerous.
3. **The INR is an Unreliable Guide:** The international normalized ratio assesses only vitamin K-dependent procoagulant proteins. Prescribing fresh frozen plasma to correct isolated laboratory INR elevations in non-bleeding patients exposes patients to circulatory overload and acute lung injury without providing hemostatic benefit.
