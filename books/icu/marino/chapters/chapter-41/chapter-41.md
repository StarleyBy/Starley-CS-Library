# Abdominal Infections in the ICU

> *My dear Watson, you see, but you do not observe.*  
> — Arthur Conan Doyle (1891)*

*\*Doyle AC. Adventures of Sherlock Holmes; Adventure I: A Scandal in Bohemia. Strand Magazine, July 2, 1891.*

The concept of the gastrointestinal tract as a noxious toxic reservoir first gained prominent medical attention in the early decades of the twentieth century, when the Scottish surgeon Sir William Arbuthnot-Lane performed prophylactic total colectomies in patients with chronic constipation to eradicate "autointoxication" from stagnant fecal contents (1). Although this radical surgical approach was deservedly abandoned, the fundamental concept of intestinal autointoxication has witnessed a remarkable scientific renaissance: the gut is now universally acknowledged as a primary engine of systemic inflammation, sepsis, and multiorgan failure in the critically ill.

This chapter describes the principal life-threatening intra-abdominal infectious syndromes encountered during an intensive care unit (ICU) admission, encompassing ischemic inflammation of the gallbladder (acalculous cholecystitis), antimicrobial-induced colonic dysbiosis (*Clostridioides difficile* colitis), and complicated secondary peritoneal infections (bowel perforations and postoperative anastomotic leaks).

### Key Principles and Clinical Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

- **The Gut as a Sepsis Driver:** The gastrointestinal tract houses billions of luminal microorganisms separated from the peritoneal cavity and vascular tree by a vulnerable single-cell mucosal layer. In critically ill patients, mucosal hypoperfusion, systemic inflammation, antibiotic-driven dysbiosis, and prolonged bowel rest disrupt this barrier, converting the gut into a major source of secondary bacteremia, peritonitis, and multiorgan failure.
- **Acalculous Cholecystitis:** A severe necroinflammatory gallbladder disorder occurring without mechanical gallstone obstruction of the cystic duct. Prevalent in patients with shock, sepsis, cardiac surgery on cardiopulmonary bypass, and prolonged total parenteral nutrition (TPN). Localized signs (RUQ tenderness) are absent in one-third of cases; bacteremia with aerobic gram-negative bacilli is documented in 90%. Ultrasound sensitivity is 81%; cholescintigraphy (HIDA scan) remains the diagnostic gold standard. Urgent intervention (laparoscopic cholecystectomy or percutaneous cholecystostomy) is mandatory.
- **Biliary Penetration of Antibiotics:** Hydrophilic beta-lactams vary markedly in biliary excretion. Ampicillin-sulbactam, ceftriaxone, piperacillin-tazobactam, ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, and tigecycline achieve high therapeutic levels in bile, whereas cefepime, cefotaxime, ceftazidime, gentamicin, meropenem, and vancomycin penetrate poorly.
- **Clostridioides difficile Colitis (CDI):** The leading cause of healthcare-associated infectious diarrhea. Colonization is driven by antimicrobial disruption of the protective colonic microbiome. Gastric acid suppression, particularly with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), markedly escalates CDI risk by neutralizing the antibacterial gastric acid barrier. Enterotoxins A and B induce mucosal necrosis, pseudomembrane formation, and watery diarrhea.
- **Two-Step Diagnostic Algorithm for CDI:** Testing mandates a two-step algorithm: initial rapid PCR screening for the Toxin B gene (high sensitivity, but cannot differentiate harmless colonization from active disease), followed by enzyme immunoassay (ELISA) for free fecal toxins A and B to confirm active infection. Treating based on a positive PCR alone leads to widespread overdiagnosis and unnecessary antibiotic administration.
- **CDI Pharmacotherapy and Step-Up Protocols:** Non-fulminant initial CDI is treated with oral fidaxomicin ($200\text{ mg}$ PO twice daily) or oral vancomycin ($125\text{ mg}$ PO four times daily) for 10 days. Metronidazole is no longer recommended for routine monotherapy. Fulminant CDI (hypotension, ileus, toxic megacolon) requires high-dose enteral vancomycin ($500\text{ mg}$ PO/NG/PR every 6 hours) combined with intravenous metronidazole ($500\text{ mg}$ IV every 8 hours); toxic megacolon mandates emergency subtotal colectomy. Recurrent CDI responds to pulsed/tapered vancomycin, fidaxomicin, or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT, cure rate 80% to 100%).
- **Complicated Intra-Abdominal Infections (cIAI):** Infections breaching the peritoneal cavity (diffuse peritonitis or localized abscesses) require immediate surgical or percutaneous source control and broad-spectrum antibiotics. Upright chest radiography reveals pneumoperitoneum in up to 85% of bowel perforations, but contrast-enhanced CT is far superior for anatomical localization. Intraperitoneal free air normally persists for up to 7 to 10 days following laparotomy, limiting its diagnostic specificity for acute postoperative leaks.
- **Empiric Antimicrobial Stewardship in cIAI:** Coverage must target enteric gram-negative bacilli, streptococci, and anaerobes (*Bacteroides fragilis*). Monotherapy with piperacillin-tazobactam is first-line for non-critically ill presentations. When extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) producers are suspected, meropenem is the agent of choice. Tigecycline covers ESBL and enterococci, but lacks activity against *Pseudomonas* and should be avoided in septic shock.
- **Empirical Antifungal Coverage:** Upper gastrointestinal perforations, recurrent anastomotic dehiscence, prolonged ICU length of stay, and prior broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure are major risk factors for *Candida* peritonitis. Empirical echinocandin therapy (anidulafungin, caspofungin, or micafungin) is indicated in critically ill patients with septic shock.
- **Postoperative Abscesses and Drainage:** Occur as occult septic foci following abdominal surgery. Localizing physical signs are absent in over 60% of cases. Abdominal CT performed after the first postoperative week is the diagnostic test of choice. Percutaneous image-guided catheter drainage achieves definitive source control in 90% to 95% of cases, avoiding reoperation.

## Acalculous Cholecystitis</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute acalculous cholecystitis (AAC) is an acute necroinflammatory disease of the gallbladder developing in the absence of cystic duct obstruction by gallstones (2,3).

Unlike routine calculous cholecystitis managed on general surgical wards, acalculous cholecystitis develops almost exclusively in critically ill patients. It carries an aggressive clinical course, high rates of gangrene and perforation, and hospital mortality exceeding 30% if recognition is delayed.

#### Pathophysiology and Predisposing Conditions

AAC develops in vulnerable ICU populations experiencing profound metabolic or circulatory stress. Predisposing clinical conditions include extensive thermal burns, major trauma, systemic sepsis, circulatory shock, prolonged mechanical ventilation, total parenteral nutrition (TPN) with complete enteral bowel rest, and major surgical procedures (particularly coronary artery bypass grafting and abdominal aortic aneurysm repair) (3–6).

The pathogenesis is multifactorial, driven by a lethal confluence of microvascular ischemia and concentrated chemical irritation:

- 1. *Microvascular Ischemia:* Splanchnic vasoconstriction during shock, vasopressor infusions, or cardiopulmonary bypass starves the end-arterial supply of the gallbladder wall, causing ischemic mucosal disruption.
- 2. *Biliary Stasis and Sludge:* Absence of enteral nutrition eliminates cholecystokinin release, producing gallbladder distension, biliary stasis, and accumulation of dense biliary sludge containing cholesterol crystals and calcium bilirubinate "microliths" (7).
- 3. *Chemical and Bacterial Toxicity:* Concentrated bile salts and lysophosphatidylcholine exert direct chemical detergent toxicity on the ischemic mucosa. Relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi permits the ascending migration of enteric bacteria into the biliary tree.

#### Clinical Manifestations

The clinical presentation of acalculous cholecystitis in the ICU is notoriously subtle and nonspecific. Patients are frequently sedated, mechanically ventilated, or encephalopathic, masking early symptoms.

Unexplained fever, progressive leukocytosis, and worsening hyperdynamic shock with escalating vasopressor requirements are classic presentations. Physical examination is frequently unreliable: *localized right upper quadrant pain, tenderness, and Murphy's sign are completely absent in one-third of affected patients* (8).

Blood cultures are positive in approximately 90% of cases (8), with aerobic gram-negative enteric bacilli (*Escherichia coli*, *Klebsiella* species) and enterococci isolated in the vast majority of episodes.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Diagnostic Evaluation of Acalculous Cholecystitis</summary><div class="details-content">

Bedside transabdominal ultrasonography is the preferred initial imaging examination for suspected acalculous cholecystitis, but its diagnostic accuracy is limited in ICU patients. Gallbladder distension ($> 5\text{ cm}$ fasting transverse diameter) and biliary sludge are common supportive findings, but they occur frequently as non-pathological artifacts of prolonged fasting.

More specific sonographic criteria include gallbladder wall thickening ($> 3.5\text{–}4.0\text{ mm}$ in the absence of ascites or hypoalbuminemia), pericholecystic fluid halo, intramural gas, and mucosal sloughing.

Figure 41.1 displays a classic sonogram of advanced gangrenous acalculous cholecystitis: marked mural thickening is accompanied by an irregular, intraluminal echogenic membrane representing sloughed, necrotic gallbladder mucosa.

#### Advanced Diagnostic Modalities

The diagnostic yield of ultrasonography varies considerably across ICU studies; a meta-analysis of 26 investigations demonstrated a pooled sensitivity of 81% and a specificity of 83% (9).

When ultrasound findings are equivocal, *hepatobiliary iminodiacetic acid (HIDA) cholescintigraphy is the diagnostic gold standard for acalculous cholecystitis* (2). Non-visualization of the gallbladder with prompt tracer excretion into the common bile duct and duodenum confirms cystic duct obstruction or severe functional exclusion. However, severe cholestasis (bilirubin $> 5\text{ mg/dL}$) or parenchymal liver failure impairs hepatic tracer uptake, yielding false-positive examinations.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provides diagnostic sensitivity and specificity comparable to ultrasonography, whereas contrast-enhanced CT displays lower diagnostic reliability in distinguishing uncomplicated sludge from early acalculous inflammation (2).

### Therapeutic Management and Biliary Antibiotics</summary><div class="details-content">

Prompt intervention is mandatory, as acalculous cholecystitis deteriorates rapidly into mural gangrene, empyema, and free perforation into the peritoneal cavity.

Laparoscopic or open cholecystectomy represents definitive treatment (2). However, for hemodynamically unstable ICU patients with severe multiorgan failure who cannot withstand general anesthesia, bedside *percutaneous cholecystostomy* under ultrasound or fluoroscopic guidance is a safe, highly effective alternative (2). Percutaneous catheter decompression arrests the ischemic-inflammatory cycle, yielding rapid hemodynamic improvement within 24 to 48 hours.

Concurrently, empirical intravenous antimicrobial therapy covering enteric gram-negative bacilli, enterococci, and anaerobes must be instituted immediately upon suspicion of disease.

##### Pharmacological Considerations in Biliary Infections

The choice of antimicrobial agent in acalculous cholecystitis must account for drug pharmacokinetics and penetration into bile, as detailed in Table 41.1.

Lipophilic fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin), ureidopenicillins (piperacillin-tazobactam), and ceftriaxone achieve high concentrations in bile exceeding serum levels.

Conversely, aminoglycosides (gentamicin), carbapenems (meropenem), and glycopeptides (vancomycin) achieve relatively poor penetration into obstructed or inflamed biliary secretions, necessitating agent selection aligned with specific microbial and pharmacokinetic profiles.

## Clostridioides difficile Colitis</summary><div class="details-content">

As described in Chapter 4, the indigenous microbial ecosystem of the human intestinal tract—the *microbiome*—exerts powerful colonization resistance against pathogenic bacterial invasion (10).

Disruption of this ecological equilibrium by systemic antibiotics, designated *dysbiosis*, permits the selective overgrowth of virulent enteric pathogens. The most prominent and lethal manifestation of intestinal dysbiosis in the ICU is *Clostridioides difficile* enterocolitis, recognized as the single most frequent healthcare-associated infection worldwide (11).

#### Pathogenesis and Cytotoxin Production

*Clostridioides difficile* (formerly *Clostridium difficile*) is an obligate anaerobic, spore-forming, gram-positive bacillus. While dormant spores colonize a small fraction of healthy adults, the organism proliferates aggressively when broad-spectrum antimicrobials eradicate the protective commensal anaerobic microbiota (12,13).

Primary clinical risk factors include advanced age, hospitalization or long-term care exposure, immunosuppression, gastrointestinal surgery, and prior antimicrobial exposure (particularly fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, clindamycin, and penicillins) (12,13). Although spores are occasionally recovered from retail meat and poultry (14), transmission in healthcare environments occurs overwhelmingly via the fecal-oral route, mediated by heat- and alcohol-resistant spores on healthcare workers' hands.

##### Exotoxin Cytotoxicity and Pseudomembranes

*C. difficile* is non-invasive; its pathogenicity is mediated by two potent exotoxins: Toxin A (an enterotoxin) and Toxin B (a cytotoxin). Both toxins glucosylate small Rho GTPases within colonocytes, causing disruption of the actin cytoskeleton, breakdown of epithelial tight junctions, and massive mucosal cell apoptosis.

The resulting inflammatory exudate, composed of fibrin, mucus, necrotic epithelial cells, and neutrophils, forms raised, yellowish mucosal plaques termed *pseudomembranes*. The presence of these lesions defines *pseudomembranous colitis*, the hallmark of severe infection.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Gastric Acid Suppression and Pathogenesis</summary><div class="details-content">

Gastric hydrochloric acid serves as a fundamental physical and chemical firewall against ingested pathogens (see Chapter 4, Figure 4.3).

Multiple epidemiological and clinical investigations demonstrate that pharmacological acid suppression, particularly the routine use of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), is associated with a significantly increased risk of developing *C. difficile* colitis (15–17). Neutralization of gastric acidity facilitates the intraluminal germination of ingested spores into vegetative, toxin-producing bacilli.

This physiological link has profound clinical implications given the rampant, frequently unjustified prescribing of proton pump inhibitors for stress ulcer prophylaxis in hospitalized patients (a topic revisited in the concluding section of this chapter).

#### Clinical Spectrum: From Diarrhea to Toxic Megacolon

The cardinal clinical manifestation of *C. difficile* infection (CDI) is new-onset, copious, non-bloody watery diarrhea (defined as $\ge 3$ unformed stools within 24 hours). Accompanying signs in uncomplicated episodes include low-grade fever ($\le 101^\circ\text{F}$ or $\le 38.3^\circ\text{C}$), lower abdominal cramping, and mild leukocytosis (white blood cell count $< 15{,}000/\text{mm}^3$).

Fulminant colitis develops in 3% to 5% of CDI episodes (18). Fulminant disease is heralded by systemic toxicity: hypotension, high-dose vasopressor requirements, severe lactic acidosis, marked leukocytosis ($\text{WBC} > 30{,}000/\text{mm}^3$ or leukemoid reaction $> 50{,}000/\text{mm}^3$), and diffuse peritonitis. Paradoxically, diarrhea may completely cease due to adynamic ileus.

The most catastrophic complication of fulminant CDI is *toxic megacolon*, illustrated in Figure 41.2: acute, non-obstructive dilatation of the colon (transverse colon diameter $> 6\text{ cm}$) accompanied by systemic toxicity, carrying an imminent hazard of free perforation and death.

### Diagnostic Stool Testing Algorithms</summary><div class="details-content">

Diagnostic evaluation requires laboratory testing performed exclusively on unformed, diarrheal stool samples. Two principal laboratory methodologies are utilized in clinical practice:

- 1. *Nucleic Acid Amplification Test (NAAT/PCR):* Detects the gene encoding Toxin B (*tcdB*). PCR is exceptionally sensitive ($> 95\%$), but it detects the mere genetic presence of toxigenic *C. difficile*, failing to distinguish harmless asymptomatic colonization from active toxin-producing disease.
- 2. *Enzyme Immunoassay (EIA/ELISA) for Free Toxins A and B:* Directly measures active vegetative cytotoxins in the stool. EIA is highly specific ($> 95\%$), confirming active disease, but exhibits suboptimal sensitivity (60% to 80%), missing up to 20% of clinically significant infections (12).

#### Interpreting the Multistep Diagnostic Algorithm

Initial screening begins with a highly sensitive NAAT/PCR (or glutamate dehydrogenase [GDH] antigen test). A negative PCR definitively excludes toxigenic *C. difficile*, safely ruling out CDI without further testing.

When PCR testing is positive, an enzyme immunoassay for Toxins A and B must be performed immediately on the same specimen. A positive ELISA establishes the definitive diagnosis of active CDI, warranting targeted antimicrobial therapy.

##### The Pitfall of Isolated PCR Overdiagnosis

A positive PCR coupled with a negative toxin ELISA indicates one of two scenarios: true infection missed by the low sensitivity of EIA, or asymptomatic colonization in a patient with diarrhea from another cause (e.g., tube feeding, osmotic laxatives).

Approximately 50% of patients testing positive by PCR lack free toxins by immunoassay (19), and clinical trials demonstrate that these PCR-positive/toxin-negative individuals do exceptionally well without specific antibiotic therapy (19). Treating patients based solely on PCR results leads to substantial overdiagnosis, unnecessary antibiotic toxicity, and escalating healthcare expenditures.

When clinical suspicion of severe colitis remains high despite discordant or negative stool assays, urgent diagnostic endoscopy (colonoscopy or flexible sigmoidoscopy) provides direct visualization of pseudomembranes, securing the diagnosis.

#### Initial Therapy and Fulminant Crisis

For initial non-fulminant episodes, oral fidaxomicin ($200\text{ mg}$ BID for 10 days) is the preferred agent, with oral vancomycin ($125\text{ mg}$ QID for 10 days) serving as an effective alternative (13). Clinical defervescence occurs within 24 to 48 hours, and diarrhea typically resolves within 4 to 5 days (20). Oral metronidazole is inferior and is no longer recommended as monotherapy.

Fulminant CDI represents a surgical and medical emergency requiring dual-route therapy: high-dose enteral vancomycin ($500\text{ mg}$ PO/NG every 6 hours) plus intravenous metronidazole ($500\text{ mg}$ IV every 8 hours) (13). In the presence of adynamic ileus or toxic megacolon, vancomycin cannot transit the small bowel; high-dose vancomycin retention enemas ($500\text{ mg}$ in $100\text{ mL}$ saline via rectal catheter every 6 hours) must be co-administered.

Patients with toxic megacolon, colonic perforation, or worsening lactic acidosis despite medical therapy require emergency surgical consultation; subtotal colectomy with end-ileostomy is the surgical procedure of choice (13).

#### Management of Recurrent Infection and Fecal Transplantation

Symptomatic recurrence occurs in approximately 25% of patients treated with vancomycin (21) and 13% of patients treated with fidaxomicin (22), typically emerging within 2 to 4 weeks of completing therapy. Recurrence is driven by the persistence of hardy spores or failure of the colonic microbiota to reconstitute.

Approximately 5% of patients suffer multiple debilitating relapses (20). For second or subsequent recurrences, *fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)* is the most effective therapeutic intervention available (13,23). FMT involves the delivery of filtered liquid stool from thoroughly screened healthy donors directly into the patient's gastrointestinal tract (via colonoscopy, retention enema, or oral lyophilized capsules).

By immediately re-establishing a diverse, physiologically balanced anaerobic microbiome, FMT achieves an extraordinary clinical cure rate of 80% to 100% in refractory recurrent CDI (23).

#### Infection Control and Probiotic Cautions

Preventing nosocomial transmission requires strict contact precautions: dedicated gloves and gowns for all patient encounters, and mandatory mechanical handwashing with soap and water (alcohol-based hand rubs do not inactivate *C. difficile* spores) (13).

Probiotics are widely promoted to support colonic health (24). However, their routine use during or following CDI is *not recommended* by the American College of Gastroenterology (12). Clinical trials have demonstrated conflicting results, and experimental evidence reveals that *probiotic administration can paradoxically delay natural recolonization and diversity recovery of the host microbiome following antibiotic exposure* (25).

## Complicated Intra-Abdominal Infections</summary><div class="details-content">

Complicated intra-abdominal infections (cIAIs) are defined as infectious processes extending beyond the organ of origin into the peritoneal cavity, manifesting either as diffuse generalized peritonitis or as localized intra-abdominal abscesses (26).

These life-threatening emergencies typically result from catastrophic disruption of the gastrointestinal tract (traumatic or spontaneous perforation) or breakdown of a surgical anastomosis following abdominal surgery. Successful management demands a rapid, coordinated dual approach: immediate anatomical source control and targeted empiric antimicrobial therapy.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Gastrointestinal Perforation</summary><div class="details-content">

Perforations of the alimentary canal arise from a wide variety of pathological processes: penetrating or blunt abdominal trauma, peptic ulcer disease, acute mesenteric ischemia, closed-loop mechanical bowel obstruction, diverticulitis, transmural Crohn's disease, and iatrogenic surgical enterotomies.

Abdominal pain is nearly universal, accompanied by physical signs of peritoneal irritation: involuntary guarding, board-like abdominal rigidity, and rebound tenderness. Progression to septic shock and distributive collapse can be precipitously rapid (26).

#### Diagnostic Imaging of Free Peritoneal Air

The definitive diagnosis of gastrointestinal perforation requires radiographical demonstration of extraluminal free air within the peritoneal cavity (pneumoperitoneum).

Erect chest radiography demonstrating crescents of free subdiaphragmatic gas under one or both hemidiaphragms exhibits a sensitivity of up to 85% for bowel perforations (27), as depicted in Figure 41.4.

##### Cross-Sectional Imaging and Postoperative Caveats

Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT is substantially more sensitive than plain radiographs, accurately identifying pneumoperitoneum and precisely localizing the site of gastrointestinal perforation in 85% of cases (28).

Importantly, *pneumoperitoneum is a non-diagnostic finding following recent abdominal surgery, as residual intra-abdominal air normally persists for at least 7 to 10 days post-laparotomy* (29). In the early postoperative period, diagnosis relies on CT demonstration of extraluminal contrast extravasation, progressive fluid collections, or unexplained clinical deterioration.

#### Perioperative Resuscitation and Hemodynamic Optimization

While immediate surgical exploration is the definitive priority to achieve source control, preoperative resuscitation is critical:

- 1. *Aggressive Fluid Resuscitation:* Gross peritoneal contamination induces massive capillary protein leakage and fluid shifts, generating severe hypovolemia. Early, controlled volume expansion with balanced crystalloids is vital.
- 2. *Judicious Vasopressor Management:* Vasoactive infusions should be minimized prior to surgical exploration whenever feasible. Alpha-adrenergic vasoconstrictors compromise splanchnic microvascular perfusion, accelerating ischemic necrosis of marginal bowel segments.

#### Specific Antibiotic Recommendations

Empiric antimicrobial selection is stratified according to patient severity, underlying risk factors, and local microbiological resistance patterns (26):

- 1. *Community-Acquired Infections without Resistance Risk:* In patients without previous hospital exposure, *piperacillin-tazobactam* ($4.5\text{ g}$ IV every 6 hours) provides excellent single-agent coverage against enteric gram-negative bacilli, streptococci, enterococci, and anaerobes (26). The combination of *ceftriaxone* ($1\text{–}2\text{ g}$ daily) plus *metronidazole* ($500\text{ mg}$ q8h) is an alternative for mild community infections, but its antimicrobial breadth is inferior.
- 2. *Healthcare-Associated and ESBL-Risk Infections:* Pathogens producing extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) are resistant to penicillins and third-generation cephalosporins. In high-risk patients (prior antibiotic therapy, nosocomial onset, or known colonization), carbapenems are indicated:
  - a. *Meropenem:* Meropenem ($1\text{ g}$ IV every 8 hours) provides the most comprehensive coverage and represents the treatment of choice in patients with septic shock (26). However, prudent stewardship is essential to curtail the emergence of carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (30). Meropenem is not active against MRSA or VRE.
  - b. *Tigecycline:* Tigecycline ($100\text{ mg}$ loading, then $50\text{ mg}$ IV q12h) covers ESBL organisms, MRSA, and VRE, but lacks activity against *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. It is associated with higher mortality and is *not recommended in septic shock* (26).
  - c. *Ceftazidime-avibactam:* Combines an anti-pseudomonal cephalosporin with a potent non-beta-lactam beta-lactamase inhibitor, providing potent activity against ESBL and carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (KPC). However, it lacks activity against enterococci and anaerobes, requiring the addition of metronidazole.
- 3. *Severe Beta-Lactam Anaphylaxis:* In patients with documented severe immediate-type hypersensitivity to beta-lactams, the combination of *amikacin* ($15\text{–}20\text{ mg/kg}$ daily) plus *metronidazole* ($500\text{ mg}$ q8h) provides adequate coverage.

#### Empirical Antifungal Therapy for Candida Peritonitis

*Candida albicans* and non-albicans *Candida* species are normal commensals of the upper gastrointestinal tract. In patients with upper GI perforations (stomach, duodenum), recurrent bowel anastomotic leaks, or prolonged ICU stays with prior antibiotic exposure, the incidence of invasive *Candida* peritonitis is high (31).

When these risk factors are present in a patient exhibiting septic shock, empirical antifungal therapy must be added to the antibacterial regimen. An echinocandin (*micafungin* $100\text{ mg}$ daily, *anidulafungin* $200\text{ mg}$ loading then $100\text{ mg}$ daily, or *caspofungin* $70\text{ mg}$ loading then $50\text{ mg}$ daily) is the preferred class of choice (31).

### Postoperative Peritonitis and Abdominal Abscess</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative peritonitis represents the most frequent intra-abdominal infection encountered in surgical ICU patients (32). It typically arises from anastomotic dehiscence following intestinal resection.

Postoperative peritonitis carries twice the mortality of community-acquired peritonitis, necessitating aggressive empirical coverage targeting multidrug-resistant ESBL pathogens and *Candida* species (32). Diagnosis is frequently delayed because the classic signs of acute peritonitis are masked by postoperative analgesia, sedation, or mechanical ventilation. Serum C-reactive protein (CRP) kinetics showing failure to decline by postoperative day 3 to 5 can provide an early warning of occult anastomotic leak (33).

#### Postoperative Intra-Abdominal Abscess

Postoperative abscesses frequently present as insidious, occult sources of persistent sepsis. Localizing physical signs are notoriously absent: abdominal tenderness is absent in over 60% of cases, and a palpable mass is detectable in fewer than 10% of patients (34,35). Plain abdominal radiographs demonstrate extraluminal gas in fewer than 15% of cases (35).

##### Diagnostic Timing and Image-Guided Drainage

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography is the gold standard for detecting postoperative abscesses. However, CT scans performed during the first postoperative week are frequently misleading, as sterile hematomas, seromas, or residual irrigation fluids mimic abscess cavities.

CT imaging achieves maximum diagnostic specificity when performed after the first postoperative week (postoperative days 7 to 10), after sterile physiological fluids have resorbed (35).

Percutaneous image-guided catheter drainage (guided by CT or ultrasonography) is the therapeutic modality of choice, achieving definitive source control and resolution in 90% to 95% of postoperative abscesses (36). Reoperative laparotomy is reserved strictly for complex multiloculated collections inaccessible to percutaneous needles or cases of ongoing anastomotic disruption.

## A Final Word: C. difficile and Gastric Acid</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### The Hazards of Routine Acid Suppression</summary><div class="details-content">

The fundamental physiological role of gastric hydrochloric acid as an antimicrobial firewall against ingested pathogens (described in detail in Chapter 4) rarely receives the critical clinical attention it demands.

A stark manifestation of this oversight is the established scientific evidence that pharmacological suppression of gastric acidity with proton pump inhibitors significantly promotes the transmission, colonization, and clinical emergence of *Clostridioides difficile* colitis (15–17).

#### Temporal Trends and Clinical Vigilance

The clinical implications are highlighted by a striking historical correlation: a marked surge in the national incidence and virulence of *C. difficile* infections occurred during the opening decade of the 21st century, precisely mirroring the widespread, uncritical adoption of proton pump inhibitors for routine stress ulcer prophylaxis in hospitalized patients (37).

Whether this relationship represents an extraordinary epidemiological coincidence or a direct causal mechanism, the vital antibacterial actions of gastric acid provide compelling justification to curb the routine, indiscriminate prescribing of gastric acid-suppressing agents in the ICU.
