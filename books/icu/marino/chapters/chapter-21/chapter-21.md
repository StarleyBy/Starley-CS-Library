# Cardiac Arrest

*When we all think alike, then no one is thinking.*
— Walter Lippmann (1889–1974)

In 1960, a landmark report in the *Journal of the American Medical Association* described closed-chest cardiac massage, demonstrating that chest compressions, electrical shocks, and artificial ventilation could reverse sudden cardiorespiratory arrest. That same year, the American Heart Association initiated training programs that evolved into modern cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Today, CPR is a mandated universal intervention, requiring recertification every 2 years. Despite its ubiquity, cardiac arrest resuscitation carries sobering outcomes, as summarized in Table 21.1.

## Executive Summary</summary>
<div class="details-content">

- **Epidemiology:** In the United States, out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) survival to hospital discharge is 9% (only 7% with favorable neurologic function); in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) survival to discharge is 19% (only 1% with good functional status). Non-shockable rhythms (PEA/asystole) account for over 75% of events.
- **Basic Life Support (BLS):** High-quality chest compressions form the bedrock of CPR.
  - *Metrics:* Rate 100–120/min, depth 2 to 2.4 inches (5 to 6 cm) over the lower sternum, full recoil between strokes, minimizing interruptions (< 10 seconds). Compressor rotation every 2 minutes.
  - *Ventilation:* Ratio 30:2 with bag-mask (500–600 mL per breath). Following endotracheal intubation, 1 breath every 6 seconds (10 breaths/min) asynchronous with compressions. Hyperventilation is dangerous; it creates intrinsic PEEP, reduces venous return, and diminishes coronary perfusion pressure.
- **Advanced Life Support (ACLS):**
  - *Shockable Rhythms (VF / Pulseless VT):* Immediate unsynchronized defibrillation (120–200 J biphasic), resumed immediately with 2 minutes of CPR before rhythm check. Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3 to 5 minutes after the second shock. Amiodarone 300 mg bolus (then 150 mg) or lidocaine 1 to 1.5 mg/kg for refractory VF/VT.
  - *Non-Shockable Rhythms (PEA / Asystole):* Epinephrine 1 mg immediately and every 3 to 5 minutes. Identify and aggressively treat reversible etiologies (the "4 Ts": Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade, Thromboembolism, and Thrombosis).
- **Physiologic Monitoring:** End-tidal $\text{PCO}_2$ ($\text{ETCO}_2$) directly reflects cardiac output during CPR. An $\text{ETCO}_2 < 10\text{--}15$ mmHg after 20 minutes of CPR strongly predicts resuscitation failure. Bedside point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) rapidly identifies pericardial tamponade and pneumothorax.
- **Post-Cardiac Arrest Care:**
  - *Post-Cardiac Arrest Syndrome:* Combines anoxic brain injury (responsible for 23% to 68% of post-ROSC deaths), myocardial stunning (resolving within 72 hours), and systemic inflammatory response.
  - *Targeted Temperature Management (TTM):* Prevention of fever (target temperature $\le 37.5^\circ\text{C}$ / $\le 99.5^\circ\text{F}$) for 72 hours is recommended for comatose survivors. Active surface cooling is initiated if temperature exceeds $37.7^\circ\text{C}$.
  - *Hemodynamics & Oxygenation:* Avoid hyperoxia (titrate $\text{FiO}_2$ for $\text{SaO}_2 \ge 90\%$). Target mean arterial pressure (MAP) $\ge 75$ mmHg using norepinephrine rather than epinephrine. Maintain blood glucose between 145 and 180 mg/dL, avoiding hypoglycemia.
- **Neuroprognostication:** Multimodal evaluation delayed for at least 72 hours after ROSC. Bilateral absence of pupillary light reflexes or corneal reflexes on day 4, status myoclonus, bilateral absence of cortical N20 somatosensory evoked potentials, or suppression-burst/isoelectric EEG indicates an extraordinarily poor prognosis.

## Basic Life Support</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Basic life support (BLS) centers on high-quality chest compressions and ventilation delivered via the standard *CAB* sequence (Circulation, Airway, Breathing).

### Epidemiology and Time Constraints

The outcome of cardiac arrest in the United States is summarized in Table 21.1. Non-shockable rhythms (pulseless electrical activity and asystole) predominate in both settings.

| Parameter | Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrests (OHCA) | In-Hospital Cardiac Arrests (IHCA) |
|---|---|---|
| **Total Annual Cases** | 143,018 | 8,619 |
| **Ventricular Tachycardia / Fibrillation** | 17% | 13% |
| **PEA / Asystole** | 75% | 78% |
| **Survived to Hospital Discharge** | 9% | 19% |
| **Survived with Favorable Functional Status** | 7% | 1% |

The whole-body oxygen reservoir in a healthy adult is approximately 1 liter. At a resting baseline oxygen consumption of 250 mL/min, total circulatory arrest completely depletes available oxygen within 4 minutes. Irreversible anoxic neuronal necrosis ensues within 4 to 5 minutes unless chest compressions are initiated promptly.

### Essential Components of BLS

The core requirements for effective basic life support are detailed in Table 21.2.

| BLS Element | Operational Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Initiation** | Start chest compressions within 10 seconds of recognizing pulselessness and apnea. |
| **Compression Depth** | Depress the lower third of the sternum by 2 to 2.4 inches (5 to 6 cm). |
| **Compression Rate** | 100 to 120 compressions per minute. |
| **Chest Recoil** | Allow full, unhindered chest recoil between compressions; avoid leaning on the chest. |
| **Compression-Ventilation Ratio** | Deliver 30 compressions followed by 2 ventilations (30:2) prior to advanced airway placement. |
| **Ventilation After Intubation** | 1 breath every 6 seconds (10 breaths/min) without pausing chest compressions. |
| **Minimizing Interruptions** | Limit interruptions in compressions to < 10 seconds (for rhythm checks or shock delivery). |
| **Compressor Rotation** | Switch the individual performing compressions every 2 minutes to prevent rescuer fatigue. |

### Chest Compressions and Perfusion

High-quality compressions generate cardiac output through a combination of direct cardiac compression (the cardiac pump) and variations in global intrathoracic pressure (the thoracic pump). Compressions generate at best 25% to 33% of baseline cardiac output and a fraction of normal coronary perfusion pressure. Any pause in compressions causes an instantaneous drop in coronary perfusion pressure that requires multiple subsequent compressions to rebuild.

### Ventilation and Dangers of Hyperventilation

Before tracheal intubation, ventilations are delivered with a self-inflating bag-mask unit:
- *Target Tidal Volume:* 500 to 600 mL per breath (visible chest rise). Adult ventilation bags typically possess a volume capacity of 1 to 2 liters. Squeezing a 1-liter bag halfway or using a gentle one-handed squeeze provides 600 to 700 mL, preventing over-inflation.
- *The Hazard of Hyperventilation:* Rescuers routinely hyperventilate patients during CPR, with recorded rates averaging 30 breaths/min (3 times the recommended rate). Rapid respiratory rates leave insufficient expiratory time, generating dynamic air trapping and progressive *intrinsic PEEP*. The resulting elevated intrathoracic pressure impedes venous return, drastically diminishes stroke volume during compressions, and reduces coronary perfusion pressure.

### Vascular Access

Vascular access must be achieved without interrupting chest compressions. While large-bore peripheral venous lines are acceptable, **intraosseous (IO) access** (proximal tibia or humeral head) is increasingly preferred in cardiac arrest because it is rapid, highly reliable, and avoids interfering with resuscitation efforts.

## Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Advanced cardiovascular life support (ACLS) integrates pharmacotherapy, advanced airway management, and electrical therapies.

### Shockable Rhythms: Ventricular Fibrillation and Pulseless VT

The primary therapeutic intervention for VF and pulseless VT is prompt electrical defibrillation.

![Defibrillation survival vs time](images/_page_4_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 21.1** Relationship between survival to hospital discharge and time from collapse to first electrical shock in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest caused by VF or pulseless VT. Survival decreases precipitously from 40% at 5 minutes to under 10% at 20 minutes.

- **Defibrillation Timing and Energy:** Biphasic defibrillators terminate VF at lower peak currents than monophasic devices. An initial shock of 120 to 200 J biphasic (or manufacturer recommendation) is delivered. Compressions are paused only for shock delivery and resumed immediately for 2 minutes without pausing to check the rhythm or pulse.
- **Epinephrine Administration:** If VF/VT persists after the second shock, epinephrine is administered as a 1 mg IV/IO bolus every 3 to 5 minutes.
- **Antiarrhythmic Therapy:** For refractory VF/VT persisting after three shocks, amiodarone is the first-line drug:
  - First dose: **300 mg IV/IO bolus**.
  - Second dose: **150 mg IV/IO bolus** if VF/VT recurs or persists.
  - *Lidocaine Alternative:* Initial bolus of 1 to 1.5 mg/kg IV/IO, followed by 0.5 to 0.75 mg/kg every 5 to 10 minutes to a maximum cumulative dose of 3 mg/kg.
- **Refractory VF and ECPR:** Survival drops to approximately 5% when three shocks and antiarrhythmic therapy fail. In specialized centers, extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR with venoarterial ECMO) provides hemodynamic stabilization and improves neurologically intact survival.

![AHA ACLS Algorithm](images/_page_6_Figure_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 21.2** American Heart Association Adult Cardiac Arrest Algorithm illustrating management branches for shockable (VF/pVT) and non-shockable (PEA/asystole) rhythms.

### Non-Shockable Rhythms: Asystole and PEA

In pulseless electrical activity (PEA) and asystole, electrical shocks are ineffective and contraindicated:
- **Vasopressor Protocol:** Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO is administered immediately upon confirmation of a non-shockable rhythm and repeated every 3 to 5 minutes.
- **Identification of Reversible Causes:** Search aggressively for correctable triggers, classically summarized as the *4 Ts*:
  1. *Tension pneumothorax:* Tracheal deviation, absent breath sounds, hyperresonance; treated with immediate needle decompression.
  2. *Tamponade (pericardial):* Distended neck veins, electrical alternans; treated with ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis.
  3. *Thromboembolism (pulmonary):* Massive PE; managed with systemic thrombolysis or surgical/catheter embolectomy.
  4. *Thrombosis (coronary):* Acute transmural occlusion; managed with post-ROSC emergent coronary angiography.

### Pharmacology and Mechanism of Epinephrine

Epinephrine remains the standard vasopressor in resuscitation algorithms. While it increases the rate of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), its impact on long-term neurologically intact survival remains contentious.

![Effect of epinephrine on coronary perfusion pressure](images/_page_7_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 21.3** Impact of intravenous epinephrine (1 mg) on coronary perfusion pressure (CPP) during CPR. Vasoconstriction induces a 30% rise in CPP that persists for approximately 3 minutes.

- **Mechanism of Benefit:** Intense $\alpha_1$-adrenergic peripheral vasoconstriction elevates aortic diastolic pressure, increasing **coronary perfusion pressure** (aortic diastolic minus right atrial diastolic pressure). A CPP exceeding 15 mmHg is necessary to achieve ROSC.
- **Adverse Actions:** Strong $\beta_1$-adrenergic stimulation increases myocardial oxygen consumption, exacerbates post-arrest myocardial stunning, and promotes lethal ventricular arrhythmias.

## Resuscitation Monitoring</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Manual pulse palpation during chest compressions is notoriously unreliable, with sensitivity below 50%, and frequently leads to prolonged interruptions in compressions. Continuous physiological monitoring provides superior real-time feedback.

### End-Tidal Carbon Dioxide ($\text{ETCO}_2$)

Continuous capnography via an endotracheal tube is the gold standard for monitoring circulation during CPR (see Figure 21.4).

- **Physiologic Principle:** When minute ventilation is kept constant, pulmonary blood flow (cardiac output) is the primary determinant of $\text{CO}_2$ delivery to the lungs. Thus, changes in $\text{ETCO}_2$ parallel changes in cardiac output generated by compressions. Baseline $\text{ETCO}_2$ is normally 40 mmHg.
- **Monitoring Compressions:** $\text{ETCO}_2$ values below 10 mmHg reflect poor compression mechanics, compressor fatigue, or severe hypovolemia.
- **Detection of ROSC:** A sudden, sustained increase in $\text{ETCO}_2$ (typically jumping to 35–45 mmHg) is an early, highly reliable indicator of ROSC, preceding palpable pulses.
- **Prognostic Threshold:** Failure to achieve an $\text{ETCO}_2 > 10\text{--}15$ mmHg after 20 minutes of CPR is strongly associated with resuscitation failure and nonsurvival. Conversely, if $\text{ETCO}_2$ remains elevated, prolonged CPR (up to 1.5 hours) may yield favorable functional recovery.

![Serial changes in ETCO2 during CPR](images/_page_8_Figure_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 21.4** Serial changes in end-tidal $\text{PCO}_2$ ($\text{ETCO}_2$) during cardiopulmonary resuscitation in patients who achieved ROSC compared to non-survivors. Non-responders demonstrate a progressive decline below 15 mmHg.

### Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS)

Focused echocardiography and thoracic ultrasound provide vital diagnostic information during brief pulse checks (< 10 seconds):
- **Pericardial Tamponade:** Readily identified in the subcostal or parasternal long-axis view as an anechoic fluid collection surrounding the myocardium (Figure 21.5). Intracardiac pressures are low during arrest, meaning classic diastolic collapse may be absent; any substantial effusion warrants immediate needle pericardiocentesis.
- **Pneumothorax:** Lung ultrasound excludes tension pneumothorax by demonstrating lung sliding (sensitivity 91%, specificity 99%).
- **Cardiac Standstill:** Complete absence of coordinated myocardial wall motion on ultrasound is highly predictive of death (100% in trauma arrests). However, because observer interpretation varies, current guidelines do not recommend using cardiac standstill alone to terminate resuscitation.

![Subcostal ultrasound of pericardial effusion](images/_page_9_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 21.5** Subcostal echocardiographic view demonstrating a large circumferential pericardial effusion visible as an anechoic space surrounding the cardiac chambers.

## Post-Cardiac Arrest Syndrome</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Return of spontaneous circulation is merely the initial step; approximately 70% of patients who achieve ROSC die prior to hospital discharge. The pathophysiologic entity responsible is the *post-cardiac arrest syndrome*, driven by whole-body ischemia followed by reperfusion injury.

### Components of the Syndrome

1. **Post-Arrest Brain Injury:** The leading cause of mortality, accounting for 23% to 68% of post-arrest deaths. Mechanisms include microvascular no-reflow, free radical-mediated lipid peroxidation, loss of autoregulation, and secondary cerebral edema.
2. **Post-Arrest Myocardial Dysfunction:** Manifests as global systolic and diastolic ventricular dysfunction (myocardial stunning) and transient low cardiac output. Fortunately, stunning is typically reversible within 72 hours under inotropic or mechanical support.
3. **Systemic Ischemia/Reperfusion Response:** A sepsis-like state characterized by massive cytokine release, endothelial activation, impaired vasoregulation, fever, and multiorgan failure.
4. **Persistent Precipitating Pathology:** Unresolved coronary thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, toxic ingestion, or sepsis.

## Targeted Temperature Management</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Elevated body temperature exacerbates ischemic neuronal death through accelerated metabolic demand, blood-brain barrier breakdown, and excitotoxicity. Temperature management is essential in post-arrest care.

### Evolution of Temperature Targets

- **1950:** Therapeutic hypothermia ("refrigeration") introduced, then abandoned due to profound sepsis and bleeding complications.
- **2002:** Landmark trials demonstrated improved neurologic recovery with mild hypothermia (32°C to 34°C) in comatose survivors.
- **2013:** A large multicenter trial demonstrated that maintaining 36°C achieved identical survival and neurologic outcomes to 33°C with fewer arrhythmias.
- **2021:** Subsequent randomized trials proved that strictly maintaining normothermia ($\le 37.5^\circ\text{C}$) was equivalent to 33°C in survival and functional recovery. Consequently, modern guidelines define the core goal of targeted temperature management (TTM) as **rigorous fever prevention**.

### Clinical Protocol for TTM

The clinical practice guidelines for TTM are outlined in Table 21.3.

| Parameter | Clinical Protocol and Recommendations |
|---|---|
| **Target Population** | All adult patients who remain comatose (unresponsive to verbal commands) following ROSC. |
| **Primary Goal** | Maintain core body temperature $\le 37.5^\circ\text{C}$ ($\le 99.5^\circ\text{F}$) continuously for **72 hours** post-ROSC. |
| **Temperature Monitoring** | Continuous core temperature monitoring via a thermistor-equipped urinary bladder catheter or esophageal probe. |
| **Mild Hypothermia Protocol** | Patients who are mildly hypothermic (32°C to 36°C) upon arrival should **not** be actively rewarmed. |
| **Active Cooling Trigger** | If core temperature exceeds **$37.7^\circ\text{C}$** ($99.9^\circ\text{F}$), initiate active surface cooling with closed-loop feedback targeting 37.5°C. |
| **Duration** | Maintain tight normothermia for 72 hours unless the patient awakens earlier. |

*Why Avoid Hypothermia (32°–34°C)?* Induced hypothermia causes cold-induced diuresis, hypokalemia, cardiac depression, bradycardia, impaired platelet function, and vigorous shivering. Controlling shivering requires high-dose sedatives and neuromuscular blockers, obscuring neurologic evaluations and prolonging mechanical ventilation.

## Post-Arrest Intensive Care Concerns</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Critical care management during the initial 72 hours post-arrest is summarized in Table 21.4.

| Clinical Parameter | Target and Management Strategy | Rationale and Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxygenation** | Titrate $\text{FiO}_2$ to maintain $\text{SaO}_2 \ge 90\%$ (avoid $\text{PaO}_2 > 200$ mmHg) | Post-arrest hyperoxia generates reactive oxygen species that worsen reperfusion neurotoxicity. |
| **Hemodynamics** | Target Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) $\ge 75$ mmHg | Cerebral autoregulation is disrupted; higher perfusion pressures ensure adequate cerebral blood flow. |
| **Vasopressor Selection** | **Norepinephrine** is preferred over epinephrine | Norepinephrine is associated with lower cardiovascular mortality, less tachycardia, and better neurologic recovery. |
| **Glycemic Control** | Maintain blood glucose between **145 and 180 mg/dL** | Both severe hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia aggravate brain injury. Avoid dextrose infusions. |

### Oxygenation and Hyperoxia

While hypoxemia ($\text{SaO}_2 < 90\%$) causes immediate secondary ischemic damage, severe hyperoxia ($\text{PaO}_2 > 200\text{--}300$ mmHg) triggers lipid peroxidation and accelerates neuronal apoptosis. Inspired oxygen should be adjusted to keep saturation between 92% and 98%.

### Hemodynamic Optimization

Impaired cerebral autoregulation renders cerebral perfusion entirely pressure-dependent. Although standard shock guidelines accept a MAP of 65 mmHg, cerebral recovery after cardiac arrest improves with a target MAP $\ge 75$ mmHg. Norepinephrine is the vasopressor of choice; epinephrine worsens metabolic acidosis and produces excessive beta-mediated tachycardia.

### Glycemic Targets

Post-arrest stress hyperglycemia stimulates cerebral lactic acidosis. However, tight glycemic control (80–110 mg/dL) carries a catastrophic risk of unrecognized hypoglycemia. A moderate target of 145 to 180 mg/dL is standard. Routine intravenous infusions of 5% dextrose solutions should be avoided.

## Neuroprognostication</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Accurate neuroprognostication prevents premature withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy while avoiding non-beneficial prolonged treatment. Prognostication must be multimodal and delayed for at least 72 hours after cardiac arrest (or after rewarming if hypothermia was used).

### Highly Reliable Predictors of Poor Outcome

Predictors of poor neurologic outcome (defined as persistent vegetative state, severe permanent disability, or brain death) with a false-positive rate approaching zero are detailed in Table 21.5.

| Diagnostic Modality | Finding Highly Predictive of Poor Neurologic Outcome | Clinical Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Pupillary Reflex** | Bilateral absence of pupillary light reflexes | Assessed on **day 4** post-ROSC |
| **Corneal Reflex** | Bilateral absence of corneal reflexes | Assessed on **day 4** post-ROSC |
| **Brainstem Reflexes** | Bilateral absence of oculocephalic or gag/cough reflexes | Assessed on **day 2** post-ROSC |
| **Motor Activity** | Status myoclonus (continuous generalized myoclonus) | Persistent within first 72 hours post-ROSC |
| **Evoked Potentials** | **Bilateral absence of cortical N20 response** on median nerve SSEP | Tested $\ge 72$ hours post-ROSC |
| **Electroencephalography** | Isoelectric EEG (< 2 $\mu$V), suppression-burst, or status epilepticus | Assessed $\ge 72$ hours post-ROSC |
| **Neuroimaging (CT/MRI)** | Diffuse cerebral edema, loss of gray-white matter differentiation | CT within 48 hours; MRI at 2 to 5 days |

### Clinical Pitfalls in Prognosis

- *Time to Awaken:* While 80% to 95% of patients who recover consciousness awaken within 72 hours, delayed awakening up to 5 days or longer occurs frequently, especially in patients with delayed sedative clearance or hypothermia. Neurologic evaluation must never be finalized prematurely.
- *Motor Posturing:* Decerebrate (extensor) posturing in response to pain is an unreliable predictor and must not be used alone to predict poor recovery.
- *Status Myoclonus vs. Isolated Myoclonus:* Status myoclonus (incessant generalized jerking lasting > 30 minutes) portends poor prognosis, whereas isolated or transient myoclonic jerks are compatible with favorable functional recovery.

## Public Perception versus Reality</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Cardiopulmonary resuscitation commands a level of public confidence that is completely divorced from clinical reality. Public opinion surveys reveal that 95% of individuals harbor unrealistic expectations regarding CPR, with a majority believing that more than 50% of cardiac arrest victims survive to return home with normal brain function. This misconception is reinforced by medical television entertainment, where CPR is portrayed as successful in up to 75% of cases.

As documented in Table 21.1, true survival to discharge with intact neurologic function is only 1% for in-hospital arrests and 7% for out-of-hospital arrests.

This disparity is of profound clinical and ethical importance. Because patients and family surrogate decision-makers determine code status and resuscitation preferences, these life-or-death decisions are frequently driven by dramatic fiction rather than factual medical prognosis. Clinicians bear the responsibility to communicate realistic outcomes when establishing advanced care directives.
