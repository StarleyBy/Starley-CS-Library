# Units and Conversions

> *The units of measurements in the medical sciences are taken from the metric system (centimeter, gram, second) and the Anglo-Saxon system (foot, pound, second). The metric units were introduced during the French Revolution and were revised in 1960. The revised units are called Système Internationale (SI) units and are currently the worldwide standard.*

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Core Measurement and Conversion Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Système Internationale (SI) Standards:** Modern clinical medicine relies on SI units derived from base dimensional parameters: mass ($M$), length ($L$), and time ($T$). Mechanical and thermodynamic variables (pressure in pascals, force in newtons, energy in joules) provide standardized equivalencies for everyday intensive care monitoring.
- **Electrolyte and Solute Equivalence:** Clinical concentrations of monovalent and divalent cations in aqueous solutions are converted between milliequivalents per liter ($\text{mEq/L}$), millimoles per liter ($\text{mmol/L}$), and milligrams per deciliter ($\text{mg/dL}$) using atomic weights and electrochemical valence.
- **Osmolality and Osmotic Activity:** Osmotic pressure is governed strictly by the number of solute particles in solution, independent of molecular size. One millimole of sodium exerting univalent osmotic activity produces the same colligative effect as one millimole of large uncharged molecules (such as glucose).
- **Vascular and Airway Sizing Scales:** Medical catheters and cannulas follow the French scale ($\text{French} = 3 \times \text{OD in mm}$), whereas hypodermic and vascular needles follow the inverse Gauge scale (where higher gauge corresponds to smaller outer diameter).

</div></details>
</div></details>

## Units of Measurement in the Système Internationale</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Mechanical and Thermodynamic Quantities</summary><div class="details-content">

The base dimensional parameters in mechanics are length ($L$), mass ($M$), and time ($T$). Derived dimensional quantities combine these fundamental dimensions to describe velocity, acceleration, force, pressure, and energy.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Principles of Dimensional Equivalencies</summary><div class="details-content">

Dimensional consistency ensures accurate calculation of hemodynamic and respiratory variables in the ICU. The conversion of conventional units into SI units facilitates universal physiological monitoring and drug dosing across clinical platforms.

## Converting Units of Solute Concentration</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Electrolytes and Charged Ions</summary><div class="details-content">

For ions that exist freely in an aqueous solution, concentration is conventionally expressed as milliequivalents per liter ($\text{mEq/L}$).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Conversion Between Milliequivalents and Millimoles</summary><div class="details-content">

To convert milliequivalents per liter to millimoles per liter ($\text{mmol/L}$):
$$\frac{\text{mEq/L}}{\text{valence}} = \text{mmol/L}$$

- **Univalent Ions:** For a univalent ion such as potassium ($\text{K}^+$) or sodium ($\text{Na}^+$), the concentration in $\text{mmol/L}$ is identical to the concentration in $\text{mEq/L}$.
- **Divalent Ions:** For a divalent ion such as magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) or calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$), the concentration in $\text{mmol/L}$ is one-half the concentration in $\text{mEq/L}$.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Complexed Ions and Mass Concentration Conversion</summary><div class="details-content">

For ions that are partially bound or complexed to other plasma proteins or organic anions (e.g., total plasma calcium), concentration is usually expressed as milligrams per deciliter ($\text{mg/dL}$).

To convert $\text{mg/dL}$ to $\text{mEq/L}$:
$$\frac{\text{mg/dL} \times 10}{\text{mol wt}} \times \text{valence} = \text{mEq/L}$$
where $\text{mol wt}$ represents the molecular weight of the substance, and the multiplier 10 converts deciliters ($100\text{ mL}$) into liters.

*Clinical Example:* Total serum calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) has a molecular weight of 40 and a valence of 2. A plasma calcium concentration of $8\text{ mg/dL}$ is equivalent to:
$$(8 \times 10 / 40) \times 2 = 4\text{ mEq/L} = 2\text{ mmol/L}$$

</div></details>
</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Uncharged Solutes and Osmotic Activity</summary><div class="details-content">

Concentrations of uncharged organic molecules (such as glucose, urea, and ethanol) are also measured in milligrams per deciliter ($\text{mg/dL}$).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Mass to Molar Conversion for Nonelectrolytes</summary><div class="details-content">

To convert mass concentration of uncharged molecules into molarity ($\text{mmol/L}$):
$$\frac{\text{mg/dL} \times 10}{\text{mol wt}} = \text{mmol/L}$$

*Clinical Example:* Glucose has a molecular weight of 180. A plasma glucose concentration of $90\text{ mg/dL}$ is equivalent to:
$$(90 \times 10 / 180) = 5\text{ mmol/L}$$

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Osmotic Activity and Colligative Osmolality Formulas</summary><div class="details-content">

The concentration of solutes can also be expressed in terms of osmotic pressure, which determines the transcellular and transcapillary distribution of water across fluid compartments. Osmotic activity in aqueous biological solutions (*osmolality*) is expressed as milliosmoles per kilogram of water ($\text{mosm/kg H}_2\text{O}$ or $\text{mosm/kg}$).

The following formulas calculate the osmolality of solute concentrations, where $n$ represents the number of dissociable particles generated per molecule in solution:
$$\text{mmol/L} \times n = \text{mosm/kg}$$
$$\frac{\text{mEq/L}}{\text{valence}} \times n = \text{mosm/kg}$$
$$\frac{\text{mg/dL} \times 10}{\text{mol wt}} \times n = \text{mosm/kg}$$

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

##### Colligative Comparisons: Plasma Sodium Versus Glucose</summary><div class="details-content">

*Comparative Examples:*
- **Plasma Sodium:** A plasma $\text{Na}^+$ concentration of $140\text{ mEq/L}$ ($n = 1$) provides:
  $$\frac{140}{1} \times 1 = 140\text{ mosm/kg}$$
- **Plasma Glucose:** A plasma glucose concentration of $90\text{ mg/dL}$ ($n = 1$) provides:
  $$\frac{90 \times 10}{180} \times 1 = 5\text{ mosm/kg}$$

Sodium in plasma exerts a far greater colligative osmotic effect than glucose because osmotic pressure is determined entirely by the *number of particles* in solution, completely independent of individual particle mass or physical dimensions (i.e., one univalent sodium ion exerts the identical osmotic activity as one large glucose molecule).

</div></details>

## Clinical Sizing and Measurement Systems</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Catheter and Cannula Dimensions: French and Gauge Scales</summary><div class="details-content">

Medical devices for vascular access, urinary catheterization, chest drainage, and endotracheal intubation are sized according to historical French and Birmingham Gauge standards.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Principles of Device Measurement</summary><div class="details-content">

Vascular catheters and chest tubes are calibrated on the French scale, where sizing corresponds directly to outside circumference. Hypodermic needles and peripheral venous cannulas are classified by Gauge size, where larger numbers designate smaller luminal diameters.
