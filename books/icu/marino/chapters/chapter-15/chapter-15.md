# Hemorrhagic Shock

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute hemorrhagic shock represents the most treatable yet time-critical form of circulatory failure in trauma and surgical intensive care:
* **Physiology of Acute Blood Loss:** Total blood volume averages 66 mL/kg in adult males and 60 mL/kg in females. The circulatory tree operates with a narrow volume reserve; loss of 30% of circulating volume produces decompensated shock, whereas parenchymal organs can tolerate loss of up to 75% of functional mass.
* **The Diagnostic Fallacy of Hemoglobin and Hematocrit:** Acute hemorrhage entails whole blood loss, depleting plasma and erythrocytes proportionately. As a consequence, initial hemoglobin concentration and hematocrit remain entirely normal. Transcapillary refill requires 8 to 12 hours (and up to several days) to establish dilutional anemia; thus, early hematocrit values reflect the volume and nature of infused resuscitation fluids rather than the magnitude of hemorrhage.
* **Vascular Access Dynamics:** Governed by the Hagen-Poiseuille equation ($Q \propto r^4 / L$), short large-bore peripheral catheters (16-gauge or 14-gauge, 1.2 inches) deliver gravity flow five times greater than an 8-inch central venous catheter of identical gauge. Rapid infusion catheters (7 French or 9 French, 2 inches) deliver up to 1 liter per minute when pressurized to 300 mm Hg.
* **Damage Control and Hemostatic Resuscitation:** Modern protocols abandon unguided crystalloid loading in favor of permissive hypotension (target systolic BP 80–90 mm Hg, MAP 50 mm Hg until surgical source control is secured) and immediate balanced 1:1:1 transfusion (1 unit packed RBCs to 1 unit FFP to 1 platelet pack per 5 units of blood).
* **Viscoelastic Hemostatic Guidance:** Thromboelastography (TEG) guides precision reversal of trauma-induced coagulopathy (TIC): prolonged reaction time ($R > 9\text{ min}$) triggers plasma transfusion; decreased clot formation angle ($\alpha < 65^\circ$) triggers cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate; reduced maximum amplitude ($MA < 55\text{ mm}$) triggers platelets; and accelerated lysis ($LY30 > 3\%$) mandates tranexamic acid.

## Pathophysiology and Classification of Acute Blood Loss</summary><div class="details-content">

Trauma represents the leading cause of death globally in individuals aged 25 to 49 years, with hemorrhagic shock being the single most treatable etiology. The human cardiovascular system functions with a volume-sensitive stroke pump and a tightly constrained circulating blood volume:
* **Estimated Normal Blood Volume:** 
  * Adult males: approximately $66\text{ mL/kg}$ lean body weight (yielding $\approx 5\text{ liters}$ in a 75 kg male).
  * Adult females: approximately $60\text{ mL/kg}$ lean body weight (yielding $\approx 3.6\text{ liters}$ in a 60 kg female).
* **Volume Intolerance:** Whereas organs such as the kidneys, liver, and lungs can survive the loss of up to 75% of their functional tissue, acute uncorrected loss of as little as 30% of total blood volume can prove rapidly fatal.

### Compensatory Physiological Mechanisms
Acute hemorrhage triggers immediate homeostatic neurohumoral responses designed to defend vital perfusion:
1. **Transcapillary Refill:** Fall in systemic capillary hydrostatic pressure permits interstitial fluid to enter the vascular compartment along oncotic gradients, restoring up to 1 liter of plasma volume at the expense of an interstitial deficit.
2. **Sympathetic Adrenergic Activation:** Baroreceptor unloading stimulates generalized arteriolar vasoconstriction and intense tachycardia to sustain systemic driving pressure.
3. **Vasopressin Secretion:** Osmotic and baroreceptor stimuli trigger hypothalamic release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH) from the posterior pituitary, promoting renal free-water retention.
4. **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone Activation:** Renal hypoperfusion triggers renin release, generating angiotensin II (inducing potent systemic vasoconstriction) and aldosterone (promoting renal sodium retention).

### The Diagnostic Fallacy of Hemoglobin and Hematocrit
The common reliance on serial hemoglobin and hematocrit (H & H) measurements to evaluate acute hemorrhage is physiologically flawed:
* **Proportional Cellular Loss:** Acute hemorrhage involves loss of whole blood, shedding erythrocytes and plasma in exact physiological proportions. Consequently, acute blood volume drops precipitously while hemoglobin concentration and hematocrit remain unchanged.
* **Delayed Dilution:** Dilutional anemia requires transcapillary refill and neurohumoral fluid retention, processes taking 8 to 12 hours to manifest and several days to fully establish.
* **Refection of Infused Fluids:** Early post-injury hematocrit values reflect the resuscitation effort (e.g., hemodilution from large crystalloid infusions versus isovolemic whole blood resuscitation) rather than the actual severity of hemorrhage.

## Resuscitation Access and Intravenous Fluids</summary><div class="details-content">

Rapid volume replacement demands optimization of vascular conduits based on hydraulic physics.

### Catheter Fluid Dynamics
Flow through intravenous conduits is governed by the Hagen-Poiseuille equation:

$$Q = \Delta P \times \left(\frac{\pi \, r^4}{8 \, \mu L}\right) \tag{15.1}$$

Where flow ($Q$) is directly proportional to the driving pressure gradient ($\Delta P = P_{\text{in}} - P_{\text{out}}$) and the fourth power of internal catheter radius ($r^4$), and inversely proportional to conduit length ($L$) and fluid viscosity ($\mu$):
* **Length vs Radius:** A short, large-bore peripheral catheter (e.g., 1.2-inch, 16-gauge or 14-gauge) delivers gravity flow approximately 5 times greater than an 8-inch central venous catheter of identical internal gauge.
* **Gauge Disparity:** Increasing catheter diameter from 18-gauge to 16-gauge doubles gravity flow capacity.

![Influence of catheter dimensions on gravity-driven flow](images/_page_4_Figure_1.jpeg)

*Figure 15.2. Influence of catheter length and internal diameter on the gravity-driven infusion of water. Short peripheral catheters vastly outperform long central lines.*

### Specialized Infusion Catheters
* **Rapid Infusion Catheters (RIC):** Very large bore peripheral catheters (7 French and 9 French, equivalent to 13-gauge and 11-gauge respectively, length $\approx 2\text{ inches} / 5\text{ cm}$). When connected to pressurized infusion systems, they deliver warmed fluid at rates up to 1 liter per minute.
* **Introducer Sheaths:** Large-bore central introducers (8.5 French and 9 French, length $10\text{ cm} / 4\text{ inches}$) can deliver up to 1 liter per minute under pressure. The integrated side infusion port possesses only 25% of the flow capacity of the main lumen and must be bypassed during rapid transfusion.

### Comparative Fluid Performance
* **Colloids vs Crystalloids:** Colloids expand plasma volume with four-fold greater efficiency than crystalloids. As shown in hemodynamic trials, infusion of 500 mL of 10% dextran-40 produces a significantly greater augmentation of cardiac index ($CI$) than 1 liter of lactated Ringer's solution.
* **Packed Red Blood Cells:** With a hematocrit of approximately 60% and unit volume of 350 mL, PRBCs exhibit high viscosity and do not promote cardiac output; their sole indication is restoring oxygen-carrying capacity.

### Whole Blood and Component Nuances
* **Cold-Stored Whole Blood:** Stored at 1°–6° C (34°–43° F) for up to 35 days, whole blood provides physiological reconstitution of oxygen transport and coagulation. While survival advantages were demonstrated in military casualties, civilian trials show benefit primarily when combined with component therapy during massive hemorrhage.
* **Low-Titer Group O Blood:** Standard group O uncrossmatched blood carries risks of anti-A and anti-B isohemagglutinin-mediated hemolysis. Low-titer group O whole blood minimizes these titers and represents a safer universal emergency product.
* **Cryoprecipitate vs Fibrinogen Concentrate:** Cryoprecipitate (volume 10–15 mL/unit) replenishes fibrinogen, factor VIII, and von Willebrand factor, but carries variable factor concentrations and requires thawing. Fibrinogen concentrates deliver standardized dosing (20 g/L), require no thawing, and undergo viral inactivation.

### Rapid Pressurized Infusion Systems
In life-threatening exsanguination requiring blood delivery rates up to $5\text{ L/hr}$ ($83\text{ mL/min}$), gravity flow is completely inadequate:
* Rapid infusion systems utilize roller pumps generating delivery pressures up to 300 mm Hg alongside high-efficiency heat exchangers delivering infusates at 37° C.
* Infusion rates through various conduits demonstrate the profound synergistic effect of catheter diameter, dilution, and external pressure.

## Resuscitation Strategies and Coagulopathy Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Resuscitation in acute blood loss requires two concurrent priorities: supporting tissue oxygen delivery and achieving surgical or angiographic source control.

### Management of Class II Hemorrhage Without Shock
Patients with moderate blood loss exhibiting mild tachycardia or oliguria without metabolic acidosis (normal plasma lactate) are resuscitated with asanguinous fluids.

<span id="tab-15-4"></span>

### Table 15.4. Asanguinous Resuscitation Volumes for Class II Hemorrhage

| Resuscitation Step | Method of Calculation |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1. Estimate Normal Plasma Volume (PV)** | $\text{PV} = 40\text{ mL/kg}$ (males)<br>$\text{PV} = 36\text{ mL/kg}$ (females) |
| **2. Estimate % Loss of Plasma Volume** | Class II Hemorrhage: $15\text{--}30\%$ volume loss |
| **3. Calculate Plasma Volume Deficit (PVD)** | $\text{PVD} = \text{PV} \times \%\text{ Loss}$ |
| **4. Estimate Infusion Resuscitation Volume (RV)** | $\text{RV} = \text{PVD} \times 1$ (for colloid solutions)<br>$\text{RV} = \text{PVD} \times 3$ (for balanced crystalloids) |

*Threshold Triggers:* Following plasma repletion, packed RBCs are indicated to sustain hemoglobin at $7\text{--}8\text{ g/dL}$, FFP is indicated if $\text{INR} > 1.5$, and platelets are transfused if counts drop below $50,000/\mu\text{L}$.

### Damage Control Resuscitation for Hemorrhagic Shock
Severe hemorrhage (defined by infusion needs $\ge 4\text{ units}$ RBCs within 1 hour or Class III/IV loss) necessitates damage control resuscitation principles:

#### 1. Permissive Hypotension
Aggressive fluid bolusing before definitive surgical control dislodges nascent hemostatic plugs and dilutes clotting factors ("pop the clot"):
* Target blood pressure is restricted to a systolic pressure of $80\text{--}90\text{ mm Hg}$ or a mean arterial pressure of $50\text{ mm Hg}$.
* Permissive hypotension is maintained only until surgical source control is established, provided tissue perfusion remains viable (monitored by plasma lactate $< 2\text{ mmol/L}$ and urine output).

#### 2. Trauma-Induced Coagulopathy (TIC)
Coagulopathy in bleeding patients was historically attributed to the "lethal triad" of hypothermia, acidosis ($\text{pH} < 7.2$), and iatrogenic crystalloid hemodilution:
* **Endogenous TIC:** Up to 40% of severely injured trauma patients manifest an acute endogenous coagulopathy prior to fluid resuscitation, associated with a 6-fold increase in mortality.
* **Mechanism:** Extensive tissue hypoperfusion and endothelial injury release soluble thrombomodulin, triggering massive activation of protein C. Activated protein C proteolytically inactivates factors V and VIII while exhausting plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), precipitating uncontrolled systemic hyperfibrinolysis.

<span id="fig-15-4"></span>

![Principal components of hemostatic resuscitation](images/_page_10_Figure_0.jpeg)

*Figure 15.4. Conceptual framework of hemostatic damage control resuscitation: 1:1:1 component replacement, adjunctive metabolic support, and targeted viscoelastic monitoring.*

#### 3. Hemostatic Resuscitation (1:1:1 Strategy)
To reconstruct whole blood equivalents and avoid dilutional coagulopathy:
* **Component Ratio:** Transfuse 1 unit of FFP for every 1 unit of packed RBCs, and 1 platelet pack (containing pooled platelets from 5 to 6 donors or 1 apheresis unit) for every 5 units of PRBCs and FFP.
* **Adjunctive Pharmacotherapy:**
  * *Calcium Chloride:* Maintain ionized calcium at $1.2\text{--}1.3\text{ mmol/L}$ ($4.6\text{--}6.0\text{ mg/dL}$) to counteract citrate toxicity and sustain clotting factor enzymatic function.
  * *Tranexamic Acid (TXA):* Administer an empiric 1-gram IV bolus over 10 minutes within 3 hours of injury, followed by a 1-gram continuous infusion over 8 hours to inhibit hyperfibrinolysis.
  * *Cryoprecipitate:* Infuse 2 adult pools (10–20 units) if plasma fibrinogen remains $< 100\text{ mg/dL}$.
* **Conventional Laboratory Endpoints:** Target fibrinogen $> 100\text{ mg/dL}$, $\text{INR} < 1.5$, $\text{aPTT} < 1.5 \times\text{ normal}$, and platelet count $\ge 100,000/\mu\text{L}$.

## Viscoelastic Hemostatic Testing (Thromboelastography)</summary><div class="details-content">

Conventional coagulation tests (PT/INR, aPTT) are performed on isolated platelet-poor plasma at 37° C, reflecting only initial thrombin burst while failing to assess platelet-fibrin interactions, clot stability, or hyperfibrinolysis. Viscoelastic assays evaluate the dynamic physical properties of whole blood clotting from initiation through fibrinolysis.

### Thromboelastography (TEG) Mechanics
A sample of whole blood is placed in an oscillating cylindrical cup with a stationary suspended torsion pin:
* Clot formation binds the rotating cup to the pin, transmitting mechanical torque.
* The amplitude of pin deflection is recorded over time, producing a characteristic symmetrical tracing.

<span id="fig-15-5"></span>

![Thromboelastography tracing with identified components](images/_page_11_Figure_7.jpeg)

*Figure 15.5. Standard thromboelastography (TEG) tracing demonstrating reaction time (R), clot kinetics (k), alpha angle ($\alpha$), maximum amplitude (MA), and clot lysis at 30 minutes (LY30).*

### TEG Parameters and Diagnostic Significance
1. **Reaction Time ($R$):** Time from test initiation until the curve opens to 2 mm amplitude (reflects enzymatic clotting factor initiation). Normal range: $5\text{--}10\text{ minutes}$.
2. **Kinetic Time ($k$):** Time from end of $R$ until amplitude reaches 20 mm (reflects speed of clot amplification). Normal range: $1\text{--}3\text{ minutes}$.
3. **Alpha Angle ($\alpha$):** Slope of tangent line tracing clot growth rate (reflects fibrinogen cross-linking and polymerization). Normal range: $53^\circ\text{--}72^\circ$.
4. **Maximum Amplitude ($MA$):** Peak width of tracing (reflects ultimate clot firmness, determined 80% by platelet count/function and 20% by fibrinogen). Normal range: $50\text{--}70\text{ mm}$.
5. **Clot Lysis at 30 Minutes ($LY30$):** Percentage reduction in amplitude 30 minutes after reaching MA (reflects fibrinolytic activity). Normal range: $2.3\text{--}5.7\%$.

## Postresuscitation Injury and Resuscitation Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

Apparent hemodynamic stabilization following severe hemorrhagic shock can be succeeded at 48 to 72 hours by secondary multiorgan failure (MODS).

### Pathophysiology of Reperfusion Injury
Postresuscitation injury represents systemic reperfusion injury rooted in splanchnic ischemia:
* **Gut-Derived Cytokine Cascade:** Splanchnic hypoperfusion breaks down mucosal tight junctions. Subsequent reperfusion flushes inflammatory mediators and endotoxins into mesenteric lymphatics and the portal circulation, provoking acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), acute kidney injury, hepatic failure, and encephalopathy. Overall mortality in postresuscitation MODS averages $50\text{--}60\%$.
* **Aggravating Factors:** Prolonged duration of shock, excessive unbuffered crystalloid infusion (driving bowel edema and abdominal compartment syndrome), and massive erythrocyte transfusion ($> 6\text{ units}$ in 12 hours). When multiorgan failure develops after 72 hours (after 3 days), occult sepsis of enteric origin must be presumed.

### Five Essential Principles of Resuscitation
Comprehensive management of traumatic and acute hemorrhagic shock is encapsulated in five cardinal tenets:
1. **Optimize Conduits and Thermal Balance:** Utilize short, large-bore peripheral catheters (16-gauge or 14-gauge) or rapid infusion catheters (7 French), warming all infusates to 37° C via pressurized rapid systems to prevent hypothermic coagulopathy.
2. **Restrict Crystalloids:** Limit crystalloid administration to 1 to 2 liters prior to initiating blood components.
3. **Execute Balanced Hemostatic Resuscitation:** Transfuse packed RBCs, plasma, and platelets in a 1:1:1 physiological ratio, alongside ionized calcium maintenance ($\ge 1.2\text{ mmol/L}$) and early tranexamic acid.
4. **Practice Permissive Hypotension:** Maintain a systolic blood pressure of $80\text{--}90\text{ mm Hg}$ or mean arterial pressure of $50\text{ mm Hg}$ until surgical source control is secured, verifying adequacy of tissue perfusion via plasma lactate ($< 2\text{ mmol/L}$) and urine output ($> 0.5\text{ mL/kg/hr}$).
5. **Utilize Viscoelastic Monitoring:** Guide blood component replacement dynamically via thromboelastography (TEG) or rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM).
