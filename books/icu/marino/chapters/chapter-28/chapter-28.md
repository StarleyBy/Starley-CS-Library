# The Ventilator-Dependent Patient

> *Eyes and ears are bad witnesses to men if they have souls that understand not their language.*
> 
> — Heraclitus

The day-to-day care of ventilator-dependent patients involves vigilance, proactive management, and anticipation of mechanical and physiological complications. The primary areas of clinical focus encompass the management of artificial airways (endotracheal and tracheostomy tubes), the clearance and rheology of respiratory secretions, and the recognition of mechanical complications inherent to positive-pressure ventilation: namely, pulmonary barotrauma and occult (intrinsic) PEEP. Infectious complications, including ventilator-associated pneumonia, are addressed in the subsequent chapter.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Endotracheal Tube Placement:** The distal tip of the endotracheal (ET) tube must rest 3–5 cm above the tracheal carina (at the T4–T5 vertebral interspace), corresponding to approximately 21 cm at the teeth in women and 23 cm in men. Neck flexion or extension displaces the tube tip by up to 2 cm.
- **Tracheostomy:** Indicated when mechanical ventilation is anticipated to exceed 2 weeks. Bedside percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy is the preferred procedure. If accidental decannulation occurs within the first week (before tract maturation), immediately reintubate orally; do not attempt blind recannulation.
- **Cuff Pressure Limits:** Maintain cuff inflation pressure $\leq 25\text{ mm Hg}$ ($\leq 34\text{ cm H}_2\text{O}$) to prevent tracheal mucosal ischemia and late post-intubation tracheal stenosis.
- **Airway Care and Suctioning:** Routine scheduled suctioning is contraindicated because catheters dislodge luminal bacterial biofilms into the distal lungs. Suction only when clinically indicated: visible secretions, coarse rhonchi on auscultation, or a sawtooth pattern on ventilator flow scalars.
- **Saline Instillation Prohibited:** Normal saline does not dissolve hydrophobic mucoprotein disulfide cross-links and should never be instilled into the airway. Instilling 5 mL of saline can dislodge over 300,000 viable bacterial colonies from tube biofilms into the alveoli.
- **Mucolytic Therapy:** N-acetylcysteine (NAC) cleaves mucoprotein disulfide bonds. Use direct tracheal instillation of 20% NAC (1 mL mixed with 1 mL saline) to treat tenacious plugs; avoid aerosolized NAC in asthmatics due to bronchospasm.
- **Barotrauma and Pneumothorax:** In the supine position, pleural air collects anteriorly and subpulmonically, frequently escaping detection on portable chest radiography. Lung ultrasound is highly sensitive: absence of lung sliding suggests pneumothorax, and the "lung point" is pathognomonic.
- **Tension Pneumothorax Emergency:** Presents as acute hemodynamic collapse, obstructive shock, absent unilateral breath sounds, and contralateral tracheal shift. Decompress immediately with a 14-gauge, 8 cm angiocatheter in the 5th intercostal space anterior to the midaxillary line, or via finger thoracostomy.
- **Chest Tube Drainage:** Three-chamber drainage systems use a 2 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ water seal and –20 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ suction. Active pleural suction should be avoided in persistent bronchopleural fistulas because negative intrapleural pressure perpetuates air leaks.
- **Occult (Auto) PEEP:** Results from dynamic hyperinflation and incomplete exhalation in airflow obstruction or tachypnea. Quantified via an end-expiratory occlusion hold in passively ventilated patients. Managed by prolonging expiratory time (increasing inspiratory flow rate, reducing tidal volume, or lowering respiratory rate).

## Artificial Airways</summary><div class="details-content">

Positive-pressure ventilation requires an artificial conduit inserted into the trachea: either through the glottis via the oral or nasal route (endotracheal tubes) or directly into the cervical trachea (tracheostomy tubes).

### Endotracheal Tubes

Endotracheal (ET) tubes range from 25 to 35 cm in total length and are designated by their internal diameter (ID) in millimeters. Sizing is traditionally correlated with patient height: size 7.0 mm ID for height $\leq 5\text{ feet, 2 inches}$ (157 cm), size 7.5 mm ID for height $5\text{ feet, 3 inches}$ to $5\text{ feet, 10 inches}$ (160–178 cm), and size 8.0 mm ID for height $\geq 5\text{ feet, 11 inches}$ (180 cm). Clinical studies demonstrate that ET tube size does not influence patient outcomes.

#### Tube Position and Verification

Following intubation, tube position must be verified by radiography (Figure 28.1). With the head in a neutral position, the tip of the ET tube should reside 3–5 cm above the carina (or midway between the thoracic inlet and carina), aligning with the T4–T5 vertebral interspace.

![Figure 28.1](images/_page_1_Picture_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.1** Portable chest radiograph showing appropriate position of an endotracheal tube, with the tip located midway between the thoracic inlet and the tracheal bifurcation (carina).

Neck flexion shifts the tube tip up to 2 cm toward the carina, whereas neck extension displaces it up to 2 cm cephalad toward the vocal cords. The depth of insertion can be estimated at the bedside: with the head fully extended, the distance from the upper incisors to the sternal angle of Louis (which shares a horizontal plane with the carina) reflects the correct tube depth.

#### Tube Migration and Malposition

Endotracheal tubes can migrate during nursing care, patient repositioning, or circuit manipulation. Distal migration typically directs the tube into the right mainstem bronchus due to its straighter anatomical angle, producing complete atelectasis of the unventilated left lung and severe hypoxemia (Figure 28.2).

![Figure 28.2](images/_page_2_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.2** Portable chest radiograph showing right mainstem bronchus intubation resulting in complete collapse (atelectasis) of the left lung.

To prevent distal migration, secure the tube so the depth marking at the incisors does not exceed 21 cm in women or 23 cm in men.

#### Laryngeal Injury

Laryngeal injury occurs in over 50% of ICU patients intubated for $> 12\text{ hours}$. Complications include vocal cord ulceration, subglottic granulomas, and laryngeal edema. Risk factors include oversized tubes, difficult intubation, and prolonged translaryngeal intubation.

#### Subglottic Secretion Drainage

Secretions pooling above the cuff constantly microaspirate into the distal airway. Specialized endotracheal tubes equipped with a dedicated subglottic evacuation lumen allow continuous or intermittent aspiration (–20 $\text{mm Hg}$), significantly reducing the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia.

### Tracheostomy

Tracheostomy is preferred when mechanical ventilation is expected to continue for $> 2\text{ weeks}$. Advantages over translaryngeal intubation include enhanced patient comfort, reduced sedation requirements, lower airway resistance, and improved clearance of bronchopulmonary secretions.

#### Timing of Tracheostomy

Trials comparing early tracheostomy (within 7 days) to late tracheostomy ($\geq 14\text{ days}$) demonstrate that early placement reduces ventilator days and ICU length of stay, but does not alter overall mortality or the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia. A standard clinical strategy is to consider tracheostomy after 7 days of mechanical ventilation when liberation within the subsequent week is deemed unlikely.

#### Surgical vs. Percutaneous Dilatational Tracheostomy

Bedside percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT), utilizing a Seldinger wire technique with progressive dilation under bronchoscopic guidance, has replaced open surgical tracheostomy as the gold standard. PDT avoids the operating room, eliminates general anesthesia costs, and produces lower wound infection rates ($< 2\%$). Emergency access when endotracheal intubation fails is achieved via cricothyroidotomy through the cricothyroid membrane; this must be converted to a formal tracheostomy once the patient stabilizes to prevent late subglottic stenosis.

#### Complications of Tracheostomy

- **Accidental Decannulation:** If a tracheostomy tube dislodges within the first 7 days (prior to tract epithelialization and maturation), blind attempts at recannulation can create false pretracheal passages. The patient must be immediately re-intubated from above with an oral endotracheal tube before attempting to re-establish the stoma.
- **Tracheal Stenosis:** Late circumferential cicatricial stenosis occurs in up to 15% of patients at the stoma site within 6 months post-decannulation. Most cases remain subclinical unless tracheal diameter narrows by $> 50\%$.

### Cuff Management

Tracheal tube cuffs provide an airtight seal during positive-pressure inspiration (Figure 28.3). Cuffs are inflated via an external pilot balloon equipped with a one-way spring valve.

![Figure 28.3](images/_page_5_Picture_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.3** Tracheostomy tube with an inflated low-pressure, high-volume distal cuff.

Tracheal capillary perfusion pressure ranges from 25 to 30 mm Hg. Cuff pressures exceeding 25 mm Hg (34 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) compress mucosal capillaries, inducing ischemic mucosal ulceration and chondritis. Cuff pressure should be measured regularly with an aneroid manometer and kept between 20 and 25 mm Hg (27–34 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$).

#### Cuff Leaks and Troubleshooting

A cuff leak manifests as an audible gas hissing around the larynx during inspiration and a disparity between inspired and exhaled tidal volumes on the ventilator. When a cuff leak appears, manually ventilate the patient and inspect the pilot balloon:

1. **Inflated Pilot Balloon:** The cuff remains inflated, indicating malposition or eccentric cuff seal against the tracheal wall. Deflate the cuff, reposition the tube slightly, and re-inflate while monitoring pressure ($\leq 25\text{ mm Hg}$). If the leak persists, reintubation with a larger tube is indicated.
2. **Deflated Pilot Balloon:** Indicates a structural tear in the cuff or a competent valve failure. Attempt inflation; if air cannot be retained, the tube must be promptly exchanged.

## Airway Care and Secretion Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Clearing airway secretions in ventilator-dependent patients requires meticulous technique to avoid iatrogenic harm.

### Endotracheal Suctioning

Aspiration of airway secretions can induce acute hypoxia, cardiac arrhythmias, elevated intracranial pressure, and bronchospasm. More importantly, endotracheal tubes become coated with bacterial biofilms (Figure 28.4).

![Figure 28.4](images/_page_7_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.4** Scanning electron micrograph displaying a dense bacterial biofilm lining the internal luminal surface of an endotracheal tube.

Passing a suction catheter through the tube shears off fragments of this infected matrix, directly inoculating pathogenic bacteria into distal lung units. Consequently, **routine scheduled suctioning is strongly contraindicated**.

#### Clinical Indications for Suctioning

Suctioning should be performed strictly on an as-needed basis when objective evidence of secretion accumulation is present:
- Visible secretions within the endotracheal tube or circuit tubing.
- Coarse, rattling rhonchi heard on chest auscultation.
- A distinctive jagged, sawtooth appearance on the ventilator inspiratory flow-time scalar.
- Sudden, unexplained deterioration in arterial oxygenation or sudden rise in peak airway pressure.

### Saline Instillation Prohibited

The routine instillation of normal saline boluses prior to suctioning is an obsolete and dangerous practice. Respiratory secretions consist of a superficial hydrophobic gel layer cross-linked by mucoprotein disulfide bonds. Normal saline cannot dissolve or dilute this hydrophobic meshwork: adding saline to mucus is analogous to pouring water onto grease.

Furthermore, laboratory studies demonstrate that instilling 5 mL of saline shears and dislodges up to 300,000 viable bacterial colonies from luminal biofilms, driving them directly into the alveoli and multiplying the risk of ventilator-associated pneumonia.

### Mucolytic Therapy

When secretions become exceptionally thick, dehydrated, and obstructive, pharmacological disruption of mucoprotein bonds can be achieved with N-acetylcysteine (NAC). NAC contains free sulfhydryl groups that chemically cleave the disulfide cross-links of mucoproteins, liquefying viscous secretions. Regimens are detailed in Table 28.1.

### TABLE 28.1 Mucolytic Therapy with N-Acetylcysteine (NAC)

| Delivery Route | Protocol and Regimen | Clinical Considerations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Direct Tracheal Instillation** | Mix 1 mL of 20% NAC with 1 mL sterile saline (2 mL total); inject directly into trachea every 8 hours as needed. | Preferred route in intubated patients; limit duration to $\leq 48\text{ hours}$ to prevent secondary bronchorrhea. |
| **Aerosol Nebulization** | Mix 2.5 mL of 10% NAC with 2.5 mL saline (5 mL total) in a small-volume nebulizer every 8 hours. | Can provoke severe bronchospasm and coughing; contraindicated in asthmatics. |

## Pulmonary Barotrauma</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary barotrauma describes the extra-alveolar escape of gas resulting from positive-pressure alveolar rupture. While volutrauma reflects tissue strain (alveolar deformation), barotrauma reflects transpulmonary stress (transmural distending pressure).

### Clinical Manifestations of Barotrauma

Ruptured gas tracks along perivascular bronchovascular sheaths into the mediastinum (**pneumomediastinum**), dissects into the soft tissues of the neck and chest wall (**subcutaneous emphysema**), tracks into the retroperitoneum (**pneumoperitoneum**), or tears through the visceral pleura into the pleural space (**pneumothorax**).

### Pneumothorax in the ICU

The incidence of pneumothorax is 3–10% in patients receiving lung-protective mechanical ventilation, rising to 35% in necrotizing viral pneumonias (e.g., severe COVID-19 ARDS).

#### Radiographic Detection in Supine Patients

In the supine patient, free pleural gas does not collect at the apex of the lung. Instead, gas rises to the most nondependent anterior and subpulmonic pleural spaces (Figure 28.5). Portable supine chest radiographs often fail to demonstrate a classic apical pleural line, revealing only a deepened costophrenic sulcus (the *deep sulcus sign*).

![Figure 28.5](images/_page_10_Picture_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.5** Portable chest radiograph and thoracic CT scan in a supine trauma patient. The anterior pneumothorax (asterisk) is clearly visualized on CT but is invisible on the portable supine chest x-ray.

#### Bedside Lung Ultrasound

Lung ultrasound is superior to supine chest radiography for rapid pneumothorax detection. A high-frequency linear probe is placed in the 3rd to 4th intercostal space at the midclavicular line (Figure 28.6).

![Figure 28.6](images/_page_11_Picture_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.6** Ultrasound appearance of the bright hyperechoic pleural line. Normal lung sliding eliminates pneumothorax, while the lung point is pathognomonic.

- **Lung Sliding:** Normal visceral and parietal pleura glide against each other during breathing, creating a shimmering, dynamic sliding sign. The presence of lung sliding 100% excludes a pneumothorax at that probe site.
- **Lung Point:** The physical junction where collapsed lung intermittently contacts the chest wall during inspiration, marking the boundary of the air collection. The lung point is 100% pathognomonic for pneumothorax.

### Tension Pneumothorax

When positive pressure drives air into the pleural space through a one-way flap tear, intrapleural pressure rapidly exceeds atmospheric pressure. The affected lung collapses completely, compressing the mediastinum, shifting the trachea contralaterally, flattening the ipsilateral hemidiaphragm, and kinking the vena cava (Figure 28.7).

![Figure 28.7](images/_page_12_Picture_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.7** Tension pneumothorax with complete collapse of the left lung, marked contralateral mediastinal shift to the right, and depression of the left hemidiaphragm.

The resulting obstruction to venous return precipitates severe obstructive shock, marked hypotension, and pulseless electrical activity (PEA) cardiac arrest.

#### Emergency Decompression

Tension pneumothorax requires immediate clinical decompression prior to obtaining any radiograph:
1. **Needle Thoracostomy:** Insert a 14-gauge, 8 cm (3.25 inch) angiocatheter into the 5th intercostal space just anterior to the midaxillary line. (Catheters shorter than 8 cm or placed in the 2nd intercostal space at the midclavicular line fail to penetrate the pleural space in up to 40% of adult patients).
2. **Finger Thoracostomy:** If needle decompression fails, perform immediate scalpel incision and blunt finger dissection through the intercostal space into the pleural space to release trapped gas.
3. **Tube Thoracostomy:** Follow decompression by inserting an 8.5 to 14 French small-bore chest tube directed apically and anteriorly.

### Pleural Evacuation Systems

Evacuation of pleural gas and fluid is managed using a closed three-compartment chest drainage unit (Figure 28.8).

![Figure 28.8](images/_page_13_Figure_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.8** Standard three-compartment closed pleural drainage unit.

- **Collection Chamber:** Collects fluid from the pleural space without exerting back-pressure on the lung.
- **Water Seal Chamber:** Contains a 2 cm column of water acting as a one-way valve, allowing air to escape while preventing atmospheric air from being drawn into the pleural cavity. Continuous bubbling during inspiration indicates an ongoing bronchopleural fistula.
- **Suction Control Chamber:** Regulates wall suction, typically set to –20 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$.

#### Avoid Routine Active Suction

Applying continuous wall suction generates negative intrapleural pressure that widens the transpulmonary pressure gradient, actively pulling air through parenchymal tears and impeding the closure of bronchopleural fistulas. In patients with persistent air leaks, wall suction should be turned off, placing the chest drain on simple water seal.

## Occult (Intrinsic) PEEP</summary><div class="details-content">

Occult PEEP (also termed intrinsic PEEP or auto-PEEP) develops when dynamic airway collapse or inadequate expiratory time prevents complete alveolar emptying prior to the subsequent breath.

### Adverse Effects of Occult PEEP

Occult PEEP exerts severe systemic and pulmonary effects:
1. **Circulatory Collapse:** Elevated mean intrathoracic pressure compresses great veins, impedes venous return, and precipitates acute hypotension.
2. **Increased Work of Breathing:** Patients must generate negative inspiratory pressure equal to the auto-PEEP level merely to trigger a breath, causing diaphragmatic exhaustion.
3. **Alveolar Overdistension:** Elevates end-inspiratory alveolar volumes and pressures, magnifying the risk of barotrauma and volutrauma.
4. **Spurious Hemodynamic Values:** Transmitted alveolar pressure falsely elevates central venous pressure (CVP) and pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) measurements.

### Detection and Measurement

Occult PEEP is invisible on proximal airway pressure gauges during routine mechanical ventilation because proximal pressure returns to zero while alveolar pressure remains elevated (Figure 28.9).

![Figure 28.9](images/_page_15_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28.9** Mechanism and measurement of occult (auto) PEEP. Ongoing expiratory flow ($V$) at end-expiration indicates trapped air. Occluding the expiratory port allows proximal pressure ($P_{\text{prox}}$) to equilibrate with elevated alveolar pressure ($P_{\text{alv}}$).

- **Flow Scalar Inspection:** Failure of the expiratory flow tracing to reach the zero baseline before the next breath initiates is diagnostic of dynamic hyperinflation.
- **End-Expiratory Occlusion Hold:** In a fully passive, non-breathing patient, closing the expiratory valve at end-expiration allows alveolar and circuit pressures to equilibrate, revealing true occult PEEP on the ventilator manometer.

### Management Strategies

The primary goal is to maximize expiratory time to allow complete lung deflation:
- **Increase Inspiratory Flow:** Set peak inspiratory flow rate to 80–100 L/min to shorten inspiratory time ($T_I$) and extend expiratory time ($T_E$).
- **Reduce Minute Ventilation:** Lower the respiratory rate and reduce tidal volume ($V_T$) to 6 mL/kg PBW.
- **Extrinsic PEEP Matching:** In spontaneously triggering patients with airflow obstruction, applying extrinsic PEEP to match approximately 80% of occult PEEP counterbalances the threshold trigger load without worsening distal hyperinflation.

## Clinical Perspective: Avoiding Complacency</summary><div class="details-content">

The daily management of ventilator-dependent patients is predominantly an exercise in proactive vigilance: preventing airway dislodgement, protecting against mucosal ischemia, avoiding inappropriate suctioning practices, and early recognition of mechanical barotrauma. 

If after 7 days of mechanical ventilation a patient shows no clear trajectory toward extubation, prompt planning for percutaneous tracheostomy optimizes airway hygiene, decreases sedative requirements, and enhances overall patient safety.
