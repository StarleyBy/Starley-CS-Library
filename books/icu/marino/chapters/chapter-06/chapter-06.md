# Analgesia and Sedation in the ICU

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Core Mandate:** The primary purpose of critical care is the relief of pain and distress. Discharged ICU survivors consistently identify unrelenting pain, breathlessness, sleep fragmentation, and profound anxiety as their most traumatic recollections.
* **Objective Pain Assessment:**
  * Pain cannot be evaluated by vital signs (heart rate and blood pressure exhibit poor correlation with perceived pain intensity).
  * In communicative patients, the **Numeric Rating Scale (NRS)** ($0\text{--}10$) is the gold standard (scores $> 3$ mandate analgesic intervention).
  * In non-verbal or mechanically ventilated patients, the **Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS)** evaluates facial grimacing, upper limb flexion, and ventilator compliance ($3\text{--}12$ points; score $\ge 6$ indicates unacceptable pain).
* **Opioid Analgesia ("Analgesia-First"):**
  * Opioids represent first-line therapy for moderate-to-severe ICU pain.
  * *Fentanyl:* Synthetic, 100-fold more potent than morphine, highly lipophilic with ultra-rapid onset ($1\text{--}2\text{ minutes}$), no active metabolites, and no histamine release. Context-sensitive half-life prolongs offset after prolonged continuous infusions ($> 72\text{ hours}$).
  * *Hydromorphone:* 5- to 7-fold more potent than morphine, lacks histamine-mediated hypotension and active neurotoxic metabolites; preferred in acute renal insufficiency.
  * *Morphine:* Classic alkaloid; releases histamine (provoking systemic vasodilation) and forms active glucuronide metabolites (M3G, M6G) that accumulate in renal impairment, provoking hyperalgesia and prolonged narcosis. Maintenance doses must be reduced by 50% in renal failure.
  * *Remifentanil:* Hydrolyzed by nonspecific blood esterases with an invariant half-life of 3 to 10 minutes regardless of renal or hepatic function; ideal for rapid neurological assessments, but requires bridging to prevent abrupt withdrawal.
  * *Meperidine:* **Strictly contraindicated** in intensive care; its hepatic metabolite normeperidine accumulates in renal failure, provoking CNS excitation, tremors, myoclonus, and intractable seizures.
* **Non-Opioid Multimodal Analgesia:**
  * *Acetaminophen:* IV 1 g every 6 hours (max 4 g/day) provides effective opioid-sparing analgesia but lacks anti-inflammatory properties.
  * *Ketorolac:* IV 30 mg every 6 hours (15 mg if age $\ge 65$, weight $< 50\text{ kg}$, or renal impairment) reduces opioid requirements by 25–50%; limit to $\le 5\text{ days}$ to prevent nephrotoxicity and gastrointestinal bleeding.
  * *Low-Dose Ketamine:* Subanesthetic infusions ($60\text{--}120\ \mu\text{g/kg/hr}$) provide potent NMDA-receptor blockade and opioid-sparing effects without psychomimetic emergence reactions.
* **Sedation Strategies and Scales:**
  * Target light sedation (RASS $-2\text{ to }0$) over deep sedation; deep sedation correlates directly with prolonged mechanical ventilation, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and increased mortality.
  * Daily spontaneous awakening trials ("sedation holidays") and protocolized nurse-driven titration shorten ICU length of stay.
* **Sedative Agents:**
  * *Benzodiazepines (Midazolam, Lorazepam):* Associated with active drug accumulation, prolonged emergence, and increased ICU delirium. Intravenous lorazepam infusions carry high risk of **propylene glycol toxicity** (lactic acidosis, hyperosmolal gap $> 10\text{ mOsm/kg}$, acute kidney injury) at rates $\ge 0.1\text{ mg/kg/hr}$ or $> 10\text{ mg/hr}$ for $> 48\text{ hours}$. Benzodiazepines are restricted to alcohol/sedative withdrawal and refractory status epilepticus.
  * *Propofol:* GABA-A agonist with ultra-rapid onset ($1\text{--}2\text{ minutes}$) and offset ($10\text{--}15\text{ minutes}$). High-dose prolonged infusions ($> 4\text{--}5\text{ mg/kg/hr}$ or $> 67\text{--}83\ \mu\text{g/kg/min}$ for $> 48\text{ hours}$) carry risk of **Propofol Infusion Syndrome (PRIS)**: metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hypertriglyceridemia, hepatomegaly, and fatal refractory bradyarrhythmias.
  * *Dexmedetomidine:* Highly selective central $\alpha_2$-adrenergic agonist providing "cooperative sedation" without respiratory depression. Preserves natural slow-wave sleep architecture and significantly reduces delirium incidence.
* **Delirium:**
  * Manifests as hypoactive (most common, worst prognosis) or hyperactive states.
  * Haloperidol remains useful for acute combative agitation but does not shorten delirium duration; risk of QTc prolongation and torsades de pointes. Atypical antipsychotics (quetiapine, olanzapine) assist in refractory behavioral disruption.

## The ICU Experience and Pain Assessment</summary><div class="details-content">

Contrary to popular perception, the primary objective of critical care medicine is not merely the prolongation of life, but the mitigation of pain and suffering. Hospitalized intensive care patients are exposed to profound physical and emotional distress, including indwelling vascular catheters, endotracheal tubes, suctioning, wound care, immobilization, and sleep deprivation.

Surveys of ICU survivors indicate that severe, unalleviated pain and overwhelming anxiety constitute their most vivid memories of critical illness. Inadequate pain control promotes autonomic hyperarousal, tachycardia, myocardial ischemia, immunosuppression, catabolism, and long-term post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).

A persistent clinical misconception is the assumption that sedative agents (such as benzodiazepines or propofol) alleviate pain ([Fig. 6.1](#fig-6-1)). Sedatives induce amnesia and blunt motor responsiveness without exerting intrinsic antinociceptive effects, trapping patients in an uncommunicative state of ongoing distress.

### Monitoring Pain Intensity
Because pain is an entirely subjective sensory and emotional experience, self-reporting is the indisputable gold standard of assessment.

#### 1. Numeric Rating Scale (NRS)
For alert, communicative patients, the 11-point Numeric Rating Scale ($0\text{ to }10$) is recommended:
* $0$ = No pain.
* $1\text{ to }3$ = Mild pain.
* $4\text{ to }6$ = Moderate pain.
* $7\text{ to }10$ = Severe, excruciating pain.

A score $> 3$ indicates clinically unacceptable pain that warrants immediate analgesic therapy and reassessment.

#### 2. Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS)
When consciousness is blunted by critical illness or mechanical ventilation, physiological surrogates must be abandoned in favor of validated behavioral observation tools ([Table 6.1](#table-6-1)).

<span id="table-6-1"></span>

| TABLE 6.1 | The Behavioral Pain Scale (BPS) | |
|---|---|---|
| **Behavioral Item** | **Description** | **Score** |
| **Facial Expression** | • Relaxed<br>• Partially tightened (brow furrowing)<br>• Fully tightened (eyelids closed)<br>• Grimacing | 1<br>2<br>3<br>4 |
| **Upper Limb Movement** | • No movement<br>• Partially bent<br>• Fully bent with finger flexion<br>• Permanently retracted | 1<br>2<br>3<br>4 |

The Behavioral Pain Scale scores three distinct response domains from 1 to 4, yielding an aggregate score ranging from 3 (no pain) to 12 (maximal pain). A BPS score $\ge 6$ reflects unacceptable pain requiring therapeutic intervention.

#### The Fallacy of Vital Signs
Relying on changes in heart rate, arterial blood pressure, or respiratory rate to infer pain intensity is fundamentally flawed. Multiple prospective trials demonstrate virtually zero correlation between hemodynamic fluctuations and patient-reported pain scores. Tachycardia and hypertension in critical illness reflect multifactorial sympathetic activation, hypercapnia, hypovolemia, sepsis, or vasopressor therapy rather than nociception.

## Opioid Analgesia</summary><div class="details-content">

Opioid analgesics represent the cornerstone of acute pain management in the ICU. The concept of **analgosedation** ("analgesia-first") mandates that pain be systematically addressed and treated prior to the administration of pure sedative-hypnotic agents.

Opioids act via presynaptic and postsynaptic G-protein-coupled opioid receptors ($\mu$, $\kappa$, and $\delta$) distributed throughout the central and peripheral nervous systems, inhibiting nociceptive transmission while inducing mild dose-dependent sedation without retrograde or anterograde amnesia.

### Comparative Opioid Pharmacology

#### 1. Fentanyl
Fentanyl is a synthetic phenylpiperidine derivative approximately 100-fold more potent than morphine ([Table 6.2](#table-6-2)):
* **Kinetics:** Highly lipophilic (600 times more lipophilic than morphine), enabling instantaneous cross-blood-brain barrier penetration with peak clinical effect in 1 to 2 minutes.
* **Hemodynamics:** Does not provoke mast cell degranulation or histamine release; excellent cardiovascular stability makes it the agent of choice in shock, bronchospasm, and hemodynamic instability.
* **Metabolism:** Metabolized in the liver to inactive norfentanyl; safe in renal failure.
* **Context-Sensitive Half-Life:** Following prolonged continuous infusions ($> 72\text{ hours}$), fentanyl extensively accumulates in adipose and skeletal muscle tissue. Upon discontinuing the infusion, terminal elimination half-life extends from 3 hours to $> 15\text{--}24\text{ hours}$.

#### 2. Hydromorphone (Dilaudid)
Hydromorphone is a semisynthetic morphine congener 5 to 7 times more potent than its parent compound:
* **Advantages:** Minimal histamine release; absence of clinically significant analgesic metabolites.
* **Renal Profile:** While its primary metabolite (hydromorphone-3-glucuronide) can accumulate in renal failure and cause rare neuroexcitation, it carries far less toxicity than morphine metabolites, making hydromorphone a reliable alternative in acute or chronic kidney disease.

#### 3. Morphine
The principal natural alkaloid of opium:
* **Histamine Release:** Stimulates cutaneous and systemic mast cell degranulation, inducing peripheral arteriolar and venular vasodilation that provokes abrupt systemic hypotension, particularly in hypovolemic patients.
* **Active Metabolites:** Hepatic glucuronidation yields morphine-6-glucuronide (M6G), a potent opioid agonist responsible for enhanced analgesia and prolonged respiratory depression, and morphine-3-glucuronide (M3G), an antagonist that provokes neuroexcitation, hyperalgesia, allodynia, and seizures.
* **Renal Failure:** Both M6G and M3G are cleared strictly by glomerular filtration. In renal insufficiency ($\text{CrCl} < 50\text{ mL/min}$), toxic accumulation occurs rapidly; maintenance doses must be reduced by 50% or avoided altogether.

#### 4. Remifentanil
Remifentanil is an ultra-short-acting esterase-metabolized opioid congener approximately 250 times more potent than morphine:
* **Unique Metabolism:** Hydrolyzed within minutes by non-specific blood and tissue esterases, yielding an organ-independent elimination half-life of 3 to 10 minutes. Clearance is unaffected by severe hepatic or renal failure.
* **Neurocritical Care Utility:** Rapid offset enables neurological assessment within 10 minutes of stopping infusion.
* **Acute Withdrawal:** Immediate clearance can precipitate severe rebound hyperalgesia and acute opioid withdrawal unless long-acting opioids are co-administered before cessation.

#### 5. Meperidine (Demerol) — The Forbidden Opioid
Meperidine must **never be used** for analgesia in the ICU. Its hepatic metabolite, normeperidine, has a prolonged half-life (15 to 40 hours) and undergoes exclusive renal elimination. Accumulation of normeperidine induces central nervous system toxicity, presenting as tremors, agitation, multifocal myoclonus, and tonic-clonic seizures unresponsive to naloxone.

### Patient-Controlled Analgesia (PCA)
In cooperative, alert patients, intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) provides superior analgesia with lower cumulative opioid consumption compared to nurse-administered PRN dosing ([Table 6.2](#table-6-2)). 

The **lockout interval** represents the mandatory refractive period during which the infusion pump will not deliver drug despite patient triggering; it is calibrated strictly to the time to peak drug effect (e.g., 5 to 10 minutes for fentanyl, 10 to 20 minutes for morphine). Basal continuous infusions should generally be avoided in opioid-naive patients due to the danger of unmonitored nocturnal hypoventilation.

### Complications of Opioid Therapy
1. **Respiratory Depression:** Centrally mediated via brainstem $\mu$-receptors, diminishing ventilatory sensitivity to hypercapnia. It is rare when dosing is titrated against monitored pain scales, but risk escalates dramatically when opioids are combined with benzodiazepines or propofol.
2. **Gastrointestinal Hypomotility and Ileus:** Opioids bind $\mu$-receptors in the myenteric plexus, abolishing coordinated peristalsis, promoting gastric distension, enteral tube feeding intolerance, and severe constipation. Methylnaltrexone, a quaternary amine peripheral $\mu$-antagonist that does not cross the blood-brain barrier, restores laxation in palliative patients, though clinical ICU data show variable efficacy.

### Acetaminophen (IV Paracetamol)
Administered as 1 g IV every 6 hours, intravenous acetaminophen exerts central cyclooxygenase inhibition and cannabinoid receptor modulation:
* **Opioid-Sparing Effect:** Decreases cumulative 24-hour opioid requirements by approximately 20% to 30% in surgical and trauma patients.
* **Safety:** Does not induce respiratory depression, platelet dysfunction, or gastrointestinal erosions. Contraindicated in fulminant hepatic failure; total daily dose must be limited to $\le 2\text{--}3\text{ g/day}$ in patients with cirrhosis, malnutrition, or chronic alcohol abuse.

### Ketorolac (IV NSAID)
Ketorolac tromethamine is an injectable pyrrolo-pyrrole nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug with analgesic efficacy comparable to modest opioid doses:
* **Mechanism:** Non-selective COX-1 and COX-2 inhibition, suppressing prostaglandin biosynthesis at inflammatory foci.
* **Opioid-Sparing Effect:** Reduces concurrent opioid dosing by 25% to 50% without altering respiratory drive.
* **Precautions:** Intramuscular injections risk hematoma formation; intravenous administration over 1 to 2 minutes is required. Therapy must not exceed 5 consecutive days. Strictly contraindicated in active gastrointestinal hemorrhage, severe renal dysfunction, advanced hypovolemia, or high-risk post-surgical bleeding.

### Subanesthetic Ketamine
Ketamine is a phencyclidine congener acting primarily as an uncompetitive antagonist of the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor complex:
* **Opioid-Sparing Dosing:** At subanesthetic rates ($60\text{--}120\ \mu\text{g/kg/hr}$ or $1\text{--}2\ \mu\text{g/kg/min}$), ketamine attenuates central hyperalgesia and prevents opioid tolerance without inducing dissociative anesthesia.
* **Physiological Profile:** Preserves pharyngeal reflexes, stimulates bronchial smooth muscle dilation (favorable in refractory bronchospasm), and induces sympathomimetic support of arterial blood pressure. Hallucinations and emergence delirium are exceedingly uncommon at these microdosing rates.

## Sedation Scales and Protocolized Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Excessive sedation in intensive care is directly linked to prolonged mechanical ventilation, increased ventilator-associated pneumonia, delirium, ICU-acquired weakness, and excess mortality. Modern practice dictates titrating sedatives to light, responsive targets.

### The Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS)
The RASS is the most validated and reliable instrument for quantifying agitation and depth of sedation in adult ICU patients ([Table 6.4](#table-6-4)).

<span id="table-6-4"></span>

| TABLE 6.4 | The Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) | |
|---|---|---|
| **Score** | **Term** | **Description** |
| **+4** | Combative | Overly combative or violent; immediate danger to staff |
| **+3** | Very agitated | Pulls on or removes tube(s) or catheter(s); aggressive behavior |
| **+2** | Agitated | Frequent non-purposeful movement or patient-ventilator asynchrony |
| **+1** | Restless | Anxious or apprehensive but movements not aggressive or vigorous |
| **0** | Alert & calm | Spontaneously attentive and calm |
| **−1** | Drowsy | Not fully alert, but sustains awakening (>10 seconds), with eye contact, to voice |
| **−2** | Light sedation | Briefly awakens (<10 seconds), with eye contact, to voice |
| **−3** | Moderate sedation | Any movement (eye opening/head turn), but no eye contact, to voice |
| **−4** | Deep sedation | No response to voice, but movement to physical stimulation |
| **−5** | Unarousable | No response to voice or physical stimulation |

#### Three-Step Protocol for Determining RASS Score
1. **Observation:** Observe patient without stimulation. If alert and calm, score is 0. If displaying restlessness or agitation, score from $+1\text{ to }+4$.
2. **Verbal Stimulation:** If patient is not alert, call patient's name loudly and instruct them to open eyes and look at you.
   * Awakens with sustained eye contact ($> 10\text{ seconds}$): Score is $-1$.
   * Awakens with brief eye contact ($< 10\text{ seconds}$): Score is $-2$.
   * Movement or eye opening without eye contact: Score is $-3$.
3. **Physical Stimulation:** If no response to voice, apply physical stimulation (shoulder shake, then vigorous sternal rub).
   * Any purposeful or reflexive movement: Score is $-4$.
   * Completely unarousable without motor response: Score is $-5$.

**Clinical Target:** Contemporary guidelines recommend maintaining patients at **light sedation** (RASS $-2\text{ to }0$). Deep sedation (RASS $-4\text{ to }-5$) is indicated solely in acute intracranial hypertension, severe patient-ventilator dyssynchrony requiring neuromuscular blockade, and refractory status epilepticus.

### Spontaneous Awakening Trials ("Sedation Holidays")
The routine practice of conducting daily **Spontaneous Awakening Trials (SAT)**—abruptly interrupting all continuous sedative infusions every morning until the patient is awake or demonstrates agitation—significantly decreases total ventilator duration, accelerates extubation, and decreases 1-year mortality.

## Sedative Pharmacology: Benzodiazepines</summary><div class="details-content">

Benzodiazepines potentiate inhibitory gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) neurotransmission by binding allosterically to the GABA-A receptor complex, increasing chloride conductance and hyperpolarizing neuronal membranes.

<span id="table-6-5"></span>

| TABLE 6.5 | Sedation with Intravenous Benzodiazepines | |
|---|---|---|
| **Feature** | **Midazolam** | **Lorazepam** |
| **Bolus Dose** | 0.01–0.05 mg/kg | 0.02–0.04 mg/kg |
| **Onset of Action** | 3–5 min | 5–15 min |
| **Duration of Single Dose** | 1–2 hr | 2–6 hr |
| **Continuous Infusion Rate** | 0.02–0.1 mg/kg/hr | 0.01–0.1 mg/kg/hr |
| **Lipid Solubility** | Very high (+++) | Moderate (++) |
| **Primary Clinical Concerns** | Active neurotoxic metabolites accumulating in renal failure | Propylene glycol toxicity with high-dose prolonged infusion |

### Midazolam
Midazolam is an imidazobenzodiazepine characterized by rapid onset ($3\text{--}5\text{ minutes}$) and short single-dose duration ($1\text{--}2\text{ hours}$) ([Table 6.5](#table-6-5)):
* **Metabolism:** Undergoes hepatic CYP3A4 conversion to 1-hydroxymidazolam, an active glucuronidated metabolite cleared by the kidneys.
* **Bioaccumulation:** Highly lipophilic; prolonged infusions result in peripheral adipose saturation. In critically ill patients receiving midazolam for $> 48\text{ hours}$, emergence can be delayed for 30 to 72 hours.
* **Drug Interactions:** CYP3A4 inhibitors (diltiazem, macrolides, azole antifungals) markedly prolong midazolam clearance.

### Lorazepam
Lorazepam is less lipid-soluble, conferring a slower onset ($5\text{--}15\text{ minutes}$) and longer duration ($2\text{--}6\text{ hours}$):
* **Metabolism:** Converted directly via hepatic glucuronidation to inactive lorazepam glucuronide; lacks active metabolites and clearance is independent of cytochrome P450 enzymes.

#### Propylene Glycol Toxicity
Due to low aqueous solubility, intravenous lorazepam is formulated in an 80% propylene glycol vehicle:
* **Pathophysiology:** Propylene glycol is converted by alcohol dehydrogenase to lactic acid, pyruvate, and acetic acid. At high infusion rates ($\ge 0.1\text{ mg/kg/hr}$ or $> 10\text{ mg/hr}$ sustained $> 48\text{ hours}$), metabolic capacity is overwhelmed.
* **Clinical Toxidrome:** Characterized by severe high anion-gap metabolic (lactic) acidosis, refractory hyperosmolality, acute tubular necrosis, and neurological deterioration.
* **Laboratory Screening:** Monitor the **osmolal gap**. An elevated serum osmolal gap $> 10\text{ mOsm/kg H}_2\text{O}$ correlates directly with toxic propylene glycol levels.

### Benzodiazepines and ICU Delirium
Extensive multicenter investigations confirm that benzodiazepine administration is an independent risk factor for the development of transition to ICU delirium. Consequently, the SCCM PADIS guidelines mandate that **non-benzodiazepine sedatives (propofol or dexmedetomidine) be utilized preferentially over benzodiazepines** for continuous sedation in mechanically ventilated patients.

### Propofol (Diprivan)
Propofol (2,6-diisopropylphenol) is an alkylphenol derivative acting via direct activation of GABA-A receptors, sodium channel inhibition, and central cannabinoid stimulation:
* **Kinetics:** Extreme lipid solubility yields onset within 1 to 2 minutes. Initial redistribution half-life is 2 to 4 minutes.
* **Caloric Value:** Formulated in a 10% lipid emulsion (soybean oil, glycerol, egg lecithin) providing $1.1\text{ kcal/mL}$. Nutritional lipid calories must be subtracted from daily parenteral/enteral regimens to prevent hypertriglyceridemia and pancreatitis.

#### Emergence Dynamics
While propofol is renowned for rapid offset ($10\text{--}15\text{ minutes}$ following brief procedures), prolonged infusions result in tissue accumulation ([Table 6.7](#table-6-7)):
* At 24 hours of light sedation, emergence occurs in 13 minutes.
* If deep sedation is maintained for $> 72\text{ hours}$ to 7 days, terminal redistribution prolongs emergence up to 59 to 71 hours.

#### Propofol Infusion Syndrome (PRIS)
PRIS is a lethal metabolic catastrophe triggered by high-dose, prolonged propofol infusions ($> 4\text{--}5\text{ mg/kg/hr}$ or $> 67\text{--}83\ \mu\text{g/kg/min}$ for $> 48\text{ hours}$):
* **Mechanism:** Propofol uncouples mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and inhibits cellular free fatty acid oxidation.
* **Clinical Tetrad:** Refractory metabolic (lactic) acidosis, severe rhabdomyolysis with hyperkalemia, acute kidney failure, and malignant cardiovascular collapse (bradycardia, Brugada-like ECG pattern, cardiogenic shock, and asystole).
* **Prevention:** Cap infusion rates $< 4\text{ mg/kg/hr}$ ($< 67\ \mu\text{g/kg/min}$); routinely monitor arterial blood gases, lactate, creatine kinase (CK), and serum triglycerides.

### Dexmedetomidine (Precedex)
Dexmedetomidine is a potent, highly selective $\alpha_2$-adrenergic agonist with an $\alpha_2:\alpha_1$ receptor affinity ratio of 1600:1 (eight-fold more selective than clonidine):
* **Site of Action:** Stimulates presynaptic $\alpha_2$-adrenoreceptors within the **locus coeruleus** of the brainstem, attenuating central noradrenergic neurotransmission.
* **"Cooperative Sedation":** Induces a state mimicking natural non-REM stage III slow-wave sleep. Patients remain easily arousable to verbal interaction, follow complex commands, and spontaneously return to a sleep-like state when stimulation ceases.
* **Absence of Respiratory Depression:** Does not suppress central hypercapnic or hypoxic respiratory drive, permitting safe continuation during non-invasive ventilation, high-flow nasal cannula, and throughout the pre- and post-extubation phases.
* **Delirium Protection:** Large randomized trials (SEDCOM, MENDS, PRODEX) confirm that dexmedetomidine significantly reduces the duration and incidence of ICU delirium compared to midazolam and propofol.
* **Hemodynamic Side Effects:** Central sympatholysis induces peripheral vasodilation and sinus bradycardia; avoid rapid IV boluses (loading doses) to prevent acute reflex hypertension followed by profound hypotension.

## Delirium Assessment and Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Delirium is an acute neurocognitive syndrome characterized by fluctuating mental status, inattention, disorganized thinking, and altered level of consciousness. It develops in 50% to 80% of mechanically ventilated ICU patients and is an independent predictor of prolonged hospitalization, cognitive impairment at 1 year, and increased 6-month mortality.

### Delirium Phenotypes
1. **Hypoactive Delirium ("Quiet Delirium"):** Characterized by lethargy, withdrawal, flat affect, and decreased motor activity. Represents the most common clinical form ($> 60\%$), carries the worst long-term prognosis, and is frequently missed without formal screening.
2. **Hyperactive Delirium:** Characterized by agitation, combative behavior, pulling at lines and endotracheal tubes, and emotional lability.
3. **Mixed Delirium:** Fluctuates unpredictably between hyperactive and hypoactive states.

### Routine Screening: CAM-ICU
All ICU patients should be screened every 8 to 12 hours using the **Confusion Assessment Method for the ICU (CAM-ICU)**:
* **Feature 1:** Acute onset of mental status change or fluctuating course.
* **Feature 2:** Inattention (evaluated by the letters test "SAVEAHAART").
* **Feature 3:** Altered level of consciousness (current RASS $\ne 0$).
* **Feature 4:** Disorganized thinking (answering basic logic questions).
* **Diagnosis:** Delirium is confirmed when Features 1 AND 2 are present, plus EITHER Feature 3 OR 4.

### Pharmacological Intervention for Delirium
Current evidence indicates that antipsychotic medications **do not reduce the duration or mortality of ICU delirium**. Pharmacotherapy is reserved strictly for controlling severe agitation that threatens patient or staff safety ([Table 6.8](#table-6-8)):
* **Haloperidol:** High-potency butyrophenone that antagonizes dopamine D<sub>2</sub> receptors. Intravenous haloperidol has minimal hemodynamic impact, but causes dose-dependent **QTc prolongation** and carries a risk of inducing torsades de pointes ventricular tachycardia. Continuous telemetry and daily 12-lead ECG monitoring are mandatory; withhold if $\text{QTc} > 500\text{ ms}$.
* **Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS):** Idiosyncratic hyperpyrexia, lead-pipe muscle rigidity, hypermetabolism, and massive rhabdomyolysis; requires immediate drug discontinuation and dantrolene/bromocriptine therapy.
* **Atypical Antipsychotics:** Quetiapine (25 to 100 mg orally/NG twice daily) and olanzapine exhibit 5-HT<sub>2A</sub> and D<sub>2</sub> antagonism with a lower incidence of extrapyramidal symptoms, assisting in facilitating extubation during hyperactive agitation.

## A Final Word: Opiophobia, Opiophilia, and the Therapeutic Pendulum</summary><div class="details-content">

### Opiophobia
In the late twentieth century, widespread clinical reluctance to prescribe opioid analgesics—termed **opiophobia**—led to severe under-recognition and undertreatment of postoperative and critical care pain. In response, advocacy campaigns branded pain as the "fifth vital sign," citing flawed literature asserting that medicinal opioid exposure carried negligible addictive liability.

### Opiophilia
The ensuing decades witnessed unrestrained opioid overprescribing (**opiophilia**), cresting at 81 prescriptions per 100 individuals in the United States by 2010. This indiscriminate escalation triggered a devastating epidemic of community addiction and fatal overdoses, claiming hundreds of thousands of lives.

### Restoring Clinical Equilibrium
In critical care medicine, swinging the therapeutic pendulum back to unreasoning opiophobia is perilous. Critically ill patients experiencing invasive mechanical ventilation, thoracostomies, or traumatic injury require aggressive, humane antinociceptive therapy. 

The modern path forward is neither indiscriminate opioid reliance nor fear-driven denial of analgesia, but **structured multimodal analgosedation**: prioritizing precise objective monitoring, deploying non-opioid adjuvants (acetaminophen, ketorolac, ketamine), utilizing non-GABAergic sedatives (dexmedetomidine), and continuously enforcing light sedation targets to optimize comfort and survival.
