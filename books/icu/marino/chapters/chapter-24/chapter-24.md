# Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome

*Physicians think they do a lot for a patient when they give his disease a name.*
— Immanuel Kant

Over the decades, acute respiratory distress syndrome has carried numerous designations, including shock lung, Da Nang lung, stiff lung, leaky capillary pulmonary edema, noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, and acute lung injury. None of these descriptive labels captures the fundamental pathophysiology of the disorder: a diffuse, neutrophil-driven inflammatory injury of the pulmonary microvasculature and alveolar architecture that remains a leading cause of acute hypoxemic respiratory failure in modern intensive care units.

## Executive Summary</summary>
<div class="details-content">

- **Pathophysiology:** ARDS is initiated by systemic or direct pulmonary activation of circulating neutrophils, which sequester in the pulmonary microvasculature, migrate into interstitial spaces, and release proteolytic enzymes and reactive oxygen species. This breaks the alveolar-capillary barrier, causing exudation of proteinaceous fluid into alveolar spaces and secondary surfactant destruction.
- **Diagnostic Criteria (Berlin Definition):**
  1. Acute onset within 7 days of a known clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms.
  2. Bilateral pulmonary opacities on chest radiography or CT not fully explained by effusions, lobar collapse, or nodules.
  3. Respiratory failure not fully explained by left ventricular failure or fluid overload (objective assessment via echocardiography recommended).
  4. Impaired oxygenation: $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 \le 300$ mmHg with positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) $\ge 5$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$.
- **Severity Stratification:**
  - *Mild:* $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 = 201\text{--}300$ mmHg (mortality 35%).
  - *Moderate:* $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 = 100\text{--}200$ mmHg (mortality 40%).
  - *Severe:* $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 < 100$ mmHg (mortality 45%).
- **Non-Ventilator Strategies:**
  - *Patient Self-Induced Lung Injury (P-SILI):* Vigorous inspiratory efforts generate massive negative pleural pressure swings, increasing vascular transmural pressure and driving fluid out of leaky capillaries. Excessive tidal volumes cause volutrauma in spontaneous breathing. Relieve respiratory drive promptly with high-flow nasal oxygen (40–60 L/min); intubate without delay if vigorous efforts persist.
  - *Fluid Management:* Conservative fluid strategy targeting a neutral or negative cumulative balance shortens ventilator days.
  - *Corticosteroids:* Dexamethasone (6 mg daily for 10 days) is proven in COVID-19 ARDS requiring oxygen. In non-COVID moderate-to-severe ARDS, methylprednisolone (1 mg/kg/day during the first 72 hours, or 2 mg/kg/day during days 7–14, tapered over 14 days) suppresses fibroproliferation.
  - *Prone Positioning:* Indicated for mechanically ventilated patients with moderate-to-severe ARDS ($\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 < 150$ mmHg with $\text{FiO}_2 \ge 60\%$ and PEEP $\ge 5$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) for at least 16 consecutive hours daily. Improves ventilation-perfusion matching and provides a proven survival benefit.
- **Lung-Protective Mechanical Ventilation:**
  - Traditional mechanical ventilation produces ventilator-induced lung injury (VILI) through volutrauma (overdistension of the small aerated "baby lung"), atelectrauma (cyclic collapse and reopening of unstable alveoli), and biotrauma (pulmonary cytokine release triggering systemic multiorgan failure).
  - *ARDSNet Protocol:* Target tidal volume of 6 mL/kg of predicted body weight (PBW), maintain end-inspiratory plateau pressure ($P_{\text{plat}}$) $\le 30$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$, apply PEEP $\ge 5$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$, and permit permissive hypercapnia (target pH 7.30–7.45; pH 7.15–7.30 well-tolerated).

## Pathogenesis and Predisposing Conditions</summary>
<div class="details-content">

The initial description of ARDS in 1967 detailed 12 patients presenting with refractory cyanosis, diffuse bilateral infiltrates on chest radiography, and noncompliant lungs. Postmortem examinations revealed dense intra-alveolar fibrinous exudates, hemorrhage, and hyaline membrane formation without evidence of active bacterial infection, establishing ARDS as a sterile, non-specific inflammatory lung injury.

### Cellular Mechanisms of Alveolar Injury

The central effector cell in ARDS is the circulating neutrophil:
1. **Margination and Sequestration:** Proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-$\alpha$, IL-1$\beta$, IL-8) upregulate endothelial selectins and integrins, trapping neutrophils within the pulmonary microvasculature.
2. **Transmigration and Degranulation:** Neutrophils migrate into the interstitial and alveolar compartments, releasing myeloperoxidase, elastase, collagenase, and reactive oxygen species.
3. **Alveolar-Capillary Disruption:** Endothelial necrosis and denudation of alveolar epithelial cells (particularly fragile Type I pneumocytes) permit massive exudation of plasma proteins, fibrinogen, erythrocytes, and platelets into the alveolar spaces (Figure 24.1).
4. **Surfactant Inactivation:** Flooding of the alveoli by plasma proteins inactivates pulmonary surfactant, precipitating widespread microatelectasis and stiffening the lung.
5. **Fibroproliferative Phase:** If acute inflammation persists beyond 7 to 14 days, intra-alveolar fibrin organizes into cellular granulation tissue, leading to irreversible pulmonary fibrosis.

### Etiologic Risk Factors

ARDS develops secondary to diverse direct (pulmonary) or indirect (extrapulmonary) insults, listed in Table 24.1.

| Category | Primary Predisposing Conditions and Prevalence |
|---|---|
| **Infection-Related Insults** | • Bacterial or Viral Pneumonia (**60%**)<br>• Extrapulmonary Sepsis (**16%**)<br>• Septic Shock (**8%**) |
| **Noninfectious Insults** | • Aspiration of Gastric Contents (**14%**)<br>• Pulmonary Contusion (**6%**)<br>• Massive Blood Transfusions / TRALI (**4%**)<br>• Multisystem Trauma (**4%**)<br>• Smoke and Toxic Inhalation Injury (**2%**) |

*Note:* Prevalences sum to > 100% because multiple predisposing conditions coexist in many patients. Approximately 10% of ARDS cases have no identifiable precipitating trigger.

### COVID-19 ARDS Phenotype

The pandemic caused by SARS-CoV-2 brought unprecedented numbers of patients with viral ARDS into intensive care units. In up to one in three hospitalized COVID-19 patients, severe hypoxemia developed.

However, a distinct feature of early COVID-19 ARDS is severe hypoxemia out of proportion to lung stiffness (normal respiratory compliance), driven by pulmonary vascular endotheliitis, microvascular thrombosis, and loss of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. Caution is required when extrapolating clinical trial findings in COVID-19 to classic ARDS.

## Diagnostic Criteria and Clinical Evaluation</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Clinical diagnostic criteria established by the Berlin Consensus Definition are outlined in Table 24.2.

| Berlin Diagnostic Criterion | Specification and Operational Requirement |
|---|---|
| **Timing** | Acute onset within **7 days** of a known clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms. |
| **Chest Imaging** | Bilateral opacities on chest radiography or CT scan, not fully explained by effusions, collapse, or nodules. |
| **Origin of Edema** | Respiratory failure not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload (objective confirmation needed). |
| **Oxygenation Deficit** | $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 \le 300$ mmHg measured with a positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) $\ge 5$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$. |

### Severity Categories and In-Hospital Mortality

The severity of ARDS is classified according to the arterial oxygenation ratio ($\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2$) on a minimum PEEP of 5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$:
- **Mild ARDS:** $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2$ between 201 and 300 mmHg. In-hospital mortality is approximately **35%**.
- **Moderate ARDS:** $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2$ between 100 and 200 mmHg. In-hospital mortality is approximately **40%**.
- **Severe ARDS:** $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 < 100$ mmHg. In-hospital mortality is approximately **45%**.

Approximately 80% of ARDS patients require endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation, predominantly during the initial 48 hours following clinical onset.

### Diagnostic Pitfalls and Clinical Dilemmas

Despite standardized definitions, diagnostic error occurs in up to 50% of cases:

- **Chest Radiography Fallibility:** The classic radiographic appearance of ARDS consists of diffuse, bilateral, finely granular "ground-glass" opacities sparing the costophrenic angles (Figure 24.2, top panel). However, this classic pattern occurs in only **15% to 25% of cases**. Asymmetrical, patchy lower-lobe consolidations simulating pneumonia or heart failure are far more common (Figure 24.2, bottom panel). Plain radiographs have a negative predictive value of only 47% compared to CT.
- **Low Specificity:** In an autopsy series of intensive care unit patients dying with a clinical diagnosis of ARDS, diffuse alveolar damage (the histologic hallmark) was confirmed in only 50% of patients.
- **Fallacy of the Pulmonary Wedge Pressure:** Historically, a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) $\le 18$ mmHg was used to exclude cardiogenic pulmonary edema. However, PCWP reflects left atrial pressure, which is lower than true microvascular capillary hydrostatic pressure. A wedge pressure $\le 18$ mmHg does *not* rule out hydrostatic edema.

### Computed Tomography Characteristics

CT imaging demonstrates regional heterogeneity of lung involvement (Figure 24.3). In early ARDS, dense consolidation is confined predominantly to dependent (dorsal) lung zones, while nondependent (ventral) regions remain aerated. In late ARDS, diffuse reticulation and cystic honeycombing reflect architectural destruction and fibrosis.

## Non-Ventilator Management</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Numerous pharmacologic therapies in ARDS—including exogenous surfactant, inhaled nitric oxide, intravenous prostacyclins, statins, beta-agonists, and antioxidants—have failed to improve survival. Supportive care remains the cornerstone of management.

### Patient Self-Induced Lung Injury (P-SILI)

Spontaneously breathing patients with severe dyspnea generate immense inspiratory efforts that exacerbate pulmonary damage through two distinct mechanisms (Figure 24.4):
1. **Negative-Pressure Pulmonary Edema:** Extreme inspiratory pleural pressure swings (up to $-30$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) dramatically increase vascular transmural pressure, pulling fluid across permeable pulmonary capillaries into the interstitium and alveoli.
2. **Pendelluft and Volutrauma:** Vigorous diaphragmatic contraction creates localized regional lung distension, shifting gas between lung units and overstretching nondependent alveoli.

![Mechanism of P-SILI](images/_page_8_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 24.4** Pathophysiologic cascade of patient self-induced lung injury (P-SILI). Excessive respiratory drive generates negative pleural pressure swings, increasing vascular transmural pressure, accelerating alveolar edema, and causing regional volutrauma.

*Clinical Implication:* Tachypneic patients who fail to settle with noninvasive support must be intubated promptly to abolish damaging spontaneous efforts.

### High-Flow Nasal Oxygen (HFNO)

High-flow nasal cannula systems deliver heated and humidified oxygen at flow rates of 40 to 60 L/min with titratable $\text{FiO}_2$.
- *Clinical Value:* High inspiratory flow satisfies high peak demand, reduces work of breathing, flushes nasopharyngeal dead space, and delivers low-level PEEP (2 to 5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$).
- *Preventing P-SILI:* By reducing dyspnea and transpulmonary pressure swings, HFNO mitigates P-SILI and reduces intubation rates in mild-to-moderate hypoxemic respiratory failure.
- *Oxygen Toxicity Hazard:* Titrate $\text{FiO}_2$ downward to maintain $\text{SpO}_2$ 88% to 95%. Prolonged exposure to $\text{FiO}_2 \ge 70\%$ depletes cellular glutathione and generates alveolar injury histologically indistinguishable from ARDS.

### Noninvasive Ventilation (NIV)

Noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation (BiPAP/CPAP) has a narrow therapeutic window in ARDS (Figure 24.5):
- *Success:* Effective primarily in mild ARDS ($\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 = 201\text{--}300$ mmHg).
- *Failure:* In moderate-to-severe ARDS, NIV carries a failure rate exceeding 50%. Patients who fail NIV have higher mortality than those intubated early, driven by persistent large tidal volumes ($\ge 9.5$ mL/kg PBW) and ongoing P-SILI. If $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2$ does not improve after 1 hour, intubation should proceed.

![Noninvasive ventilation in ARDS](images/_page_10_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 24.5** Outcomes of noninvasive ventilation in ARDS. Left panel: NIV succeeds predominantly in mild ARDS. Right panel: failure of NIV requiring delayed intubation is associated with a sharply higher mortality rate.

### Conservative Fluid Management

Increased microvascular permeability accelerates alveolar extravasation at any given hydrostatic pressure. A conservative fluid strategy (avoiding fluid boluses, utilizing loop diuretics to achieve neutral or negative cumulative balance) significantly increases ventilator-free days and shortens ICU stay without increasing acute kidney injury or shock.

### Corticosteroid Therapy

Indications and regimens for corticosteroid therapy in ARDS are outlined in Table 24.3.

| Clinical Indication | Evidence-Based Steroid Regimen |
|---|---|
| **COVID-19 ARDS** | **Dexamethasone:** 6 mg IV or orally once daily for up to **10 days**. |
| **Early Moderate-to-Severe ARDS** (within first 72 hours) | **Methylprednisolone:** 1 mg/kg/day (ideal body weight) by continuous IV infusion, slowly tapered over **14 days**. |
| **Persistent ARDS** (days 7 to 14 of onset) | **Methylprednisolone:** 2 mg/kg/day (ideal body weight) by continuous IV infusion, slowly tapered over **14 days**. |

*Caveats:* Corticosteroids started after day 14 of ARDS provide no benefit and increase mortality. Continuous infusions are preferred to avoid hyperglycemic spikes.

## Prone Positioning</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Prone positioning is one of the few interventions with a proven survival benefit in severe ARDS.

### Physiological Mechanisms

In the supine position, gravity, the weight of the overlying lung tissue, and the dorsal displacement of the heart compress dependent dorsal lung segments, causing alveolar collapse.
- *Dorsal Recruitment:* Placing the patient prone relieves hydrostatic compressive forces, opening collapsed dorsal alveoli while maintaining blood flow, dramatically improving ventilation-perfusion matching.
- *Homogeneous Strain:* Prone positioning makes chest wall elastance more uniform, distributing ventilator tidal volumes evenly and decreasing regional shear stress.
- *Right Ventricular Unloading:* Alveolar recruitment decreases pulmonary vascular resistance and relieves acute cor pulmonale.

### Clinical Protocol and Indications

Guidelines for prone positioning are summarized in Table 24.4.

| Indications | Absolute Contraindications |
|---|---|
| 1. Mechanically ventilated patients with **moderate-to-severe ARDS** ($\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 < 150$ mmHg with $\text{FiO}_2 \ge 60\%$ and PEEP $\ge 5$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) receiving lung-protective ventilation (~ 6 mL/kg PBW).<br>2. Awake, spontaneously breathing patients on HFNO or NIV to avoid intubation. | • Active severe hemorrhage<br>• Unstable spinal fractures<br>• Multiple pelvic/facial fractures<br>• Severe open intracranial hypertension (ICP > 30 mmHg)<br>• Recent sternotomy or tracheostomy (< 2 weeks) |

- **Session Duration:** To achieve a mortality reduction, mechanically ventilated patients must remain prone for **at least 16 consecutive hours daily**. For awake patients, aim for at least 8 hours daily.
- **Discontinuation Criteria:** Prone therapy is discontinued when $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 \ge 150$ mmHg is maintained with PEEP $\le 10$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{FiO}_2 \le 60\%$ in the supine position for at least 4 hours following a session.

## Mechanical Ventilation in ARDS</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Mechanical ventilation is a supportive bridge, not a curative therapy. Improperly delivered positive-pressure ventilation inflicts secondary lung injury indistinguishable from ARDS itself.

### Ventilator-Induced Lung Injury (VILI)

VILI was first described in 1967 as the "respirator lung syndrome." Electron microscopy demonstrates that excessive mechanical distension creates physical tears in the alveolar-capillary barrier (Figure 24.6).

![Alveolar tear in VILI](images/_page_15_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 24.6** High-power electron micrograph of lung tissue showing a stress fracture across the alveolar-capillary membrane caused by positive-pressure alveolar overdistension during mechanical ventilation.

VILI arises from three distinct mechanical and biological mechanisms:
1. **Volutrauma:** The ARDS lung is not stiff throughout; rather, widespread consolidation leaves only a small, normally compliant aerated fraction—the **"baby lung"** (operating at 50% or less of normal capacity). Delivering conventional tidal volumes (12 to 15 mL/kg) into this diminished lung causes catastrophic alveolar overdistension and stress rupture.
2. **Atelectrauma:** Low end-expiratory alveolar pressures permit unstable, surfactant-deficient alveoli to collapse at end-expiration and violently snap open upon inspiration. This cyclic reopening generates high shear stress that denudes the alveolar epithelium.
3. **Biotrauma:** Cyclic mechanical stress stimulates pulmonary parenchymal cells to release proinflammatory cytokines into the systemic circulation, driving extrapulmonary multiorgan dysfunction.

### Lung-Protective Ventilation Protocol

The ARDS Network low-tidal-volume protocol (Table 24.5) is the only ventilatory strategy demonstrated in randomized trials to improve survival in ARDS.

| Ventilator Phase | ARDSNet Operational Protocol |
|---|---|
| **Phase 1: Initial Settings** | • Calculate Predicted Body Weight (PBW):<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;**Males:** $\text{PBW (kg)} = 50 + 2.3 \times (\text{Height in inches} - 60)$<br>&nbsp;&nbsp;**Females:** $\text{PBW (kg)} = 45.5 + 2.3 \times (\text{Height in inches} - 60)$<br>• Set initial tidal volume ($V_T$) at **8 mL/kg PBW**.<br>• Set initial PEEP at **5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$**.<br>• Titrate $\text{FiO}_2$ for target $\text{SpO}_2$ **88% to 95%**.<br>• Reduce $V_T$ by 1 mL/kg every 2 hours until **$V_T = 6$ mL/kg PBW**. |
| **Phase 2: Plateau Pressure Target** | • Measure end-inspiratory plateau pressure ($P_{\text{plat}}$) with a 0.5-second inspiratory pause.<br>• If $P_{\text{plat}} > 30$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$, decrease $V_T$ by 1 mL/kg PBW increments down to **4 mL/kg**.<br>• If $P_{\text{plat}} < 25$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ and $V_T < 6$ mL/kg, increase $V_T$ by 1 mL/kg until $P_{\text{plat}} \ge 25$ or $V_T = 6$ mL/kg. |
| **Phase 3: Acidosis Management** | • Target arterial pH **7.30 to 7.45**.<br>• If pH is 7.15 to 7.30, increase respiratory rate (RR) up to **35 breaths/min**.<br>• If pH < 7.15, increase RR to 35 breaths/min; if pH remains < 7.15, increase $V_T$ by 1 mL/kg increments until pH > 7.15. |

*Core Targets:* $V_T = 6$ mL/kg PBW, $P_{\text{plat}} \le 30$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{SpO}_2 = 88\text{--}95\%$, pH 7.30–7.45.

### Airway Pressure Release Ventilation (APRV)

APRV is an alternative pressure-limited mode that delivers continuous high positive airway pressure ($P_{\text{high}}$ set to previous plateau pressure, $\le 30$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) for a prolonged inspiratory time (4 to 6 seconds), interrupted by brief releases (0.4 to 0.8 seconds) to low pressure ($P_{\text{low}} = 0\text{--}5$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) for $\text{CO}_2$ clearance (Figure 24.7). The patient breathes spontaneously throughout the cycle. While APRV facilitates recruitment, definitive mortality reductions have not been established.

![APRV ventilator tracing](images/_page_18_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 24.7** Pressure-time scalar during airway pressure release ventilation (APRV), showing sustained alveolar recruitment pressure ($P_{\text{high}}$) with brief periodic releases ($P_{\text{low}}$) to facilitate carbon dioxide elimination.

### Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO)

Venovenous (VV) ECMO provides artificial gas exchange, permitting ultra-protective mechanical ventilation ($V_T$ 2 to 4 mL/kg, PEEP 10 to 15 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$, $P_{\text{plat}} < 25$ $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$). Indications include severe refractory hypoxemia: $\text{PaO}_2/\text{FiO}_2 < 80$ mmHg for $> 6$ hours, or $< 50$ mmHg for $> 3$ hours, or uncorrectable respiratory acidosis (pH < 7.15).

### Extrapulmonary Multiorgan Failure as the Primary Cause of Death

In ARDS, fewer than 20% of deaths result from refractory hypoxemia. Up to **70% of deaths are caused by progressive extrapulmonary multiorgan failure** (Figure 24.8). This underscores the systemic, inflammatory nature of the disease and emphasizes that lung-protective ventilation is vital because it halts the biotrauma that fuels distant organ destruction.

## The Systemic Burden of Inflammation</summary>
<div class="details-content">

A unifying theme in critical care medicine is the lethal supremacy of dysregulated inflammation. In acute respiratory distress syndrome, as in septic shock, organ dysfunction is mediated not primarily by tissue hypoxia, but by widespread endothelial destruction, microvascular thrombosis, and cellular apoptosis driven by inflammatory cascades.

Until therapies that modulate the host inflammatory response are discovered, clinical management must remain strictly supportive: preventing secondary iatrogenic injury with lung-protective ventilation, minimizing fluid overload, avoiding unnecessary hyperoxia, and facilitating systemic recovery.
