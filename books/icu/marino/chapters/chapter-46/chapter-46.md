# Disorders of Movement

> *When we contemplate this life, we see motion as its principal characteristic, and when we take a farther view, we see that this motion must necessarily waste the machine in which it resides.*  
> — John Young (1800)*  
> *\*Young J. An Essay on the Philosophy of the Human Mind (1800).*

This chapter examines three major movement disorders encountered in the intensive care unit: (*a*) involuntary hyperkinetic movements (seizures and status epilepticus), (*b*) weak or ineffective movements (neuromuscular weakness syndromes, including myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, and critical illness polyneuromyopathy), and (*c*) absent movements (drug-induced neuromuscular blockade) (1).

These conditions represent profound aberrations of the physical motion described by John Young, and all share an alarming propensity to exhaust and damage the critically ill human organism.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Key Principles and Clinical Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Seizures and Status Epilepticus (SE):** Status epilepticus is defined clinically as $\ge 5\text{ minutes}$ of continuous seizure activity or $\ge 2$ discrete seizures without intervening recovery of consciousness. It occurs most frequently in traumatic brain injury (20%–50%), anoxic-ischemic brain injury (40%), encephalitis (35%–45%), and intracerebral hemorrhage (20%–30%).
- **Nonconvulsive Status Epilepticus (NCSE):** A frequently overlooked cause of unexplained coma or fluctuating encephalopathy in the ICU, detected in 16% of comatose patients undergoing continuous electroencephalography (cEEG). Cardinal manifestations in 105 reported cases include impaired consciousness in 80% (confusion 49%, coma 22%, lethargy 21%), speech disturbances (15%), myoclonus (13%), bizarre behavior (11%), agitation (8%), and hallucinations (6%). Urgent cEEG is essential for diagnosis.
- **Three-Step Pharmacotherapy for Status Epilepticus:**
  - *Step 1 — Emergent Benzodiazepines (0–10 min):* First-line therapy is intravenous lorazepam ($0.1\text{ mg/kg}$ or $4\text{ mg}$ IV over $2\text{ min}$, repeated once at $5\text{ min}$) or intramuscular midazolam ($0.15\text{ mg/kg}$ or $10\text{ mg}$ IM, equally effective when IV access is delayed).
  - *Step 2 — Urgent Intravenous Anticonvulsants (10–30 min):* Administered if SE persists after benzodiazepines. Preferred agents are IV levetiracetam ($60\text{ mg/kg}$ over 5–10 min, max $4{,}500\text{ mg}$), IV fosphenytoin ($20\text{ mg PE/kg}$ at $\le 150\text{ mg PE/min}$, max $1{,}500\text{ mg PE}$), or IV valproic acid ($40\text{ mg/kg}$ over 5–10 min, max $3{,}000\text{ mg}$). All three terminate SE in approximately 50% of cases; levetiracetam is preferred due to superior safety, absence of hypotension, and lack of drug interactions.
  - *Step 3 — Refractory Status Epilepticus (30–60 min):* Develops in 10% to 15% of patients; requires general anesthesia, endotracheal intubation, and cEEG targeting electrographic burst suppression for 24 to 48 hours. Agents include continuous infusions of propofol ($1\text{–}2\text{ mg/kg}$ bolus, infusion $1\text{–}5\text{ mg/kg/hr}$, avoiding propofol infusion syndrome [PRIS] if $> 48\text{ hours}$ or $> 5\text{ mg/kg/hr}$), midazolam ($0.2\text{ mg/kg}$ loading, infusion $0.2\text{–}4\text{ mg/kg/hr}$), or pentobarbital ($5\text{–}15\text{ mg/kg}$ over $1\text{ hour}$, infusion $1\text{–}5\text{ mg/kg/hr}$).
- **Myasthenia Gravis (MG):** Postsynaptic autoantibodies against nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (AChR) cause fatigable skeletal muscle weakness without sensory deficits. Ocular and bulbar involvement (ptosis, diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia) dominates early. The bedside ice pack test (applying an ice pack to a ptotic eyelid for 2 minutes producing $\ge 2\text{ mm}$ improvement in palpebral fissure) has 90% sensitivity and specificity. Myasthenic crisis represents acute diaphragmatic/bulbar respiratory failure requiring elective intubation when vital capacity drops below $15\text{–}20\text{ mL/kg}$ or negative inspiratory force (NIF) is worse than $-20\text{ to } -30\text{ cm H}_2\text{O}$. Hold pyridostigmine to minimize airway secretions; treat with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, $2\text{ g/kg}$ over 2–5 days) or plasma exchange (PLEX, 5 exchanges over 10 days). Strictly avoid exacerbating medications (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, beta-blockers, magnesium, neuromuscular blockers).
- **Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS):** Post-infectious autoimmune acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (AIDP) triggered by *Campylobacter jejuni*, CMV, or EBV via molecular mimicry. Presents as progressive, symmetrical ascending flaccid quadriparesis with diminished or absent deep tendon reflexes, severe autonomic instability (arrhythmias, labile blood pressure), and respiratory failure in 25%. Lumbar puncture demonstrates albuminocytologic dissociation (elevated CSF protein with normal cell count). Treatment with IVIG ($2\text{ g/kg}$ over 5 days) or PLEX is equivalent; corticosteroids are completely ineffective.
- **Critical Illness Polyneuromyopathy (CINM):** Acquired sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy (CIP) or primary myosin-depleting myopathy (CIM) developing in 30% to 50% of septic, multi-organ failure patients with prolonged mechanical ventilation. Manifests as flaccid quadriparesis, muscle wasting, and refractory failure to wean from ventilatory support. No specific pharmacotherapy exists; prevention hinges on tight glycemic control, early mobilization, and minimizing neuromuscular blockers and systemic corticosteroids.
- **Neuromuscular Blocking Agents (NMBAs):** Indicated for rapid sequence intubation, shivering control in therapeutic hypothermia, and synchronous ventilation in refractory ARDS. Succinylcholine ($1\text{–}1.5\text{ mg/kg}$ IV; onset 1 min, duration 7–12 min) is the sole depolarizing agent, contraindicated in hyperkalemia, malignant hyperthermia, burns $> 48\text{ hours}$, spinal cord injury, and extensive denervation. Non-depolarizing agents include rocuronium ($1.0\text{ mg/kg}$ intubating, $5\text{–}12\ \mu\text{g/kg/min}$ infusion; hepatic elimination) and cisatracurium ($0.15\text{–}0.2\text{ mg/kg}$ intubating, $1\text{–}3\ \mu\text{g/kg/min}$ infusion; organ-independent Hofmann elimination, preferred in renal/hepatic failure).
- **Monitoring and Reversal of Blockade:** Train-of-four (TOF) monitoring on the adductor pollicis targets 1 to 2 twitches out of 4 to prevent drug accumulation. Traditional reversal with neostigmine ($0.05\text{–}0.07\text{ mg/kg}$ IV plus glycopyrrolate $0.01\text{ mg}$ per $1\text{ mg}$ neostigmine) requires at least 2 spontaneous twitches. Sugammadex is a modified cyclodextrin that selectively encapsulates aminosteroid relaxants (rocuronium/vecuronium): $2\text{ mg/kg}$ for TOF 2 twitches, $4\text{ mg/kg}$ for post-tetanic count 1–2, and $16\text{ mg/kg}$ for emergent immediate reversal (within 3 minutes). Avoid prolonged chemical paralysis to prevent ICU-acquired weakness and post-traumatic stress disorder from awareness under paralysis.

### Clinical Semiology and Nonconvulsive Status Epilepticus</summary><div class="details-content">

Seizures are classified according to clinical semiology (convulsive vs nonconvulsive), motor manifestation (tonic, clonic, or myoclonic), and neuroanatomical distribution (generalized vs focal) (1,4).

- 1. *Motor Patterns:* Tonic activity represents sustained, rigid muscle contraction; clonic activity manifests as rhythmic, repetitive jerking of regular frequency and amplitude; myoclonus consists of non-rhythmic, shock-like jerks of variable amplitude. Repetitive stereotyped movements (lip smacking, swallowing, chewing) represent motor automatisms.
- 2. *Generalized vs Focal Seizures:* Generalized seizures originate from bilateral cortical networks, always produce loss of consciousness, and may be convulsive or nonconvulsive. Focal seizures arise within neuronal networks limited to one cerebral hemisphere; they may manifest with preserved or impaired awareness and frequently generalize secondarily (1).
- 3. *Partial Complex Seizures:* Characterized by behavioral arrest and glassy staring in an awake but unresponsive patient (mimicking absence seizures), frequently preceded by an olfactory or epigastric aura.

#### Continuous EEG Monitoring and Diagnostic Pitfalls

Status epilepticus (SE) is defined as continuous seizure activity lasting $\ge 5\text{ minutes}$, or $\ge 2$ discrete seizures without intervening recovery of consciousness (5).

While generalized convulsive SE is obvious, *nonconvulsive status epilepticus (NCSE)* represents an elusive, underdiagnosed emergency in up to 80% of comatose patients (Table 46.2) (6).

Continuous electroencephalography (cEEG) reveals that nonconvulsive seizures occur in 16% of ICU patients with unexplained altered consciousness monitored for 24 to 48 hours (7), in 4% to 8% evaluated with brief intermittent EEG recordings (7,8), and in 37% of EEGs ordered specifically for coma evaluation (9). Any unexplained decline in sensorium warrants immediate cEEG.

### Three-Step Management Algorithm for Status Epilepticus</summary><div class="details-content">

Protracted seizure activity induces progressive excitotoxic neuronal death mediated by excessive intracellular calcium entry, mitochondrial dysfunction, and pharmacoresistance caused by $\text{GABA}_\text{A}$ receptor internalization and $\text{NMDA}$ glutamate receptor up-regulation.

Treatment must proceed expeditiously through a standardized three-step protocol, summarized in Table 46.3 (1,10).

#### Step 1: Emergent Benzodiazepine Therapy

Benzodiazepines are the undisputed first-line agents for terminating early status epilepticus (10–12).

Intravenous lorazepam terminates seizure activity within 2 to 3 minutes, with an anticonvulsant duration of 6 to 12 hours due to its low volume of distribution (10). If intravenous access cannot be established within minutes, intramuscular midazolam ($10\text{ mg}$ IM) is equally effective and acts faster than attempting repeated venipunctures (12).

#### Step 2: Urgent Intravenous Anticonvulsants

Approximately one-third of generalized SE episodes fail to terminate after two doses of benzodiazepines (13).

In the landmark Established Status Epilepticus Treatment Trial (ESETT), levetiracetam ($60\text{ mg/kg}$), fosphenytoin ($20\text{ mg PE/kg}$), and valproic acid ($40\text{ mg/kg}$) demonstrated equivalent efficacy, each terminating status epilepticus in approximately 50% of patients (14).

Levetiracetam is widely preferred in the ICU because it lacks cardiac depressant effects, avoids hypotension, does not require therapeutic drug monitoring, and has zero cytochrome P450 interactions (10,14). Fosphenytoin, a water-soluble prodrug, avoids the cardiac toxicity and propylene glycol vehicle of parenteral phenytoin, allowing rapid infusion up to $150\text{ mg PE/min}$ (15). Valproic acid carries a 40% risk of hyperammonemia, which can precipitate encephalopathy (16,17).

#### Step 3: Refractory Status Epilepticus and Anesthetic Infusions

Approximately 10% to 15% of status epilepticus cases fail to respond to Step 1 and Step 2 agents, progressing to *refractory status epilepticus* (RSE) (1,10).

Management mandates immediate endotracheal intubation, mechanical ventilation, invasive arterial pressure monitoring, and continuous general anesthetic infusions, as detailed in Table 46.4 (1,10).

##### Anesthetic Titration, PRIS, and Outcomes

Anesthetic infusions are titrated against continuous cEEG to achieve electrographic burst suppression (defined as 8 to 12 seconds of electrical silence punctuated by brief bursts of activity) or complete seizure cessation for 24 to 48 hours before gradual weaning.

Propofol is fast-acting and highly effective, but infusions exceeding $5\text{ mg/kg/hr}$ for $> 48\text{ hours}$ carry a lethal risk of *Propofol Infusion Syndrome (PRIS)*, characterized by severe refractory metabolic acidosis, hyperkalemia, rhabdomyolysis, hyperlipidemia, and fulminant biventricular heart failure.

Status epilepticus mortality ranges from 15% to 20% in adult ICU populations, rising to $> 40\%$ in elderly patients with anoxic-ischemic brain injury or super-refractory SE (1,5).

## Neuromuscular Weakness Syndromes</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Myasthenia Gravis and Myasthenic Crisis</summary><div class="details-content">

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by autoantibody-mediated destruction and functional blockade of nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (AChR) on the postsynaptic junctional folds of skeletal muscle (18).

Skeletal muscle is the sole target. Thymic hyperplasia is present in 65% of patients, and thymoma occurs in 10% (18).

MG can be exacerbated by physiological stress, infections, surgery, electrolyte shifts, and an extensive array of pharmacological agents that impair neuromuscular transmission, listed in Table 46.5 (19,20).

#### Clinical Semiology and Bedside Diagnostic Testing

The hallmark of MG is fluctuating skeletal muscle weakness that worsens with repetitive physical activity and improves with rest (18).

Ocular involvement (asymmetric ptosis and binocular diplopia) is the initial presentation in 50% to 60% of patients and occurs in 90% over time. Bulbar involvement manifests as dysarthria, dysphagia, chewing fatigue, and neck flexor weakness.

**FIGURE 46.1** Illustration of a positive ice test for the diagnosis of myasthenia gravis. An increase in the palpebral fissure of $\ge 2\text{ mm}$ after applying an ice pack for 2 minutes is considered a positive test. Data from Reference 22.

The *ice pack test* is a simple, highly reliable bedside diagnostic test illustrated in Figure 46.1. Cooling the eyelid inhibits acetylcholinesterase activity, improving neuromuscular transmission (21,22).

An ice pack (or ice-filled surgical glove) is placed over the closed ptotic eyelid for 2 minutes. A post-application widening of the palpebral fissure by $\ge 2\text{ mm}$ constitutes a positive test, demonstrating a sensitivity of 90% and specificity of 97% for ocular MG (22).

##### Intensive Care Management of Myasthenic Crisis

Myasthenic crisis is defined as acute respiratory failure resulting from severe diaphragm, intercostal, or upper airway bulbar weakness (23,24).

Elective endotracheal intubation should be performed before clinical collapse occurs, utilizing the "20/30/40" rule: vital capacity $< 15\text{–}20\text{ mL/kg}$, negative inspiratory force (NIF) worse than $-20\text{ to } -30\text{ cm H}_2\text{O}$, or maximum expiratory pressure (MEP) $< 40\text{ cm H}_2\text{O}$ (23).

*Management Principles in the ICU:*

- 1. *Discontinue Acetylcholinesterase Inhibitors:* Oral pyridostigmine must be temporarily discontinued upon intubation to eliminate profuse tracheobronchial secretions and exclude cholinergic crisis (23,24).
- 2. *Targeted Immunotherapy:* Rapid removal or neutralization of circulating pathogenic AChR antibodies is paramount. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, $2\text{ g/kg}$ total dose administered over 2 to 5 days) and plasma exchange (PLEX, 5 exchanges of 1.0 to 1.5 plasma volumes every other day) demonstrate equivalent clinical efficacy, inducing remission within 3 to 7 days (25,26).
- 3. *Corticosteroid Induction:* High-dose oral prednisone ($60\text{–}80\text{ mg/day}$) or IV methylprednisolone is initiated once ventilatory support or PLEX/IVIG is established.

### Guillain-Barré Syndrome</summary><div class="details-content">

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS) is an acute, post-infectious autoimmune polyradiculoneuropathy characterized by immune-mediated attack against peripheral nerves and spinal nerve roots (27).

In two-thirds of cases, GBS is preceded 1 to 3 weeks earlier by an acute gastrointestinal illness (most notably *Campylobacter jejuni*) or viral upper respiratory tract infection (cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, Zika virus). Pathogenesis involves molecular mimicry, where antibodies directed against bacterial lipooligosaccharides cross-react with neural gangliosides (such as GM1 and GD1a) on peripheral axons and myelin sheaths (27).

#### Diagnostic Workup and Immunotherapy

Clinical GBS presents with symmetrical ascending weakness, distal paresthesias ("glove-and-stocking" distribution), and universal hyporeflexia or areflexia.

Up to 25% of patients develop acute respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation (28). Autonomic instability (severe paroxysmal hypertension, alternating with bradycardia or sinus arrest) occurs in advanced disease, necessitating continuous ECG monitoring (29).

##### Miller Fisher Variant and Diagnostic Workup

*Miller Fisher Syndrome:* A classic regional variant of GBS characterized by the clinical triad of acute ophthalmoplegia, profound ataxia, and areflexia, strongly associated with anti-GQ1b IgG antibodies (30).

Diagnostic evaluation encompasses:

- 1. *Lumbar Puncture:* Confirms albuminocytologic dissociation, typically emerging after the first week of illness (31).
- 2. *Electromyography and Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS):* Reveal reduced conduction velocity, delayed F-waves, and multifocal conduction blocks indicative of demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP) or reduced compound muscle action potential amplitudes in acute motor axonal neuropathy (AMAN).

##### Immunomodulatory Therapy Protocol

Patients with progressive GBS unable to walk unaided or displaying respiratory compromise require immediate immunomodulatory therapy (31). Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG, $0.4\text{ g/kg/day}$ for 5 consecutive days, total dose $2\text{ g/kg}$) or plasma exchange (PLEX, 5 sessions over 7 to 14 days) are equally effective in accelerating motor recovery and reducing ventilator dependency (31). Combining IVIG and PLEX confers no added benefit. *Systemic corticosteroids are completely ineffective in GBS and should not be administered* (31).

### Critical Illness Polyneuromyopathy</summary><div class="details-content">

Critical illness polyneuromyopathy (CINM) is an acquired acute neuromuscular weakness syndrome that develops in 30% to 50% of critically ill patients suffering from severe sepsis, septic shock, and multi-organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) (32,33).

CINM encompasses two pathologically distinct entities that frequently coexist:

- 1. *Critical Illness Polyneuropathy (CIP):* A primary distal axonal sensorimotor polyneuropathy triggered by microvascular ischemia of peripheral nerves, endothelial activation, and toxic metabolic injury (34,35).
- 2. *Critical Illness Myopathy (CIM):* An acute non-necrotizing primary myopathy characterized by selective loss of thick myosin filaments, sarcolemmal inexcitability, and muscle atrophy (36,37).

#### Clinical Features, Prevention, and Outcomes

CINM manifests as severe generalized, flaccid quadriparesis and muscle wasting, typically recognized when a patient fails repeated weaning trials from mechanical ventilation despite resolution of primary pulmonary pathology (38,39).

Facial and extraocular muscles are characteristically spared, while deep tendon reflexes are diminished or absent. Sensory examination is difficult in critically ill patients, but electrophysiologic studies differentiate CIP (reduced sensory and motor nerve amplitudes with normal conduction velocities) from CIM (reduced muscle action potentials with normal nerve conduction, direct muscle inexcitability, and elevated plasma creatine kinase) (40).

##### Preventive Strategies and Rehabilitation

No specific pharmacotherapy reverses CINM. Management is entirely preventive and supportive: rigorous glycemic control (avoiding marked hyperglycemia), aggressive early physical and occupational therapy, minimizing the duration and dosage of continuous neuromuscular blocking agents, and judiciously avoiding systemic corticosteroids (41). Complete functional recovery occurs in about 50% of patients over several months, but severe axonal injury leaves permanent disability in up to one-third of survivors (41).

## Pharmacologic Neuromuscular Blockade</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Pharmacology of Depolarizing and Non-Depolarizing Agents</summary><div class="details-content">

Neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) induce flaccid skeletal muscle paralysis by interrupting neurotransmission at the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor ($N_\text{M}$) of the neuromuscular junction (42).

In the ICU, their use is strictly restricted to three clinical indications: (*a*) facilitating endotracheal intubation, (*b*) controlling shivering during targeted temperature management following cardiac arrest, and (*c*) eliminating severe ventilator dyssynchrony in patients with refractory hypoxemic respiratory failure (moderate-to-severe ARDS) (42).

#### Depolarizing vs Non-Depolarizing Relaxants

*Succinylcholine* is a depolarizing neuromuscular blocker composed of two linked acetylcholine molecules. It binds postsynaptic $N_\text{M}$ receptors, producing persistent depolarization and initial transient fasciculations, followed by flaccid block. Plasma pseudocholinesterase rapidly hydrolyzes the drug, conferring an ultrashort duration ($7\text{–}12\text{ minutes}$).

*Life-Threatening Hyperkalemia Risk:* In normal patients, succinylcholine transiently elevates serum potassium by $0.5\text{ mEq/L}$. However, in denervated muscle, prolonged immobility, muscular dystrophy, or thermal burns $> 48\text{ hours}$ old, immature extrajunctional acetylcholine receptors proliferate across the sarcolemma. Succinylcholine triggers massive, lethal potassium efflux, causing immediate hyperkalemic cardiac arrest.

*Non-Depolarizing Agents:* Rocuronium and cisatracurium act by competitive antagonism of acetylcholine at postsynaptic receptors without depolarization. Rocuronium is cleared primarily by hepatic excretion, leading to prolonged paralysis in cirrhosis. Cisatracurium undergoes spontaneous, non-enzymatic degradation at physiological pH and temperature (Hofmann elimination), making it the paralytic agent of choice in patients with hepatic or acute renal failure (42,43).

### Neuromuscular Monitoring and Reversal Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

Continuous infusions of neuromuscular blockers mandate objective peripheral nerve stimulation monitoring to avoid excessive paralysis and cumulative drug toxicity.

The *Train-of-Four (TOF)* method applies four sequential electrical supramaximal stimuli ($2\text{ Hz}$ over 2 seconds) to a peripheral motor nerve, usually the ulnar nerve (monitoring adductor pollicis contraction) or the facial nerve (monitoring orbicularis oculi).

In the ICU, the therapeutic goal is the suppression of 2 to 3 twitches, titrating NMBA infusions to maintain 1 to 2 palpable twitches out of 4 (representing 80% to 90% receptor occupancy) (42). Complete absence of twitches (0/4) indicates profound overdosing and risks prolonged quadriparesis.

#### Reversal with Neostigmine vs Sugammadex

Active reversal of neuromuscular blockade is indicated upon extubation or when paralysis is no longer required:

- 1. *Cholinesterase Inhibitors (Neostigmine):* Administered at $0.05\text{–}0.07\text{ mg/kg}$ IV. Neostigmine inhibits acetylcholinesterase, allowing acetylcholine to accumulate and outcompete non-depolarizing agents. To prevent muscarinic cholinergic side effects (severe bradycardia, bronchoconstriction, hypersalivation), neostigmine must always be co-administered with glycopyrrolate ($0.01\text{ mg}$ per $1\text{ mg}$ of neostigmine) or atropine. *Neostigmine cannot reverse deep block and requires at least two spontaneous TOF twitches prior to administration*.
- 2. *Selective Relaxant Binding Agent (Sugammadex):* A modified $\gamma$-cyclodextrin that forms a high-affinity $1:1$ molecular inclusion complex with aminosteroid relaxants (rocuronium $\gg$ vecuronium), terminating their neuromuscular action without interacting with cholinergic receptors (44). Dosing is based on real-time block depth: $2\text{ mg/kg}$ IV for moderate block (reappearance of 2 TOF twitches), $4\text{ mg/kg}$ IV for deep block (post-tetanic count 1–2), and $16\text{ mg/kg}$ IV for emergent immediate reversal (e.g., failed airway 3 minutes after $1.2\text{ mg/kg}$ rocuronium) (44).

##### Complications of Prolonged Paralysis and ICU Awareness

Prolonged chemical paralysis carries devastating hazards (42):

- 1. *Awareness Under Paralysis:* Paralytic agents have zero sedative, anxiolytic, or analgesic properties. Inadequate concurrent sedation leads to terrifying conscious paralysis, sensory deprivation, and catastrophic long-term post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).
- 2. *Accelerated Critical Illness Myopathy:* Prolonged chemical denervation combined with systemic inflammation triggers rapid muscle atrophy.
- 3. *Supportive Vulnerabilities:* Inability to blink causes corneal exposure keratopathy and ulceration; lack of muscle tone predisposes to deep vein thrombosis and peripheral nerve compression palsies (e.g., peroneal nerve palsy).

### The Systemic Inflammatory Thread in Movement Disorders</summary><div class="details-content">

A unifying pathophysiologic thread connects the diverse movement disorders presented in this chapter: systemic inflammation.

In critical illness, excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines impairs blood-brain barrier integrity, lowers the seizure threshold, and provokes status epilepticus. Similarly, autoimmune molecular mimicry drives antibodies against the peripheral nerve and motor endplate in Guillain-Barré syndrome and myasthenia gravis, while circulating inflammatory mediators precipitate the muscle wasting and sarcolemmal inexcitability of critical illness polyneuromyopathy.

Recognizing the ubiquitous influence of systemic inflammation reinforces the fundamental principle that restoring neurological and motor function in the ICU requires addressing not only the motor dysfunction itself, but the underlying systemic inflammatory cascade.
