# Oxygen Inhalation

> *I brought fire into your midst, and it consumed you, and reduced you to ashes.*
> 
> — Ezekiel (28:17)

There is a fondness for oxygen that is unmatched by any other treatment modality, as is clearly evident in the unbridled use of oxygen in hospitalized patients. The popularity of oxygen is apparent in emergency rooms, where inhaled oxygen is a knee-jerk response to acute illness, and in ICUs, where a patient who is not connected to a source of oxygen is a rare sight. Oxygen is given without evidence of tissue need, and without regard for the damaging effects of oxygen. This latter disregard is perplexing, since we protect our food from oxygen (with vacuum sealing, tightly sealed plastic containers, and cellophane wrapping), yet we don't have the same consideration for our patients.

This chapter begins with an examination of the indications and effects of oxygen inhalation, and then describes the different delivery systems for oxygen. The final section is devoted to the dark side of oxygen: oxygen-related tissue injury.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Indications:** Consensus guidelines define hypoxemia as $\text{PaO}_2 < 60\text{ mm Hg}$ or $\text{SaO}_2 < 90\%$. Target $\text{SpO}_2$ is 88–92% in patients with hypercapnia risk and 90–94% in other acute medical patients.
- **Arterial Oxygen Content:** The threshold of $\text{SaO}_2 = 90\%$ results in only an 8% drop in arterial oxygen content ($\text{CaO}_2$ drops from 197 to 181 mL/L), whereas transfusion threshold ($\text{Hb} = 7\text{ g/dL}$) represents a 64% decrease (to 92 mL/L). Tissue hypoxia does not typically occur at $\text{SaO}_2 \geq 90\%$.
- **Hypoxemia Tolerance:** Patients with severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease breathing room air with $\text{PaO}_2$ 22–39 mm Hg maintain normal blood lactate levels ($\leq 2.0\text{ mmol/L}$), demonstrating tolerance to severe hypoxemia.
- **Lack of Metabolic Benefit:** Increasing $\text{FiO}_2$ raises $\text{PaO}_2$ but does not increase systemic oxygen consumption ($\text{VO}_2$). Inhaled oxygen does not enhance aerobic metabolism.
- **Systemic Vasoconstriction:** Hyperoxia causes systemic and coronary vasoconstriction via destruction of endothelial nitric oxide by superoxide radicals, decreasing cardiac output and counteracting gains in arterial oxygen delivery.
- **High-Flow Nasal Oxygen (HFNO):** Delivers heated, humidified gas up to 60 L/min and $\text{FiO}_2$ 24–100%. Flushes anatomical dead space, generates modest positive end-expiratory pressure (2–3 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ with closed mouth), increases end-expiratory lung volume, reduces work of breathing, and prevents patient self-induced lung injury (P-SILI).
- **Oxygen Toxicity and ROS:** Reduction of oxygen in mitochondria generates reactive oxygen species (ROS): superoxide ($\text{O}_2^{\bullet-}$), hydrogen peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$), and destructive hydroxyl radicals ($\text{OH}^{\bullet}$), catalyzed by free ferrous iron ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$). Neutrophil respiratory burst generates additional massive oxidant bursts.
- **Antioxidant Defenses:** Kept in check by superoxide dismutase, glutathione peroxidase, selenium, vitamin E (protecting membrane lipids from chain peroxidation), vitamin C, ceruloplasmin, and transferrin. Depletion is common in critical illness.
- **Clinical Directive:** Titrate $\text{FiO}_2$ to the lowest tolerable level to maintain acceptable oxygenation while minimizing tissue hyperoxia and oxidant damage.

## Indications for Oxygen Therapy</summary><div class="details-content">

The first clinical practice guideline for $\text{O}_2$ therapy was published in 1984, stating that supplemental oxygen therapy is appropriate in acute conditions when there is laboratory documentation of an arterial $\text{PO}_2$ ($\text{PaO}_2$) $< 60\text{ mm Hg}$ or an arterial $\text{O}_2$ saturation ($\text{SaO}_2$) $< 90\%$, under the common assumption that tissue hypoxia is present below these values.

This statement is consistent with the consensus definition of hypoxemia as a $\text{PaO}_2 < 60\text{ mm Hg}$ or an $\text{SaO}_2 < 90\%$. Subsequent guidelines recommend a target $\text{SpO}_2$ of 88–92% for patients at risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure, and 90–94% for other patients. The major issue with this recommendation is the assumption that tissue oxygenation is impaired when the $\text{PaO}_2$ falls below 60 mm Hg or the $\text{SpO}_2$ falls below 90%.

### Arterial Oxygen Content

The threshold for $\text{O}_2$ inhalation is associated with a very small change in the arterial $\text{O}_2$ content ($\text{CaO}_2$). This is demonstrated in Figure 25.1, which shows the $\text{CaO}_2$ at the threshold for both $\text{O}_2$ inhalation and red blood cell (RBC) transfusions (two interventions aimed at augmenting tissue oxygenation). The $\text{CaO}_2$ is calculated using the following equation:

$$\text{CaO}_2\text{ (mL/L)} = 1.34 \times \text{Hb} \times \text{SaO}_2 \times 10 \tag{25.1}$$

where 1.34 is the $\text{O}_2$-carrying capacity of hemoglobin (in mL/g), $\text{Hb}$ is the hemoglobin concentration (in g/dL), $\text{SaO}_2$ is arterial oxyhemoglobin saturation (expressed as a decimal rather than a percentage), and the factor of 10 converts mL/dL to mL/L.

#### Clinical Threshold Comparisons

Using normal values for $\text{Hb}$ (15 g/dL) and $\text{SaO}_2$ (0.98) yields a $\text{CaO}_2$ of 197 mL/L. When $\text{SaO}_2$ is reduced to 0.90 (the threshold for $\text{O}_2$ therapy), $\text{CaO}_2$ is 181 mL/L, representing an 8% decrease. In comparison, using a $\text{Hb}$ of 7 g/dL as the threshold for RBC transfusions, the corresponding $\text{CaO}_2$ is 92 mL/L, a 64% decrease from baseline.

![Figure 25.1](images/_page_2_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 25.1** The arterial $\text{O}_2$ content ($\text{CaO}_2$) at the thresholds for $\text{O}_2$ inhalation ($\text{SaO}_2 = 90\%$) and red blood cell transfusions ($\text{Hb} = 7\text{ g/dL}$).

The following deductions can be derived from these comparisons:

1. The threshold for $\text{O}_2$ inhalation corresponds to a minor (8%) change in arterial oxygenation.
2. Tissue oxygenation is not compromised until $\text{CaO}_2$ falls below 92 mL/L (the threshold for RBC transfusions), so it cannot be impaired at the much higher threshold for $\text{O}_2$ inhalation.
3. It follows then that the threshold for $\text{O}_2$ inhalation can be lowered.

### Tolerance to Severe Hypoxemia

Because hypoxemia is usually corrected immediately with supplemental oxygen, limited information exists regarding tolerance to severe hypoxemia. Available data come from observational studies and case reports. Table 25.1 presents data from a study of eight patients with acute exacerbation of COPD who maintained a $\text{PaO}_2$ below 40 mm Hg while breathing room air for at least one hour. The $\text{PaO}_2$, $\text{SaO}_2$, and plasma lactate levels are listed in ascending order of hypoxemia severity. None of the patients demonstrated impaired tissue oxygenation, as indicated by normal lactate concentrations ($\leq 2.0\text{ mmol/L}$). Similar findings have been reported in patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome, indicating that tolerance to severe hypoxemia is an intrinsic capability rather than an adaptation requiring weeks to develop.

#### Hypobaric Hypoxemia

Tolerance to extreme hypoxemia was also demonstrated in healthy individuals placed in a decompression chamber simulating the barometric pressure at the summit of Mount Everest (253 mm Hg, approximately one-third of sea-level pressure). At this pressure, the mean $\text{PaO}_2$ was 30 mm Hg and $\text{SaO}_2$ was 58%, yet mean plasma lactate remained normal at 1.7 mmol/L. Similar values have been recorded directly in climbers near the summit of Mount Everest without supplemental oxygen.

#### Reinhold Messner

A historic demonstration occurred in 1978 when Reinhold Messner and Peter Habeler made the first ascent of Mount Everest (29,029 feet) without supplemental oxygen. Prior to the ascent, experts warned that attempting to summit without oxygen would be fatal, causing loss of consciousness and permanent brain damage above 26,000 feet (the "death zone"). Recognizing this as an untested assumption, Messner tested himself by flying in a depressurized aircraft cabin to Everest's summit altitude while breathing ambient air. Messner remained fully alert and conscious, noting that he was able to talk, think, and perceive clearly.

### Implications

Considerable evidence indicates that $\text{O}_2$ inhalation is not based on demonstrated tissue $\text{O}_2$ needs. Applying oxygen therapy at traditional high thresholds leads to excessive oxygenation, which can provoke oxidant-mediated cell injury.

## Physiological Effects of Oxygen</summary><div class="details-content">

### Metabolic Rate

The rationale for $\text{O}_2$ inhalation is to support aerobic metabolism. However, because aerobic metabolism is not impaired at standard hypoxemia thresholds, supplemental oxygen does not increase oxygen consumption. Figure 25.2 displays the effects of breathing 24% and 28% oxygen on arterial $\text{PO}_2$ ($\text{PaO}_2$) and aerobic metabolic rate ($\text{VO}_2$) in patients with acute exacerbations of COPD. Although $\text{PaO}_2$ rises substantially with each increment in inspired oxygen, $\text{VO}_2$ remains unchanged. Supplemental oxygen inhalation does not promote or enhance aerobic metabolism.

![Figure 25.2](images/_page_4_Figure_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 25.2** Results of a study in patients with severe hypoxemia showing that the increase in arterial $\text{PO}_2$ ($\text{PaO}_2$) from $\text{O}_2$ therapy is not accompanied by an increase in aerobic metabolism ($\text{VO}_2$). Numbers in parentheses are mean values for each measurement. $\text{FiO}_2$ = fraction of inspired oxygen.

### Oxygen as a Vasoconstrictor

Oxygen functions as a vasoconstrictor in all major systemic vascular beds, while acting as a vasodilator in the pulmonary circulation. The primary mechanism of systemic vasoconstriction is the destruction of endothelial nitric oxide, which is oxidized by the superoxide radical. This vasoconstriction leads to several critical clinical consequences:

1. **Coronary Vasoconstriction:** Oxygen induces vasoconstriction in coronary arteries and can significantly decrease coronary blood flow in patients with coronary artery disease.
2. **Reduced Cardiac Output:** Systemic vasoconstriction increases left ventricular afterload and reduces cardiac stroke output, counteracting the ability of arterial oxygenation to augment systemic oxygen delivery.
3. **Capillary Obliteration:** In animal models, hyperoxia can completely obliterate perfused capillary networks in skeletal muscle.

### Teleological Implications

Because oxygen inhalation does not increase aerobic metabolism, oxygen-induced vasoconstriction can be viewed as an intrinsic protective mechanism shielding tissues from superfluous, toxic oxygen. The tissues receive adequate oxygen and restrict surplus delivery.

## Oxygen Delivery Systems</summary><div class="details-content">

A variety of oxygen delivery systems are available for spontaneously breathing patients, as summarized in Table 25.2.

### Flow Rate and FiO2

Supplemental oxygen is supplied as a 100% gas delivered into the nasopharynx or mouth at a set flow rate. The inspired fraction of oxygen ($\text{FiO}_2$) is determined by the balance between the rate of oxygen delivery and the patient's inspiratory flow rate. If the patient's inspiratory flow rate exceeds the delivery rate, ambient air is entrained, diluting the delivered oxygen and lowering $\text{FiO}_2$. 

Normal inspiratory flow during quiet resting breathing is approximately 15 L/min (0.25 L/sec), but can reach 120 L/min in patients with acute respiratory failure. Consequently, low-flow oxygen devices (flows $< 15\text{ L/min}$) cannot deliver reliable or high $\text{FiO}_2$ levels in tachypneic or distressed patients.

### Low-Flow Systems

Low-flow systems deliver oxygen at rates $\leq 15\text{ L/min}$ and are suitable for patients with mild hypoxemia without severe respiratory distress.

- **Low-Flow Nasal Cannula:** Oxygen is delivered via nasal prongs at 1–6 L/min (up to 15 L/min with large-bore cannulas). Humidification is typically added for flows $> 4\text{ L/min}$. $\text{FiO}_2$ during quiet breathing ranges from 24% (at 1 L/min) to approximately 40% (at 6 L/min). Advantages include patient acceptance, comfort, and the ability to eat and speak. The primary limitation is an inability to provide high $\text{FiO}_2$.
- **Standard Simple Face Masks:** Deliver oxygen at 5–10 L/min (a minimum flow of 5 L/min is required to wash out exhaled carbon dioxide). Side ports allow ambient air entrainment and gas exhalation. Maximum achievable $\text{FiO}_2$ is approximately 50–60%. Masks interfere with eating and speaking and are frequently poorly tolerated.

### High-Flow Systems

High-flow systems include reservoir masks, air-entrainment devices, diffuser masks (OxyMask™), and high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO).

#### Reservoir Masks

Reservoir masks incorporate an attached reservoir bag supplied by a continuous oxygen flow. When the bag remains inflated, the patient inhales primarily from the reservoir, achieving higher oxygen concentrations.

In a **non-rebreather mask** (Figure 25.3), a one-way valve between the bag and mask allows oxygen inhalation while preventing exhaled gas from entering the bag. Lateral flaps allow exhaled gas to escape while blocking room air entry during inspiration. Although theoretically capable of 100% $\text{FiO}_2$, practical limits due to air leaks and valve resistance restrict $\text{FiO}_2$ to approximately 60–80%. Non-rebreather masks have fallen out of favor due to risks of $\text{CO}_2$ rebreathing and hypoxemia if the reservoir bag collapses.

![Figure 25.3](images/_page_7_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 25.3** Non-rebreather mask. Oxygen is inhaled from the reservoir bag, and a one-way valve prevents exhaled gas from entering the bag. Flaps on the mask allow exhaled gas to escape while preventing inhalation of ambient air.

#### Air-Entrainment Masks

Air-entrainment masks (Venturi masks) provide a constant and precise $\text{FiO}_2$. As illustrated in Figure 25.4, oxygen flows through a narrowed orifice at high velocity, creating viscous shear forces that entrain room air through adjustable ports (*viscous drag* and *jet mixing*). As oxygen flow increases, entrained air volume increases proportionally, keeping $\text{FiO}_2$ stable across a 24–50% range. Total flow (oxygen plus entrained air) often reaches $\geq 60\text{ L/min}$, exceeding patient peak inspiratory demands.

![Figure 25.4](images/_page_8_Figure_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 25.4** Air-entrainment device. A narrowing at the oxygen inlet creates a high-velocity stream of gas that creates viscous drag, pulling in room air (RA). This jet mixing maintains a constant concentration of inhaled oxygen regardless of changes in total gas flow.

#### The OxyMask™

The OxyMask™ features an open design with a specialized diffuser that focuses an oxygen stream directly toward the nose and mouth. This allows high $\text{FiO}_2$ levels (up to 90%) over a wide range of flows (1 to $> 15\text{ L/min}$) while large open ports allow unhindered escape of exhaled $\text{CO}_2$ without rebreathing.

### High-Flow Nasal Oxygen

High-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO) represents the most significant advance in oxygen delivery. The circuit (Figure 25.5) delivers heated ($37^\circ\text{C}$) and fully humidified gas at flow rates of 40–60 L/min, with an air-oxygen blender allowing independent adjustment of flow and $\text{FiO}_2$ (21–100%).

The physiologic advantages of HFNO include:
1. **Dead Space Washout:** High-velocity flow continuously flushes anatomical dead space in the nasopharynx, clearing exhaled $\text{CO}_2$ and establishing an oxygen reservoir for the next breath.
2. **Positive End-Expiratory Pressure:** Generates low-level PEEP (2–3 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$), primarily when the mouth remains closed.
3. **Increased Lung Volume and Compliance:** Augments end-expiratory lung volume, improving dynamic compliance and markedly diminishing the work of breathing.
4. **Reduction in P-SILI:** Blunts intense inspiratory efforts, minimizing excessive transpulmonary pressure swings that provoke patient self-induced lung injury.

## Oxygen-Induced Cell Injury</summary><div class="details-content">

Oxygen decomposes organic matter through oxidation of carbohydrates, proteins, nucleic acids, and membrane lipids. The chemical derivatives of oxygen, termed reactive oxygen species (ROS), are even more cytotoxic than molecular $\text{O}_2$ and can inflict lethal cellular injury. In critical illness, excess oxygen is a primary instigator of cellular injury rather than a protective shield.

### Oxygen Metabolism

Oxygen metabolism occurs at the terminus of the mitochondrial electron transport chain (cytochrome c oxidase), where four electrons are added sequentially to reduce molecular $\text{O}_2$ to water. Each univalent reduction step yields reactive intermediates (Figure 25.6):

$$\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{e^-} \text{O}_2^{\bullet-} \xrightarrow{e^-} \text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{e^-} \text{OH}^\bullet \xrightarrow{e^-} \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

![Figure 25.6](images/_page_12_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 25.6** Reaction sequence for the univalent chemical reduction of oxygen to water occurring at the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Free radicals possess an unpaired electron (denoted by a superscript dot).

- **Superoxide Radical ($\text{O}_2^{\bullet-}$):** Formed by the addition of a single electron; a reactive free radical that initiates further oxidant cascades.
- **Hydrogen Peroxide ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$):** Not a free radical, but a potent, stable oxidant that readily diffuses across cell membranes throughout tissues.
- **Hydroxyl Radical ($\text{OH}^\bullet$):** Formed via the Fenton reaction catalyzed by ferrous iron ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$). It is the most reactive chemical species known in biology, reacting within 3 molecular diameters of its origin, causing irreversible fragmentation of DNA, protein cross-linking, and lipid peroxidation.

Under basal conditions, $\geq 95\%$ of oxygen is reduced completely to water, and only approximately 3% generates ROS. In systemic inflammation, however, neutrophil activation dramatically escalates ROS production.

### Neutrophil Activation and Respiratory Burst

Activated neutrophils undergo a 20-fold surge in oxygen consumption (the respiratory burst) mediated by NADPH oxidase, which generates massive amounts of superoxide radicals to kill phagocytosed pathogens. Neutrophil myeloperoxidase concurrently reacts $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ with chloride ions to yield hypochlorite ($\text{OCl}^-$), the active oxidizing agent in bleach.

Free radical attacks trigger autocatalytic chain reactions: a radical extracts an electron from a stable lipid molecule, converting it into a fatty acid radical, which in turn attacks adjacent lipids. This chain reaction propagates multiorgan damage long after the inciting infectious stimulus has resolved.

### Antioxidant Protection

Endogenous antioxidants neutralize reactive oxidants through coordinated pathways (Figure 25.7).

![Figure 25.7](images/_page_13_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 25.7** Mechanisms of endogenous and exogenous antioxidants. $\text{SOD}$ = superoxide dismutase, $\text{GSH}$ = reduced glutathione, $\text{GSSG}$ = oxidized glutathione, $\text{Se}$ = selenium.

- **Superoxide Dismutase (SOD):** Catalyzes the dismutation of $\text{O}_2^{\bullet-}$ into $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$. Although it eliminates superoxide, the generation of $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ can perpetuate oxidative injury if peroxide clearance is impaired.
- **Glutathione System:** Reduced glutathione (GSH), a sulfur-containing tripeptide, is the principal intracellular antioxidant. Glutathione peroxidase (a selenium-dependent enzyme) reduces $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ to water:

$$\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 + 2\,\text{GSH} \rightarrow 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{GSSG} \tag{25.2}$$

- **N-Acetylcysteine:** Intracellular glutathione is frequently depleted in critical illness. Because exogenous glutathione does not cross cell membranes effectively, N-acetylcysteine serves as a cell-permeable precursor that restores intracellular cysteine and glutathione reserves.
- **Selenium:** An essential cofactor for glutathione peroxidase. The recommended daily dietary allowance is 55 $\mu\text{g/day}$. Serum levels (normal 0.72–1.33 $\mu\text{mol/L}$) are depressed in sepsis, and supplementation has been studied to bolster antioxidant capacity.
- **Vitamin E ($\alpha$-Tocopherol):** A lipid-soluble chain-breaking antioxidant located in cell membranes. It halts lipid peroxidation by intercepting peroxyl radicals. Normal plasma concentration is 1.0 mg/dL; levels $< 0.5\text{ mg/dL}$ indicate deficiency.
- **Vitamin C (Ascorbate):** Water-soluble free radical scavenger that directly reduces superoxide and hydroxyl radicals while regenerating oxidized vitamin E radicals back to functional tocopherol.
- **Ceruloplasmin and Transferrin:** Limit oxidant generation by sequestering free iron. Ceruloplasmin oxidizes toxic ferrous iron ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$) into ferric iron ($\text{Fe}^{3+}$), which is tightly bound by transferrin, preventing Fenton-mediated hydroxyl radical generation.

### Oxidative Stress

Oxidative stress represents an imbalance where oxidant production overwhelms endogenous antioxidant defenses. In the ICU, extensive inflammation combined with depleted micronutrients creates severe oxidative stress, driving endothelial dysfunction, microvascular thrombosis, and organ failure.

## Pulmonary Oxygen Toxicity</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary oxygen toxicity is an inflammatory lung injury morphologically indistinguishable from acute respiratory distress syndrome, resulting from sustained exposure to high inspired oxygen fractions.

### Species Differences and Human Data

Tolerance to hyperoxia varies across species: laboratory rats succumb to fatal pulmonary injury after 5–7 days of 100% $\text{O}_2$, whereas non-human primates develop lung injury after 7 days. In healthy human volunteers, inhaling 100% $\text{O}_2$ for 6–12 hours causes substernal chest discomfort, acute tracheobronchitis, and decreased vital capacity due to absorption atelectasis (alveolar collapse when soluble oxygen replaces poorly absorbed alveolar nitrogen). Prolonged exposure to 100% $\text{O}_2$ beyond 48–72 hours produces diffuse alveolar damage, capillary endothelial swelling, and proteinaceous alveolar exudates.

### Toxic FiO2 Threshold

The historical safety threshold of $\text{FiO}_2 \leq 60\%$ was derived from healthy volunteer studies. However, in critically ill patients with systemic inflammation and depleted antioxidants, even lower levels of $\text{FiO}_2$ (e.g., $> 40–50\%$) can accelerate pulmonary and systemic oxidant injury. Best clinical practice dictates titrating $\text{FiO}_2$ to the lowest setting necessary to maintain $\text{SpO}_2$ in the target range (88–92% or 90–94%).

### Antioxidant Supplementation

Trials evaluating high-dose antioxidant cocktails (e.g., vitamin C 1000 mg every 8 hours, vitamin E 1000 IU every 8 hours, and selenium 200 $\mu\text{g/day}$) in trauma and surgical ICU patients demonstrated significant reductions in pulmonary complications and duration of mechanical ventilation, reinforcing the concept that restoring antioxidant balance mitigates hyperoxic tissue injury.

## Teleological Perspective: Why Is Oxygen a Vasoconstrictor?</summary><div class="details-content">

The systemic vasoconstrictor response to hyperoxia provides insight into biological regulation. Systemic oxygen delivery ($\text{DO}_2$) is governed by arterial oxygen content ($\text{CaO}_2$) and cardiac output ($\text{CO}$):

$$\text{DO}_2 = \text{CaO}_2 \times \text{CO} \tag{25.3}$$

Inhaling high concentrations of oxygen increases $\text{CaO}_2$ minimally (by dissolving extra $\text{O}_2$ in plasma), but the resulting oxygen-induced systemic vasoconstriction increases afterload and decreases cardiac output. The net result is that systemic oxygen delivery remains unchanged or may actually decrease.

### Autoregulatory Defense

Viewed through a teleological lens, systemic vasoconstriction is a protective autoregulatory defense mechanism designed to prevent toxic tissue hyperoxia and shield cells from oxidative devastation. Liberal and unmonitored oxygen administration directly bypasses this evolutionary defense mechanism, causing harm rather than benefit.
</div></details>
