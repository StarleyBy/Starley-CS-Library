# Venous Thromboprophylaxis

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Epidemiology and Clinical Significance:** Venous thromboembolism (VTE), comprising deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), represents the leading cause of preventable hospital mortality. VTE is common, frequently clinically covert, catastrophic, yet highly correctable.
* ** Virchow's Triad Re-examined:** Venous stasis is not synonymous with immobility. In a closed cardiovascular system with normal cardiac output, venous return equals arterial flow. The predominant inciting trigger for acute hospital-acquired DVT is systemic inflammation and tissue factor activation, not immobility alone.
* **Patient Risk Profiles:** Without thromboprophylaxis, the incidence of DVT reaches 58% in major trauma, 40–80% in elective orthopedic arthroplasty, and 10–40% in general medical and surgical ICU admissions.
* **Parenteral Anticoagulant Prophylaxis:**
  * *Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (LMWH):* Preferred over unfractionated heparin (UFH) due to superior bioavailability (90% vs 20%), predictable kinetics without laboratory monitoring, once- or twice-daily dosing, superior efficacy in trauma and orthopedics, and a 10-fold lower risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT, 0.2% vs 2.6%).
  * *Enoxaparin:* Standard prophylactic dosing is 40 mg SC once daily, or 30 mg SC every 12 hours in high-risk patients. When creatinine clearance falls below $30\text{ mL/min}$, reduce dose to 30 mg once daily or substitute UFH. In morbid obesity ($\text{BMI} \ge 40\text{ kg/m}^2$), increase dose to 40 mg SC every 12 hours.
  * *Dalteparin:* 2,500–5,000 units SC once daily. Requires *no dose reduction in renal insufficiency*.
  * *Unfractionated Heparin (UFH):* 5,000 units SC every 8–12 hours; increase to 7,500 units SC every 8 hours in morbid obesity. Preferred in severe acute renal failure with $\text{CrCl} < 20\text{ mL/min}$.
  * *Fondaparinux:* Synthetic selective factor Xa inhibitor (2.5 mg SC daily); reserved for patients with a history of HIT or undergoing major orthopedic surgery. Contraindicated if $\text{CrCl} < 30\text{ mL/min}$.
* **Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs):** Dabigatran, apixaban, and rivaroxaban are approved for thromboprophylaxis only in elective hip and knee arthroplasty. All DOACs are contraindicated in severe renal insufficiency.
* **Mechanical Prophylaxis:**
  * *Intermittent Pneumatic Compression (IPC):* Highly effective at augmenting venous pulsatility and stimulating endothelial release of endogenous tissue plasminogen activator (tPA). Preferred first-line modality when active bleeding or severe coagulopathy precludes anticoagulation.
  * *Graduated Compression Stockings (GCS):* Ineffective in acute stroke and critically ill patients, offering zero reduction in DVT while causing skin necrosis and lower-extremity ulcerations. Routine use is not recommended.
* **Clinical Special Populations:**
  * *Major Trauma and TBI:* Initiate enoxaparin 12–24 hours post-injury once bleeding is controlled. In traumatic brain injury, start IPC immediately and add LMWH once repeat CT at 24 hours demonstrates stable intracranial hemorrhage.
  * *Cancer Surgery:* Extended post-discharge prophylaxis with LMWH for 4 weeks is mandatory.

## Risk Factors and Clinical Epidemiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), encompassing deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is the single leading cause of preventable in-hospital death. 

Thrombus formation originates predominantly within the deep veins draining the lower extremities (the soleal and gastrocnemius sinuses, femoral veins, and external iliac veins). Over 80% of deep vein thrombi remain completely asymptomatic until detached fragments embolize to the pulmonary arterial circulation, precipitating acute right heart failure, hemodynamic collapse, or sudden death.

### Spectrum of Risk Factors
The development of VTE in hospitalized patients involves multiple overlapping pathophysiologic factors summarized in ([Table 5.1](#table-5-1)).

| **Surgical Procedures** | Major surgery (operative duration >45 minutes under general or spinal anesthesia), particularly hip and knee arthroplasty, bariatric procedures, and cancer resections. |
| **Major Trauma** | Traumatic brain injury (TBI), fractures of the spine, pelvis, hip, or femur; severe soft-tissue crush injury and extensive burns. |
| **Malignancy** | Metastatic adenocarcinomas (particularly pancreatic, gastric, and ovarian carcinomas), hematologic malignancies, and systemic cytotoxic chemotherapy. |
| **Acute Medical Illness** | Acute ischemic stroke with paretic extremity, decompensated congestive heart failure with peripheral edema, acute sepsis, COVID-19, and systemic inflammatory states. |
| **ICU-Specific Factors** | Mechanical ventilation, neuromuscular paralysis, femoral and internal jugular central venous catheters, septic or cardiogenic shock, high-dose vasopressor therapy, and platelet transfusions. |
| **Patient-Specific Traits** | Prior episode of VTE, family history of unprovoked thrombosis, inherited thrombophilias (Factor V Leiden, prothrombin gene mutation, antithrombin III deficiency), age >70 years, obesity, pregnancy, and the postpartum state. |

### Major Surgical Risk
Major operative procedures (anesthesia duration $> 45\text{ minutes}$) account for approximately one-third of all hospital-associated VTE fatalities. The postoperative prothrombotic state is initiated by intraoperative tissue factor and thromboplastin release, systemic surgical inflammation, microvascular trauma, and subsequent bed rest. 

The thrombotic vulnerability persists well beyond the period of hospitalization, continuing for 6 to 12 weeks postoperatively. The risk is extreme following total joint arthroplasty, bariatric surgery, and extended pelvoabdominal resections for malignancy.

### Major Trauma
Trauma patients carry the highest absolute risk of VTE among all hospital admissions; without prophylaxis, the objective incidence of DVT exceeds 58% ([Fig. 5.1](#fig-5-1)). Predisposing factors include massive release of tissue factor from soft-tissue and skeletal injuries, direct venous wall laceration, circulating fat emboli from long bone fractures, and obligatory immobilization.

The trauma conditions carrying the highest thrombotic risk are:
1. Traumatic brain injury and spinal cord injuries.
2. Pelvic ring fractures and acetabular disruptions.
3. Femoral and tibial shaft fractures.

### Acute Medical Illness
Hospitalization for acute medical disease is associated with an 8-fold increase in VTE incidence. Although the relative percentage of VTE in any individual medical patient is lower than in major trauma or orthopedics ([Fig. 5.1](#fig-5-1)), medical admissions constitute the vast majority of hospital beds. Consequently, *medical patients account for 50% to 75% of all fatal in-hospital pulmonary embolisms*.

The highest-risk medical cohorts include patients with acute ischemic stroke complicated by hemiplegia, active metastatic neoplasms, and right ventricular failure with bilateral dependent lower-extremity edema.

## Parenteral Anticoagulant Prophylaxis</summary><div class="details-content">

Subcutaneous administration of parenteral anticoagulants represents the principal pharmacological strategy for preventing hospital-acquired VTE.

### Contraindications to Anticoagulation
Before initiating pharmacological thromboprophylaxis, patients must be screened for hemorrhagic risk:
* **Absolute Contraindications:** Active intracranial hemorrhage, active bleeding within the spinal canal, and clinically important systemic hemorrhage (defined as blood loss accompanied by a decrease in systolic blood pressure $\ge 20\text{ mm Hg}$, an acute fall in hemoglobin $\ge 2\text{ g/dL}$, or transfusion requirement of $\ge 2\text{ units of packed red blood cells}$).
* **Relative Contraindications:** Recent acute intracranial trauma, active peptic ulceration with recent hemorrhage, severe thrombocytopenia ($\text{platelets} < 50 \times 10^9\text{/L}$), or severe baseline coagulopathy ($\text{INR} > 2.0$).
* **Non-Contraindications:** Menstrual bleeding is not a contraindication to prophylactic anticoagulation.

### Unfractionated Heparin (UFH)
Heparin is an unbranched, highly sulfated glycosaminoglycan. Native "unfractionated" heparin consists of a heterogeneous mixture of polysaccharide chains ranging in molecular weight from 3,000 to 30,000 Daltons (mean 15,000 Daltons).

#### Mechanism of Action
Heparin binds circulating antithrombin III (AT) via a unique high-affinity pentasaccharide sequence, inducing a conformational change that accelerates AT inhibition of activated factor X (Xa) by a factor of 1,000 ([Fig. 5.2](#fig-5-2)). 

Additionally, polysaccharide chains longer than 18 saccharide units (molecular weight $> 5,400\text{ Da}$) bridge AT directly to thrombin (factor IIa), forming a ternary complex that inactivates thrombin. In UFH, the anti-IIa inhibitory effect exceeds the anti-Xa effect by approximately 10:1.

#### Pharmacokinetic Limitations
Because of its extensive negative charge, UFH binds nonspecifically to positively charged plasma proteins, endothelial cells, and macrophages:
* Nonspecific plasma protein binding limits bioavailability to 15–30% at low doses, generating unpredictable anticoagulant responses.
* Rapid saturable endothelial clearance yields a nonlinear, dose-dependent serum half-life (30 to 90 minutes).
* Heparin binds to platelet factor 4 (PF4), creating an immunogenic neoepitope that triggers IgG antibody formation and **heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)** in 2.6% of exposed surgical patients.

#### Dosing and Adjustments
* **Standard Dosing:** 5,000 units SC every 8 or 12 hours. The every-8-hour regimen is preferred in higher-risk surgical patients, though clinical trials demonstrate comparable protection with both schedules.
* **Morbid Obesity:** In patients with $\text{BMI} \ge 40\text{ kg/m}^2$, expanded volume of distribution reduces drug efficacy; 7,500 units SC every 8 hours restores thromboprophylactic protection.
* **Renal Clearance:** UFH is cleared by endothelial reticuloendothelial mechanisms and requires no dosage alteration in renal failure, making it the preferred parenteral agent in dialysis patients or acute kidney injury with $\text{CrCl} < 20\text{ mL/min}$.

### Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (LMWH)
Low-molecular-weight heparins are manufactured by controlled enzymatic or chemical depolymerization of native heparin, yielding shorter fragments with an average molecular weight of 4,500 Daltons.

#### Pharmacological Advantages of LMWH
1. **High Bioavailability:** Shorter polysaccharide chains exhibit minimal nonspecific binding to plasma proteins and endothelial surfaces, producing bioavailability exceeding 90%.
2. **Predictable Dose-Response:** Elimination occurs strictly via first-order renal filtration with a stable half-life (4 to 6 hours), yielding linear kinetics that eliminate the need for routine laboratory monitoring.
3. **Preferential Anti-Xa Activity:** Only 25–50% of LMWH fragments are long enough ($> 18\text{ saccharides}$) to bridge thrombin and AT. Consequently, LMWH exhibits an anti-Xa to anti-IIa activity ratio between 3:1 and 4:1 ([Fig. 5.2](#fig-5-2)).
4. **Markedly Reduced HIT Incidence:** LMWH forms significantly fewer multimolecular PF4-heparin complexes, reducing the clinical incidence of HIT by 10-fold compared to UFH (0.2% vs 2.6%).

#### Clinical Regimens
* **Enoxaparin (Lovenox):** 
  * Standard regimen: 40 mg SC once daily, or 30 mg SC every 12 hours in major trauma and high-risk surgical patients.
  * **Renal adjustment:** Enoxaparin relies completely on renal excretion. In severe renal failure ($\text{CrCl} < 30\text{ mL/min}$), drug accumulation provokes major bleeding; the dose must be reduced to 30 mg SC once daily, or switched to UFH.
  * **Morbid obesity:** 40 mg SC every 12 hours for $\text{BMI} \ge 40\text{ kg/m}^2$.
* **Dalteparin (Fragmin):** 
  * Standard regimen: 2,500 to 5,000 units SC once daily.
  * **The Dalteparin Advantage:** Clinical pharmacokinetic trials (such as the DIRECT trial) confirm that dalteparin does not bioaccumulate in critically ill patients with severe renal dysfunction. *No dosage reduction is needed in renal insufficiency*.

#### Anti-Factor Xa Therapeutic Monitoring
Although routine laboratory testing is unwarranted, measuring peak anti-factor Xa levels (drawn 4 hours after subcutaneous injection) is recommended in clinical scenarios with altered pharmacokinetics: morbid obesity ($\text{BMI} \ge 40\text{ kg/m}^2$), cachexia ($\text{weight} < 45\text{ kg}$), and fluctuating renal failure in major trauma. The validated target prophylactic range is a peak anti-Xa level of $0.2\text{ to }0.5\text{ IU/mL}$.

### Fondaparinux
Fondaparinux is a fully synthetic pentasaccharide identical to the antithrombin-binding sequence of heparin. It exclusively inhibits factor Xa without neutralizing thrombin (anti-Xa to anti-IIa ratio $> 100:1$) ([Fig. 5.2](#fig-5-2)).

* **Pharmacokinetics:** 100% subcutaneous bioavailability, elimination half-life of 17 hours, allowing once-daily dosing (2.5 mg SC daily).
* **Renal Elimination:** Exclusively excreted by the kidneys. Reduce dose to 1.5 mg daily if $\text{CrCl}$ is $35\text{--}50\text{ mL/min}$; completely contraindicated if $\text{CrCl} < 30\text{ mL/min}$.
* **Clinical Indications:** Proven efficacy in orthopedic arthroplasty and acute medical illness. Crucially, fondaparinux does not interact with platelet factor 4 and serves as an off-label prophylactic option in patients with a confirmed or suspected history of HIT.

### Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)
The DOACs comprise the direct thrombin inhibitor dabigatran and three direct factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban). Unlike warfarin, DOACs exert immediate competitive enzyme inhibition within 1 to 4 hours of ingestion and require no routine coagulation monitoring.

#### Clinical Restrictions in Critical Care
1. **Approved Indications:** Currently, professional guidelines (ASH, ACCP) endorse DOACs for thromboprophylaxis *only in elective total hip and total knee arthroplasty*.
2. **Severe Renal Failure:** All DOACs undergo renal elimination (dabigatran 80%, rivaroxaban 33%, apixaban 27%). They are strictly contraindicated in severe renal insufficiency ([Table 5.3](#table-5-3)).
3. **ICU Limitations:** Unpredictable gastrointestinal absorption, altered plasma protein binding, impossibility of rapid reversal in emergent hemorrhage, and interactions with P-glycoprotein inducers make DOACs unsuitable for unselected medical and surgical ICU patients.

### Warfarin Obsolescence
Warfarin inhibits the hepatic synthesis of vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X, protein C, protein S). It has fallen out of favor for inpatient thromboprophylaxis due to its delayed onset of action (5 to 7 days), requirement for continuous INR monitoring, numerous food and drug interactions, and transient early hypercoagulability secondary to rapid protein C depletion.

## Mechanical Prophylaxis</summary><div class="details-content">

Mechanical thromboprophylaxis reduces venous stasis by accelerating venous blood velocity without exposing patients to hemorrhagic hazards.

### Graduated Compression Stockings (GCS)
Elastic compression stockings exert graduated circumferential pressure (highest at the ankle, $\approx 18\text{ mm Hg}$, decreasing to $8\text{ mm Hg}$ at the upper thigh).

#### Clinical Inefficacy and Complications
Despite widespread historical utilization, evidence-based appraisal does not support routine GCS:
* **The CLOTS Trial:** The landmark CLOTS trial in acute stroke patients demonstrated that graduated compression stockings *did not reduce the incidence of DVT or mortality*, but caused a significant 4-fold increase in skin breakdown, leg ulcers, and subcutaneous necrosis.
* **Medical ICU Patients:** Meta-analyses show zero independent reduction in VTE events from GCS in medical ICU admissions.
* **Recommendation:** GCS is no longer recommended as monotherapy for thromboprophylaxis in surgical, trauma, or ICU patients.

### Intermittent Pneumatic Compression (IPC)
Intermittent pneumatic compression devices utilize inflatable circumferential cuffs fitted over the calves or calves and thighs, cycling inflation pressures of 35 to 55 mm Hg for 10 to 15 seconds per minute.

#### Mechanisms of Protection
1. **Hemodynamic Pulsatility:** Rapid cuff inflation compresses deep venous sinusoids, emptying venous pooling and augmenting peak femoral vein flow velocities by up to 200%.
2. **Endothelial Fibrinolysis:** Cyclical vessel compression stimulates vascular endothelial cells to synthesize and discharge endogenous **tissue plasminogen activator (tPA)** into the systemic circulation, promoting endogenous thrombolysis.

#### Clinical Indications
IPC represents the primary prophylactic modality in patients with active bleeding, high bleeding risk, or acute intracranial pathology. In extreme-risk patients (major trauma, orthopedic arthroplasty), combining IPC with LMWH provides superior thrombotic protection compared to either modality alone.

### Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) Filters
Prophylactic placement of retrievable IVC filters in high-risk trauma patients without documented DVT is strongly discouraged by contemporary guidelines. Large randomized trials (such as PREPIC) demonstrate that while IVC filters trap emboli from reaching the lungs, they significantly increase the 2-year incidence of lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis, carry risks of caval perforation and strut fracture, and confer zero overall survival benefit.

## Clinical Recommendations in Specific Settings</summary><div class="details-content">

Optimal thromboprophylaxis requires tailoring modalities to specific surgical and medical disease categories:

### General, Abdominal, and Pelvic Surgery
* **Risk Stratification:** Calculate the Caprini Score. Patients undergoing major surgery for intra-abdominal or pelvic malignancy are categorized as high risk.
* **Regimen:** LMWH (enoxaparin 40 mg SC once daily) combined with intraoperative IPC.
* **Extended Prophylaxis:** In patients undergoing major laparotomy or pelvic resection for malignancy, *pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis must be continued for 4 weeks post-discharge*. Over 40% of post-surgical VTE events occur after hospital discharge.

### Orthopedic Arthroplasty
Total hip arthroplasty (THA) and total knee arthroplasty (TKA) carry a baseline unprophylaxed DVT rate of 40% to 80%.
* **First-Line Regimens:** LMWH (enoxaparin 30 mg SC q12h or 40 mg SC daily), DOACs (rivaroxaban 10 mg daily or apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily), or fondaparinux (2.5 mg daily).
* **Aspirin Transition:** Following an initial 5 to 7 days of LMWH or DOAC therapy, low-dose aspirin (81 to 100 mg daily) can be substituted to complete the course in low-bleeding-risk patients.
* **Duration:** Minimum of 10 to 14 days for TKA; minimum of 35 days for THA.

### Bariatric Surgery
Morbidly obese patients undergoing bariatric surgery face high thrombotic morbidity alongside altered drug pharmacokinetics:
* **Regimen:** Enoxaparin at an intensified dose of 40 mg SC every 12 hours (or UFH 7,500 units SC every 8 hours) initiated preoperatively and combined with IPC.
* **Post-Discharge:** Extended prophylaxis for 10 to 14 days post-discharge is advised.

### Neurosurgery
* **Craniotomy and Intracranial Procedures:** Mechanical prophylaxis with IPC is the exclusive modality perioperatively. Once the risk of intracranial hemorrhage is deemed minimal by postoperative imaging (typically 24 to 48 hours post-op), LMWH may be added in high-risk tumor resections.

### Cardiac Surgery
* **Uncomplicated Cardiac Surgery:** Routine mechanical prophylaxis with IPC is recommended until fully ambulatory.
* **Complicated ICU Course:** In patients with prolonged mechanical ventilation, low cardiac output state, or open chest, add LMWH (enoxaparin 30–40 mg SC daily) once surgical bleeding ceases.

### Major Trauma
* **Extremity and Solid Organ Trauma:** Enoxaparin 30 mg SC every 12 hours, initiated within 12 to 24 hours of injury once active bleeding has stabilized, combined with bilateral IPC.
* **Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI):** Apply bilateral IPC immediately. Perform repeat non-contrast head CT at 24 hours; if hemorrhage is stable or improved, initiate enoxaparin 30 mg SC every 12 hours.
* **Spine Fractures and Spinal Cord Injury:** Combine IPC with enoxaparin 30 mg SC every 12 hours once primary spine surgery is completed or acute bleeding is excluded.

### Acute Medical Illness and Medical ICU
* **Indication:** All hospitalized medical patients with reduced mobility and acute medical illness (sepsis, heart failure, respiratory failure, acute infection).
* **Regimen:** LMWH (enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily) for the duration of acute hospitalization. Extended post-discharge prophylaxis is not recommended for general medical patients because randomized trials demonstrated excess bleeding without a net reduction in mortality.

## A Final Word: The Riddle of Venous Stasis</summary><div class="details-content">

### Riddle Me This
If physical immobility is truly the primary driving cause of deep vein thrombosis, why is DVT not rampant among bedridden nursing home residents with chronic limb contractures, and why is lifelong prophylactic anticoagulation never prescribed for stable paraplegic individuals?

### Virchow's Triad Re-appraised
The standard assumption that bed rest causes DVT is an overextension of Rudolf Virchow's 1856 triad (stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability). 

However, *physical immobility is not synonymous with venous stasis*:
1. The cardiovascular system is a closed hydrodynamic loop. According to the physical law of conservation of mass, volumetric blood flow through any vascular cross-section must equal total circuit flow.
2. In an immobilized patient with a normal cardiac output, systemic venous return precisely equals left ventricular cardiac output. Blood does not pool motionless in the veins; circulation continues unobstructed.

### Inflammation as the Primary Thrombotic Trigger
What actually precipitates acute deep vein thrombosis in hospitalized medical and surgical patients is **systemic and local inflammation**. The inflammatory and hemostatic cascades are molecularly coupled:
* Proinflammatory cytokines (interleukin-1, tumor necrosis factor) induce endothelial cell expression of tissue factor and selectins while downregulating thrombomodulin.
* Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) activate factor XII and aggregate platelets.

This explains why in spinal cord injury resulting in traumatic paraplegia, the incidence of DVT is extreme (up to 60%) *only during the initial 3 to 4 weeks following trauma*—a window of acute tissue injury and intense systemic inflammation. Once acute inflammation subsides, the risk of DVT returns to baseline, and thromboprophylaxis is discontinued despite permanent lifelong immobility.

Appreciating that inflammation—rather than immobility alone—drives thrombosis is critical: it prevents the reflexive, unnecessary administration of long-term prophylactic anticoagulants to chronically bedridden individuals who lack acute systemic inflammatory triggers.
