# Cardiogenic Shock

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiogenic shock represents a severe state of end-organ hypoperfusion and cellular dysoxia caused by primary cardiac pump failure:
* **Epidemiological Scope and Mortality:** Acute myocardial infarction accounts for approximately two-thirds of cases, while non-ischemic decompensation, acute myocarditis, postcardiotomy failure, and Takotsubo cardiomyopathy constitute the remainder. Despite prompt revascularization and advanced mechanical circulatory support, in-hospital mortality remains approximately 50%.
* **Mechanical Complications of Infarction:** In acute myocardial infarction, pump failure typically requires loss of $\ge 40\%$ of left ventricular functional myocardium. Severe shock can also arise from acute mechanical catastrophes (papillary muscle rupture with acute mitral regurgitation [7%], ventricular septal rupture [4%], and free wall rupture with tamponade [1%]), which predominantly occur within the initial 24 hours.
* **Hemodynamic and Microcirculatory Derangements:** Classic profile features a low cardiac index ($CI < 2.2\text{ L/min/m}^2$), elevated filling pressures ($PAWP > 18\text{ mm Hg}$), and elevated vascular resistance ($SVRI > 30\text{ Wood Units}$). However, 30% of patients exhibit normal filling pressures, and 20% to 40% manifest systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) with vasodilation and severe microcirculatory decoupling.
* **Pharmacological Support and Inotropes:** Norepinephrine is the first-line vasopressor ($5\text{--}30\text{ }\mu\text{g/min}$), offering lower arrhythmic risk than epinephrine or dopamine. Inotropic support is dominated by dobutamine ($3\text{--}20\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$), while milrinone and levosimendan are constrained by vasodilatory hypotension. All pharmacological agents increase myocardial oxygen consumption and ventricular workload.
* **Mechanical Circulatory Support (MCS):** When shock is refractory to initial pharmacotherapy, rapid transition to MCS is vital: intra-aortic balloon counterpulsation (IABP) unloads the left ventricle; transvalvular axial flow pumps (Impella) deliver active forward output ($2.5\text{--}5.5\text{ L/min}$); and veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) provides full biventricular and respiratory support ($5\text{--}6\text{ L/min}$), though requiring left ventricular venting to prevent hydrostatic pulmonary edema.

## Pathophysiology and Etiology</summary><div class="details-content">

The human circulatory system operates with an automatic stroke pump executing approximately 100,000 cardiac cycles daily, circulating 8,000 liters through over 60,000 miles of vascular channels. Primary pump failure spans a spectrum from chronic compensated heart failure to acute cardiogenic shock and catastrophic cardiac arrest:
* **Cellular Definition:** Cardiogenic shock is defined as primary cardiac pump failure resulting in inadequate systemic oxygen delivery and impaired cellular oxidative metabolism.
* **Prognosis:** Approximately 50% of patients do not survive despite aggressive intensive care and revascularization.

### Acute Myocardial Infarction
Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) accounts for approximately two-thirds of all cases of cardiogenic shock, developing in 5% to 10% of AMI presentations:
* **Extensive Left Ventricular Infarction:** Accounts for roughly 80% of infarct-related shock cases, typically requiring acute necrosis or severe ischemia of at least 40% of left ventricular myocardial mass.
* **Right Ventricular Infarction:** Accounts for less than 5% of cases, producing marked right ventricular dilation and systemic venous congestion.

### Acute Mechanical Complications
Mechanical disruption of cardiac structures can precipitate sudden catastrophic shock:
* **Papillary Muscle Rupture (7%):** Causes acute, severe mitral regurgitation due to loss of leaflet coaptation during ventricular systole.
* **Ventricular Septal Rupture (4%):** Creates an acute, high-volume left-to-right shunt, flooding the pulmonary circulation while depriving the systemic arterial tree of forward flow.
* **Free Wall Rupture (1%):** Results in rapid hemopericardium, acute pericardial tamponade, and electromechanical dissociation.
* *Clinical Timing:* Contrary to historical impressions that mechanical ruptures are late sequelae, they typically occur within the initial 24 hours of acute infarction.

### Special Clinical Syndromes
* **Dynamic Left Ventricular Outflow Tract (LVOT) Obstruction:** In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or severe hyperdynamic hypovolemia, high-velocity systolic flow across a narrowed LVOT creates a Venturi suction effect. This draws the anterior mitral valve leaflet toward the septum (systolic anterior motion [SAM]), obstructing ejection. Diagnosed by continuous-wave Doppler echocardiography measuring peak subaortic pressure gradients. *Therapeutic Caveat:* Inotropes and vasodilators are strictly contraindicated; therapy requires volume repletion and nonvasodilating beta-blockers (e.g., metoprolol, nadolol) to slow heart rate and expand diastolic ventricular filling.
* **Postcardiotomy Shock:** Occurs in 2% to 5% of open-heart surgical procedures, originating from prolonged myocardial ischemia, ischemia-reperfusion stunning, or inadequate intraoperative cardioprotection.
* **Peripartum Cardiomyopathy:** Manifests as idiopathic systolic failure in late pregnancy or within the initial months postpartum, carrying a mortality of 7% to 20% in high-risk demographics (age $> 30\text{ years}$, multiparity).
* **Extrinsic Obstructive Shock:** Extrinsic mechanical impediments to filling or ejection (massive pulmonary embolism, pericardial tamponade, tension pneumothorax) produce hemodynamics identical to cardiogenic shock and require emergent physical decompression.

## Hemodynamic Profiles and the Microcirculation</summary><div class="details-content">

Primary pump failure provokes a distinct constellation of invasive hemodynamic alterations.

<span id="fig-16-2"></span>

![Hemodynamic alterations in cardiac pump failure](images/_page_3_Figure_0.jpeg)

*Figure 16.2. Classic hemodynamic response pattern in acute pump failure: marked depression of cardiac output accompanied by reciprocal elevation of ventricular filling pressures and systemic vascular resistance.*

### Classical Hemodynamic Pattern
The physiological response to primary pump failure is governed by compensatory neurohumoral reflex pathways:
* **Output and Pressures:** Cardiac output falls precipitously (cardiac index typically $< 2.2\text{ L/min/m}^2$, normal $2.4\text{--}4.0\text{ L/min/m}^2$). Ventricular filling pressures rise dramatically ($CVP$ in right heart failure, $PAWP$ in left heart failure).
* **Compensatory Afterload:** Systemic vascular resistance increases secondary to intense sympathetic outflow and activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis.
* **Cardiogenic Shock vs Decompensated Heart Failure:** Cardiogenic shock is differentiated from simple congestive heart failure by the presence of end-organ hypoperfusion (oliguria, altered mentation) and cellular dysoxia (elevated plasma lactate).

### Atypical Presentations and Right Heart Failure
* **Right Ventricular Shock:** Characterized by elevated $CVP$, increased $CVP/PAWP$ ratio, right ventricular dilation, and preserved left ventricular intrinsic contractility. Severe RV distension shifts the interventricular septum leftward, impeding LV diastolic filling and compounding shock. Mortality in isolated RV shock is lower than in LV failure.
* **Normovolemic Cardiogenic Shock:** Up to 30% of patients with infarct-related cardiogenic shock present without elevated cardiac filling pressures ($PAWP \le 18\text{ mm Hg}$).
* **Normotensive Shock:** Approximately 5% of patients maintain systolic blood pressure $\ge 90\text{ mm Hg}$ through extreme compensatory vasoconstriction despite severe tissue hypoperfusion.

### Systemic Inflammation and Microcirculatory Derangement
* **Systemic Inflammatory Response (SIRS):** Extensive myocardial necrosis triggers systemic cytokine release and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) activation in 20% to 40% of AMI shock patients, resulting in paradoxical vasodilation, lower $SVR$, and significantly higher mortality.
* **Microcirculatory Decoupling:** Sublingual videomicroscopy demonstrates profound capillary rarefaction and perfusion heterogeneity that operates independently of systemic macrohemodynamic restoration. Normalizing macrocirculatory blood pressure and cardiac output fails to improve survival unless microcirculatory perfusion is successfully reestablished.

## Hemodynamic Monitoring and Resuscitation Goals</summary><div class="details-content">

Effective management of cardiogenic shock requires invasive hemodynamic surveillance.

### Invasive Monitoring Modalities
* **Pulmonary Artery Catheters (PAC):** PAC thermodilution remains the definitive tool for cardiogenic shock, continuously measuring cardiac index ($CI$), pulmonary artery wedge pressure ($PAWP$), systemic vascular resistance index ($SVRI$), and mixed venous oxygen saturation ($SvO_2$). Modern consensus statements reaffirm that PAC guidance confers measurable outcome benefits in cardiogenic shock.
* **Perfusion and Oxygenation Markers:** Microcirculatory surveillance relies on serial measurements of the venoarterial $\text{PCO}_2$ gap ($< 6\text{ mm Hg}$), hourly urine output ($> 0.5\text{ mL/kg/hr}$), $SvO_2$ ($> 50\%$), and serial plasma lactate clearance ($< 2\text{ mmol/L}$).

### Optimizing Left Ventricular Filling Pressures
When cardiac filling pressures are not elevated upon initial presentation, cautious fluid administration can augment stroke volume:
* **Colloid Osmotic Pressure Limits:** Normal plasma colloid osmotic pressure (COP) is approximately 28 mm Hg, but drops to 20 to 25 mm Hg in critical illness due to hypoalbuminemia.
* **Target Wedge Pressure:** To augment cardiac output along the Starling curve without precipitating hydrostatic alveolar flooding, the optimal target $PAWP$ is $18\text{--}20\text{ mm Hg}$ (or $19\text{--}20\text{ mm Hg}$).

## Pharmacotherapy: Vasopressors and Inotropes</summary><div class="details-content">

Pharmacotherapy serves as a temporizing bridge to definitive revascularization, structural repair, or mechanical assist device placement.

### Vasopressor Therapy: Norepinephrine
Vasopressors are required when hypotension threatens coronary and cerebral perfusion:
* **Agent of Choice:** Norepinephrine is the first-line vasopressor in cardiogenic shock. Comparative clinical trials confirm that norepinephrine is associated with significantly lower rates of tachyarrhythmias and reduced mortality compared with dopamine or epinephrine.
* **Mechanism:** Potent $\alpha_1$-adrenergic vasoconstriction restores mean arterial pressure, while mild $\beta_1$-adrenergic inotropic activity preserves stroke volume in the lower dose range.
* **Dosing:** Continuous infusion starting at $5\text{ }\mu\text{g/min}$ (or $0.05\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$), titrating by increments every 5 minutes. The usual therapeutic range is $5\text{--}30\text{ }\mu\text{g/min}$ ($0.05\text{--}0.5\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$).
* *Exception:* In dynamic LVOT obstruction, pure $\alpha_1$-agonists (phenylephrine) are indicated to induce reflex bradycardia and avoid inotropic aggravation of subaortic narrowing.

### Inotropic Therapy
Because vasopressors increase left ventricular afterload without reversing myocardial contractile depression, inotropic agents are added to augment forward stroke volume.

### The Dilemma of Myocardial Oxygen Consumption
All positive inotropes and vasopressors carry a physiological penalty:
* Vasopressors elevate left ventricular afterload, increasing wall stress.
* Positive inotropes increase cardiac contractility and heart rate, escalating myocardial oxygen consumption in an already ischemic or failing heart.
* Consequently, medical therapy cannot heal a failing myocardium. Failure to achieve rapid hemodynamic stabilization warrants immediate escalation to mechanical circulatory support.

## Mechanical Circulatory Support (MCS)</summary><div class="details-content">

Mechanical circulatory support is indicated for refractory cardiogenic shock as a "bridge to recovery" (myocardial healing post-AMI or postcardiotomy) or a "bridge to decision/intervention" (surgical revascularization, ventricular assist device, or heart transplantation).

### Intra-Aortic Balloon Counterpulsation (IABP)
The oldest form of MCS, introduced in 1960, the IABP remains the most widely accessible device:
* **Operational Mechanics:** A polyurethane balloon is inserted via the femoral artery into the descending thoracic aorta, with its tip positioned just distal to the left subclavian artery. Rapidly shuttles 35 to 40 mL of low-density helium gas triggered by the surface ECG (R wave) or arterial pressure wave.
* **Hemodynamic Actions:**
  1. *Early Diastolic Inflation:* Occurring immediately after aortic valve closure, balloon inflation augments peak diastolic pressure, significantly increasing coronary perfusion and mean arterial pressure.
  2. *End-Diastolic Deflation:* Occurring immediately prior to ventricular systole during isovolumic contraction, rapid deflation creates a vacuum effect, dramatically reducing left ventricular afterload and stroke work.
* **Clinical Limitations:** Randomized trials have demonstrated no reduction in all-cause mortality with routine IABP use. It is strictly contraindicated in aortic regurgitation or aortic dissection.
* **Complications:** Limb ischemia from femoral thrombosis or vessel trauma occurs in up to 10% of cases. Fever occurs in 50% of patients, though documented bacteremia is present in only 15%.

### Microaxial Transvalvular Pumps (Impella)
Catheter-based axial-flow Archimedes-screw pumps placed across the aortic valve:
* **Mechanism:** Inserted percutaneously via the femoral artery and positioned across the aortic valve into the left ventricle (with an atraumatic pigtail). A miniature electric motor drives a high-speed impeller, continuously aspirating blood from the left ventricle and expelling it into the ascending aorta.
* **Hemodynamic Flow:** Generates non-pulsatile forward flow rates of $2.5\text{--}5.5\text{ L/min}$, actively unloading the left ventricle and suppressing left ventricular end-diastolic pressure.
* **Contraindications:** Severe aortic stenosis, mechanical aortic valve prostheses, and left ventricular thrombus.
* **Complications:** Vascular injury and major bleeding occur more frequently than with IABP. Hemolysis develops in 5% to 10% of cases due to extreme shear stress. Clinical trials show comparable mortality to IABP in unselected AMI shock.

### Veno-Arterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (VA-ECMO)
VA-ECMO provides comprehensive mechanical circulatory and respiratory support:
* **Circuit Dynamics:** Deoxygenated blood is drained from the right atrium or inferior vena cava via a large femoral venous cannula, propelled through an external centrifugal pump, passed through a polymethylpentene membrane oxygenator/heat exchanger (warmed to 37° C), and returned under arterial pressure into the femoral artery.
* **Biventricular and Pulmonary Support:** Generates non-pulsatile full circulatory support at flow rates of $5\text{--}6\text{ L/min}$. VA-ECMO is the preferred modality when cardiogenic shock is compounded by severe respiratory failure.
* **Left Ventricular Distension Problem:** Retrograde flow up the descending aorta significantly increases left ventricular afterload. In severe ventricular failure where the aortic valve fails to open, this leads to acute ventricular stasis, thrombus formation, and hydrostatic pulmonary edema. To mitigate afterload, an unloading strategy ("venting") is frequently combined with VA-ECMO, utilizing an IABP, Impella (ECPELLA), or surgical septostomy.
* **Complications:** Substantial adverse event profile: major bleeding (41%), nosocomial sepsis (30%), lower limb ischemia (17%), compartment syndrome (10%), ischemic stroke (6%), and lower limb amputation (5%).
* **Survival:** The Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) registry reports an overall survival to hospital discharge of approximately 42% for VA-ECMO in cardiogenic shock.

### Summary Principles in Cardiogenic Shock
1. **Primary Goal:** Shock therapy is designed to "buy time" for myocardial recovery or definitive surgical/percutaneous revascularization.
2. **Early Mechanical Transition:** Pharmacotherapy escalates myocardial oxygen consumption. Refractory shock demands immediate transition to mechanical support before multiorgan failure supervenes ("bridge to nowhere").
3. **Device Selection:** VA-ECMO is mandatory if profound respiratory failure coexists; otherwise, device choice depends on local institutional expertise, with no single platform proving universally superior.
