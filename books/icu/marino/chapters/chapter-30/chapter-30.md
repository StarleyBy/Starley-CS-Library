# Discontinuing Mechanical Ventilation

> *The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak.*
> 
> — Matthew (26:41)

Liberation from mechanical ventilation is frequently rapid and uncomplicated, yet approximately one out of every three intensive care patients encounters significant difficulty transitioning to unassisted spontaneous breathing. Prolonged mechanical ventilation multiplies the hazards of airway trauma, nosocomial infection, and muscular atrophy, whereas premature extubation precipitates respiratory exhaustion and hazardous crash reintubations.

This chapter examines the evidence-based protocol for discontinuing mechanical ventilation, covering readiness screening, the execution and interpretation of spontaneous breathing trials, the pathophysiology of weaning failure, and the prevention of post-extubation airway failure.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Ventilator-Induced Diaphragm Dysfunction (VIDD):** Controlled mechanical ventilation without spontaneous patient effort rapidly produces diaphragmatic proteolysis and muscle fiber atrophy, reducing contractile force by roughly 50% within 3 days. Preserving patient-triggered breaths mitigates VIDD.
- **Supportive Strategies:** Daily sedation holidays, light sedation targets, avoidance of continuous benzodiazepine infusions, and early progressive physical mobilization significantly shorten the duration of mechanical ventilation.
- **Readiness Criteria:** Patients are screened daily for: $\text{SpO}_2 \geq 90\%$ on $\text{FiO}_2 \leq 50\%$ with $\text{PEEP} \leq 8\text{ cm H}_2\text{O}$; normal or baseline $\text{PaCO}_2$; hemodynamic stability without high-dose vasopressors; and an arousable, cooperative neurological state.
- **Spontaneous Breathing Trial (SBT):** Performed either on the ventilator (with continuous positive airway pressure of 0–5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ or low-level pressure support of 5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) or using a T-piece circuit for 30–120 minutes. A rapid shallow breathing index ($\text{RSBI} = \text{RR}/V_T$) $< 105\text{ breaths/min/L}$ is a classic predictor of liberation success.
- **Cardiovascular Failure During Weaning:** Cardiovascular dysfunction accounts for up to 40% of failed weaning attempts. Transition to spontaneous breathing generates negative intrathoracic pressure, abruptly increasing left ventricular afterload and precipitating occult ischemia or acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema. A concurrent fall in central venous oxygen saturation ($\text{ScvO}_2$) signals weaning-induced circulatory failure.
- **Airway Protection:** Prior to extubation, assess protective airway reflexes. The "white card test" (moistening a card held 1–2 cm from the tube during coughing) confirms effective cough strength.
- **Cuff-Leak Test and Laryngeal Edema:** In patients at high risk for post-extubation laryngeal edema (prolonged intubation, female gender, large tube size), perform a cuff-leak test. An expired leak volume $< 110\text{ mL}$ identifies a 7-fold increased risk of post-extubation stridor.
- **Steroid Prophylaxis:** When a cuff-leak is absent or deficient, administer intravenous methylprednisolone (20 mg IV every 4 hours, initiated at least 12 hours prior to extubation). This regimen reduces post-extubation laryngeal edema 7-fold and halves reintubation rates.
- **Post-Extubation Support:** High-risk patients should be extubated directly onto prophylactic noninvasive ventilation (NIV) or high-flow nasal oxygen (HFNO).

## Ventilator Strategies to Support Liberation</summary><div class="details-content">

Proactive management during the course of mechanical ventilation preserves respiratory muscle integrity and accelerates liberation.

### Patient-Triggered Breaths and Diaphragm Protection

The diaphragm is an involuntary muscle that undergoes rapid disuse atrophy when mechanical ventilation completely assumes the work of breathing. Controlled mechanical ventilation (where patient effort is suppressed by sedation or neuromuscular blockade) induces *ventilator-induced diaphragm dysfunction* (VIDD). 

Experimental studies demonstrate that 3 days of fully controlled ventilation produces a ~ 50% reduction in diaphragmatic contractile force across all stimulation frequencies (Figure 30.1). 

![Figure 30.1](images/_page_1_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 30.1** Diaphragmatic contractile force at varying stimulation frequencies during spontaneous breathing compared to 3 days of assisted ventilation (spontaneous trigger preserved) and controlled ventilation (no patient effort).

Maintaining patient-triggered breaths (via assist-control or pressure support modes) maintains muscle fiber architecture and attenuates VIDD.

### Early Mobilization and Physiotherapy

Protracted bed rest induces generalized musculoskeletal deconditioning and critical illness myopathy. Protocolized progressive physiotherapy (in-bed cycling, sitting at the edge of the bed, standing, and ambulation while intubated) shortens mechanical ventilation duration, enhances functional independence, and accelerates ICU discharge.

### Sedation Practices

Continuous deep sedation and sustained benzodiazepine infusions (e.g., midazolam) delay awakening, double the duration of mechanical ventilation, and impede extubation. Modern clinical guidelines emphasize:
1. Maintaining a light level of sedation where the patient is readily arousable and interactive.
2. Executing daily spontaneous awakening trials (sedation holidays).
3. Utilizing non-benzodiazepine sedatives (propofol or dexmedetomidine) when sedation is indicated.

## The Spontaneous Breathing Trial</summary><div class="details-content">

Ventilator-dependent patients should be screened daily for readiness to undergo a spontaneous breathing trial (SBT).

### TABLE 30.1 Parameters for a Spontaneous Breathing Trial

| Readiness Screening Criteria | Predictors of Success During SBT |
| :--- | :--- |
| **1. Oxygenation:** $\text{SpO}_2 \geq 90\%$ on $\text{FiO}_2 \leq 50\%$ | **1. Tidal Volume ($V_T$):** 4–6 mL/kg PBW |
| **2. PEEP:** $\leq 8\text{ cm H}_2\text{O}$ | **2. Respiratory Rate (RR):** $< 40\text{ breaths/min}$ |
| **3. Ventilation:** $\text{PaCO}_2$ normal or at baseline | **3. Rapid Shallow Breathing Index ($\text{RR}/V_T$):** 60–105 bpm/L |
| **4. Hemodynamics:** Stable, minimal/no vasopressors | **4. Maximal Inspiratory Pressure ($P_{I\text{max}}$):** $> -20\text{ cm H}_2\text{O}$ |
| **5. Neuromuscular:** Awake, arousable, cooperative | |

*Note:* PBW = predicted body weight. Serial trends during the trial carry far greater prognostic value than isolated spot measurements at onset.

### Methods of Conducting an SBT

- **On-Ventilator SBT:** The patient remains attached to the ventilator circuit on CPAP (0–5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) or low-level pressure support (5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$). This allows continuous real-time monitoring of tidal volume, minute ventilation, and respiratory rate. Although low-level pressure support is traditionally thought to overcome circuit resistance, measured work of breathing during PSV at 5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ does not differ significantly from unassisted breathing (Figure 30.2).
- **T-Piece Circuit:** The patient is disconnected from the ventilator and connected to an open T-piece adapter supplied with high-flow humidified oxygen (Figure 30.3). While realistic, it precludes continuous electronic volume and rate monitoring.

![Figure 30.2](images/_page_3_Figure_6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 30.2** Inspiratory work of breathing (in Joules per liter) during spontaneous breathing trials conducted with and without pressure support ventilation (PSV at 5 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$), and 1 hour post-extubation.

![Figure 30.3](images/_page_4_Picture_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 30.3** High-flow T-piece circuit for spontaneous breathing trials. High flow rates facilitate oxygenation and clear exhaled $\text{CO}_2$.

Comparative randomized trials show equivalent extubation success rates between on-ventilator and T-piece techniques. On-ventilator trials are preferred for convenience and monitoring safety.

### Assessing Trial Success vs. Failure

The standard SBT duration is 30 to 120 minutes. Approximately 80% of patients who tolerate a 2-hour trial can be successfully extubated. Objective indicators of failure include:
- Severe diaphoresis, facial grimacing, or psychomotor agitation.
- Tachypnea ($\text{RR} > 35\text{ breaths/min}$) or rapid shallow breathing ($\text{RSBI} > 105$).
- Abdominal paradox (inward motion of the abdominal wall during inspiration).
- Worsening gas exchange ($\text{SpO}_2 < 90\%$ or acute rise in $\text{PaCO}_2 > 8\text{ mm Hg}$).
- Hemodynamic instability (heart rate $> 140\text{ bpm}$, acute hypertension, or hypotension).

#### Tachypnea vs. Anxiety

Tachypnea during an SBT can stem from psychological panic rather than ventilatory exhaustion. Examining tidal volume differentiates the two: anxiety generates hyperventilation (high respiratory rate with large tidal volume), whereas true ventilatory muscle failure produces rapid, shallow breathing (high respiratory rate with small tidal volume). Judicious low-dose opioids blunt dyspnea and anxiety without suppressing respiratory drive.

## Causes of Weaning Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

When a patient repeatedly fails spontaneous breathing trials, reversible cardiac and neuromuscular factors must be investigated.

### Cardiovascular Dysfunction

Occult cardiovascular dysfunction is responsible for up to 40% of all weaning failures. Mechanical ventilation with positive intrathoracic pressure assists the failing left ventricle by decreasing afterload. Transition to unassisted breathing abruptly reintroduces negative pleural pressure swings (up to –15 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$), which markedly increases transmural left ventricular systolic wall tension (afterload) and increases venous return.

In patients with borderline systolic reserve or coronary artery disease, this acute workload increase precipitates myocardial ischemia, left ventricular diastolic stiffening, and acute pulmonary edema. 

![Figure 30.4](images/_page_7_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 30.4** Serial mixed venous oxygen saturation ($\text{SvO}_2$) during successful and failed spontaneous breathing trials. A progressive decline in $\text{SvO}_2$ reflects compromised cardiac output.

A progressive decline in central venous ($\text{ScvO}_2$) or mixed venous ($\text{SvO}_2$) oxygen saturation during an SBT strongly signals an inability of cardiac output to meet systemic and diaphragmatic metabolic demands (Figure 30.4).

#### Management of Weaning-Induced Heart Failure

1. **Pre-weaning Diuresis:** In patients with hypervolemia or elevated filling pressures, targeted diuresis significantly improves weaning success.
2. **Afterload Reduction:** Intravenous nitroglycerin or ACE inhibitors mitigate the afterload surge during spontaneous breathing.
3. **Noninvasive Post-Extubation CPAP:** Applying positive airway pressure post-extubation neutralizes negative pleural pressure and supports left ventricular function.

### Respiratory Muscle Weakness

- **Critical Illness Polyneuromyopathy:** Axonal neuropathy and acute myopathy develop in severe sepsis and prolonged critical illness, leaving patients quadriplegic or unable to sustain unassisted breathing.
- **Electrolyte Depletion:** Hypophosphatemia and hypomagnesemia impair diaphragmatic ATP synthesis and contractile force; both should be aggressively corrected.
- **Maximal Inspiratory Pressure ($P_{I\text{max}}$):** Measured via an occluded airway manometer. Normal values are –120 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ in men and –84 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ in women. Values weaker than –20 to –30 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$ predict ventilatory failure.

## Extubation and Post-Extubation Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

Extubation is the definitive physical removal of the endotracheal tube. Work of breathing typically increases immediately post-extubation due to upper airway resistance (Figure 30.2), which should not be mistaken for failure if the patient remains comfortable.

### Airway Protection Assessment

Successful extubation requires intact airway protective reflexes and secretion clearance:
- **Cough Strength (White Card Test):** Hold a white card 1–2 cm from the proximal end of the endotracheal tube and instruct the patient to cough. Visible moisture droplets confirm adequate expiratory muscle velocity.
- **Secretion Volume:** Patients requiring suctioning more frequently than every 2 hours have higher rates of extubation failure.

### Post-Extubation Laryngeal Edema and Stridor

Translaryngeal intubation produces pressure necrosis and edema of the vocal cords and subglottic tissues. Symptomatic post-extubation laryngeal edema occurs in 1–10% of unselected patients and up to 30% of high-risk individuals (intubation $> 6\text{ days}$, females, large tube size, or reintubation).

#### The Cuff-Leak Test

The cuff-leak test assesses the patency of the peritubal space. During volume-control ventilation with a stable baseline tidal volume, the cuff is completely deflated:
- **Negative Test (Low Risk):** Expired leak volume $> 110\text{ mL}$ (or $> 15\%$ of delivered tidal volume). Confirms sufficient airway caliber around the tube with a 95% negative predictive value.
- **Positive Test (High Risk):** Expired leak volume $< 110\text{ mL}$. Predicts a 7-fold increase in the risk of post-extubation stridor and reintubation.

#### Prophylactic Corticosteroid Therapy

In patients with a failed or deficient cuff-leak test, intravenous corticosteroids must be initiated **at least 12 hours prior to planned extubation** to allow for genomic anti-inflammatory suppression. Administering steroids minutes before extubation is ineffective.

![Figure 30.5](images/_page_10_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 30.5** Impact of prophylactic intravenous methylprednisolone (20 mg IV every 4 hours initiated 12 hours prior to extubation) on post-extubation laryngeal edema and reintubation rates in high-risk patients.

The standard regimen is methylprednisolone 20 mg IV every 4 hours for 4 doses (starting 12 hours prior to extubation). This protocol reduces laryngeal edema 7-fold and halves reintubation rates (Figure 30.5).

### Post-Extubation Stridor and Reintubation

Post-extubation stridor is a harsh, high-pitched inspiratory sound audible without a stethoscope, caused by dynamic collapse of the extrathoracic airway under negative inspiratory pressure. Stridor typically manifests within 30 minutes of extubation.

- **Emergency Management:** If stridor is accompanied by respiratory distress, intercostal retractions, or falling oxygen saturation, **reintubate immediately**. Nebulized racemic epinephrine and intravenous steroids have no proven efficacy in established severe post-extubation edema.
- **Prophylactic NIV and HFNO:** High-risk patients (e.g., hypercapnic COPD, morbid obesity, congestive heart failure) should be extubated directly to prophylactic noninvasive ventilation or high-flow nasal oxygen. However, applying NIV as a "rescue" measure once post-extubation respiratory failure is established increases mortality and should be avoided.

## Clinical Perspective: Avoiding Delays in Liberation</summary><div class="details-content">

Delays in discontinuing mechanical ventilation unnecessarily prolong ICU stay and expose patients to iatrogenic harm. 

Liberation should be viewed as an active, daily resuscitation goal: maintain light sedation, initiate early physical rehabilitation, screen daily for readiness, execute protocolized spontaneous breathing trials, aggressively optimize cardiac afterload, and pretreat with steroids whenever laryngeal edema is suspected.
</div></details>
