# Acute Heart Failure(s)

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute decompensated heart failure represents a spectrum of clinical syndromes defined by ventricular filling or contractile failure that produces pulmonary congestion, systemic hypoperfusion, or both:
* **Classification and Phenotypes:** Differentiated by left ventricular ejection fraction into heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF, $\text{LVEF} \ge 50\%$; accounting for $\ge 50\%$ of modern presentations), mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF, $\text{LVEF } 41\text{--}49\%$), and reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF, $\text{LVEF} \le 40\%$). End-diastolic volume index ($> 97\text{ mL/m}^2$) rather than filling pressure distinguishes systolic from diastolic dysfunction.
* **Right Heart Failure and Interdependence:** Right ventricular failure (from pulmonary hypertension, RV infarction, or ARDS) manifests with elevated $CVP$ ($CVP \ge PAWP$). Leftward diastolic shift of the interventricular septum directly impedes left ventricular filling via ventricular interdependence, causing secondary left ventricular diastolic dysfunction.
* **Venous Congestion and the Cardiorenal Syndrome:** Progressive heart failure transitions from pulmonary venous congestion to forward stroke failure. Renal impairment in acute heart failure correlates not with low cardiac output, but with elevated renal venous pressure reducing transglomerular perfusion pressure; early decongestion restores renal function.
* **Biomarkers:** B-type natriuretic peptide ($\text{BNP} < 100\text{ pg/mL}$) and $\text{NT-proBNP} < 300\text{ pg/mL}$ provide high negative predictive value to rule out heart failure. However, elevated levels occur non-specifically across critical illness (sepsis, renal failure, pulmonary embolism, ARDS).
* **Acute Management Priorities:**
  * *Hypertensive Acute Heart Failure:* Vasodilators (intravenous nitroglycerin $5\text{--}100\text{ }\mu\text{g/min}$ using non-PVC tubing) are first-line to rapidly lower preload and afterload.
  * *Normotensive Decongestion:* Intravenous loop diuretics (furosemide 40–80 mg IV bolus or equivalent daily outpatient dose) administered within 1 hour. Diuretics must be used cautiously in HFpEF and "flash" ischemic pulmonary edema, where acute myocardial stiffening rather than total-body hypervolemia predominates.
  * *Noninvasive Positive-Pressure Ventilation:* CPAP or BiPAP rapidly reduces left ventricular transmural afterload and clears pulmonary edema.

## Classification and Mechanisms of Heart Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

The prevalence of heart failure continues to escalate, affecting over 6.7 million adults in the United States and contributing to 1 in every 8 deaths. Modern critical care classifies heart failure by the compromised phase of the cardiac cycle (systole vs diastole) and the involved ventricle (left vs right).

### Systolic vs Diastolic Dysfunction
Cardiac performance depends on ventricular contractility and passive chamber compliance:
* **Ventricular Function Curves:** In systolic heart failure, the Frank-Starling curve shifts downward and to the right, showing decreased stroke volume despite elevated end-diastolic pressure ($EDP$).
* **Diastolic Pressure-Volume Curves:** Ventricular compliance represents the change in volume per unit change in pressure:

$$\text{Compliance} = \frac{\Delta EDV}{\Delta EDP} \tag{18.1}$$

* **Volume as the Discriminator:** Because ventricular end-diastolic pressure rises in both systolic and diastolic failure, $EDP$ cannot differentiate between them. Diastolic failure is characterized by a normal or reduced end-diastolic volume index ($\text{EDV} \le 97\text{ mL/m}^2$), whereas systolic failure features chamber dilation ($\text{EDV} > 97\text{ mL/m}^2$).

### Ejection Fraction Categories
Left ventricular ejection fraction ($LVEF$) represents the fraction of end-diastolic volume ejected per stroke:

$$EF = \left(\frac{SV}{EDV}\right) \times 100 \tag{18.2}$$

Normal resting $LVEF$ is $\ge 55\%$, though $\ge 50\%$ is adopted as the clinical threshold in heart failure guidelines:

<span id="tab-18-1"></span>

### Table 18.1. Classification of Heart Failure by Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF)

| Heart Failure Classification | Acronym | LVEF Range | Primary Underlying Pathophysiology |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Preserved Ejection Fraction** | HFpEF | $\ge 50\%$ | Diastolic dysfunction (impaired compliance/relaxation) |
| **Mildly Reduced Ejection Fraction** | HFmrEF | $41\text{--}49\%$ | Mild systolic dysfunction with variable diastolic impairment |
| **Reduced Ejection Fraction** | HFrEF | $\le 40\%$ | Moderate-to-severe systolic contractile failure |

*Diastolic Dysfunction Primacy:* HFpEF accounts for at least 50% of all clinical heart failure presentations. Impaired ventricular distensibility limits diastolic filling, reducing stroke volume despite a preserved ejection fraction. Key etiologies include concentric ventricular hypertrophy, myocardial ischemia, restrictive fibrosis, and external pericardial constraint.

### Right-Sided Heart Failure
Right ventricular (RV) failure typically arises secondary to pulmonary arterial hypertension, acute pulmonary embolism, severe ARDS, or inferior/RV myocardial infarction:
* **Pericardial Constraint:** Because the right ventricle is thin-walled and highly compliant, acute dilation is constrained by the rigid pericardium. Central venous pressure ($CVP$) and jugular venous distension do not rise until pericardial stretch limits are exceeded.
* **Interventricular Interdependence:** The right ventricular cavity is normally two-thirds the size of the left ventricle. In acute RV failure, the dilating right ventricle flattens and displaces the interventricular septum leftward during diastole. This septal shift compresses the left ventricular cavity, elevating left ventricular filling pressure and depressing stroke output.
* **Hemodynamic Clues:** A $CVP$ that equals or exceeds the pulmonary artery wedge pressure ($CVP \ge PAWP$) strongly suggests RV failure or pericardial tamponade. Normal right ventricular ejection fraction ($RVEF$) is $> 45\%$.

## Pathophysiology and Clinical Manifestations</summary><div class="details-content">

Decompensated heart failure evolves through three sequential stages of hemodynamic compensation and subsequent decompensation.

<span id="fig-18-3"></span>

![Changes in cardiac performance during progressive stages of left-sided heart failure](images/_page_5_Figure_0.jpeg)

*Figure 18.3. Hemodynamic stages of progressive left ventricular failure: Stage 1 (isolated rise in filling pressure), Stage 2 (compensatory tachycardia maintaining cardiac output), Stage 3 (decompensation with fall in cardiac output).*

### Progressive Stages of Heart Failure
1. **Stage 1 (Isolated Congestion):** The initial sign is an elevation in left ventricular end-diastolic pressure ($PAWP$). Stroke volume remains preserved, but high pulmonary capillary pressure produces dyspnea.
2. **Stage 2 (Compensated Output):** Stroke volume declines, but compensatory baroreceptor-mediated sinus tachycardia preserves total cardiac output.
3. **Stage 3 (Overt Decompensation):** Stroke volume falls further and cardiac output declines despite maximal tachycardia. This transition marks overt clinical decompensation.

### Neurohumoral Compensatory Cascade
* **Natriuretic Peptides:** Elevated myocardial wall tension stimulates myocyte release of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) and atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), promoting natriuresis, diuresis, and systemic vasodilation.
* **Sympathetic Activation:** Arterial baroreceptor unloading triggers intense central sympathetic outflow, producing peripheral arteriolar vasoconstriction, tachycardia, and stimulation of renal renin release.
* **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS):** Renal hypoperfusion stimulates renin secretion, producing angiotensin II (potent vasoconstrictor and efferent glomerular regulator), aldosterone (stimulating distal renal sodium retention), and arginine vasopressin (ADH). While initially sustaining arterial pressure, sustained neurohumoral activation accelerates ventricular remodeling, induces endothelial dysfunction, and worsens pulmonary congestion.

### Venous Congestion and the Cardiorenal Syndrome
The clinical presentation of acute decompensated heart failure is dominated by venous congestion rather than forward hypoperfusion:
* **Cardiogenic Pulmonary Edema:** High pulmonary capillary hydrostatic pressure causes transudation into the interstitium and alveoli, presenting with severe dyspnea, orthopnea, and crackles.
* **The Congestive Cardiorenal Paradigm:** Worsening renal function in acute heart failure was historically attributed to reduced cardiac output and renal hypoperfusion. However, clinical studies confirm that renal dysfunction correlates directly with central venous congestion. Elevated renal venous pressure increases renal interstitial pressure, collapsing intrarenal veins and narrowing the transglomerular arteriovenous gradient. Unless systemic hypotension ($\text{MAP} < 70\text{ mm Hg}$) is present, improving renal function requires relieving venous congestion through aggressive diuresis.

### Diagnostic Biomarkers
B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) is synthesized as a prohormone (proBNP) and cleaved into biologically active BNP and inert N-terminal proBNP (NT-proBNP):
* **Clearance and Adiposity:** Cleared via renal excretion and neutral endopeptidase (neprilysin) degradation. Adipocytes express natriuretic peptide clearance receptors; consequently, circulating BNP levels are paradoxically lower in obese patients.
* **Diagnostic Thresholds:** Heart failure is highly improbable when $\text{BNP} < 100\text{ pg/mL}$ or $\text{NT-proBNP} < 300\text{ pg/mL}$ (negative predictive value $> 95\%$).

## Acute Management: Vasodilators and Diuretics</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute management of non-shock decompensated heart failure is guided by baseline arterial pressure and volume status.

### Hypertensive Acute Heart Failure: Vasodilator Therapy
When acute pulmonary edema is accompanied by elevated arterial pressure, immediate reduction of ventricular afterload and preload with intravenous vasodilators takes precedence over diuretics.

<span id="tab-18-3"></span>

### Table 18.3. Intravenous Vasodilator Regimens in Acute Heart Failure

| Vasodilator Agent | Infusion Regimen | Clinical Profile and Precautions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Nitroglycerin** | Start at $5\text{ }\mu\text{g/min}$; increase by $5\text{ }\mu\text{g/min}$ every 5 min. Typical effective range: $5\text{--}100\text{ }\mu\text{g/min}$ (maximum recommended: $200\text{ }\mu\text{g/min}$). | First-line vasodilator ($\text{venodilation} > \text{arteriodilation}$). Reduces preload and coronary ischemia. Up to 80% adsorbs onto PVC tubing (requires glass/polyethylene sets). Tachyphylaxis develops within 24 hours. Prolonged high-dose infusions carry risks of propylene glycol solvent toxicity and methemoglobinemia. |
| **Sodium Nitroprusside** | Start at $0.2\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$; titrate upward every 5 min. Effective range: $2\text{--}5\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$ (maximum: $10\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$). | Balanced arterial and venous dilator. Used when resistant to nitroglycerin. Contraindicated in renal/hepatic failure and coronary artery disease (causes coronary steal). Risk of cyanide toxicity with infusions $> 3\text{ }\mu\text{g/kg/min}$; co-administration of sodium thiosulfate (500 mg) binds free cyanide. |

*Mechanism of Action:* Vasodilators rapidly unload the left ventricle, increasing stroke volume while lowering pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ($PAWP$) and alleviating alveolar flooding. Intravenous furosemide stimulates an acute, transient neurohumoral vasoconstrictor spike; hence, diuretic administration should be delayed until blood pressure is controlled by vasodilators.

### Normotensive Acute Heart Failure: Diuretic Protocols
In normotensive patients presenting with volume overload, loop diuretics represent the cornerstone of therapy. Early administration (within 1 hour of hospital presentation) is associated with reduced in-hospital mortality.

<span id="tab-18-4"></span>

### Table 18.4. Diuretic Dosing Protocols in Acute Heart Failure

| Diuretic Agent | Clinical Administration Protocol |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Furosemide** | • Diuretic-Naïve: Initial dose of $40\text{ mg}$ IV (normal renal function) or $60\text{--}80\text{ mg}$ IV (renal impairment).<br>• Chronic Outpatient Therapy: Initial IV dose equal to total daily outpatient oral dose.<br>• Escalation: If urine output $< 1\text{ liter}$ at 2 hours, double the dose up to a maximum of $200\text{ mg}$ IV bolus.<br>• Maintenance: Effective dose administered twice daily. (Oral bioavailability is 50%; oral dose = $2 \times\text{ IV dose}$). |
| **Bumetanide & Torsemide** | • Equipotent loop diuretics with higher oral bioavailability ($> 80\%$).<br>• Equivalence ratio: $40\text{ mg}$ furosemide $\approx 1\text{ mg}$ bumetanide $\approx 20\text{ mg}$ torsemide. |
| **Metolazone** | • Thiazide-like diuretic inhibiting distal tubule sodium reabsorption; overcomes loop diuretic resistance.<br>• Dose: $2.5\text{--}10\text{ mg}$ PO once daily, administered 2 to 3 hours prior to loop diuretic.<br>• High risk of severe hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia. |

### Continuous-Infusion Furosemide Regimens
For patients refractory to intermittent boluses or exhibiting massive volume overload:
1. Administer a loading bolus of $80\text{ mg}$ IV.
2. If estimated $\text{GFR} \ge 30\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2$: Initiate continuous infusion at $5\text{ mg/hr}$. If urine output is $< 100\text{ mL/hr}$, repeat bolus and increase to $10\text{ mg/hr}$, titrating up to a maximum of $40\text{ mg/hr}$.
3. If estimated $\text{GFR} < 30\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2$: Initiate continuous infusion at $20\text{ mg/hr}$. If urine output is $< 100\text{ mL/hr}$, repeat bolus and increase to $40\text{ mg/hr}$.

### Noninvasive Positive-Pressure Ventilation (NIPPV)
Application of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP 5–10 $\text{cm H}_2\text{O}$) or bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) is highly effective in acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema:
* Increases mean intrathoracic pressure, decreasing left ventricular transmural systolic pressure and afterload.
* Augments forward stroke output while expanding collapsed fluid-filled alveoli and improving gas exchange, reducing intubation rates.

## Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT)</summary><div class="details-content">

Following acute stabilization and decongestion, evidence-based neurohumoral therapies must be initiated or resumed prior to hospital discharge to reduce rehospitalization and mortality in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).

<span id="tab-18-5"></span>

### Table 18.5. Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT) for HFrEF

| Pharmacological Class | Representative Agents | Starting Dose | Target Maintenance Dose |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **RAAS / Neprilysin Inhibition** | Sacubitril / Valsartan (ARNI) | $24/26\text{ mg}$ BID (normotensive)<br>$49/51\text{ mg}$ BID (hypertensive) | $97/103\text{ mg}$ BID (titrated over 4–8 weeks) |
| **Beta-Adrenergic Blockers** | Carvedilol<br>Metoprolol succinate extended-release | $3.125\text{ mg}$ BID<br>$12.5\text{--}25\text{ mg}$ once daily | $25\text{--}50\text{ mg}$ BID<br>$200\text{ mg}$ once daily |
| **SGLT2 Inhibitors** | Dapagliflozin or Empagliflozin | $10\text{ mg}$ once daily | $10\text{ mg}$ once daily |
| **Mineralocorticoid Antagonists** | Spironolactone<br>Eplerenone | $12.5\text{--}25\text{ mg}$ once daily<br>$25\text{ mg}$ once daily | $25\text{ mg}$ once daily<br>$50\text{ mg}$ once daily |

### Pharmacological Highlights and Safety Precautions
* **Angiotensin Receptor-Neprilysin Inhibitor (ARNI):** Sacubitril/valsartan is superior to ACE inhibitors in promoting reverse ventricular remodeling. Contraindicated with prior angioedema; requires a 36-hour washout when switching from an ACE inhibitor.
* **Beta-Blockers:** Initiate only after complete clinical euvolemia is achieved. Abrupt discontinuation precipitates rebound hyperadrenergic crisis and ventricular dysrhythmias.
* **SGLT2 Inhibitors:** Proven to reduce cardiovascular death and hospitalizations in both HFrEF and HFpEF, regardless of diabetic status. Risk of euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis.
* **Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonists (MRA):** Concomitantly reduce mortality; contraindicated if serum potassium $> 5.0\text{ mEq/L}$ or estimated $\text{GFR} < 30\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2$.

### Cautions Regarding Overzealous Diuresis
1. Pulmonary edema is frequently precipitated by acute ischemia-induced diastolic stiffening ("flash pulmonary edema") rather than true intravascular hypervolemia.
2. Intravenous furosemide acutely depresses cardiac output by combining a neurohumoral afterload surge with preload depletion.
3. In HFpEF (50% of heart failure admissions), over-diuresis collapses ventricular filling, producing severe prerenal azotemia and systemic hypoperfusion.
