# Anemia and Red Blood Cell Transfusions

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

Anemia is exceedingly common in the intensive care unit, affecting over 95% of patients after one week of critical illness. Historically, transfusion of packed red blood cells (PRBCs) was routinely performed to restore hemoglobin to arbitrary target levels. Contemporary critical care has fundamentally challenged this practice:
* **The Transfusion Dilemma:** Up to 50% of ICU patients receive blood transfusions, with 90% of transfusions administered to non-bleeding patients solely to treat low laboratory hemoglobin concentrations.
* **Dilutional Pseudoanemia:** Because hemoglobin and hematocrit measure concentration rather than absolute circulating mass, volume expansion and the recumbent position falsely lower these values. A shift to the supine position drops hematocrit by 4.1% (a 420 mL fluid shift), and a $20\text{ mL/kg}$ saline bolus lowers hematocrit by 4.2%—both mimicking the loss of 1 unit of whole blood ($500\text{ mL}$) without any change in red cell mass.
* **Physiological Adaptation to Anemia:** Whole blood viscosity decreases non-linearly with hemodilution. This reduction in vascular resistance, combined with the non-Newtonian shear-thinning behavior of blood, augments cardiac output and systemic flow. Together with a compensatory expansion in microvascular oxygen extraction ($\text{O}_2\text{ER}$ rising up to 50%), systemic oxygen uptake ($\text{VO}_2$) remains rock-stable until hematocrit drops below 10% (hemoglobin $\approx 3\text{ g/dL}$).
* **Transfusions Do Not Improve Tissue Oxygenation:** Transfusion of 1 to 2 units of PRBCs in normovolemic patients increases arterial oxygen delivery ($\text{DO}_2$), but tissue oxygen consumption ($\text{VO}_2$) remains unchanged due to a reciprocal decline in oxygen extraction. Microvascular storage lesions (depletion of 2,3-DPG and ATP, loss of erythrocyte deformability, and scavenging of endogenous nitric oxide) impair microvascular flow.
* **Restrictive Transfusion Strategy:** Multicenter trials demonstrate that maintaining a restrictive transfusion threshold ($\text{Hb} < 7\text{ g/dL}$ in general ICU and septic shock patients, and $\text{Hb} < 8\text{ g/dL}$ in cardiovascular or orthopedic surgery patients) is safe, reduces transfusion requirements by 41%, and avoids transfusion-related morbidity.
* **Transfusion Risks Outweigh Benefits:** In large-scale critical care registries (45 studies, 272,596 patients), PRBC transfusion was an independent predictor of death in 17 of 18 observational studies, with risks exceeding benefits in 42 of 45 investigations.

## Anemia in the Intensive Care Unit</summary><div class="details-content">

Anemia is observed in approximately two-thirds of patients upon ICU admission, with its prevalence escalating to 97% by day 7 of critical illness.

### The Dilutional Fallacy of Concentration Measures
* **Absolute Mass vs. Concentration:** True oxygen carrying capacity is governed by total circulating red cell mass (normally $26\text{ mL/kg}$ in men, $24\text{ mL/kg}$ in women). Because direct measurement requires radioisotope tagging ($^{51}\text{Cr}$), clinical practice relies on concentration-based metrics: hemoglobin and hematocrit.
* **Postural Pseudoanemia:** Changing position from upright to supine decreases lower-extremity capillary hydrostatic pressure, drawing $420\text{ mL}$ of interstitial fluid into the vascular compartment. This produces a $4.1\%$ absolute reduction in hematocrit ([Fig. 12.1](#fig-12-1)).
* **Iatrogenic Hemodilution:** Infusing a standard crystalloid bolus ($20\text{ mL/kg}$) lowers hematocrit by $4.2\%$ at 90 minutes. In fluid-overloaded ICU patients, laboratory anemia reflects plasma volume expansion rather than red cell depletion.

### Pathophysiological Mechanisms of ICU Anemia
1. **Anemia of Systemic Inflammation:** Driven by cytokine cascades (IL-6, TNF-$\alpha$) and hepatic overproduction of hepcidin. Hepcidin degrades ferroportin on enterocytes and macrophages, trapping iron within reticuloendothelial storage pools and starving erythropoiesis. This produces a microcytic or normocytic hypochromic anemia with low serum iron, low transferrin, and elevated serum ferritin ($> 100\text{ }\mu\text{g/L}$, distinguishing it from iron-deficiency anemia where ferritin is $< 30\text{ }\mu\text{g/L}$).
2. **Diagnostic Phlebotomy Blood Loss:** Daily diagnostic blood draws in ICU patients average $40\text{--}70\text{ mL/day}$, generating a cumulative weekly blood loss of roughly $500\text{ mL}$ (equivalent to 1 unit of whole blood). Discarding 5 mL of blood before drawing samples from arterial lines constitutes half of this volume; closed reinfusion sampling systems mitigate blood loss by 50%.

## Physiological Compensations in Anemia</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute isovolemic reduction in red cell mass triggers compensatory hemodynamic and microvascular adaptations that maintain cellular oxygen delivery.

<span id="tab-12-2"></span>

### Table 12.2. Mathematical Relationship Between Hematocrit and Blood Viscosity

| Hematocrit (%) | Relative Viscosity (Water = 1.0) | Absolute Viscosity (centipoise, cP) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **0% (Plasma)** | 1.4 | — |
| **10%** | 1.8 | 1.2 |
| **20%** | 2.1 | 1.5 |
| **30%** | 2.8 | 1.8 |
| **40%** | 3.7 | 2.3 |
| **50%** | 4.8 | 2.9 |
| **60%** | 5.8 | 3.8 |

*Note:* Plasma viscosity is slightly higher than water. Whole blood at a normal hematocrit of 45% is approximately 3 times more viscous than plasma and 4 times more viscous than water.

### Blood Viscosity and Poiseuille Flow
The viscosity of blood ($\mu$) is determined by red blood cell aggregation cross-linked by plasma fibrinogen:
* **Hagen-Poiseuille Hydrodynamics:**
  $$Q = \frac{\Delta P \cdot \pi r^4}{8\mu L}, \quad R = \frac{8\mu L}{\pi r^4}$$
  *(where $Q$ is laminar flow, $\Delta P$ is pressure drop, $r$ is vessel radius, $\mu$ is dynamic viscosity, and $L$ is length).*
* **Viscosity Drop in Anemia:** As hematocrit falls from 40% to 20%, blood viscosity decreases by nearly 50% ([Table 12.2](#tab-12-2)). This lowers systemic vascular resistance, enhancing venous return and ventricular stroke volume.

### Cardiac Output and Non-Newtonian Behavior
Blood is a non-Newtonian, pseudoplastic fluid exhibiting shear-thinning: as velocity of flow rises, red cells orient along flow lines, further reducing effective viscosity ([Fig. 12.2](#fig-12-2)). Consequently, cardiac output increases proportionally more than the decrement in hematocrit, offsetting reduced arterial oxygen content.

![](_page_6_Figure_1.jpeg)

### Oxygen Extraction Reserve
The fundamental relationship governing systemic oxygen uptake is:
$$\text{VO}_2 = \text{DO}_2 \times \text{O}_2\text{ER}$$
* **Supply Independence:** As demonstrated in [Fig. 12.3](#fig-12-3), falling hemoglobin reduces $\text{DO}_2$, but tissues recruit microvascular extraction reserve ($\text{O}_2\text{ER}$ rises from $25\%$ toward $50\%$).
* **Critical Extraction Threshold:** Oxygen consumption ($\text{VO}_2$) remains constant until hematocrit reaches a critical nadir of 10% ($\text{Hb} \approx 3\text{ g/dL}$). At this threshold, maximal extraction ($\approx 50\%$) is exhausted, $\text{VO}_2$ becomes supply-dependent, and lactic acidosis ensues.
* **Clinical Tolerance of Extreme Anemia:** Provided intravascular volume is sustained with crystalloids or colloids, animals tolerate hematocrits of 5–10% ($\text{Hb } 1.5\text{--}3\text{ g/dL}$) while breathing ambient air. In Jehovah's Witness surgical registries, 50% of patients survived with postoperative hemoglobin levels of 2–3 g/dL, and 75% survived with hemoglobin levels of 3–4 g/dL. In healthy volunteers, isovolemic hemodilution down to $5.0\text{ g/dL}$ produces no organ dysfunction.

## The Transfusion Trigger: Restrictive versus Liberal Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

In 1942, an arbitrary hemoglobin threshold of $\le 10\text{ g/dL}$ ($\text{Hct} \le 30\%$) was introduced as the universal transfusion trigger and persisted for 60 years.

### The Restrictive Revolution
Landmark randomized clinical trials (such as TRICC and TRISS) demonstrated that a restrictive transfusion threshold ($\text{Hb} < 7\text{ g/dL}$) is clinically equivalent or superior to liberal transfusion ($\text{Hb} < 10\text{ g/dL}$):
* A systematic review of 48 randomized trials confirmed no difference in mortality, myocardial infarction, or stroke between restrictive ($7\text{--}8\text{ g/dL}$) and liberal ($9\text{--}10\text{ g/dL}$) strategies, while the restrictive strategy achieved a 41% reduction in blood component utilization.

### Current Clinical Practice Guidelines
Over 20 international critical care guidelines endorse standardized restrictive thresholds for non-bleeding adult patients:
1. **General ICU Patients (including Septic Shock):** Transfuse only when hemoglobin falls below $7\text{ g/dL}$ (target range $7.0\text{--}9.0\text{ g/dL}$).
2. **Cardiovascular Disease and Major Surgery:** Transfuse when hemoglobin falls below $8\text{ g/dL}$ in patients with acute coronary syndrome, active coronary artery disease, or those undergoing cardiac or major orthopedic surgery.
3. **Practice Gaps:** Observational audits indicate that up to 60% of PRBC transfusions in clinical practice violate established restrictive guidelines.

### Flaws of Hemoglobin as a Transfusion Trigger
Transfusing purely based on a laboratory concentration is fundamentally flawed:
* Hemoglobin concentration does not reflect microcirculatory oxygen availability or cellular metabolic adequacy.
* Acute drops in hemoglobin frequently represent hemodilution rather than red cell depletion.
* **Physiological Transfusion Triggers:** A functional approach employs indices of extraction exhaustion: an oxygen extraction ratio $\text{O}_2\text{ER} \ge 50\%$ or a central venous oxygen saturation $\text{ScvO}_2 < 70\%$ signals true microvascular supply limitation.

## Red Blood Cell Preparations and Infusion Logistics</summary><div class="details-content">

Donated whole blood is centrifuged and fractionated into components (red blood cells, platelets, fresh frozen plasma, and cryoprecipitate) to maximize resource utility.

<span id="tab-12-3"></span>

### Table 12.3. Red Blood Cell Formulations Available for Transfusion

| Preparation | Unit Characteristics and Composition | Clinical Indications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Packed Red Blood Cells (PRBCs)** | Unit volume $\approx 350\text{ mL}$, $\text{Hct} \approx 60\%$. Contains residual leukocytes and 15–30 mL plasma. Stored at 1–6 °C for up to 42 days in adenine preservatives. | Standard restoration of red cell mass in acute symptomatic anemia and active hemorrhage. |
| **Leukocyte-Reduced PRBCs** | Filtration removes $> 99.9\%$ of donor leukocytes ($< 1 \times 10^6$ leukocytes/unit), decreasing cytokines and alloimmunization. | Prevents recurrent febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reactions and CMV transmission; indicated in transplant candidates. |
| **Washed Red Blood Cells** | Saline washing eliminates $> 98\%$ of residual donor plasma proteins and extracellular electrolytes; short shelf-life (24 hours). | Indicated in patients with severe plasma allergic reactions and in IgA deficiency with anti-IgA antibodies. |

### Administration Dynamics and Microaggregates
* **Viscosity and Catheter Gauge:** Because PRBCs have a high hematocrit (60%), gravity-driven infusion through a standard 20-gauge peripheral catheter requires nearly 2 hours ([Table 12.4](#tab-12-4)). Diluting the unit with 100 mL of 0.9% saline lowers viscosity and reduces infusion time to 19 minutes. In non-emergent settings, routine saline dilution is unnecessary, and standard infusion over 2 hours is recommended.
* **Standard Blood Filters:** Infusion sets require a standard in-line screen filter with a pore size of 170 to 260 microns ($\mu\text{m}$) to trap macroaggregates of fibrin and cellular debris. Standard filters do not trap leukocytes and cannot substitute for leukocyte-reduction filters.
* **Expected Yield:** In an average 70 kg adult, transfusion of 1 unit of PRBCs increases circulating hemoglobin by approximately $1\text{ g/dL}$ and hematocrit by $3\%$.

### Transfusion Failure: Why PRBCs Do Not Enhance Oxygen Consumption
In clinical investigations of critically ill patients, PRBC transfusions routinely fail to augment whole-body oxygen consumption ($\text{VO}_2$) ([Fig. 12.4](#fig-12-4)):
* **The Storage Lesion:** Refrigeration of donor erythrocytes triggers progressive biochemical decay:
  1. **Depletion of 2,3-DPG:** Increases hemoglobin oxygen affinity, shifting the dissociation curve to the left and impeding peripheral oxygen unloading.
  2. **Depletion of ATP and Loss of Deformability:** Red cells lose membrane elasticity, converting from flexible biconcave discs into rigid spherocytes that plug microvessels.
  3. **Endothelial Dysfunction:** Free hemoglobin and microvesicles in stored blood avidly scavenge endothelial nitric oxide (NO), inducing microvascular vasoconstriction and microthrombosis.

## Transfusion Hazards and Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

Red blood cell transfusion is accompanied by substantial immunologic, infectious, and circulatory hazards.

### Acute Hemolytic Transfusion Reactions (AHTR)
Caused by pre-formed recipient anti-A or anti-B antibodies reacting against incompatible donor erythrocyte surface antigens:
* **Etiology:** Overwhelmingly caused by human clerical error (misidentifying blood tubes or administering blood to the wrong patient).
* **Clinical Presentation:** Abrupt onset of fever, rigors, dyspnea, lumbar back pain, flushing, and acute hypotension within minutes of starting the infusion. Intravascular hemolysis triggers complement cascades, DIC, and acute renal failure.
* **Management:** Immediately halt the transfusion. Send donor unit and recipient blood to the blood bank for repeat ABO typing, direct antiglobulin (Coombs) testing, and plasma-free hemoglobin assays. Provide vigorous crystalloid hydration, vasopressors, and forced alkaline diuresis.

### Febrile Nonhemolytic Transfusion Reactions (FNHTR)
The most common adverse transfusion reaction (1 in 60 units):
* **Mechanism:** Recipient antileukocyte antibodies bind to donor white blood cell antigens, or accumulated pyrogenic cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, TNF-$\alpha$) are infused from stored units.
* **Presentation and Care:** Temperature elevation $> 1\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ ($1.8\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$) occurring during or up to 6 hours after transfusion. After ruling out hemolysis and bacterial sepsis, treatment is symptomatic (antipyretics). Over 75% of nonhemolytic febrile episodes do not recur on subsequent transfusions; leukocyte-reduced units are indicated if recurrent.

### Hypersensitivity and Anaphylactic Reactions
* **Mild Urticaria (1:100):** Mediated by preformed IgE against donor plasma proteins. It does not mandate permanent cessation; infusion can be paused, diphenhydramine ($25\text{--}50\text{ mg}$) administered, and transfusion resumed if symptoms resolve without fever.
* **Severe Anaphylaxis (1:1,000):** Characterized by acute bronchospasm, laryngeal edema, and shock. Often occurs in IgA-deficient recipients who possess anti-IgA IgG antibodies. Stop the transfusion immediately, administer intramuscular epinephrine, and use washed PRBCs for future transfusions.

### Transfusion-Related Acute Lung Injury (TRALI)
The leading cause of transfusion-related mortality, carrying a fatality rate of 30–50% ([Fig. 12.5](#fig-12-5)):
* **Pathophysiology:** In 80% of cases, donor antibodies directed against human leukocyte antigens (HLA) or human neutrophil antigens (HNA) bind to primed recipient neutrophils in the pulmonary microvasculature, triggering neutrophil activation, capillary leak, and severe non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
* **Presentation:** Acute hypoxemic respiratory failure developing within 6 hours of transfusion (often within the first hour), accompanied by fever and bilateral pulmonary infiltrates without elevated left atrial pressure. Treatment is supportive mechanical ventilation identical to ARDS protocols.

### Transfusion-Associated Circulatory Overload (TACO)
The most frequent pulmonary complication of blood transfusion, occurring in up to 1% of recipients with a 20% mortality rate:
* **Pathophysiology:** Rapid intravascular volume expansion in patients with underlying cardiac dysfunction or chronic renal insufficiency produces acute hydrostatic pulmonary edema.
* **Distinction from TRALI:** TACO is characterized by elevated CVP/PCWP, elevated brain natriuretic peptide (BNP/NT-proBNP), widened pulse pressure, and clinical response to intravenous loop diuretics, whereas TRALI features normal hydrostatic pressures and high-protein pulmonary edema fluid.

### Transfusion-Related Immunomodulation (TRIM) and Nosocomial Sepsis
Transfusion of allogeneic cellular elements suppresses recipient cell-mediated immunity (evidenced by historical improvements in renal allograft survival following pre-transplant transfusions):
* Multiple prospective trials confirm that allogeneic blood transfusion is an independent risk factor for secondary nosocomial infections (pneumonia, surgical site infections, sepsis), with risk correlating directly with the volume of transfused blood and its storage duration.

## A Final Word: Blood Volume versus Blood Cells</summary><div class="details-content">

The dogma of routine blood transfusion in critical care assumes that anemia poses an acute threat to tissue oxygenation. However, experimental and clinical evidence confirms that extreme reductions in hemoglobin are well tolerated as long as intravascular circulating volume is preserved.

<span id="fig-12-6"></span>
![](_page_16_Picture_2.jpeg)

<span class="caption">**FIGURE 12.6** Left: The traditional promotional slogan of the American Red Cross. Right: An amended conceptual framework recognizing that sustaining circulating blood volume, rather than the isolated concentration of erythrocytes, is the true determinant of microvascular survival in acute shock.</span>

### The Supremacy of Intravascular Volume
* **Shock versus Anemia:** Hypovolemia causes circulatory shock, cellular dysoxia, and cardiovascular collapse. Anemia alone, in the presence of normal blood volume, preserves microvascular perfusion and tissue oxygenation through compensatory rises in cardiac output and oxygen extraction.
* **Clinical Principle:** Restoring and sustaining intravascular circulating volume is the true foundation of critical care resuscitation. Transfusing red blood cells into a normovolemic, non-bleeding patient does not enhance oxygen uptake, but exposes the patient to substantial immunologic, pulmonary, and infective morbidity.
