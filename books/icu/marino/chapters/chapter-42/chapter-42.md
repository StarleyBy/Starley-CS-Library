# Urinary Tract Infections in the ICU

> *Throughout nature, infection without disease is the rule rather than the exception.*  
> — René Dubos (1965)*

*\*Dubos R. Man Adapting. 3rd ed., New Haven: Yale University Press, 1965.*

Indwelling urinary bladder catheters are ubiquitous in the management of critically ill patients, and catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTIs) are frequently cited as the most prevalent hospital-acquired infections in the United States (1).

However, this epidemiological assertion can be misleading, as clinicians routinely confuse asymptomatic bacteriuria (*colonization without disease*) with true invasive infection. This chapter reviews the essential clinical features of catheter-associated urinary tract infections in the intensive care unit, focusing on pathogenesis, evidence-based prevention, diagnostic criteria, and antimicrobial management.

### Key Principles and Clinical Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Infection vs Colonization:** Indwelling urethral catheters accumulate intraluminal and extraluminal bacterial biofilms at a daily rate of 3% to 8% of catheterization. By day 30, bacteriuria ($\ge 10^5\text{ CFU/mL}$) is near 100%. However, presence of bacteria in the bladder does not constitute invasive disease. Asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) requires neither diagnostic surveillance nor antibiotic treatment in the ICU.
- **The Epithelial Adherence Barrier:** In healthy individuals, bladder urothelial cells are coated with protective commensal microflora (*Lactobacillus acidophilus*), competitively blocking the adherence of uropathogens. Catheter placement incites localized mechanical trauma, mucosal exfoliation, and loss of this commensal barrier, exposing uroplakin glycoprotein receptors to which pathogenic *Escherichia coli* attach via fimbrial adhesins (pili).
- **Prevention Through Timely Removal:** The duration of urethral catheterization is the single most important determinant of CAUTI risk. Daily multidisciplinary review and prompt catheter removal when no longer clinically indicated is the most effective preventive intervention. Daily meatal antiseptic cleansing, antimicrobial bladder irrigation, and prophylactic systemic antibiotics do not prevent infection, promote resistance, and are contraindicated.
- **CDC/NHSN Diagnostic Criteria for CAUTI:** Definite diagnosis requires: (1) an indwelling catheter in place for $\ge 2$ consecutive calendar days (or removed within the preceding 24 hours), (2) at least one systemic or localized sign (fever $> 38^\circ\text{C}$ or $> 100.4^\circ\text{F}$, suprapubic tenderness, or costovertebral angle pain), and (3) a quantitative urine culture yielding $\ge 10^5\text{ CFU/mL}$ of no more than two microbial species.
- **The Pitfalls of Fever and Pyuria:** Fever is highly nonspecific in the ICU and occurs with identical frequency in catheterized patients with and without bacteriuria. Pyuria ($> 10\text{ WBCs/HPF}$) merely reflects catheter-induced urothelial inflammation and does not differentiate colonization from true infection; however, the *absence of pyuria* strongly argues against a CAUTI.
- **Asymptomatic Bacteremic UTI (ABUTI):** When the identical bacterial species ($\ge 10^5\text{ CFU/mL}$) is recovered simultaneously from urine and blood cultures in an asymptomatic catheterized patient, secondary bacteremic UTI is diagnosed, mandating systemic antimicrobial therapy.
- **Antimicrobial Regimens for CAUTI:** Symptomatic CAUTI requires targeted therapy directed against enteric gram-negative bacilli (*E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas*) and enterococci. First-line empiric agents include piperacillin-tazobactam or meropenem. Catheters in place for $> 14\text{ days}$ must be replaced or removed prior to initiating antimicrobial therapy. Course duration is 7 days for rapid responders, extending to 10–14 days for delayed resolution.
- **Candiduria Spectrum:** Isolating *Candida* species in urine overwhelmingly represents benign mucosal colonization. Quantitative colony counts do not distinguish colonization from upper tract or disseminated infection. Asymptomatic candiduria requires no antifungal therapy unless the patient is severely neutropenic (where an echinocandin is indicated) or undergoing invasive urologic surgery.
- **Symptomatic Candida UTIs:** Manifesting with fever or costovertebral tenderness, symptomatic candiduria requires renal ultrasonography to exclude obstructive fungus balls. Fluconazole-susceptible *C. albicans* cystitis/pyelonephritis is treated with oral fluconazole ($200\text{–}400\text{ mg/day}$ for 14 days). Fluconazole-resistant species (*Candida glabrata*, *Candida krusei*) require intravenous amphotericin B deoxycholate ($0.3\text{–}0.6\text{ mg/kg/day}$ for 1–7 days); echinocandins achieve negligible urinary excretion and are ineffective for fungal cystitis.
- **Bacterial Adherence and Future Therapeutics:** Competitive inhibition of microbial adhesins (e.g., mannoside blockers targeting FimH pili on uropathogenic *E. coli*) represents a promising non-antibiotic frontier, preventing colonization without selective antimicrobial pressure.

## Pathogenesis of Catheter-Associated Infections</summary><div class="details-content">

The presence of an indwelling urethral catheter is associated with a 3% to 8% incidence of new bacteriuria (defined as $\ge 10^5\text{ CFU/mL}$) per day of catheterization (2).

Bacteria originating predominantly from the patient's own colonic and perineal flora migrate along the external and internal surfaces of the catheter, generating dense *biofilms* (3). These biofilms protect microbes from host immune defenses and antibiotics, establishing continuous intraluminal colonization.

However, the presence of intraluminal bacteria is insufficient by itself to generate tissue invasion: *direct instillation of uropathogenic bacteria into the bladder of healthy volunteers fails to induce cystitis or systemic disease* (4).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Bacterial Adherence and Urothelial Defense</summary><div class="details-content">

The decisive factor in transition from harmless colonization to invasive infection is the adherence of pathogenic bacteria to the urothelial surface.

In health, the bladder urothelium is coated with non-pathogenic commensal microorganisms, predominantly *Lactobacillus acidophilus*, as shown in Figure 42.1 (5). These commensals block epithelial receptors, preventing pathogenic attachment.

#### Epithelial Disruption by Catheters

The physical presence of an indwelling balloon catheter induces mechanical irritation and localized urothelial inflammation. This causes desquamation of protective superficial facet cells, stripping away the commensal layer.

Denuded transitional epithelium exposes underlying uroplakin receptors, facilitating avid binding of pathogenic bacteria (3). This dynamic mirrors oral mucosal colonization by pathogens in mechanically ventilated patients (see Figure 5.5), which serves as a prelude to nosocomial pneumonia.

### Microbiology of CAUTI</summary><div class="details-content">

A multistate surveillance study of pathogen distribution in intensive care unit CAUTIs demonstrated the following microbial frequencies (6):

- 1. *Escherichia coli:* 28% of isolated pathogens.
- 2. *Klebsiella pneumoniae:* 23% of cases.
- 3. *Enterococcus* species: 17% of cases.
- 4. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa:* 10% of cases.
- 5. *Candida* species: 5% of cases.
- 6. *Staphylococcus* species: 3% of cases.
- 7. *Streptococcus* species: 3% of cases.

Short-term catheterization ($< 30\text{ days}$) is typically characterized by monomicrobial infection, whereas prolonged catheterization ($\ge 30\text{ days}$) almost universally yields polymicrobial biofilms and multidrug-resistant isolates (7).

### Evidence-Based Preventive Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

The risk of catheter-associated infection is determined overwhelmingly by catheter duration. Figure 42.2 illustrates pooled clinical trial data demonstrating that early catheter removal drastically reduces both asymptomatic bacteriuria and clinical infection (8).

#### Ineffective and Harmful Practices

Prompt catheter removal when no longer clinically essential is the single most effective preventive measure. Other common clinical practices have failed to demonstrate efficacy in clinical trials:

- 1. *Meatal Cleansing:* Routine daily cleansing of the urethral meatus with antiseptic solutions, antibiotic ointments, or soap and water is *not recommended*; clinical trials show that vigorous meatal scrubbing increases mucosal trauma and accelerates bacteriuria (2).
- 2. *Systemic Prophylaxis:* Prophylactic systemic antibiotics do not prevent CAUTIs and accelerate the emergence of multidrug-resistant pathogens (2).
- 3. *Antimicrobial Catheters:* Silver alloy or antibiotic-impregnated catheters achieve modest reductions in bacteriuria only when combined with routine, scheduled catheter replacement protocols (9).

## Diagnostic Evaluation and Treatment</summary><div class="details-content">

The standardized diagnostic framework for catheter-associated urinary tract infections is established by the National Healthcare Safety Network (NHSN) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) (10).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### CDC/NHSN Diagnostic Criteria</summary><div class="details-content">

Surveillance and clinical diagnosis of CAUTI require satisfying all three of the following components (10):

- 1. *Catheter Exposure:* An indwelling urethral catheter in place for at least two consecutive calendar days, which remains in place or was removed within the preceding 24 hours.
- 2. *Clinical Signs/Symptoms:* Presence of at least one of the following:
  - a. Documented fever ($> 38.0^\circ\text{C}$ or $> 100.4^\circ\text{F}$)
  - b. Suprapubic tenderness on palpation
  - c. Costovertebral angle pain or tenderness
  - d. Dysuria, urgency, or frequency (applicable only after catheter removal)
- 3. *Quantitative Microbiological Growth:* Urine culture yielding $\ge 10^5\text{ CFU/mL}$ of no more than two microbial species, with at least one bacterial species identified.

#### Diagnostic Pitfalls: Fever and Pyuria

The reliance on fever as a diagnostic criterion for CAUTI is deeply problematic in ICU settings. Critically ill patients exhibit numerous non-infectious and infectious sources of fever (see Chapter 44). Prospective investigations demonstrate that the incidence of fever is identical between catheterized ICU patients with bacteriuria and those with sterile urine (11).

Similarly, pyuria (defined as $> 10\text{ leukocytes/HPF}$ in centrifuged urine) reflects non-specific mechanical inflammation from the catheter balloon. Pyuria does not differentiate colonization from invasive disease. However, *the complete absence of pyuria strongly argues against a diagnosis of CAUTI* (2).

#### Bacteremia and Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections account for approximately 20% of all hospital-acquired bacteremias (8). The CDC/NHSN defines *Asymptomatic Bacteremic Urinary Tract Infection (ABUTI)* as the isolation of the identical bacterial pathogen ($\ge 10^5\text{ CFU/mL}$) from both urine and blood cultures in an asymptomatic catheterized patient.

Conversely, *asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB)* requires: (1) urine growth of a single bacterium at $\ge 10^5\text{ CFU/mL}$, (2) blood cultures without growth of that organism, and (3) complete absence of systemic or localized signs of infection.

### Antimicrobial Therapy for CAUTI</summary><div class="details-content">

Antibiotic stewardship guidelines strictly dictate that *asymptomatic bacteriuria must not be treated with antibiotics* (12). Antimicrobial therapy in ASB does not prevent subsequent CAUTI or improve survival, but significantly increases adverse drug events and selects for resistant pathogens. The only exception is patients scheduled for traumatic urological procedures associated with mucosal bleeding (12).

In patients with confirmed or clinically suspected symptomatic CAUTI, empiric therapy should provide coverage for enteric gram-negative bacilli and enterococci:

- 1. *First-Line Empiric Agents:* Intravenous *piperacillin-tazobactam* ($4.5\text{ g}$ every 6 hours) or a carbapenem (*meropenem* $1\text{ g}$ every 8 hours) provides excellent broad-spectrum activity (13).
- 2. *Alternative Regimens:* Cefepime ($2\text{ g}$ every 8 hours), ceftazidime ($2\text{ g}$ every 8 hours), or levofloxacin ($750\text{ mg}$ daily) are suitable alternatives depending on local antibiograms (13).
- 3. *Catheter Replacement:* Catheters in place for longer than 2 weeks ($> 14\text{ days}$) harbor entrenched biofilms and should be removed or replaced prior to antimicrobial initiation (2).
- 4. *Treatment Duration:* A 7-day course is recommended for patients with prompt clinical resolution; a 10 to 14-day course is indicated for patients with delayed response or bacteremia (2,13).

### Candiduria in Critical Illness</summary><div class="details-content">

The isolation of *Candida* species in urine occurs frequently in ICU patients with indwelling catheters. In over 90% of cases, candiduria reflects benign mucosal colonization rather than invasive parenchymal infection.

However, candiduria can occasionally represent the sole initial manifestation of hematogenously disseminated candidiasis. Blood cultures remain negative in more than 50% of confirmed cases of systemic candidiasis (14).

Quantitative fungal colony counts have zero predictive utility in differentiating benign colonization from invasive upper tract or disseminated infection (14). *Candida albicans* accounts for roughly 50% of isolates, followed by *Candida glabrata* (14,15), which exhibits intrinsic or acquired resistance to azoles.

#### Management of Asymptomatic Candiduria

Asymptomatic candiduria does not warrant antifungal therapy in immunocompetent patients (15,16). Removal of the urethral catheter alone results in spontaneous clearance of candiduria in over one-third of patients.

Antifungal therapy for asymptomatic candiduria is restricted strictly to patients with severe neutropenia ($\text{ANC} < 500/\text{mm}^3$) or those undergoing invasive urological manipulation with anticipated mucosal disruption (15,16). In neutropenic patients, systemic echinocandin therapy is recommended.

#### Symptomatic Candiduria and Renal Infection

Candiduria accompanied by fever, leukocytosis, or flank tenderness requires full evaluation: repeat blood cultures and renal ultrasonography or CT to exclude fungal pyelonephritis, renal microabscesses, or obstructing "fungus balls."

- 1. *Systemic Candidiasis:* When disseminated candidiasis is suspected, an intravenous echinocandin (*caspofungin*, *micafungin*, or *anidulafungin*) is the treatment of choice (16).
- 2. *Candida Cystitis and Pyelonephritis:* Therapeutic selection depends on species-specific susceptibility (16):
  - a. *Fluconazole-Susceptible Isolates (C. albicans):* Oral *fluconazole* ($200\text{–}400\text{ mg}$ daily for 14 days) is the agent of choice, achieving high urinary concentrations (17).
  - b. *Fluconazole-Resistant Isolates (C. glabrata, C. krusei):* Intravenous *amphotericin B deoxycholate* ($0.3\text{–}0.6\text{ mg/kg/day}$ for 1 to 7 days) is required. Echinocandins undergo hepatic elimination and achieve negligible urinary concentrations, rendering them ineffective for primary lower urinary tract fungal infections.
- 3. *Fungus Balls:* Obstructing fungal bezoars require surgical or percutaneous removal. If nephrostomy tubes are present, local continuous irrigation with *amphotericin B* ($25\text{–}50\text{ mg}$ in $200\text{–}500\text{ mL}$ sterile water) is recommended (16).

## A Final Word: Bacterial Adherence and Nosocomial Infections</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### The Molecular Basis of Mucosal Colonization</summary><div class="details-content">

A unifying pathophysiological mechanism across hospital-acquired infections of the gastrointestinal, respiratory, and urinary tracts is the replacement of normal commensal flora by virulent pathogens on mucosal surfaces.

In healthy subjects, epithelial linings are densely populated by protective commensals. In critical illness, severe systemic stress and antibiotic exposure alter epithelial receptor expression, permitting virulent pathogens to displace commensals and initiate invasive disease.

#### Receptor-Ligand Dynamics and Novel Therapeutics

This pathogenic shift is governed not by simple bacterial density, but by specific molecular receptor-ligand interactions. For example, uropathogenic *E. coli* utilize specialized filamentous appendages termed *fimbriae* or *pili* that bind to mannosylated glycoproteins on urothelial cells (3).

Competitive blockade of these binding sites using small-molecule receptor analogues (such as exogenous mannosides) can prevent pathogen adhesion without exerting selective antimicrobial pressure. This anti-adherence strategy represents an appealing frontier in nosocomial infection prevention, averting the development of antimicrobial resistance.
