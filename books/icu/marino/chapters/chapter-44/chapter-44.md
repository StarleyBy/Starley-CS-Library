# Fever in the ICU

> *Give me the power to produce fever, and I will cure all diseases.*  
> — Parmenides (ca. 500 B.C.)

The appearance of a new fever is always a matter of concern in a hospitalized patient. This chapter presents a practical approach to new-onset fever in the ICU, covering the preferred methods of measuring body temperature, obtaining diagnostic blood cultures, differentiating noninfectious from infectious etiologies, and addressing the physiologic and clinical impact of antipyretic therapy (1).

The final section of the chapter explores the biological rationale for preserving the febrile response, and explains why Parmenides would have vigorously disapproved of the routine suppression of fever in critically ill patients.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Key Principles and Clinical Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Fever vs Hyperthermia:** Fever represents a regulated elevation of body temperature driven by endogenous pyrogens (pyrogenic cytokines acting on the preoptic hypothalamus to raise the thermal set point), whereas hyperthermia is an uncontrolled thermal failure without set-point change.
- **Definition of Fever in the ICU:** A single core temperature measurement $\ge 38.3^\circ\text{C}$ ($101^\circ\text{F}$) defines fever requiring diagnostic evaluation. In neutropenic patients (absolute neutrophil count $< 500/\mu\text{L}$), a lower threshold of $\ge 38.0^\circ\text{C}$ ($100.4^\circ\text{F}$) sustained for $\ge 1\text{ hour}$ warrants emergent evaluation and immediate broad-spectrum empiric antimicrobial coverage.
- **Accuracy of Thermometry:** Thermistor-equipped catheters (pulmonary artery catheter, esophageal probe, or urinary bladder thermistor) provide the most accurate and continuous core measurements. Oral temperatures underestimate core temperature by $0.5^\circ\text{C}$ ($0.9^\circ\text{F}$) in tachypneic patients. Peripheral thermometry (tympanic, temporal artery, axillary) lacks clinically acceptable accuracy and must not guide clinical decision-making.
- **Inflammation vs Infection:** Approximately 50% of new fevers in the ICU are noninfectious in origin. The presence, degree, or spike of fever does not correlate with the presence or severity of infection. Noninfectious etiologies include early postoperative tissue injury, venous thromboembolism, drug fever, blood transfusion reactions, myocardial infarction, alcohol/drug withdrawal, acute pancreatitis, and ARDS.
- **The Atelectasis Myth:** Atelectasis does *not* cause fever. While radiographic atelectasis occurs in $> 90\%$ of general anesthesia cases and is present in nearly 90% of febrile postoperative cardiac surgery patients, 75% of patients with atelectasis remain completely afebrile. Early postoperative fever (first 24–48 hours) reflects sterile tissue injury from surgical trauma.
- **Drug Fever:** An underappreciated hypersensitivity or idiosyncratic reaction. The offending drug may have been started days to weeks prior. Rigors occur in 53% and hypotension in 18%, often mimicking septic shock, whereas classic hypersensitivity signs (rash, eosinophilia) are absent in $> 75\%$ of cases. Common culprits include beta-lactams, cephalosporins, carbamazepine, phenytoin, heparins, procainamide, quinidine, and amphotericin. De-escalation leads to defervescence within 72 hours.
- **Diagnostic Blood Cultures:** The volume of blood collected is paramount: 20 to $30\text{ mL}$ per venipuncture site ($10\text{ mL}$ into aerobic and $10\text{ mL}$ into anaerobic bottles; an additional $10\text{ mL}$ into the aerobic bottle yields a 10% higher detection rate). Drawing 3 to 4 blood cultures over 24 hours detects $> 90\%$ of non-endocarditis bacteremias (two suffice for endocarditis due to continuous bacteremia). For multilumen central venous catheters, simultaneous paired cultures from two lumens and a peripheral venipuncture site are required; growth in catheter blood $\ge 2\text{ hours}$ before peripheral blood confirms catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI).
- **Fever as an Adaptive Host Defense:** Febrile temperatures enhance neutrophil chemotaxis, phagocytosis, T-cell activation, and cytokine expression, while directly retarding bacterial and viral replication. In clinical sepsis and septic shock, higher body temperatures independently correlate with lower in-hospital mortality.
- **Antipyretic Therapy Considerations:** Routine suppression of fever with antipyretic drugs or cooling blankets is *not* recommended in clinical guidelines (SCCM/IDSA), as antipyresis does not improve outcomes and may increase mortality in sepsis. Fever suppression is strictly indicated only in acute ischemic brain injury (cardiac arrest, stroke, severe traumatic brain injury) to prevent secondary cerebral damage.
- **Antipyretic Agents and Cooling Blankets:** Acetaminophen ($650\text{ mg}$ q6h PO/PR/IV or $1{,}000\text{ mg}$ q8h IV; maximum safe dose $3\text{ g/day}$; contraindicated in liver failure). NSAIDs (IV ibuprofen $10\text{ mg/kg}$ up to $800\text{ mg}$ q6h; ketorolac $0.5\text{ mg/kg}$ IV single dose). External cooling blankets are contraindicated in infectious fever because they provoke cutaneous vasoconstriction and shivering, exacerbating metabolic stress; they are reserved for hyperthermic syndromes (heat stroke).

### Thermometry and Definition of Fever</summary><div class="details-content">

Accurate clinical assessment begins with proper thermometric methodology (1):

- 1. *Thermistors in Core Sites:* The gold standard method of measuring body temperature is a thermistor-equipped catheter positioned in either the pulmonary artery, lower third of the esophagus, or urinary bladder (1). These catheters provide the most dependable measure of core body temperature and permit continuous electronic monitoring.
- 2. *Oral and Rectal Thermometry:* When core thermistors are unavailable, oral or rectal temperatures serve as practical alternatives (1). However, oral temperatures underestimate core temperature by approximately $0.5^\circ\text{C}$ ($0.9^\circ\text{F}$) in patients with tachypnea due to evaporative mucosal cooling (3). Rectal probes respond sluggishly to rapid thermal shifts and carry risks of rectal perforation.
- 3. *Peripheral Thermometry Inaccuracy:* Peripheral measurement modalities (tympanic membrane infrared scanners, temporal artery scanners, and axillary probes) lack clinically acceptable accuracy, exhibiting wide variability compared to core readings (4). They should never be utilized to make critical clinical decisions in the ICU.

#### Clinical Definition and Diurnal Variation

Normal core body temperature exhibits a circadian diurnal rhythm, reaching its nadir in the early morning (between 04:00 and 08:00) and peaking in the late afternoon (between 16:00 and 18:00).

The magnitude of this diurnal excursion varies individually and can attain $1.3^\circ\text{C}$ ($2.4^\circ\text{F}$) in healthy subjects (5). While fever is fundamentally defined as a temperature exceeding an individual's personal baseline diurnal variation, baseline curves are rarely known in critically ill patients.

The clinical practice guidelines established by the Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM) and the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) define fever as follows (1):

- 1. *General ICU Population:* A single core temperature measurement of $\ge 38.3^\circ\text{C}$ ($101^\circ\text{F}$) constitutes a fever that warrants thorough clinical evaluation.
- 2. *Neutropenic Population:* A lower threshold of $\ge 38.0^\circ\text{C}$ ($100.4^\circ\text{F}$) is applied to immunocompromised and neutropenic patients (absolute neutrophil count $< 500/\mu\text{L}$) if sustained for $\ge 1\text{ hour}$, triggering immediate evaluation and empiric antimicrobial therapy.

## The Febrile Response and Noninfectious Etiologies</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Pathophysiology and Differential Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Fever develops when pyrogenic cytokines—primarily interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor ($\text{TNF}-\alpha$), and interferon-gamma ($\text{IFN}-\gamma$)—stimulate the organum vasculosum of the lamina terminalis in the preoptic anterior hypothalamus.

This stimulation induces local cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) to produce prostaglandin $\text{E}_2$ ($\text{PGE}_2$), which elevates the hypothalamic thermoregulatory set point (8).

![FIGURE 44.1](images/_page_3_Figure_1.jpeg)

Because any condition triggering systemic inflammation induces endogenous pyrogen production, *fever is a cardinal hallmark of inflammation, not exclusively infection*. Approximately 50% of ICU patients who develop fever have no evidence of infection (6,7).

Furthermore, the absolute height or pattern of fever does not correlate with the presence or severity of infection. Severe noninfectious systemic inflammation can generate extreme fevers, while life-threatening septic shock may occur without fever or with hypothermia.

### Postoperative Fever and Atelectasis Myth</summary><div class="details-content">

Major surgical procedures cause extensive mechanical tissue trauma, releasing damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that trigger cytokine-mediated inflammation.

Consequently, fever during the first 24 to 48 hours postoperatively occurs in 15% to 40% of major surgical cases (10–12). In the vast majority of these patients, thorough investigations reveal no infectious focus (10,11), and the temperature elevation spontaneously defervesces within 24 to 48 hours.

#### Debunking the Atelectasis Myth

A deeply entrenched medical myth attributes early postoperative fevers to pulmonary atelectasis. This assumption stems from an invalid logical deduction: because atelectasis is extraordinarily common after surgery, patients who develop a fever frequently display atelectasis on chest radiographs.

<span id="fig-44-2"></span>
![FIGURE 44.2](images/_page_4_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 44.2** The relationships between fever and atelectasis in the first postoperative day following open heart surgery for 100 patients. The graph on the left shows that most patients with fever had atelectasis, but the graph on the right shows that most patients with atelectasis did not have a fever. Data from Reference 12.

As shown in Figure 44.2, in a clinical trial of 100 patients undergoing open-heart surgery, nearly 90% of patients with a fever on postoperative day 1 had radiographic atelectasis (left panel) (12).

However, the converse analysis (right panel) revealed that 75% of patients with documented atelectasis remained entirely afebrile. Experimental animal studies demonstrated over five decades ago that acute lobar atelectasis produced by complete bronchus ligation does not evoke a febrile response (13).

##### Surgical Trauma vs Atelectasis

Atelectasis occurs in over 90% of patients undergoing general anesthesia due to loss of diaphragmatic tone and compression (14). It is a mechanical complication of surgery, not a pyrogenic inflammatory focus.

*Early postoperative fevers occurring within 24 to 48 hours of surgery are overwhelmingly driven by sterile surgical tissue injury, not atelectasis or early infection*. Extensive septic workups and empiric antibiotics during the first 48 hours in stable patients are unjustified.

### Vascular, Transfusion, and Drug-Related Causes</summary><div class="details-content">

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE) are frequent hazards in critically ill patients, especially after trauma, major orthopedic operations, or prolonged bedrest (see Chapter 5).

While deep vein thrombosis is predominantly asymptomatic, acute pulmonary embolism provokes local and systemic inflammatory responses, generating a low-grade to moderate fever that can persist for up to 7 days (16). The diagnostic pathway for PE is covered in Chapter 22.

Transfusions of red blood cells (RBCs) and platelet concentrates are capable of eliciting febrile reactions. Febrile nonhemolytic transfusion reactions (FNHTR) represent the most frequent adverse event during RBC transfusion (see Chapter 12), occurring during or within 6 hours of completion. FNHTR is mediated by recipient antileukocyte antibodies reacting against donor white blood cells or accumulated cytokines in storage bags. With single-donor platelets, FNHTR occurs in approximately 1 in 1,000 transfusions (17).

#### Drug-Associated Fever

Drug-induced fever is an underrecognized adverse reaction characterized by either immunological hypersensitivity or non-immunologic idiosyncratic mechanisms (18,19).

There is no stereotyped clinical picture. The onset ranges from several days to several weeks following drug initiation. While it can present as an isolated fever, it frequently mimics severe systemic sepsis, as outlined in Table 44.3 (20).

Over half of patients with drug fever experience intense rigors (53%), and 18% manifest arterial hypotension, leading clinicians to erroneously diagnose septic shock (20).

Crucially, the classic hallmarks of drug allergy—rash and peripheral eosinophilia—are *absent in over 75% of patients with drug fever* (20). Suspicion must arise when no infectious source is discovered; discontinuation of the causative agent characteristically achieves complete defervescence within 72 hours (18).

##### Withdrawal, Endocrine, and Iatrogenic Fevers

Several additional noninfectious entities must be included in the differential diagnosis:

- 1. *Substance Withdrawal:* Severe withdrawal from ethanol (delirium tremens), opioids, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, and gabapentinoid agents provokes marked autonomic storming and hyperpyrexia, typically emerging 48 to 72 hours after cessation.
- 2. *Acute Adrenal Insufficiency:* Characterized by unexplained fever, refractory hypotension, and hypoglycemia. It may arise spontaneously from bilateral adrenal hemorrhage secondary to therapeutic anticoagulation, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, or DIC (see Chapter 51).
- 3. *Acute Lung Injury (ARDS):* Severe sterile alveolar inflammation in ARDS directly releases pyrogenic cytokines, causing persistent low-grade fever without pulmonary infection (see Chapter 24).
- 4. *Tissue Infarction:* Acute myocardial infarction, ischemic cerebral stroke, and mesenteric bowel infarction generate sterile tissue necrosis and secondary fever.
- 5. *Iatrogenic Thermal Transference:* Faulty temperature control units in circulating-water heating blankets or heated respiratory humidifiers can passively overheat patients (21). A rapid inspection of device temperature dials can solve an otherwise perplexing fever.

### Surgical Site and Necrotizing Infections</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical site infections (SSIs) represent a major cause of postoperative fever and morbidity. While they classically emerge 5 to 7 days after surgery, any incision infection presenting within 30 days of an operative procedure is classified as an SSI (23).

Signs include erythema, edema, induration, tenderness, and purulent drainage. Superficial incisional SSIs rarely cause prominent fever, whereas deep fascial or space infections produce high fevers and systemic toxicity.

The baseline risk is 1% to 2% for clean procedures (where viscus, respiratory, or gastrointestinal tracts are not entered), climbing to 4% to 5% for contaminated or dirty procedures (e.g., perforated diverticulitis) (24). Pathogens mirror the operative site: clean procedures are predominantly caused by *Staphylococcus aureus*, while contaminated abdominal cases involve polymicrobial gram-negative enteric bacilli and anaerobes (24).

#### Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infections

Necrotizing fasciitis and myonecrosis are surgical emergencies that can declare themselves within the first 24 to 72 hours postoperatively.

Early findings include severe edema, bronze discoloration, and *excruciating local pain that is markedly out of proportion to visible skin changes* (due to ischemic destruction of cutaneous nerves) (26). Crepitus, hemorrhagic bullae, and gray dishwater drainage indicate advanced tissue necrosis.

Infections may be monomicrobial (group A Streptococcus or *Clostridium* species) or polymicrobial (type I necrotizing fasciitis involving enteric bacilli and anaerobes) (26). Immediate, radical surgical debridement is mandatory for survival, combined with broad antimicrobial therapy (e.g., penicillin plus clindamycin for streptococcal/clostridial toxic shock, or meropenem plus vancomycin for polymicrobial necrotizing fasciitis) (25).

#### Specific Infectious Considerations: Endocarditis, Peritonitis, Candidiasis

Three specific clinical scenarios require targeted diagnostic evaluation:

- 1. *Infective Endocarditis:* Nosocomial endocarditis should be systematically investigated in any patient with bacteremia caused by streptococci, enterococci, or *Staphylococcus aureus* (mandatory in all *S. aureus* bacteremias regardless of valve history), and in patients with prosthetic heart valves (27). Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) exhibits poor sensitivity (60%–70% for native valves, 50% for prosthetic valves); a negative TTE must be promptly followed by transesophageal echocardiography (TEE), which exceeds 90% sensitivity (27).
- 2. *Intra-abdominal Infections:* Deep intra-abdominal abscesses or peritonitis should be suspected in patients undergoing extensive gastrointestinal surgery who develop persistent or late-onset fever ($\ge 1\text{ week}$ postop) (see Chapter 41). Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis must be ruled out via diagnostic paracentesis in any patient with cirrhosis and ascites presenting with new fever (see Chapter 39).
- 3. *Invasive Candidiasis:* Blood cultures detect only 20% of invasive candidemia (29). Whole-blood PCR assays (T2Candida) offer $> 90\%$ sensitivity and specificity within hours (30). Because non-*albicans* species account for approximately 50% of ICU isolates, empiric coverage requires an echinocandin (micafungin, caspofungin, or anidulafungin) rather than fluconazole.

### Diagnostic Blood Cultures and Molecular Testing</summary><div class="details-content">

Diagnostic blood cultures are the foundational investigation for suspected bloodstream infection in the ICU. Yield is determined by blood collection volume and the number of separate culture sets.

The blood volume inoculated per venipuncture site directly governs sensitivity. Optimal recovery requires 20 to $30\text{ mL}$ of blood per venipuncture set (32).

The standard protocol allocates $20\text{ mL}$ divided equally ($10\text{ mL}$ each) into one aerobic and one anaerobic bottle. Increasing total volume from $20\text{ mL}$ to $30\text{ mL}$ by inoculating an extra $10\text{ mL}$ into the aerobic bottle increases the yield by approximately 10% (33).

The cumulative detection rate of bacteremia as a function of the number of blood cultures drawn over a 24-hour period is illustrated in Figure 44.3 (34).

In infective endocarditis, the continuous nature of bacteremia allows two blood culture sets to detect 94% of cases (34).

Conversely, in other systemic infections, bacteremia is transient and intermittent; two culture sets detect only 70% to 80% of bacteremias, whereas 3 to 4 blood culture sets are required to surpass a 90% detection threshold (34). Current guidelines recommend drawing 3 to 4 culture sets over 24 hours for suspected nosocomial bacteremia (1).

##### Multilumen Catheters and Differential Time to Positivity

When assessing potential catheter-related bloodstream infection (CRBSI) in a patient with a multilumen central venous catheter:

- 1. *Paired Luminal and Peripheral Cultures:* Blood samples must be obtained simultaneously from two catheter lumens and a separate peripheral venipuncture site (1). Sampling only a single catheter lumen misses up to 40% of intraluminal catheter colonization (35), because each individual lumen represents an independent conduit for bacterial biofilm (36).
- 2. *Differential Time to Positivity (DTP):* When the same microorganism is recovered from both catheter and peripheral blood cultures, growth detected $\ge 2\text{ hours}$ earlier in the catheter bottle compared to the peripheral bottle establishes the definitive diagnosis of CRBSI without requiring catheter removal.

##### Rapid Molecular Diagnostics and Empiric Coverage

Traditional blood cultures require 48 to 72 hours for microbial identification. Rapid non-culture molecular diagnostics utilize magnetic resonance and PCR amplification (e.g., T2Bacteria panel) to detect 6 priority bacterial pathogens (*E. coli, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, E. faecium,* and *A. baumannii*) within 3 to 5 hours with $90\%$ sensitivity and specificity directly from whole blood (37).

Empiric antimicrobial coverage should never be delayed in neutropenic patients (absolute neutrophil count $< 500/\mu\text{L}$) or patients displaying septic shock, where every hour of delay increases mortality (38).

Core empiric regimens should encompass agents active against resistant gram-negative aerobic bacilli (piperacillin/tazobactam, cefepime, or meropenem), combined with vancomycin when MRSA is suspected (VAP, vascular line infections), and an echinocandin for patients with high-risk candidiasis indicators (persistent fever, central lines, recent bowel surgery, or persistent candiduria).

## Antipyretic Therapy and Thermal Management</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Fever as an Adaptive Host Defense</summary><div class="details-content">

The widespread clinical urge to suppress fever reflects cultural misconceptions that treat temperature elevation as a disease rather than an adaptive physiological defense.

Phylogenetically preserved across hundreds of millions of years in vertebrates and invertebrates, the febrile response enhances host antimicrobial competence through multiple coordinated mechanisms (9):

- 1. *Immunological Activation:* Hyperthermic temperatures accelerate the proliferation and differentiation of T-lymphocytes, stimulate the synthesis of protective immunoglobulins, and promote the release of activating cytokines (39,40).
- 2. *Phagocyte Enhancement:* Neutrophil chemotaxis, transendothelial migration, intracellular phagocytosis, and superoxide anion generation are significantly enhanced at temperatures between $38^\circ\text{C}$ and $40^\circ\text{C}$ (39).
- 3. *Microbial Growth Inhibition:* Elevated temperatures directly impair the replication of common pathogens by inducing heat-shock pathways and depriving bacteria of essential trace metals (iron, zinc).

As demonstrated in Figure 44.4, an elevation in body temperature of $4^\circ\text{C}$ within the physiological febrile range completely arrests the multiplication of *Pasteurella multocida* in the blood of infected animals (41).

#### Clinical Studies and Sepsis Survival

The protective role of fever in human infection is supported by large-scale observational studies in critically ill septic patients (42–44).

<span id="fig-44-5"></span>
![FIGURE 44.5](images/_page_13_Figure_5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 44.5** Inverse relationship between body temperature and in-hospital mortality rate in 2,225 patients with severe sepsis and septic shock. Differences between categories are significant at the p<0.001 level. Data from Reference 42.

As shown in Figure 44.5, in a prospective cohort of 2,225 patients admitted to ICUs with severe sepsis and septic shock, body temperature exhibited a significant inverse relationship with hospital mortality ($p < 0.001$) (42).

Patients with hypothermia ($< 36^\circ\text{C}$) or normal temperatures had markedly higher mortality rates than patients manifesting robust fevers ($\ge 39^\circ\text{C}$ or $\ge 102.2^\circ\text{F}$). Additional clinical investigations demonstrated that the active administration of antipyretic pharmacotherapy in septic patients was independently associated with increased in-hospital mortality (45).

##### Therapeutic Hyperthermia and Practice Guidelines

The therapeutic benefit of elevated core temperature prompted an interventional study in which afebrile septic patients were treated with external warming garments to elevate their core temperature by $1.5^\circ\text{C}$ (46). This induced hyperthermia produced a statistically significant reduction in hospital mortality compared to afebrile controls (46).

Reflecting this clinical evidence, the joint SCCM/IDSA clinical practice guidelines recommend *against* the routine suppression of fever in adult ICU patients (1).

*Critical Clinical Exception:* Fever suppression is strictly indicated in patients with acute ischemic brain injury (cerebrovascular accident, post-cardiac arrest anoxic encephalopathy, or severe traumatic brain injury). In injured neuronal tissue, fever accelerates metabolic demand, exacerbates intracellular lactic acidosis, promotes free radical production, and worsens neurological outcomes.

### Pharmacologic and Physical Antipyresis</summary><div class="details-content">

When antipyresis is clinically necessary, pharmacologic agents that suppress prostaglandin $\text{E}_2$ synthesis are employed.

Acetaminophen (paracetamol) is the most widely utilized antipyretic drug in the ICU (47).

It can be administered orally or per rectum at $650\text{ mg}$ every 6 hours, or intravenously at $650\text{ mg}$ every 6 hours or $1{,}000\text{ mg}$ every 8 hours. *Acetaminophen carries serious hepatotoxic potential; the maximum safe daily dose in adults is $3\text{ g/day}$* (FDA guidance). It is strictly contraindicated in patients with acute or chronic hepatic impairment (see Chapter 52).

#### NSAIDs and Cooling Blankets

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) evaluated for antipyresis in critically ill patients include intravenous ibuprofen ($10\text{ mg/kg}$ up to $800\text{ mg}$ every 6 hours) (48) and intravenous ketorolac ($0.5\text{ mg/kg}$ single dose) (49). Use of NSAIDs in the ICU remains constrained by concerns regarding acute kidney injury and gastrointestinal bleeding.

*Physical Cooling Blankets are Counterproductive in Fever:* External cooling blankets are widely used but physiologically detrimental in infection-induced fever.

The febrile hypothalamus commands the body to achieve a higher temperature via cutaneous vasoconstriction (to limit heat loss) and shivering (to augment heat production). Applying an external cooling blanket to a febrile patient directly stimulates peripheral cold receptors, aggravating cutaneous vasoconstriction and triggering violent shivering. This dramatically elevates systemic oxygen consumption and metabolic workload. Cooling blankets are indicated in hyperthermia and heat stroke, *not* in infectious fever.

### Wagner-Jauregg and Pyrotherapy</summary><div class="details-content">

The 1927 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to Austrian psychiatrist Julius Wagner-Jauregg for his pioneering discovery of the therapeutic value of fever in the treatment of neurosyphilis.

Wagner-Jauregg noticed that psychiatric patients suffering from dementia paralytica (tertiary neurosyphilis) occasionally achieved long-lasting clinical remissions following intercurrent febrile infections (such as erysipelas). In 1917, he deliberately inoculated 9 patients suffering from dementia paralytica with tertian malaria (*Plasmodium vivax*) to induce controlled recurrent spikes of high fever (50).

Remarkably, 6 of the 9 patients experienced dramatic psychiatric remissions, with 3 returning to full productive lives (50). Subsequent histopathological examinations confirmed that *Treponema pallidum* spirochetes disappeared from brain tissue during episodes of hyperpyrexia. While pyrotherapy was ultimately abandoned following the discovery of penicillin, the profound lesson of Wagner-Jauregg's Nobel work—that fever is a potent antimicrobial weapon—remains a timeless biological principle for modern intensivists.
