# Asthma and COPD in the ICU

*Asthmatic distress calls for procedures which stimulate the sympathetic and dilate the bronchi.*
— Lawrason Brown, MD (1931)

Admission to an intensive care unit represents an ominous turn for patients with acute exacerbations of asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Among COPD patients admitted to the ICU, one in four will not survive hospitalization, and 40% of survivors require hospital readmission within one year. In acute severe asthma, the need for invasive mechanical ventilation is the single strongest predictor of mortality. Over 90 years after Brown's observation in 1931, adrenergic bronchodilation remains the cornerstone of critical care management.

## Executive Summary</summary>
<div class="details-content">

- **Monitoring Obstruction:** Clinical examination is unreliable for estimating the severity of airflow limitation. A peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) < 200 L/min or < 40% of predicted signifies severe obstruction. In acute severe asthma, a "normal" $\text{PaCO}_2$ (42 mmHg) reflects respiratory muscle exhaustion and CO2 retention in a patient who should be severely hyperventilating.
- **Aerosol Delivery Devices:**
  - *Jet Nebulizers:* Reservoir volume 3 to 6 mL. Only 12% of the aerosolized dose reaches the lung parenchyma; 66% impacts on the delivery apparatus and 20% is exhaled.
  - *Metered-Dose Inhalers (MDI):* High muzzle velocity (> 30 m/s or 60 mph) causes 80% oropharyngeal impaction. Adding a valved holding chamber (spacer) increases lung deposition from 9% to 20%. MDI with spacer achieves bronchodilation equivalent to nebulizers at one-seventh of the nominal drug dose.
- **Acute Asthma Pharmacotherapy:**
  - *Short-Acting $\beta_2$-Agonists (SABA):* Albuterol 2.5 to 5 mg by nebulizer (or 4 to 8 puffs by MDI with spacer) every 20 minutes for 3 doses, then 5 to 15 mg/hr by continuous nebulization for severe attacks. Aggressive dosing causes hypokalemia, tremors, and hyperlactatemia.
  - *Anticholinergics:* Inhaled ipratropium bromide (0.5 mg every 20 min for 3 doses) provides modest additive bronchodilation during the first 3 hours; it should be discontinued upon hospital admission.
  - *Systemic Corticosteroids:* 40 to 80 mg daily of oral prednisone or IV methylprednisolone. Benefit is not immediate (takes up to 12 hours). A 7- to 10-day course requires no tapering. Dexamethasone is the most potent anti-inflammatory agent (30 to 40 times hydrocortisone) but is not recommended.
  - *Adjunctive Agents:* Intravenous magnesium sulfate (2 g over 15 to 30 min) for severe refractory exacerbations. Ketamine infusion (0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg bolus, then 0.15 to 0.25 mg/kg/hr) provides bronchodilation and sedation.
- **Acute COPD Exacerbation:**
  - *Therapy:* Inhaled bronchodilators, systemic steroids (prednisone 40 mg daily for 5 days; NNT = 9 to prevent one treatment failure), and empiric antibiotics for 5 to 7 days targeting *H. influenzae*, *S. pneumoniae*, and *P. aeruginosa*.
  - *Controlled Oxygen Therapy:* Titrate $\text{FiO}_2$ strictly to an $\text{SaO}_2$ of 88% to 92% to prevent worsening hypercapnia from the Haldane effect and altered V/Q matching.
  - *Noninvasive Ventilation (NIV):* BiPAP is first-line; failure to reduce $\text{PaCO}_2$ by 10 mmHg within 1 hour indicates the need for invasive intubation.
- **Mechanical Ventilation & Dynamic Hyperinflation:** Expiratory flow limitation causes air trapping, dynamic hyperinflation, and intrinsic PEEP (auto-PEEP), increasing work of breathing and depressing venous return. Ventilator strategy: tidal volumes 6 to 8 mL/kg, low respiratory rates, and prolonged expiratory time.

## Basics of Airway Obstruction</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Clinical evaluation alone fails to quantify the severity of acute bronchospasm. Physical signs such as tachycardia, tachypnea, accessory muscle use, diaphoresis, and pulsus paradoxus (inspiratory drop in systolic blood pressure > 12 mmHg) reflect acute respiratory work but correlate poorly with the actual caliber of the airways.

### Peak Expiratory Flow Measurements

For spontaneously breathing patients, the peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR) provides an objective bedside assessment of airflow obstruction. PEFR is expressed as a percentage of standard predicted values based on age, gender, and height:

| % Predicted PEFR | Severity of Airflow Obstruction |
|---|---|
| **$\ge 70\%$** | Mild obstruction |
| **40% to 69%** | Moderate obstruction |
| **< 40%** | Severe obstruction |

- **Critical Thresholds:** A PEFR < 200 L/min indicates severe airway narrowing. Hypercapnia and acute ventilatory failure rarely occur until PEFR drops below 25% of predicted.
- **Effort Dependency:** Accurate PEFR requires a maximal inspiration to total lung capacity followed by a forced expiratory blast to residual volume. Patients in extreme respiratory distress are frequently unable to cooperate, limiting PEFR monitoring during initial resuscitation.

### Intrinsic PEEP and Dynamic Hyperinflation

When severe airway resistance prolongs expiration, the lungs cannot empty back to functional residual capacity before the next inspiratory cycle begins. Progressive air trapping creates positive alveolar pressure at end-expiration, designated *intrinsic PEEP* (auto-PEEP).

## Aerosol Drug Delivery Systems</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Inhaled bronchodilators are delivered via jet nebulizers or pressurized metered-dose inhalers (MDI), illustrated in Figure 23.1.

![Aerosol delivery devices](images/_page_3_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 23.1** Inhalation devices for bronchodilator therapy: pneumatic jet nebulizer (left) and pressurized metered-dose inhaler with spacer (right).

### Pneumatic Jet Nebulizers

Jet nebulizers pass high-pressure gas (50 psi) through a narrow orifice, creating a Venturi effect that shears liquid medication into an aerosol droplet mist:
- *Chamber Volume:* Small-volume nebulizers possess a reservoir volume of 3 to 6 mL and nebulize to completion in < 10 minutes. Large-volume nebulizers (> 200 mL) are utilized for continuous aerosolization.
- *Lung Deposition:* As shown in Table 23.1, only 12% of the nominal dose reaches the pulmonary airways; 66% is lost on the nebulizer walls and tubing, and 20% is exhaled.

| Site of Deposition | Small-Volume Nebulizer (2.5 mg) | MDI without Spacer (200 mcg) | MDI with Spacer (200 mcg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exhaled Gas** | 20% | 1% | 1% |
| **Apparatus / Device** | 66% | 10% | 78% |
| **Oropharynx** | 2% | 80% | 1% |
| **Lungs** | 12% | 9% | 20% |

*Note:* A nominal MDI dose of 200 mcg corresponds to 2 actuations (puffs). Data from Reference 11.

### Pressurized Metered-Dose Inhalers

An MDI contains active drug dissolved in a liquid fluorocarbon propellant under high pressure. When depressed, a metering valve dispenses a calibrated droplet volume:
- *Inertial Impaction:* The aerosol exits the canister at high muzzle velocity (> 30 m/s or 60 mph). Without an attachment, 80% of droplets impact the posterior oropharyngeal wall, with only 9% reaching the lower respiratory tract.
- *The Value of Spacers:* Adding a valved holding chamber (spacer) allows large propellant droplets to evaporate and decelerate, reducing oropharyngeal impaction to 1% and increasing pulmonary deposition to 20%. Valved holding chambers are mandatory whenever MDIs are utilized.

### Nebulizer versus Metered-Dose Inhaler Equivalence

As illustrated in Figure 23.2, clinical trials demonstrate equivalent bronchodilator efficacy between jet nebulizers and MDIs with spacers.

![Bronchodilator response nebulizer vs MDI](images/_page_4_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 23.2** Comparison of bronchodilator response ($\text{FEV}_1$) to albuterol administered via nebulizer (2.5 mg) versus metered-dose inhaler with spacer (0.36 mg, 4 puffs) in acute asthma. Three treatments were administered at 30-minute intervals. Both modalities produce identical bronchodilation despite a 7-fold nominal dose difference.

Accounting for pulmonary deposition (12% of 2.5 mg = 300 mcg for nebulizer vs 20% of 0.36 mg = 72 mcg for MDI), MDIs deliver one-fourth the lung-delivered dose of albuterol while achieving identical improvements in $\text{FEV}_1$.

### Inhaled Delivery during Mechanical Ventilation

During invasive mechanical ventilation, aerosolized drug is introduced into the inspiratory limb of the circuit. Endotracheal tubes and circuit humidification promote droplet rain-out, further reducing pulmonary delivery. Effective delivery is maximized by placing the spacer or vibrating mesh nebulizer 15 cm proximal to the Y-piece, reducing inspiratory flow rates, and applying an end-inspiratory pause.

## Acute Exacerbation of Asthma</summary>
<div class="details-content">

The protocol for managing acute severe asthma exacerbations in adults is outlined in Figure 23.3.

![Asthma management algorithm](images/_page_6_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 23.3** Stepwise clinical algorithm for the emergency management of acute asthma exacerbations based on the National Asthma Education and Prevention Program.

### The "Normal" $\text{PaCO}_2$ Danger Sign

Severe asthma attacks produce extreme respiratory drive and minute ventilation, typically lowering arterial $\text{PCO}_2$ to 25–30 mmHg. The finding of a "normal" $\text{PaCO}_2$ (e.g., 42 mmHg) indicates severe respiratory muscle exhaustion and inability to maintain alveolar ventilation. A normal or rising $\text{PaCO}_2$ represents impending respiratory arrest, mandating admission to the ICU.

### Pharmacologic Regimens

Pharmacotherapy for acute asthma is summarized in Table 23.2.

| Drug Class | Agent | Acute Dosing Regimen | Clinical Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Short-Acting $\beta_2$-Agonist** | Albuterol | Nebulizer: 2.5–5 mg q20min $\times 3$ doses, or MDI: 4–8 puffs q20min $\times 3$. Severe: continuous nebulization at 5–15 mg/hr for up to 3 hr. Maintenance: 2.5 mg q4–6h. | Rapid onset (< 5 min), peak 15–60 min, duration 2–5 hr. High doses cause hypokalemia, tremor, and lactic acidosis. |
| **Short-Acting $\beta_2$-Agonist** | Levalbuterol | Nebulizer: 1.25–2.5 mg q20min $\times 3$ doses, then hourly up to 3 hr. Maintenance: 1.25 mg q4–6h. | R-enantiomer of albuterol; achieves identical bronchodilation at half the dose but offers no clinical outcome advantage. |
| **Anticholinergic** | Ipratropium | 0.5 mg by nebulizer (or 8 puffs MDI) q20min $\times 3$ doses; can add 0.5 mg/hr to continuous albuterol. | Synergistic with albuterol during initial 3 hr. Discontinue upon hospital admission (no proven benefit in inpatient care). |
| **Systemic Corticosteroid** | Prednisone / Methylprednisolone | 40–80 mg daily orally (prednisone) or IV (methylprednisolone) in 1 or 2 divided doses for 7–10 days. | Anti-inflammatory onset takes up to 12 hr. Oral route is equal to IV. No taper required after 10 days. |
| **Intravenous Magnesium** | Magnesium Sulfate | 2 g $\text{MgSO}_4$ IV in 100 mL saline over 15–30 min. | Inhibits smooth muscle calcium entry; indicated for severe refractory attacks (PEFR < 40%). Inhaled route ineffective. |

### Systemic Corticosteroid Pharmacology

Systemic corticosteroids suppress airway mucosal inflammation, reduce vascular permeability, and upregulate $\beta_2$-adrenergic receptors. Key clinical facts include:
- Oral and intravenous routes demonstrate identical bioavailability and clinical efficacy.
- Steroids do not produce immediate bronchodilation; therapeutic benefits emerge approximately 12 hours after administration.
- Higher doses (> 100 mg prednisone daily) offer no additional benefit over standard regimens (40 to 80 mg daily).
- Table 23.3 details the relative potency of therapeutic corticosteroids.

| Corticosteroid | Equivalent Dose (mg) | Relative Anti-inflammatory Potency | Relative Mineralocorticoid Potency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hydrocortisone** | 20 | 1 | 20 |
| **Prednisone** | 5 | 3.5 | 1 |
| **Methylprednisolone** | 4 | 5 | 0.5 |
| **Dexamethasone** | 0.75 | 30 to 40 | 0 |

### Parenteral and Adjunctive Therapies

- **Subcutaneous $\beta$-Agonists:** In patients with intractable coughing or uncooperative states, subcutaneous epinephrine (0.3 to 0.5 mg every 20 min for 3 doses) or subcutaneous terbutaline (0.25 mg every 20 min for 3 doses) provides effective parenteral bronchodilation.
- **Intravenous Ketamine:** Ketamine exerts direct bronchodilator properties by inhibiting catecholamine reuptake and blocking NMDA receptors. In severe asthma refractory to standard therapy, ketamine (loading dose 0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg IV, followed by infusion of 0.15 to 0.25 mg/kg/hr for 1 to 5 hours) provides effective sedation and bronchodilation.
- **Oxygen Therapy:** Administer supplemental oxygen to keep $\text{SaO}_2 \ge 90\%$. Because peak inspiratory flows in severe asthma exceed 60 L/min, high-flow nasal cannula systems (delivering heated, humidified gas at 40 to 60 L/min) are frequently necessary.

## Acute Exacerbation of COPD</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) requiring ICU admission typically present with hypoxemic and hypercapnic respiratory failure triggered by viral or bacterial bronchitis, occult pulmonary embolism, or acute heart failure.

### Bronchodilator Therapy

Although chronic COPD exhibits fixed airway remodeling, acute bronchospasm contributes to exacerbations. Dosing regimens are detailed in Table 23.4.

| Medication | Inhalation Regimen | Clinical Considerations |
|---|---|---|
| **Albuterol** | 2.5–5 mg by nebulizer, or 2–8 puffs by MDI with spacer, every 4–6 hr | MDI with spacer is preferred over nebulizer due to equivalent efficacy at lower nominal dose. |
| **Levalbuterol** | 1.25–2.5 mg by nebulizer, or 2–8 puffs by MDI with spacer, every 4–6 hr | Purified active enantiomer; no proven superiority over racemic albuterol. |
| **Ipratropium** | 0.5 mg by nebulizer, or 2–8 puffs by MDI with spacer, every 4–6 hr | Combined with albuterol when response to single-agent $\beta_2$-agonist is incomplete. |
| **Corticosteroids** | 40 mg prednisone-equivalent orally or IV daily for 5 days | Accelerates recovery of lung function and shortens hospital stay. |

### Systemic Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids improve airflow and shorten recovery times in AECOPD:
- *Number Needed to Treat (NNT):* 9 patients must be treated with corticosteroids to prevent one treatment failure.
- *Number Needed to Harm (NNH):* A steroid-related adverse event (predominantly hyperglycemia) occurs in one of every 6 patients treated.
- *Regimen:* 40 mg oral prednisone daily for 5 days. Prolonged courses (> 5 days) provide no added clinical benefit and increase infectious complications.

### Antimicrobial Therapy

Approximately 50% of COPD exacerbations involve bacterial tracheobronchitis.
- *Indications:* Antibiotics are indicated if the patient demonstrates an increase in sputum purulence, an increase in sputum volume, or requires mechanical ventilation (invasive or noninvasive). Thus, virtually all ICU admissions for AECOPD warrant antibiotics.
- *Pathogens and Regimen:* Common pathogens include *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*, and, in advanced obstruction or ventilator dependency, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. A 5- to 7-day course of broad-spectrum coverage (e.g., cefepime or levofloxacin) is standard.

### Controlled Oxygen Therapy

In patients with chronic hypercapnia, administration of high-flow oxygen can trigger acute hypercapnic coma:
- *Mechanisms:* Worsening hypercapnia is driven primarily by worsening ventilation-perfusion mismatch (inhibition of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction) and the **Haldane effect** (oxygenation of hemoglobin displaces $\text{CO}_2$ into blood plasma), rather than depression of hypoxic ventilatory drive.
- *Target Saturation:* Supplemental oxygen must be strictly titrated to maintain an **$\text{SaO}_2$ of 88% to 92%** (and no higher).

### Noninvasive Ventilation (NIV)

Bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) is the first-line ventilatory support modality for acute hypercapnic respiratory failure in COPD. It reduces work of breathing, unloads fatigued inspiratory muscles, and reduces intubation rates by 60%. Failure of BiPAP to decrease $\text{PaCO}_2$ by at least 10 mmHg after 1 hour indicates the need for invasive endotracheal intubation.

## Mechanical Ventilation in Obstructive Disease</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Positive-pressure mechanical ventilation in patients with severe airflow obstruction carries high risks of barotrauma, pneumothorax, and hemodynamic collapse.

### Dynamic Hyperinflation and Auto-PEEP

In normal lungs, expiration is completed before the next breath, returning alveolar pressure to zero (Figure 23.4, lower loop). In severe asthma or COPD, prolonged expiratory resistance prevents complete exhalation, shifting ventilation to higher lung volumes (Figure 23.4, upper loop).

![Dynamic hyperinflation pressure-volume curve](images/_page_12_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 23.4** Pressure-volume loops demonstrating dynamic hyperinflation. Airflow obstruction shifts the tidal breath to the flat, noncompliant portion of the curve (upper loop), dramatically increasing the elastic work of breathing.

This trapped gas generates **intrinsic PEEP (auto-PEEP)**:
- *Increased Work of Breathing:* Breathing on the upper, noncompliant segment of the pressure-volume curve requires high inspiratory muscular effort.
- *Hemodynamic Compromise:* High intrathoracic pressure compresses the pulmonary vascular bed, impedes vena caval return, decreases right and left ventricular preload, and produces profound arterial hypotension.

### Waveform Detection of Air Trapping

Dynamic hyperinflation is identified at the bedside by observing the expiratory flow-time scalar on the ventilator display (Figure 23.5).

![Ventilator flow waveforms](images/_page_13_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 23.5** Expiratory flow-time waveforms during mechanical ventilation. Top panel: normal tracing where expiratory flow returns to zero before the next breath. Bottom panel: ongoing expiratory flow at the instant the next breath begins (arrows), diagnostic of dynamic hyperinflation and intrinsic PEEP.

### Protective Ventilator Strategies

Mechanical ventilation in obstructive disease is adjusted to prioritize lung emptying:
1. **Low Tidal Volumes:** Use 6 to 8 mL/kg of predicted body weight to limit end-inspiratory alveolar distension.
2. **Prolong Expiratory Time ($T_E$):**
   - Reduce the mandatory respiratory rate (10 to 14 breaths/min).
   - Increase the inspiratory flow rate (70 to 80 L/min) to deliver the breath rapidly, ensuring an inspiratory-to-expiratory (I:E) ratio of 1:3 or 1:4.
3. **Permissive Hypercapnia:** Accept elevated $\text{PaCO}_2$ (60 to 80 mmHg) and respiratory acidosis (pH $\ge 7.20$) to avoid aggressive ventilation that worsens air trapping.
4. **Emergency Decompression:** Sudden cardiovascular collapse in an intubated asthmatic indicates tension pneumothorax or severe auto-PEEP. Immediately disconnect the endotracheal tube from the ventilator circuit and allow the lungs to passively deflate for 20 to 30 seconds.

## Principles of Practical ICU Management</summary>
<div class="details-content">

The intensive care approach to life-threatening exacerbations of obstructive airways disease is summarized as follows:
1. Administer aggressive inhaled $\beta_2$-agonists (albuterol) and systemic corticosteroids (40 to 80 mg prednisone-equivalent) to all patients.
2. Add inhaled ipratropium for the first 3 hours of severe asthma, and initiate empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (5 to 7 days) for COPD exacerbations.
3. In COPD, strictly titrate oxygen to an $\text{SaO}_2$ of 88% to 92%.
4. Utilize noninvasive positive-pressure ventilation (BiPAP) as the initial support for acute hypercapnia, maintaining a low threshold for intubation if $\text{PaCO}_2$ does not improve by 10 mmHg within 1 hour.
5. If invasive mechanical ventilation is required, prevent barotrauma and auto-PEEP by employing low tidal volumes (6 to 8 mL/kg), slow respiratory rates, high peak inspiratory flows, and permissive hypercapnia.
