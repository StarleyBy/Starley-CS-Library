# Acute Stroke in the ICU

Acute stroke is a devastating neurovascular emergency, presenting either as ischemic cerebral infarction (accounting for approximately 85% of cases) or acute intracranial hemorrhage (spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage [10%–15%] and aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage [5%]) (1–3).

This chapter presents an evidence-based approach to the critical care management of acute stroke, addressing cardiorespiratory stabilization, blood pressure titration, reperfusion safety, reversal of antithrombotic therapy, hyperosmolar control of cerebral edema, and the prevention of delayed cerebral ischemia (1–3).

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Key Principles and Clinical Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

- **General Neuroprotective Measures in Acute Ischemic Stroke (AIS):** Initial care mandates airway protection, normoxia ($\text{SaO}_2 \ge 94\%$, supplemental oxygen reserved strictly for hypoxemic patients with $\text{SaO}_2 \lt 90\%$), strict glycemic control (target blood glucose 140–180 mg/dL, avoiding dextrose-containing intravenous fluids), and aggressive normothermia (treating fever $\gt 38.0^\circ\text{C}$ with antipyretics to limit ischemic penumbral injury).
- **Permissive Hypertension vs Blood Pressure Reduction in AIS:**
  - *Reperfusion Candidates:* Blood pressure must be lowered to $< 185/110\text{ mmHg}$ prior to intravenous thrombolysis (alteplase or tenecteplase), and maintained $< 180/105\text{ mmHg}$ for $\ge 24\text{ hours}$ post-thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy to prevent catastrophic intracranial hemorrhage.
  - *Non-Reperfusion Patients:* Permissive hypertension is essential to preserve collateral microvascular perfusion in the ischemic penumbra; antihypertensive pharmacotherapy is withheld unless blood pressure exceeds $> 220/120\text{ mmHg}$ during the first 72 hours, targeting a modest 15% reduction in the first 24 hours. Preferred IV agents: labetalol ($10\text{ mg}$ IV bolus, infusion $2\text{–}8\text{ mg/min}$), nicardipine ($5\text{–}15\text{ mg/hr}$), or clevidipine ($1\text{–}21\text{ mg/hr}$). Pure beta-blockers and sodium nitroprusside are contraindicated.
- **Malignant Cerebral Edema and Osmotherapy:** Vasogenic and cytotoxic brain swelling peaks 3 to 5 days post-stroke. In patients demonstrating neurological deterioration or intracranial hypertension, bolus hypertonic saline ($3\%\ \text{NaCl}\ 250\text{ mL}$ or $5\text{ mL/kg}$; $23.4\%\ \text{NaCl}\ 30\text{ mL}$ or $0.68\text{–}2\text{ mL/kg}$) is preferred over mannitol, targeting serum sodium 145–155 mEq/L. Emergent decompressive hemicraniectomy is lifesaving in malignant middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarction if performed within 48 hours in patients $\le 60\text{ years}$ of age.
- **Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ICH):** 30-day mortality approaches 50%, driven by early hematoma expansion within the first 6 hours. Acute systolic blood pressure reduction to a target of $< 140\text{ mmHg}$ (maintaining SBP between 130 and $140\text{ mmHg}$) within 2 to 6 hours safely limits hematoma growth.
- **Urgent Reversal of Anticoagulants in Intracranial Bleeding:**
  - *Warfarin:* Intravenous Vitamin K $10\text{ mg}$ plus 4-factor Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (4-factor PCC): INR 2–4: $25\text{ U/kg}$; INR 4–6: $35\text{ U/kg}$; INR $> 6$: $50\text{ U/kg}$ (maximum single dose $5{,}000\text{ U}$).
  - *Dabigatran (Direct Thrombin Inhibitor):* Idarucizumab $5\text{ g}$ IV ($2 \times 2.5\text{ g}$ boluses separated by 15 min); high-dose 4-factor PCC ($50\text{ U/kg}$) if idarucizumab is unavailable.
  - *Oral Factor Xa Inhibitors (Apixaban, Rivaroxaban):* Andexanet alfa: Low-dose regimen ($400\text{ mg}$ bolus at $30\text{ mg/min}$, then $4\text{ mg/min}$ for 120 min) for last dose apixaban $\le 5\text{ mg}$ / rivaroxaban $\le 10\text{ mg}$ or $\ge 8\text{ hours}$ ago; High-dose regimen ($800\text{ mg}$ bolus at $30\text{ mg/min}$, then $8\text{ mg/min}$ for 120 min) for last dose apixaban $\gt 5\text{ mg}$ / rivaroxaban $\gt 10\text{ mg}$ or $\lt 8\text{ hours}$ ago. High-dose 4-factor PCC ($50\text{ U/kg}$) is the alternative.
  - *Heparins:* Protamine sulfate ($1\text{ mg}$ per 100 U unfractionated heparin; $1\text{ mg}$ per $1\text{ mg}$ enoxaparin).
  - *Antiplatelet Agents:* Desmopressin (DDAVP) $0.4\ \mu\text{g/kg}$ IV. Routine platelet transfusions increase mortality and are contraindicated in spontaneous non-surgical ICH.
- **Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (aSAH):** Ruptured saccular aneurysm carries a 30-day mortality of 40%–50%. Rebleeding risk peaks in the first 24 hours (with 70% mortality); maintain systolic blood pressure $< 160\text{ mmHg}$ until the aneurysm is secured via endovascular coiling or surgical clipping within 24 to 72 hours. Delayed Cerebral Ischemia (DCI / vasospasm) peaks at days 4 to 14: enteral nimodipine ($60\text{ mg}$ orally every 4 hours for 21 days) improves neurological outcomes; treat clinical DCI with induced euvolemic hypertension (norepinephrine or phenylephrine), abandoning legacy "Triple-H" therapy.

### Hemodynamic Management: Permissive Hypertension vs BP Reduction</summary><div class="details-content">

Elevated arterial blood pressure is documented in over 60% of patients presenting with acute stroke (9). This hypertensive surge represents a homeostatic compensatory reflex aimed at driving collateral blood flow through the circle of Willis and leptomeningeal anastomoses into the ischemic penumbra, where cerebral autoregulation is paralyzed.

Clinical guidelines establish distinct blood pressure targets depending on whether reperfusion therapy is administered (2):

- 1. *Thrombolytic / Thrombectomy Reperfusion:* Prior to initiating intravenous thrombolysis (alteplase or tenecteplase), blood pressure must be safely lowered to $< 185/110\text{ mmHg}$. Following thrombolysis or successful endovascular thrombectomy, blood pressure must be maintained below $180/105\text{ mmHg}$ for at least 24 hours to prevent reperfusion injury and fatal intracerebral hemorrhage (2,10).
- 2. *Non-Reperfusion Patients (Permissive Hypertension):* In patients ineligible for reperfusion, arterial hypertension must be permitted during the first 48 to 72 hours. Antihypertensive therapy is withheld unless systolic blood pressure exceeds $220\text{ mmHg}$ or diastolic blood pressure exceeds $120\text{ mmHg}$ (2). When treatment is required, blood pressure should be reduced by no more than 15% during the first 24 hours.

#### Antihypertensive Drug Selection and Hypotension Avoidance

First-line intravenous antihypertensives in acute stroke are labetalol, nicardipine, and clevidipine, which demonstrate equivalent efficacy and safety in randomized trials (Table 47.2) (12,13).

Pure beta-blockers (e.g., metoprolol, propranolol) are contraindicated in acute stroke because they reduce cardiac output without reducing peripheral vascular resistance, precipitating cerebral hypoperfusion (2). Sodium nitroprusside is avoided due to the risk of intracranial vasodilation, which increases intracranial pressure and provokes "intracerebral steal".

*Danger of Iatrogenic Hypotension:* Uncontrolled blood pressure drops are catastrophic in acute ischemic stroke. A systolic blood pressure drop below $140\text{ mmHg}$ in non-recanalized large vessel occlusion leads to rapid penumbral infarction. If blood pressure inadvertently plummets, intravenous fluids and vasopressors (phenylephrine or norepinephrine) must be administered immediately.

### Echocardiography and Cardioembolic Sources</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardioembolic mechanisms account for 20% to 30% of all ischemic strokes (14). Atrial fibrillation is the dominant etiology, but other sources encompass left ventricular mural thrombi following acute anterior myocardial infarction, dilated cardiomyopathy, infective or nonbacterial endocarditis, and paradoxical embolism via a patent foramen ovale (PFO).

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is routinely performed, but transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) exhibits far superior sensitivity for detecting left atrial appendage thrombi, aortic arch atheromas, and interatrial septal abnormalities (14,25).

#### Patent Foramen Ovale and Paradoxical Embolism

A patent foramen ovale (PFO) is present in approximately 25% of the general population (25). In cryptogenic stroke patients under 60 years of age, PFO prevalence exceeds 50%, implicating paradoxical embolism from occult deep vein thrombosis as the causative mechanism (25).

As illustrated in Figure 47.1, agitated saline contrast echocardiography during transesophageal imaging definitively confirms right-to-left intracardiac shunting when microbubbles appear in the left atrium within 3 cardiac cycles of right atrial opacification (26).

Coexisting atrial septal aneurysm (defined as hypermobile interatrial septal excursion $\ge 10\text{ mm}$) markedly amplifies stroke recurrence risk. In young patients with cryptogenic stroke and high-risk PFO anatomy, transcatheter percutaneous PFO closure combined with antiplatelet therapy significantly reduces recurrent stroke compared to medical therapy alone (25).

### Neurological Complications: Hemorrhagic Transformation and Cerebral Edema</summary><div class="details-content">

The most feared complication of acute ischemic stroke and reperfusion therapy is hemorrhagic transformation (HT), illustrated in Figure 47.2 (27).

Symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage occurs in approximately 6% of patients treated with intravenous alteplase and up to 10% following mechanical thrombectomy (27,28). HT is driven by ischemic breakdown of the blood-brain barrier, enzymatic microvascular degradation by matrix metalloproteinases, and reperfusion injury.

<span id="fig-47-2"></span>
![FIGURE 47.2](images/_page_6_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 47.2** Noncontrast CT images of the brain from a patient who experienced an abrupt neurological deterioration following thrombolytic therapy, showing massive hemorrhagic transformation. Data from Reference 27.

Any sudden neurological deterioration, severe headache, acute hypertension, or vomiting following thrombolytic therapy mandates immediate cessation of the infusion, emergent noncontrast head CT, and stat laboratory coagulation testing (fibrinogen, PT/INR, PTT, platelets) (2).

Reversal of thrombolytic-induced bleeding requires cryoprecipitate (10 units IV to restore fibrinogen $> 150\text{ mg/dL}$), tranexamic acid ($1{,}000\text{ mg}$ IV over 10 min), and neurosurgical consultation (2,28).

#### Osmotherapy for Malignant Cerebral Edema

Ischemic cerebral edema begins within hours due to cellular cytotoxic swelling, followed by vasogenic capillary leak that peaks between days 3 and 5 post-stroke (29,30).

When swelling produces mass effect, midline shift, or clinical deterioration, hyperosmolar therapy is indicated to establish an osmotic gradient, drawing water from brain parenchyma into the intravascular space (31,32).

Hypertonic saline is superior to mannitol for lowering elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) in acute stroke, as detailed in Table 47.3 (31–33).

##### Post-Stroke Seizures and Decompressive Hemicraniectomy

Bolus administration of hypertonic saline produces rapid ICP reduction without the osmotic diuresis, hypovolemia, and renal toxicity characteristic of mannitol (32,33). Plasma sodium should be monitored every 4 to 6 hours, maintaining a therapeutic ceiling of 145 to $155\text{ mEq/L}$ and serum osmolality $< 320\text{ mOsm/kg}$ (32).

Symptomatic post-stroke seizures occur in 20% of patients during the first week; approximately 25% recur within 4 years (34). Therapeutic anticonvulsant therapy (e.g., intravenous levetiracetam $1{,}000\text{–}1{,}500\text{ mg}$ q12h) is indicated for documented seizures, but routine primary seizure prophylaxis is not recommended (2).

*Surgical Decompressive Hemicraniectomy:* In patients $\le 60$ years of age with malignant middle cerebral artery (MCA) infarction (involving $> 50\%$ of the MCA territory), emergent decompressive hemicraniectomy performed within 48 hours reduces mortality from 70% to 22% and doubles favorable functional outcomes (30).

## Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Epidemiology, Presentation, and Hemodynamic Goals</summary><div class="details-content">

Spontaneous (non-traumatic) intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) accounts for 10% to 15% of all acute strokes, predominantly driven by chronic hypertension (causing lipohyalinosis of deep penetrating arterioles) and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (affecting lobar cortical vessels in the elderly) (3,35).

ICH carries a dismal prognosis: 30-day mortality approaches 50% (35), and two-thirds of survivors suffer permanent moderate-to-severe neurological disability (36).

![FIGURE 47.3](images/_page_9_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 47.3** Noncontrast CT image showing a large hyperdense intracerebral hematoma in the right hemisphere with intraventricular extension. Data from Reference 36.

As shown in Figure 47.3, large parenchymal hematomas frequently dissect into the ventricular system, causing acute obstructive hydrocephalus and catastrophic intracranial hypertension (36).

Active hematoma expansion occurs in up to 38% of patients within the first 6 hours of onset, representing the primary modifiable determinant of mortality and poor functional outcome (3,35).

*Acute Blood Pressure Lowering:* Immediate blood pressure reduction is paramount to arrest hematoma extravasation. The AHA/ASA guidelines recommend that in patients presenting with SBP between 150 and $220\text{ mmHg}$, acute lowering of SBP to a target of $< 140\text{ mmHg}$ (maintaining SBP between 130 and $140\text{ mmHg}$) using intravenous nicardipine, labetalol, or clevidipine is safe and effectively attenuates hematoma expansion (3).

##### Antiplatelet Reversal, DVT Prophylaxis, and Neurosurgical Consultation

In patients with antiplatelet-associated ICH (aspirin or clopidogrel), the randomized PATCH trial proved that *routine platelet transfusions increase in-hospital mortality and worsen disability*, likely by promoting systemic thromboembolism and local inflammatory neurovascular injury (3). Platelet transfusion is reserved exclusively for patients undergoing emergency craniotomy. Desmopressin (DDAVP, $0.4\ \mu\text{g/kg}$ IV) enhances platelet adhesion safely without transfusional risks (41).

*DVT Prophylaxis and Monitoring:* Sequential compression devices (SCDs) must be applied on admission. Pharmacologic thromboprophylaxis with low-dose subcutaneous LMWH can be safely initiated 24 to 48 hours post-bleed once follow-up neuroimaging confirms hematoma stability (3). Continuous EEG for 24 to 36 hours is indicated for any ICH patient with unexplained stupor or coma.

*Neurosurgical Intervention:* Emergent surgical hematoma evacuation or decompressive craniectomy is indicated for cerebellar hemorrhages $\ge 3\text{ cm}$ in diameter producing brainstem compression or hydrocephalus (3). Minimally invasive catheter evacuation with intraventricular alteplase is employed for obstructive intraventricular hemorrhage.

## Aneurysmal Subarachnoid Hemorrhage</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Pathophysiology, Clinical Features, and Early Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) accounts for 5% of acute strokes, resulting from the rupture of an intracranial saccular ("berry") aneurysm on the circle of Willis into the subarachnoid basal cisterns (Figure 47.4) (42,43).

The classic clinical hallmark is the sudden onset of an excruciating "thunderclap" headache, universally described by patients as the worst headache of their life, frequently accompanied by loss of consciousness, neck stiffness, and photophobia (42).

<span id="fig-47-4"></span>
![FIGURE 47.4](images/_page_12_Figure_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 47.4** Postmortem specimen showing blood in the subarachnoid space at the base of the brain following rupture of a saccular aneurysm. Data from Reference 43.

The 30-day mortality of aSAH approaches 40% to 50%, with one-third of deaths occurring within the first 24 hours (42,43).

*Aneurysmal Rebleeding:* The greatest immediate hazard is aneurysm re-rupture, which occurs in 8% to 23% of patients within the first 72 hours (peaking within 24 hours) and carries a catastrophic mortality of 70% (42). Prior to securing the aneurysm, systolic blood pressure must be strictly maintained $< 160\text{ mmHg}$ using intravenous titratable agents (nicardipine or labetalol) (42).

*Acute Hydrocephalus:* Occurs in 20% to 30% of cases due to mechanical obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid arachnoid granulation outflow by erythrocyte sludge, requiring emergent placement of an external ventricular drain (EVD).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Aneurysm Securing and Delayed Cerebral Ischemia</summary><div class="details-content">

Definitive prevention of rebleeding requires securing the ruptured aneurysm as expeditiously as feasible, ideally within 24 to 72 hours of admission (42).

In the International Subarachnoid Aneurysm Trial (ISAT), endovascular coiling achieved superior 1-year functional survival compared to open surgical clipping for anatomically amenable anterior circulation aneurysms (44).

#### Delayed Cerebral Ischemia (DCI) and Vasospasm

Delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI) is the primary cause of secondary mortality and long-term disability in patients surviving the initial aSAH rupture (42,45).

DCI typically manifests 4 to 14 days following rupture (peaking at days 7 to 8), characterized by new focal neurological deficits (hemiparesis, aphasia) or a decrease of $\ge 2$ points on the Glasgow Coma Scale lasting $\ge 1\text{ hour}$.

While traditionally attributed solely to cerebral arterial vasospasm, modern neurobiology recognizes DCI as a multifaceted neurovascular disorder involving microvascular thrombosis, cortical spreading depolarization, and neuroinflammation (42,45).

##### Nimodipine Neuroprotection

Enteral nimodipine ($60\text{ mg}$ orally or via nasogastric tube every 4 hours for 21 consecutive days) is the sole pharmacologic agent proven in randomized clinical trials to reduce DCI and improve neurological outcomes in aSAH (46). Nimodipine acts as a neuroprotective calcium channel antagonist; it must *never* be given intravenously (which causes fatal hypotension).

##### Induced Euvolemic Hypertension and Endovascular Rescue

Daily transcranial Doppler (TCD) ultrasonography screens for arterial vasospasm (mean flow velocity in the middle cerebral artery $> 120\text{ cm/s}$ indicates moderate vasospasm; $> 200\text{ cm/s}$ indicates severe vasospasm; Lindegaard ratio $> 3$).

When clinical DCI develops, current practice mandates *induced euvolemic hypertension* utilizing intravenous vasopressors (norepinephrine or phenylephrine) to titrate systolic blood pressure upward (target SBP 160–180 mmHg or MAP $> 100\text{ mmHg}$), driving collateral cerebral perfusion (42,45).

Legacy "Triple-H therapy" (hypervolemia, hemodilution, hypertension) has been abandoned because hypervolemia and hemodilution induce pulmonary edema, iatrogenic hyponatremia, and myocardial ischemia without improving brain oxygen delivery. Refractory vasospasm unresponsive to medical hypertension is treated via catheter-directed endovascular intra-arterial vasodilators (verapamil or nicardipine) or balloon angioplasty (42).

##### Hyponatremia and Cerebral Salt Wasting

Hyponatremia occurs in 30% to 50% of aSAH patients, primarily driven by *cerebral salt wasting (CSW)*—an acute natriuresis and volume contraction mediated by brain natriuretic peptide (BNP)—rather than the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). Free-water restriction is strictly contraindicated as it precipitates hypovolemic stroke; management relies on volume expansion with isotonic crystalloids, hypertonic saline, and mineralocorticoid therapy (fludrocortisone $0.1\text{–}0.2\text{ mg/day}$) (42).

### The Evolution of Evidence-Based Stroke Critical Care</summary><div class="details-content">

For decades, the intensive care management of acute stroke was dominated by physiological dogma and empirical interventions that often lacked rigorous clinical validation.

As highlighted throughout this chapter, modern neurocritical care has discarded several longstanding paradigms: the myth of prophylactic platelet transfusions in intracerebral hemorrhage (which proved harmful in the PATCH trial), the uncritical use of "Triple-H therapy" in subarachnoid hemorrhage (which caused volume overload without benefit), and the premature suppression of compensatory post-ischemic hypertension.

By substituting unsubstantiated therapeutic enthusiasm with rigorous randomized trial evidence, intensivists can provide neurovascular care that reliably preserves cerebral tissue, minimizes secondary systemic insults, and maximizes functional recovery for patients confronting acute stroke.
