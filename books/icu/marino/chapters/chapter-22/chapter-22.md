# Acute Pulmonary Embolism

*Doubt is not a pleasant condition, but certainty is absurd.*
— Voltaire

Venous thromboembolism (deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism) is the third leading cause of cardiovascular mortality worldwide, after myocardial infarction and stroke, with an annual risk approaching 10% in adults over the age of 45. In intensive care unit patients, this risk is heightened significantly. Autopsy studies reveal pulmonary emboli in up to 30% of hospitalized patients, most of whom were undiagnosed prior to death. Conversely, when PE is clinically suspected, diagnostic imaging confirms the diagnosis in as few as 10% of cases.

## Executive Summary</summary>
<div class="details-content">

- **Diagnostic Dilemma:** Clinical manifestations of acute pulmonary embolism (PE) lack sensitivity and specificity. Approximately 30% of patients with acute PE have a normal arterial $\text{PO}_2$, and the alveolar-arterial oxygen gradient can be normal. Plasma D-dimer is elevated in up to 80% of ICU patients due to baseline critical illness, rendering it useless for ruling out PE in the ICU.
- **Diagnostic Imaging:**
  - *Bedside Duplex Ultrasound:* Identifies proximal deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in 30% to 50% of acute PE cases. A positive lower-extremity study confirms the diagnosis of venous thromboembolism and mandates anticoagulation, avoiding risky patient transport.
  - *CT Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA):* The diagnostic gold standard, with a negative predictive value of 95%. Radiation exposure averages 15 mSv (versus 0.02 mSv for a chest radiograph). Withholding anticoagulation is safe only for isolated, single subsegmental emboli without residual leg DVT or right ventricular dysfunction.
  - *Echocardiography:* Diagnostic findings include mobile right-heart thrombi (seen in 2% to 18%) and McConnell's sign (akinesis of the RV free wall with sparing of the apex, seen in 20%).
- **Risk-Stratified Management:**
  - *Low-Risk PE:* Stable hemodynamics, normal RV function. Immediate oral anticoagulation with direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs): apixaban (10 mg twice daily for 7 days, then 5 mg twice daily) or rivaroxaban (15 mg twice daily for 3 weeks, then 20 mg once daily). If $\text{CrCl} \le 30$ mL/min/1.73 $\text{m}^2$, use warfarin bridged with enoxaparin (1 mg/kg once daily).
  - *Intermediate-Risk (Submassive) PE:* RV dysfunction or elevated troponin without systemic hypotension. Requires hospital admission and anticoagulation with enoxaparin (1 mg/kg subcutaneously every 12 hours) for 24 to 48 hours to monitor for decompensation.
  - *High-Risk (Massive) PE:* Hemodynamic instability (systolic BP < 90 mmHg or shock). Immediate weight-based unfractionated heparin (80 U/kg bolus, then 18 U/kg/hr; use adjusted weight if BMI $\ge 40$ $\text{kg/m}^2$). Systemic thrombolysis with alteplase (100 mg IV over 2 hours, or 0.6 mg/kg over 15 minutes up to 50 mg for collapse). Catheter-directed thrombolysis or percutaneous mechanical thrombectomy for rescue.
- **Hemodynamics & Filters:** Aggressive fluid boluses worsen RV failure by pushing the septum into the LV; restrict fluid boluses to 500 mL. Norepinephrine is the vasopressor of choice. Inferior vena cava (IVC) filters are reserved for patients with absolute contraindications to anticoagulation or recurrent PE despite therapeutic anticoagulation.

## Clinical Evaluation and Diagnostic Challenges</summary>
<div class="details-content">

The classical triad of acute pulmonary embolism consists of dyspnea, tachycardia, and hypoxemia. However, these signs are prevalent among critically ill patients with non-embolic cardiopulmonary disease. The positive and negative predictive values of clinical findings are shown in Table 22.1.

| Clinical Finding | Positive Predictive Value | Negative Predictive Value |
|---|---|---|
| **Dyspnea** | 37% | 75% |
| **Tachycardia** | 47% | 86% |
| **Tachypnea** | 48% | 75% |
| **Pleuritic Chest Pain** | 39% | 71% |
| **Hemoptysis** | 32% | 67% |
| **Pulmonary Infiltrate** | 33% | 71% |
| **Pleural Effusion** | 40% | 69% |
| **Hypoxemia** | 34% | 70% |

- *Predictive Limits:* No single physical finding provides a positive predictive value greater than 50%. A negative predictive value of $\ge 98\%$ is required to reliably exclude disease.
- *Arterial Oxygenation:* Hypoxemia has a negative predictive value of only 70%, meaning that **30% of patients with acute PE have a normal arterial $\text{PO}_2$**. The alveolar-arterial $\text{PO}_2$ gradient is also normal in a significant subset of patients.
- *Atypical Manifestations:* Unexplained fever (< 102°F or 38.9°C) occurs in 15% of cases, and syncope occurs in 10% (indicating acute right ventricular strain). Acute PE accounts for < 10% of acute exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

### Limitations of the D-Dimer Assay in Critical Care

Cross-linked fibrin D-dimers are generated by endogenous clot fibrinolysis. In outpatients, a normal D-dimer has a high negative predictive value (92%) and rules out PE when clinical probability is low.

However, in intensive care units, D-dimer is non-specifically elevated by sepsis, surgery, trauma, systemic inflammation, acute renal impairment, malignancy, and advanced age. Up to 80% of ICU patients have elevated baseline D-dimer levels, yielding a positive predictive value of only 27%. Consequently, the D-dimer assay is clinically uninformative and not recommended for ICU patients.

## Diagnostic Imaging</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Definitive confirmation or exclusion of pulmonary embolism requires specialized imaging modalities, outlined in Table 22.2.

| Imaging Modality | Diagnostic Utility and Advantages | Limitations and Risks |
|---|---|---|
| **Duplex Ultrasound** | Identifies proximal lower-extremity DVT in 30% to 50% of acute PE cases, avoiding patient transport. | Negative study does not exclude PE (clot may have embolized completely or arisen from pelvic veins). |
| **CT Pulmonary Angiography (CTPA)** | Diagnostic method of choice; negative predictive value is 95%. | Radiocontrast nephrotoxicity if $\text{CrCl} < 30$ $\text{mL/min/1.73 m}^2$; high radiation dose (15 mSv). |
| **V/Q Lung Scintigraphy** | Alternative when contrast is contraindicated; low radiation (~ 2 mSv). | High rate of indeterminate scans (70% to 80%) in ICU patients with baseline lung disease. |
| **Echocardiography** | Identifies right ventricular strain, mobile right heart thrombi, and McConnell's sign. | Normal echocardiogram does not rule out submassive or non-massive PE. |

### Lower and Upper Extremity Venous Ultrasound

Because over 90% of pulmonary emboli originate from proximal deep vein thrombosis in the iliofemoral and popliteal veins, bedside duplex ultrasound is an ideal first-line examination in critically ill patients.

- **Duplex Ultrasound Accuracy:** In the lower extremities, compression duplex ultrasound demonstrates $\ge 95\%$ sensitivity, $\ge 97\%$ specificity, 97% positive predictive value, and 98% negative predictive value for proximal DVT.
- **Diagnostic Implication:** Approximately 30% to 50% of patients with acute PE have ultrasound-detectable proximal DVT. Detecting an incompressible proximal vein establishes the diagnosis of venous thromboembolism, justifying immediate anticoagulation without subjecting unstable patients to transport for CTPA.
- **Upper Extremity DVT:** Central venous catheters promote thrombosis of axillary and subclavian veins. Although upper-extremity DVT produces symptomatic PE in < 10% of cases, ultrasound provides 97% sensitivity and 96% specificity. Management requires immediate catheter removal and anticoagulation.

### Computed Tomographic Pulmonary Angiography

Multidetector helical CTPA is the definitive reference standard for diagnosing acute PE. Intravenous contrast visualizes filling defects down to subsegmental pulmonary arterial branches (Figure 22.1).

![CT pulmonary angiogram](images/_page_4_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22.1** Contrast-enhanced CT pulmonary angiogram showing an occlusive saddle filling defect in the left main pulmonary artery. AO = aorta, PA = pulmonary artery.

- **Subsegmental Emboli Management:** Isolated subsegmental emboli (vessel diameter < 2.5 mm) pose a clinical dilemma. Anticoagulation may be safely withheld only if:
  1. There is an isolated, single subsegmental filling defect.
  2. Bilateral lower-extremity duplex ultrasound shows no residual DVT.
  3. Echocardiography shows no right ventricular dysfunction.
  4. The patient is hemodynamically stable without severe hypoxemia.
- **Contrast Nephropathy:** Contrast dye carries significant nephrotoxic risk when renal clearance is impaired. Avoid contrast if $\text{CrCl} < 30$ $\text{mL/min/1.73 m}^2$ unless emergency CTPA is lifesaving.
- **Radiation Exposure:** CTPA delivers an average radiation dose of 15 mSv, compared with 0.02 mSv for a single-view chest radiograph and an annual occupational limit of 50 mSv. Alternative imaging should be prioritized in pregnancy and lactation.

### Ventilation-Perfusion Scintigraphy (V/Q Scan)

V/Q scanning utilizes inhaled technetium-99m DTPA aerosol and intravenous macroaggregated albumin. A normal scan reliably excludes PE, whereas multiple segmental mismatched perfusion defects confirm PE. However, pre-existing alveolar infiltrates, atelectasis, and COPD produce mismatched or matched defects, rendering 70% to 80% of scans in ICU patients indeterminate. Diagnostic utility is restricted to patients with normal baseline chest radiographs and contraindications to iodinated contrast.

### Echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiography evaluates the hemodynamic consequences of vascular obstruction on the right ventricle:
- **Direct Thrombus Visualization:** Mobile serpiginous thrombi transit through the right atrium or ventricle in 2% to 18% of acute PE cases (predominantly in massive PE).
- **McConnell's Sign:** Distinctive regional wall motion abnormality characterized by akinesia of the mid-free wall of the right ventricle with preserved or hyperdynamic apical contraction, reported in 20% of acute PE cases.

## Risk Stratification and Antithrombotic Management</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Acute pulmonary embolism is categorized into three clinical risk tiers to dictate therapeutic urgency, outlined in Table 22.3.

| Risk Classification | Clinical Criteria | Recommended Antithrombotic Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Low-Risk PE** | Hemodynamically stable, no RV dysfunction, no biomarker release | Start direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC):<br>• **Apixaban:** 10 mg BID for 7 days, then 5 mg BID.<br>• **Rivaroxaban:** 15 mg BID for 3 weeks, then 20 mg once daily.<br>• If $\text{CrCl} \le 30$ $\text{mL/min/1.73 m}^2$: warfarin bridged with enoxaparin 1 mg/kg once daily. |
| **Intermediate-Risk PE (Submassive)** | Normal systemic arterial pressure, but evidence of RV dysfunction or elevated troponin | Administer **enoxaparin** 1 mg/kg subcutaneously every 12 hours.<br>Monitor closely for 24 to 48 hours; switch to oral DOAC once stable. |
| **High-Risk PE (Massive)** | Hemodynamic instability: systolic BP < 90 mmHg, drop $\ge 40$ mmHg, or shock | Administer **unfractionated heparin** (80 U/kg IV bolus, then 18 U/kg/hr; target PTT 46–70 s).<br>Immediate **systemic thrombolysis** (alteplase 100 mg over 2 hours). Rescue: mechanical thrombectomy or ECMO. |

### Low-Risk Pulmonary Embolism

Hemodynamically stable patients without right ventricular strain are treated with direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs), which provide equivalent efficacy to warfarin without routine coagulation monitoring:
- *Apixaban:* 10 mg orally twice daily for 7 days, followed by 5 mg twice daily.
- *Rivaroxaban:* 15 mg orally twice daily with food for 3 weeks, followed by 20 mg once daily.
- *Renal Impairment:* DOACs are contraindicated if $\text{CrCl} \le 30$ $\text{mL/min/1.73 m}^2$. In this setting, warfarin is initiated with enoxaparin bridging (1 mg/kg subcutaneously once daily) until the INR reaches 2.0 to 3.0 for 2 consecutive days. Enoxaparin can be utilized down to a CrCl of 15 mL/min.

### Intermediate-Risk (Submassive) Pulmonary Embolism

Patients with normotension but echocardiographic RV dilation or positive cardiac troponins are hospitalized and observed for 24 to 48 hours due to the risk of decompensation.
- Initial therapy consists of therapeutic subcutaneous enoxaparin (1 mg/kg every 12 hours).
- Systemic thrombolysis is generally withheld because major bleeding (10% to 12%) and intracranial hemorrhage (1% to 2%) outweigh the mortality reduction in stable patients. Thrombolysis is reserved for patients who deteriorate clinically during observation.

### High-Risk (Massive) Pulmonary Embolism

Massive PE presents with obstructive shock, syncope, or cardiac arrest due to acute right ventricular failure.

- **Unfractionated Heparin:** UFH is preferred because its short half-life allows rapid reversal with protamine if emergency intervention or bleeding occurs. The weight-based protocol employs an 80 U/kg IV bolus, followed by 18 U/kg/hr titrated to an aPTT of 46 to 70 seconds (checked at 6 hours).
- **Heparin Dosing in Severe Obesity:** In patients weighing over 130 kg (286 lbs) or with a $\text{BMI} \ge 40$ $\text{kg/m}^2$, dosing based on actual weight leads to overdosing. Dosing should be calculated using the Adjusted Body Weight:

$$\text{Adjusted Weight (kg)} = \text{IBW} + 0.4 \times (\text{Actual Weight} - \text{IBW})$$
*(Equation 22.1)*

- **Systemic Fibrinolysis:** Recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (alteplase) is infused at **100 mg IV over 2 hours**. In impending or active cardiovascular collapse, an accelerated regimen of **0.6 mg/kg IV over 15 minutes** (maximum dose 50 mg) is administered. Major contraindications include recent stroke (< 6 months), active bleeding, or major surgery/trauma within 3 weeks.
- **Percutaneous Mechanical Thrombectomy:** Catheter-based suction thrombectomy can rapidly extract massive clots from the pulmonary trunk (Figure 22.2). This produces immediate reduction in pulmonary artery pressures and right ventricular strain, serving as a primary alternative when thrombolysis is contraindicated or unsuccessful.

## Hemodynamic Management and Inferior Vena Cava Filters</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Acute right ventricular afterload elevation causes acute RV dilation, shifting the interventricular septum toward the left ventricle and impairing left ventricular filling.

### Hemodynamic Support

- **Cautious Volume Resuscitation:** Aggressive crystalloid loading exacerbates right ventricular dilatation, worsens tricuspid regurgitation, and compromises coronary perfusion to the strained RV free wall. Fluid boluses should be restricted to a single trial of $\le 500$ mL, guided by real-time bedside echocardiography. A normal central venous pressure (4 to 6 mmHg) indicates hypovolemia that may benefit from fluid.
- **Vasopressor Selection:** **Norepinephrine** is the agent of choice for PE-induced shock. It restores systemic arterial pressure, increases right coronary artery perfusion pressure, and improves RV contractility without significantly increasing pulmonary vascular resistance.
- **Inotropic Support:** Dobutamine may be infused at low doses (2.5 to 5 mcg/kg/min) in normotensive patients with low cardiac output and RV strain, but must be avoided as monotherapy in hypotensive patients due to peripheral vasodilation.

### Inferior Vena Cava (IVC) Filters

Anticoagulation prevents clot propagation and recurrence, but does not dissolve existing clots. When anticoagulation cannot be administered safely, mechanical interception with an IVC filter is indicated.

![Greenfield vena cava filter](images/_page_10_Picture_7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22.3** The conical Greenfield vena cava filter. The conical geometry allows trapped thrombi to fill the apex up to 75% of filter volume while preserving central laminar blood flow along the perimeter.

- **Indications:**
  1. Proven acute PE or proximal DVT in a patient with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation (active major bleeding, impending brain surgery).
  2. Recurrent, documented pulmonary embolism despite therapeutic anticoagulation.
- **Filter Design and Complications:** Modern conical filters (Figure 22.3) prevent caval occlusion by trapping thrombi within the central apex. Retrievable filters should be removed once the bleeding risk abates, typically within 60 days. Prophylactic filter placement in trauma patients reduces acute PE by 60%, but causes a 70% increase in deep venous thrombosis, showing no net long-term mortality benefit. Filter-related infection is extraordinarily rare (< 1%).

## Diagnostic Uncertainty in Practice</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Clinical experience with acute venous thromboembolism reveals several unavoidable realities:
1. When pulmonary embolism is clinically suspected based on routine physical examination, diagnostic imaging confirms the diagnosis in as few as 10% of cases.
2. In the majority of fatal pulmonary emboli discovered on autopsy, the source DVT and initial embolization were entirely clinically silent.
3. Therefore, meticulous primary venous thromboprophylaxis (pharmacologic and mechanical), rather than diagnostic vigilance alone, remains the most effective strategy for reducing pulmonary embolism mortality in intensive care units.
