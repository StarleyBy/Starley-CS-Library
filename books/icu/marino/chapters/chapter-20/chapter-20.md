# Acute Coronary Syndromes

*The study of the causes of things must be preceded by the study of things caused.*
— John Hughlings Jackson (1835–1911)

In 1980, the discovery that transmural myocardial infarction (MI) resulted from an occlusive thrombus in a coronary artery revolutionized cardiac critical care. This led to thrombolytic therapy in the mid-1980s, followed by percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) with balloon angioplasty and stenting. Reperfusion therapies contributed to a 20% decline in annual coronary mortality from 2010 to 2020. Nevertheless, coronary artery disease remains the leading cause of death in the United States.

## Executive Summary</summary>
<div class="details-content">

- **Pathogenesis:** Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) result from rupture or erosion of an inflamed atherosclerotic plaque, triggering platelet aggregation and coronary thrombosis. Myeloperoxidase (MPO) and reactive oxygen species play a central role in plaque vulnerability.
- **Classification:** Categorized by 12-lead ECG and cardiac biomarkers into:
  - *ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI):* Complete coronary occlusion resulting in transmural ischemia.
  - *Non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI):* Incomplete occlusion with myocardial necrosis (elevated high-sensitivity cardiac troponin).
  - *Unstable angina (UA):* Transient ischemia without myocardial necrosis (normal troponin).
- **Diagnosis:** 12-lead ECG acquired within 10 minutes of arrival. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) measured at presentation and at 1 hour (a rise or fall > 10% confirms acute necrosis).
- **Reperfusion for STEMI:**
  - *Primary PCI:* Treatment of choice if door-to-balloon time is $\le 90$ minutes (or $\le 120$ minutes if transferred to a PCI-capable center).
  - *Fibrinolytic therapy:* Indicated when PCI transfer time exceeds 120 minutes, provided symptom onset is < 12 hours. Target door-to-needle time is $\le 30$ minutes. Preferred agents are tenecteplase, reteplase, or alteplase.
- **Cardioprotective Measures:**
  - *Supplemental Oxygen:* Indicated **only if $\text{SaO}_2 < 90\%$**. Hyperoxia induces coronary vasoconstriction and augments reperfusion injury.
  - *Nitroglycerin:* Sublingual or IV infusion for persistent ischemic pain. Contraindicated in right ventricular infarction, severe aortic stenosis, or recent use (within 24–48 hours) of phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors.
  - *Beta-Blockers:* Metoprolol IV or orally reduces myocardial oxygen demand. Contraindicated in acute decompensated heart failure, systolic BP < 120 mmHg, bradycardia, AV block, or cocaine-induced ischemia.
- **Antithrombotic Therapy:**
  - *Dual Antiplatelet Therapy (DAPT):* Chewable aspirin (162–325 mg) immediately, plus a P2Y12 inhibitor (ticagrelor 180 mg, prasugrel 60 mg, or clopidogrel 300–600 mg).
  - *Anticoagulation:* Unfractionated heparin (60 U/kg bolus, max 4,000 U, then 12 U/kg/hr) for PCI, or enoxaparin (30 mg IV bolus, then 1 mg/kg SC q12h).
- **Acute Aortic Dissection:** Must be excluded in patients presenting with sharp, tearing chest or back pain. Spontaneous temporary pain relief occurs in many patients and represents a dangerous diagnostic trap. Contrast-enhanced CT or MRI confirms the intimal flap. Medical therapy requires aggressive impulse control (esmolol or labetalol to heart rate < 60 bpm and systolic BP 100–120 mmHg) before any vasodilator is added. Ascending (Type A) dissection is a surgical emergency.

## Coronary Thrombosis and Pathogenesis</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Acute myocardial infarction is caused by an occlusive thrombus overlying a disrupted coronary atherosclerotic plaque. As illustrated in Figure 20.1, rupture or superficial erosion of the fibrous cap exposes thrombogenic lipid core components and collagen to circulating platelets and clotting factors.

Plaque rupture is driven by intense vascular inflammation and hemodynamic shear stress, which is highest at branch points in the coronary arterial tree.

![Pathogenesis of acute coronary syndromes](images/_page_1_Figure_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20.1** Pathogenesis of acute coronary syndromes. Rupture of a vulnerable atherosclerotic plaque releases thrombogenic core contents, triggering platelet adhesion, activation, and thrombus formation (upper panel), culminating in luminal occlusion (lower panel).

### Role of Oxidative Stress and Myeloperoxidase

Vascular inflammation damages the endothelium through the generation of reactive oxygen species. A central mediator is *myeloperoxidase* (MPO), an enzyme abundantly released by activated neutrophils and monocytes.

- MPO generates hypochlorite (the oxidizing component of household bleach) from hydrogen peroxide and chloride ions.
- Hypochlorite oxidizes low-density lipoproteins (LDL) and high-density lipoproteins (HDL), consumes endothelial nitric oxide, and activates latent matrix metalloproteinases that degrade the fibrous cap.
- Human autopsy and clinical studies demonstrate dense MPO localization at sites of coronary plaque rupture, with plasma MPO levels correlating directly with CAD severity and short-term risk of adverse cardiac events.

## Clinical Syndromes and Diagnostic Evaluation</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Acute coronary thrombosis produces three distinct clinical syndromes grouped under the umbrella of acute coronary syndromes (ACS):
1. **ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (STEMI):** Complete coronary occlusion producing transmural myocardial necrosis.
2. **Non-ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (NSTEMI):** Incomplete or intermittent occlusion producing subendocardial necrosis with biomarker release.
3. **Unstable Angina (UA):** Severe myocardial ischemia without sufficient necrosis to release detectable troponin into the bloodstream.

NSTEMI and unstable angina share common pathophysiologic and diagnostic features and are collectively designated *non-ST-elevation ACS* (NSTE-ACS).

### Clinical Presentation

Approximately 80% of ACS patients present with retrosternal chest discomfort, commonly described as pressure, squeezing, fullness, or heaviness lasting > 15 minutes. Discomfort frequently radiates to the left arm, shoulders, neck, jaw, or epigastrium. Accompanying symptoms include diaphoresis, dyspnea, nausea, and lightheadedness.

- *Atypical presentations:* Women, diabetics, and elderly patients frequently present with dyspnea alone, unexplained fatigue, syncope, or delirium without chest pain.
- *Non-ischemic features:* Chest pain that is pleuritic, sharp, reproducible by chest wall palpation, or lasting only a few seconds is unlikely to represent myocardial ischemia.
- *Nitroglycerin response:* Relief of chest pain following sublingual nitroglycerin is *not* a reliable diagnostic criterion for myocardial ischemia, because nitroglycerin readily relieves pain from esophageal spasm and non-cardiac conditions.

### Electrocardiography

A 12-lead ECG must be obtained and interpreted within 10 minutes of first medical contact. Key diagnostic criteria are summarized in Table 20.1.

| Syndrome | Electrocardiographic Pattern | Diagnostic Criteria |
|---|---|---|
| **STEMI** | Persistent ST-segment elevation | New ST-segment elevation at the J-point in $\ge 2$ contiguous leads: $\ge 1.5$ mm in women and $\ge 2.0$ mm in men in leads V2–V3, or $\ge 1.0$ mm in other contiguous leads. |
| **Posterior STEMI** | Reciprocal ST-segment depression in V1–V3 | Horizontal ST depression in leads V1–V3 with tall, upright T waves and tall R waves ($R/S > 1$). |
| **NSTE-ACS** | ST-segment depression | Horizontal or downsloping ST-segment depression $\ge 0.5$ mm in leads V2–V3, or $\ge 1.0$ mm in other contiguous leads. |
| **NSTE-ACS** | T-wave inversion | Symmetric T-wave inversion > 1.0 mm in $\ge 2$ contiguous leads with prominent R waves. |

*Diagnostic Caveat:* When coronary ischemia is clinically suspected, a new or presumed-new left bundle branch block (LBBB) or right bundle branch block (RBBB) is managed according to STEMI reperfusion protocols.

### High-Sensitivity Cardiac Troponin

Cardiac troponins I and T are specific markers of myocardial cell injury. High-sensitivity assays (hs-cTn) detect minute concentrations of circulating troponin within 3 hours of symptom onset.

- **0/1-Hour Protocol:** An initial blood sample is drawn at presentation, followed by a repeat sample 1 hour later.
- **Myocardial Infarction Rule-In:** An initial hs-cTn above the 99th percentile (99% upper reference limit) combined with a significant change (> 10% or assay-specific absolute rise) at 1 hour confirms acute myocardial necrosis.
- **Non-Coronary Elevations:** Chronic stable elevations of hs-cTn occur in heart failure, myocarditis, pulmonary embolism, sepsis, and end-stage renal disease. The hallmark of acute infarction is the dynamic rise or fall of troponin levels over time.

## Reperfusion Strategies</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Timely reperfusion of the ischemic myocardium limits infarct size, preserves left ventricular function, and reduces mortality. Three reperfusion modalities are available:
1. **Percutaneous Coronary Intervention (PCI):** Emergency coronary angiography followed by balloon angioplasty and stenting of the culprit lesion. This is the treatment of choice.
2. **Thrombolytic Therapy:** Intravenous administration of a fibrinolytic agent to dissolve the occluding thrombus when PCI is not promptly available.
3. **Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG):** Emergent surgical revascularization for failed PCI, ongoing ischemia, or mechanical complications (e.g., papillary muscle rupture).

### Reperfusion in STEMI

The choice of reperfusion strategy in STEMI depends primarily on the time from medical contact and the availability of a cardiac catheterization laboratory:

- **Primary PCI:** The preferred strategy when it can be performed within a door-to-balloon time of 90 to 120 minutes.
- **Interhospital Transfer:** Over 70% of hospitals lack on-site PCI capabilities. If a patient presents to a non-PCI center, transfer for primary PCI is indicated if the total door-to-balloon time (including transfer) remains $\le 120$ minutes.
- **Door-to-Balloon Timing:** As shown in Figure 20.2, mortality rises steeply when the door-to-balloon time exceeds 120 minutes. Guidelines establish 90 minutes as the institutional standard to maintain a 30-minute safety buffer.

![Mortality in relation to door-to-balloon time](images/_page_5_Figure_0.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20.2** In-hospital mortality in relation to time from hospital arrival to primary coronary angioplasty in STEMI. Prolonging door-to-balloon time beyond 120 minutes significantly increases mortality.

### Reperfusion in NSTE-ACS

Thrombolytic therapy has **no proven survival benefit in NSTE-ACS** and is contraindicated because it increases bleeding and intracranial hemorrhage risks without restoring patency. Revascularization is achieved exclusively via catheterization:

- **Immediate Invasive Strategy (< 2 hours):** Indicated for very-high-risk patients exhibiting refractory angina, hemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, acute heart failure, or life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias.
- **Early Invasive Strategy (within 24 hours):** Indicated for patients with established NSTEMI (elevated hs-cTn) or dynamic ST-T wave changes.
- **Selective Invasive Strategy:** Clinically stable low-risk patients with unstable angina may undergo noninvasive risk stratification prior to considering angiography.

## Thrombolytic Therapy</summary>
<div class="details-content">

When primary PCI cannot be accomplished within 120 minutes of first medical contact, fibrinolytic therapy should be administered within a target door-to-needle time of 30 minutes, provided there are no contraindications.

### Time-Dependent Survival Benefit

The efficacy of thrombolytic therapy declines dramatically with time. As demonstrated in Figure 20.3, the greatest mortality reduction occurs when lytics are administered within the first 1 to 2 hours of symptom onset. The survival benefit diminishes rapidly thereafter and becomes clinically insignificant beyond 12 hours. Consequently, thrombolysis is not recommended after 12 hours from symptom onset.

![Thrombolytic survival benefit versus time](images/_page_6_Figure_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20.3** Survival benefit of thrombolytic therapy in relation to the duration of symptoms prior to treatment. Mortality reduction is greatest within the first 2 hours and approaches zero after 12 hours.

### Fibrinolytic Regimens

Approved fibrin-specific thrombolytic agents are detailed in Table 20.2.

| Agent | Molecular Mechanism | Dosing Regimen | 90-Minute Patency Rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tenecteplase (TNK-tPA)** | Genetically engineered mutant tPA; high fibrin specificity and prolonged half-life | Single IV bolus over 5 seconds based on body weight:<br>• < 60 kg: **30 mg**<br>• 60–69 kg: **35 mg**<br>• 70–79 kg: **40 mg**<br>• 80–89 kg: **45 mg**<br>• $\ge 90$ kg: **50 mg** | 85% |
| **Reteplase (rPA)** | Non-glycosylated deletion mutant of tPA | **10 Units IV bolus**, repeated in 30 minutes as a second 10-Unit IV bolus | 84% |
| **Alteplase (tPA)** | Recombinant human tissue plasminogen activator | **15 mg IV bolus**, then **0.75 mg/kg** (max 50 mg) over 30 min, then **0.5 mg/kg** (max 35 mg) over 60 min (max total dose 100 mg over 90 min) | 73% to 84% |

*Clinical Note:* Tenecteplase is the preferred agent due to single-bolus administration, which minimizes dosing errors and accelerates time to treatment.

### Major Hemorrhagic Complications

Major bleeding requiring transfusion occurs in approximately 10% of patients receiving thrombolytic therapy, and intracranial hemorrhage occurs in 0.5% to 1.0% of cases.
- Bleeding is accompanied by profound systemic hypofibrinogenemia (plasma fibrinogen < 100 mg/dL).
- Emergency management includes immediate cessation of antithrombotic drugs and administration of cryoprecipitate (10 units) or fibrinogen concentrates to maintain fibrinogen > 150 mg/dL. Antifibrinolytics (e.g., tranexamic acid) are generally avoided because of the risk of coronary re-occlusion.

## Cardioprotective Measures</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Immediate medical interventions in ACS aim to restore the balance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand and alleviate pain. Recommended regimens are summarized in Table 20.3.

| Intervention | Dosing and Administration | Clinical Considerations and Cautions |
|---|---|---|
| **Supplemental Oxygen** | Administer via nasal cannula only if $\text{SaO}_2 < 90\%$ | Avoid in normoxemic patients. Causes coronary vasoconstriction and generates toxic reactive oxygen species during reperfusion. |
| **Nitroglycerin** | Sublingual tablet or oral spray 0.4 mg every 5 min up to 3 doses; IV infusion: initial 5 mcg/min, titrate by 5–10 mcg/min q5min | Relieves ischemic pain, lowers preload and afterload. Contraindicated in RV infarction, severe aortic stenosis, and after PDE-5 inhibitors (24–48 hr). Tachyphylaxis develops after 24 hr. |
| **Morphine Sulfate** | 4–8 mg IV bolus, followed by 2–8 mg IV every 5–15 min as needed | Reserved for severe pain refractory to nitrates. Causes venodilation and sedation; may delay gastrointestinal absorption of oral P2Y12 inhibitors. |
| **Metoprolol** | 2.5–5 mg IV over 2 min q5min up to 3 doses (total 15 mg); oral: 25–50 mg q6h | Reduces myocardial oxygen consumption and arrhythmogenicity. Contraindicated in acute heart failure, SBP < 120 mmHg, PR > 0.24 s, and cocaine use. |

### Judicious Use of Supplemental Oxygen

Supplemental oxygen should **not** be given routinely to normoxemic patients with ACS ($\text{SaO}_2 \ge 90\%$). Hyperoxia produces coronary vasoconstriction, reduces coronary blood flow by up to 30%, and augments reperfusion injury through burst production of superoxide and hydroxyl radicals. Oxygen is indicated only when arterial saturation drops below 90%.

### Nitroglycerin

Nitroglycerin induces systemic venodilation (reducing preload and wall stress) and dilates epicardial coronary arteries:
- *Administration:* 0.4 mg sublingual tablet or spray every 5 minutes up to 3 doses. Persistent pain, hypertension, or pulmonary congestion warrants continuous intravenous infusion (starting at 5 mcg/min, titrated upward).
- *Strict Contraindications:*
  1. *Right Ventricular Infarction:* Venodilation reduces preload in a preload-dependent failing right ventricle, precipitating profound shock.
  2. *Severe Aortic Stenosis:* Vasodilation causes precipitous drops in systemic arterial pressure.
  3. *Phosphodiesterase-5 Inhibitors:* Sildenafil or vardenafil within 24 hours, or tadalafil within 48 hours, induces life-threatening refractory hypotension.

### Morphine

Intravenous morphine (4 to 8 mg) is indicated for severe ischemic discomfort unresolved by sublingual nitroglycerin. It reduces sympathetic tone and vascular resistance. Morphine and fentanyl delay gastric emptying, potentially delaying absorption of oral antiplatelet agents, though the clinical impact on mortality is unproven.

### Beta-Receptor Antagonists

Early beta-blocker therapy reduces myocardial oxygen consumption, limits infarct size, and suppresses ventricular arrhythmias. Oral administration of metoprolol tartrate (25 to 50 mg every 6 hours) is standard. Intravenous metoprolol is reserved for severe acute hypertension or tachyarrhythmias.

- *Avoidance Criteria:* Beta-blockers must be withheld in acute heart failure, low-output states, systolic blood pressure < 120 mmHg, heart rate < 60 bpm, PR interval > 0.24 seconds, second- or third-degree heart block, and active bronchospasm.
- *Cocaine-Induced Ischemia:* Beta-blockers are contraindicated because unopposed alpha-adrenergic receptor stimulation exacerbates coronary vasoconstriction and hypertension.

## Antithrombotic Therapy</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Antithrombotic therapy prevents propagation of the culprit thrombus and prevents recurrent ischemic events. It comprises dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) and parenteral anticoagulation, summarized in Table 20.4.

| Drug Class | Agent | Loading Dose | Maintenance Dosing Regimen |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cyclooxygenase-1 Inhibitor** | Aspirin | 162–325 mg chewable non-enteric | 81 mg orally once daily indefinitely |
| **P2Y12 Receptor Inhibitor** | Ticagrelor | 180 mg orally | 90 mg orally twice daily |
| **P2Y12 Receptor Inhibitor** | Prasugrel | 60 mg orally | 10 mg orally once daily (5 mg if wt < 60 kg or age $\ge 75$) |
| **P2Y12 Receptor Inhibitor** | Clopidogrel | 300–600 mg orally | 75 mg orally once daily |
| **GP IIb/IIIa Inhibitor** | Eptifibatide | 180 mcg/kg IV bolus (max 22.6 mg) $\times 2$ (10 min apart) | 2.0 mcg/kg/min IV infusion (reduce by 50% if CrCl < 50 mL/min) |
| **GP IIb/IIIa Inhibitor** | Tirofiban | 25 mcg/kg IV bolus over 3 min | 0.15 mcg/kg/min IV infusion (reduce by 50% if CrCl < 30 mL/min) |

### Antiplatelet Regimens

Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin combined with a P2Y12 inhibitor) is mandatory for all ACS patients.

- **Aspirin:** Irreversibly acetylates platelet cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), blocking thromboxane A2 synthesis. A chewable, non-enteric coated dose of 162 to 325 mg should be administered immediately and chewed for buccal absorption. Daily low-dose aspirin (81 mg) is continued indefinitely.
- **P2Y12 Inhibitors:**
  - *Ticagrelor:* Direct-acting, reversible P2Y12 inhibitor with more rapid and potent platelet inhibition than clopidogrel. Preferred in both STEMI and NSTE-ACS. Contraindicated in patients with a history of intracranial hemorrhage.
  - *Prasugrel:* Potent, irreversible thienopyridine prodrug. Produces greater reduction in ischemic events and stent thrombosis than clopidogrel, but carries higher bleeding risk. Contraindicated in patients with prior stroke or TIA.
  - *Clopidogrel:* Irreversible thienopyridine prodrug requiring two-step hepatic CYP450 bioactivation. Used primarily when ticagrelor and prasugrel are contraindicated or unavailable, or when triple therapy with an anticoagulant is necessary.
- **Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa Inhibitors:** Eptifibatide and tirofiban block the final common pathway of platelet aggregation by preventing fibrinogen cross-linking. Reserved for high-risk patients undergoing PCI with high thrombus burden or "no-reflow" phenomenon.

### Anticoagulation Regimens

Parenteral anticoagulation is administered to all ACS patients upon diagnosis:
- **Unfractionated Heparin (UFH):** Preferred for patients proceeding to primary PCI. Dosed as an IV bolus of 60 U/kg (maximum 4,000 U), followed by an initial infusion of 12 U/kg/hr (maximum 1,000 U/hr), titrated to an activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) of 1.5 to 2.0 times control (50 to 70 seconds).
- **Low-Molecular-Weight Heparin (Enoxaparin):** Preferred in NSTE-ACS managed non-emergently. Administered as a 30 mg IV bolus, followed in 15 minutes by 1 mg/kg subcutaneously every 12 hours (dose reduced to 1 mg/kg once daily for CrCl < 30 mL/min; IV bolus omitted in age $\ge 75$ years).
- **Alternative Anticoagulants (HIT):** In patients with suspected or confirmed heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT):
  - *Bivalirudin:* Direct thrombin inhibitor for PCI (0.75 mg/kg bolus, 1.75 mg/kg/hr infusion; reduce to 1.0 mg/kg/hr for CrCl < 30 mL/min).
  - *Fondaparinux:* Synthetic Factor Xa inhibitor for medical management or thrombolysis (2.5 mg SC daily; contraindicated if CrCl < 30 mL/min).

## Long-Term Secondary Prevention</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Following stabilization and successful reperfusion, long-term medical therapy is instituted to prevent recurrent infarction, adverse ventricular remodeling, and death:

- **High-Intensity Statin Therapy:** Atorvastatin 80 mg daily reduces major adverse cardiovascular events regardless of baseline LDL cholesterol levels and is indicated for all ACS patients.
- **Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS) Inhibition:** Indicated in all patients with left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVEF $\le 40\%$), anterior infarction, hypertension, or diabetes:
  - *Angiotensin Receptor-Neprilysin Inhibitor (ARNI):* Sacubitril/valsartan (target 97/103 mg twice daily) provides superior neurohormonal blockade.
  - *ACE Inhibitors or ARBs:* Standard alternatives if ARNI is not tolerated.
- **Beta-Blockers:** Metoprolol succinate or carvedilol continued indefinitely, particularly in patients with reduced ejection fraction or residual ischemia.

## Acute Aortic Dissection</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Acute aortic dissection involving the ascending aorta (Stanford Type A) represents a catastrophic surgical emergency that frequently mimics acute coronary syndromes. Approximately 60% of untreated patients die within the first 48 hours, and 30% of cases are initially misdiagnosed.

### Pathophysiology

An intimal tear allows pulsatile arterial blood to dissect between the intima and media, creating a false lumen. Propagation can extend antegradely along the aorta and retrogradely toward the aortic root:
- Retrograde extension causes acute severe aortic valve regurgitation, coronary artery ostial compression (predominantly the right coronary artery, mimicking inferior STEMI), and pericardial tamponade.
- Antegrade extension compromises the brachiocephalic, carotid, or subclavian arteries, resulting in acute stroke, upper extremity pulse deficits, or visceral ischemia.

### Clinical Presentation and Diagnostic Traps

The classic presentation is the sudden onset of excruciating chest, back, or neck pain, characteristically described as "tearing", "ripping", or "sharp":
- **Spontaneous Pain Resolution:** In many patients, *chest pain subsides completely for hours or days* as the dissection decompresses into the false lumen. The recurrence of pain heralds impending fatal aortic rupture.
- **Physical Findings:** Severe hypertension is present in 50% of patients; hypotension indicates cardiac tamponade or acute aortic valve disruption. An early diastolic decrescendo murmur of aortic insufficiency occurs in 50%. Pulse deficits or a blood pressure differential > 20 mmHg between arms occurs in only 15% of cases.

### Diagnostic Imaging

Immediate definitive imaging is essential. Contrast-enhanced CT angiography is the primary modality due to rapid availability and 94% sensitivity and 87% specificity. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) have sensitivities of 98%.

![Contrast CT of ascending aortic dissection](images/_page_13_Picture_2.jpeg)

### Acute Medical Management

Medical therapy in acute aortic dissection focuses on "impulse control" — reducing the velocity of ventricular contraction ($dP/dt$) and shearing stress across the aortic wall. Recommended medications are listed in Table 20.5.

| Medication | Class / Mechanism | Dosing Regimen | Clinical Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Esmolol** | Ultra-short-acting $\beta_1$-blocker | 500 mcg/kg IV bolus, then 50 mcg/kg/min; titrate by 25 mcg/kg/min to max 200 mcg/kg/min | First-line agent of choice. Rapidly titratable; allows immediate reversal if hemodynamic collapse occurs. |
| **Labetalol** | Combined $\alpha$- and $\beta$-blocker | 20 mg IV over 2 min, then 20–40 mg q10min, or infuse 1–2 mg/min (max 300 mg) | Effective monotherapy controlling both heart rate and blood pressure. |
| **Metoprolol** | Selective $\beta_1$-blocker | 5 mg IV bolus q5min up to 3 doses, then 5–10 mg IV q4–6h | Standard alternative for initial beta-blockade. |
| **Nicardipine** | Dihydropyridine calcium blocker | 5 mg/hr IV infusion; titrate by 2.5 mg/hr q5–15min to max 15 mg/hr | Potent vasodilator; **must always be administered after or with adequate beta-blockade** to avoid reflex tachycardia. |
| **Sodium Nitroprusside** | Nitric oxide donor vasodilator | 0.2 mcg/kg/min IV, titrate to effect (usual 2–5 mcg/kg/min; max 3 mcg/kg/min prolonged) | Rapid vasodilator; **never use as monotherapy**. Add sodium thiosulfate (500 mg) to prevent cyanide toxicity. |

#### Principles of Blood Pressure and Impulse Reduction

1. **Beta-Blockade First:** Vasodilators (nicardipine, nitroprusside) cause reflex sympathetic tachycardia, increasing aortic wall shear stress ($dP/dt$) and propagating the dissection. Beta-blockers must be administered **before** initiating vasodilators.
2. **Hemodynamic Targets:** Maintain heart rate between 55 and 60 bpm, and lower systolic blood pressure to 100 to 120 mmHg (or the lowest pressure consistent with cerebral and renal perfusion).
3. **Emergent Surgery:** Immediate cardiothoracic surgical consultation is mandatory for all Type A dissections. Surgical repair reduces operative mortality to 10% to 15%.

## The Oxygen Supply-Demand Dogma</summary>
<div class="details-content">

The classical teaching that acute myocardial infarction results simply from an imbalance between myocardial oxygen delivery ($\text{DO}_2$) and oxygen consumption ($\text{VO}_2$) is fundamentally flawed. Conditions characterized by profound systemic hypoxia or severe anemia produce diffuse myocardial dysfunction, but they do **not** produce focal acute myocardial infarction.

Acute myocardial infarction is an occlusive thromboembolic vascular event caused by local plaque rupture and platelet activation, not a generalized failure of systemic oxygen delivery. Even in profound circulatory shock, acute transmural infarction does not develop in the absence of acute coronary thrombosis. Management must remain relentlessly focused on acute plaque stabilization, antithrombotic therapy, and emergency mechanical revascularization.

</div></details>
