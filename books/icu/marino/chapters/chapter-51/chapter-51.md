# Adrenal and Thyroid Dysfunction

> *Judge a tree from its fruit, not from the leaves.*
> 
> — Euripides (484–406 BC)

Adrenal and thyroid disorders are rarely the primary reason for admission to an intensive care unit (ICU) (1–3). However, critical illness exerts profound neuroendocrine and metabolic influences on both the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the thyroid axis. There has long been clinical concern that systemic illness-induced endocrine suppression might adversely impact patient survival (1–4,8).

This chapter examines the physiological alterations, diagnostic controversies, and therapeutic protocols surrounding adrenal suppression in critical illness—specifically critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency (CIRCI)—alongside the clinical management of thyroid dysfunction, including thyrotoxicosis, thyroid storm, severe hypothyroidism, myxedema coma, and the pervasive euthyroid sick syndrome (1–4,8–11).

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Key Principles and Clinical Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Adrenal Suppression in Critical Illness (CIRCI):** Critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency occurs in 10% to 20% of general ICU patients and up to 60% of patients with septic shock (1,4,5). The underlying mechanism predominantly involves central suppression at the hypothalamic-pituitary level, mediated by systemic inflammatory cytokines, rather than structural adrenal necrosis (3,5).
- **Refractory Hypotension as Cardinal Sign:** The hallmark clinical manifestation of adrenal insufficiency in the ICU is arterial hypotension that fails to respond to adequate intravascular volume resuscitation and requires escalating vasopressor support (1,3–5). Classical electrolyte disturbances (hyponatremia, hyperkalemia) characteristic of chronic Addisonian disease are typically absent in acute CIRCI.
- **Diagnostic Pragmatism:** Serum cortisol testing during critical stress is hampered by dramatic decreases (up to 50%) in corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG) (3,7). The rapid ACTH (cosyntropin $250\ \mu\text{g}$) stimulation test is widely practiced but unreliable for detecting secondary (pituitary) suppression (1,3). Current consensus guidelines recommend foregoing routine cosyntropin testing in septic shock; instead, empiric intravenous hydrocortisone is indicated when shock remains refractory to fluids and moderate-to-high doses of norepinephrine (1,4).
- **Corticosteroid Replacement Regimens:** When indicated, hydrocortisone is administered intravenously at $200\text{–}300\text{ mg/day}$ (either as $100\text{ mg}$ every 8 hours or as a continuous infusion of $200\text{ mg/24 hr}$) (1,4). Supplemental mineralocorticoid (fludrocortisone $50\ \mu\text{g/day}$) is optional, as hydrocortisone possesses substantial intrinsic mineralocorticoid activity (4,7). Hydrocortisone should be tapered gradually once vasopressors are withdrawn and shock has resolved (4).
- **Thyroid Function Test Interpretation:** Systemic illness alters thyroid hormone economy in up to 90% of critically ill patients (8,9). Serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) remains the single most reliable parameter to differentiate primary thyroid disease from nonthyroidal influences (10). Free $\text{T}_4$ is preferred over total $\text{T}_4$, as circulating binding proteins are markedly reduced in critical illness (10).
- **Thyrotoxic Crisis (Thyroid Storm):** Characterized by hyperpyrexia ($> 104^\circ\text{F}$ or $> 40^\circ\text{C}$), delirium, high-output cardiac failure, and tachyarrhythmias, thyroid storm carries a mortality rate of up to 25% (11). Therapy requires a coordinated multimodal bundle: (a) nonselective $\beta$-blockade with propranolol (or ultra-short-acting esmolol); (b) thionamides (propylthiouracil is preferred over methimazole in thyroid storm because it additionally blocks peripheral $\text{T}_4$-to-$\text{T}_3$ conversion); (c) inorganic iodine (Lugol’s solution, administered at least 1 hour *after* thionamides); and (d) stress-dose hydrocortisone ($300\text{ mg}$ IV loading dose, then $100\text{ mg}$ every 8 hours) to treat relative adrenal insufficiency and block hormone conversion (1,11,16).
- **Myxedema Coma Emergency Protocol:** Life-threatening decompensated hypothyroidism carries up to 60% mortality (22). Treatment mandates immediate empiric intravenous hydrocortisone ($100\text{ mg}$ IV every 8 hours) *prior* to thyroid replacement to prevent precipitating acute adrenal crisis, followed by intravenous levothyroxine ($\text{T}_4$, loading dose $200\text{–}400\ \mu\text{g}$ IV) with or without liothyronine ($\text{T}_3$, $5\text{–}20\ \mu\text{g}$ IV loading dose) (22,23).
- **Euthyroid Sick Syndrome:** Characterized by depressed free $\text{T}_3$ and low or normal free $\text{T}_4$ with normal TSH levels, this physiological adaptation is present in 30% to 50% of ICU patients (8–10). Thyroid hormone replacement does not improve clinical outcomes and is strictly not recommended (23).

## Adrenal Suppression in the ICU</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis and Cortisol Physiology</summary><div class="details-content">

The adrenal gland plays a central role in the neuroendocrine response to systemic physiological stress (1,2). The adrenal cortex synthesizes and secretes glucocorticoids (primarily cortisol) and mineralocorticoids (aldosterone) that promote fuel availability, maintain vascular endothelial integrity, and sustain extracellular fluid volume (1–3). Simultaneously, the adrenal medulla releases catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine) that provide immediate cardiovascular support.

Attenuation or loss of this adrenal response leads to progressive hemodynamic collapse, refractory vascular dilation, and lethal metabolic failure (1–3). Adrenal insufficiency can remain completely occult in unstressed individuals until acute physiological insult demands an urgent surge in steroid synthesis.

Cortisol secretion is physiologically controlled by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis. Hypothalamic corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) stimulates the anterior pituitary to synthesize and release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), which acts directly on the adrenal zona fasciculata to trigger cortisol synthesis and release (1,3).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Plasma Cortisol and Protein Binding</summary><div class="details-content">

Cortisol (hydrocortisone) is the primary endogenous glucocorticoid released by the adrenal cortex. Basal cortisol production in an unstressed adult ranges from 15 to $25\text{ mg/day}$, but during periods of severe physiological stress, production can surge dramatically up to $350\text{ mg/day}$ (3).

In normal plasma, approximately 90% of circulating cortisol is biologically inactive, tightly bound to corticosteroid-binding globulin (CBG, transcortin) and albumin, while only 10% circulates as unbound, biologically active free cortisol (3). Standard commercial assays measure total plasma cortisol (both bound and unbound fractions).

During acute critical illness, hepatic synthesis of CBG and albumin falls precipitously by as much as 50%, while neutrophil elastase cleaves CBG to release free cortisol at sites of tissue inflammation (3,7). Consequently, total cortisol levels can be deceptively depressed despite normal or elevated biologically active free cortisol concentrations (1,7). Although direct measurement of serum free cortisol provides a more accurate physiological appraisal, free cortisol assays are technically complex and unavailable in routine emergency laboratories; therefore, total plasma cortisol remains the pragmatic standard for clinical evaluation (1).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Critical Illness-Related Corticosteroid Insufficiency (CIRCI)</summary><div class="details-content">

Adrenal suppression is a frequent finding in the intensive care unit. The overall incidence is estimated at 10% to 20% across unselected critically ill cohorts (4), but exceeds 50% to 60% in patients presenting with severe sepsis and septic shock (5).

Unlike classical primary Addison’s disease, adrenal impairment in critical illness is characteristically transient and reversible following recovery from the acute insult. This clinical entity is formally designated *critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency* (CIRCI) (1,3,4).

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying CIRCI are multifaceted (Figure 51.1). Central suppression at the hypothalamic-pituitary level plays the dominant role: circulating pro-inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-$\alpha$, interleukin-1, and interleukin-6) directly inhibit hypothalamic CRH and pituitary ACTH gene transcription and release (3,5). Hypothalamic-pituitary suppression accounts for up to 75% of all documented cases of adrenal insufficiency in septic shock (5). In addition, tissue-level glucocorticoid receptor expression and affinity are substantially downregulated, resulting in peripheral tissue glucocorticoid resistance (1,3).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Predisposing Infectious and Noninfectious Etiologies</summary><div class="details-content">

Severe sepsis and septic shock represent the predominant triggers of CIRCI in the ICU (1,5). Specific infectious and noninfectious conditions that precipitate acute adrenal failure include (3,6):

- **Infectious Causes:** Disseminated meningococcemia (*Neisseria meningitidis*) inducing massive bilateral adrenal hemorrhage and necrosis (Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome); systemic fungal infections (histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, cryptococcosis); severe disseminated tuberculosis; and advanced human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection (3,6).
- **Abrupt Withdrawal of Exogenous Steroids:** Patients receiving long-term corticosteroid therapy (prednisone $\ge 5\text{ mg/day}$ for $> 3\text{ weeks}$) develop profound atrophy of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis. Abrupt cessation in the face of acute surgical stress, trauma, or infection precipitates acute, life-threatening adrenal crisis (3,6).
- **Adrenal Hemorrhage and Infarction:** Bilateral adrenal infarction or hemorrhage triggered by disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), systemic heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), or therapeutic anticoagulation (3,6).
- **Pharmacological Adrenal Suppression:** Etomidate—a widely used rapid-sequence induction sedative—inhibits mitochondrial $11\beta$-hydroxylase, suppressing adrenal cortisol synthesis for up to 24 to 48 hours after even a single induction dose (1,3,6). Ketoconazole similarly blocks cytochrome P450 steroidogenic enzymes. Conversely, hepatic enzyme inducers (rifampin, phenytoin, phenobarbital, carbamazepine) accelerate hepatic cortisol clearance, precipitating adrenal crisis in patients with borderline reserve (3,6).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Clinical Manifestations and Refractory Shock</summary><div class="details-content">

The hallmark clinical manifestation of CIRCI in the intensive care unit is *arterial hypotension refractory to adequate volume resuscitation and requiring escalating vasopressor infusions* (1,3–5).

Unlike ambulatory Addison’s disease, classic electrolyte hallmarks of primary mineralocorticoid deficiency—namely hyponatremia and hyperkalemia—are frequently absent in CIRCI (1,3). Aldosterone secretion in critical illness can remain partially sustained by direct hyperkalemic stimulation of the adrenal cortex or via renin-independent pathways (3). Consequently, clinicians must not rely on the presence of hyponatremia or hyperkalemia to suspect acute adrenal insufficiency in critically ill patients.

Other clinical clues that should heighten suspicion of occult adrenal suppression include unexplained hypoglycemia, persistent hyperpyrexia without identifiable infection, eosinophilia, unexplained abdominal pain, and sudden hemodynamic deterioration following withdrawal of stress-dose corticosteroids (1,3).

</div></details>
</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Diagnostic Evaluation of Adrenal Suppression</summary><div class="details-content">

Adrenal suppression should be actively considered in any ICU patient exhibiting unexplained cardiovascular instability, progressive vasopressor dependency, or persistent shock disproportionate to the apparent clinical illness (1,4).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Diagnostic Criteria and Random Plasma Cortisol</summary><div class="details-content">

Establishing a definitive biochemical diagnosis of CIRCI remains steeped in controversy and diagnostic uncertainty (1,4):

- **Random Total Plasma Cortisol $\lt 10\ \mu\text{g/dL}$:** A baseline (random) total plasma cortisol concentration below $10\ \mu\text{g/dL}$ ($276\text{ nmol/L}$) in a patient experiencing severe physiological stress constitutes clear evidence of adrenal failure (1,4).
- **Random Total Plasma Cortisol $\ge 35\ \mu\text{g/dL}$:** A random plasma cortisol level of $35\ \mu\text{g/dL}$ ($966\text{ nmol/L}$) or higher indicates an intact and robust adrenal response, virtually excluding adrenal insufficiency (1,4).
- **Indeterminate Range ($10\text{–}34\ \mu\text{g/dL}$):** The vast majority of critically ill patients fall within this diagnostic grey zone. In this intermediate range, total cortisol levels cannot reliably confirm or exclude adrenal insufficiency due to unpredictable fluctuations in binding proteins and receptor sensitivity (1,4).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### The Rapid ACTH Stimulation Test</summary><div class="details-content">

The short (rapid) ACTH stimulation test is widely utilized in hospitalized patients. A baseline blood sample is obtained for total plasma cortisol, followed by intravenous administration of $250\ \mu\text{g}$ of synthetic ACTH (cosyntropin). A repeat cortisol level is measured exactly 60 minutes later (1,4).

An absolute post-stimulation increment in total plasma cortisol of $\lt 9\ \mu\text{g/dL}$ ($250\text{ nmol/L}$) over baseline was historically considered diagnostic of CIRCI (4,5).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

##### Diagnostic Limitations and Modern Consensus</summary><div class="details-content">

However, current consensus guidelines (SCCM/ESICM) advise against relying on the rapid ACTH stimulation test in the ICU (1). First, $250\ \mu\text{g}$ of cosyntropin represents a supramaximal pharmacological dose (nearly 100-fold higher than physiological stress-induced ACTH levels), which can forcefully stimulate an exhausted adrenal gland to release an increment $\ge 9\ \mu\text{g/dL}$, yielding a false-negative result. Second, the test directly assesses adrenocortical reserve; *a normal adrenal response to exogenous ACTH does not exclude secondary or tertiary adrenal insufficiency resulting from hypothalamic-pituitary shutdown*, which represents the primary lesion in 75% of septic patients (1,5). While measuring plasma ACTH levels can distinguish primary from secondary adrenal failure, plasma ACTH assays are rarely available in clinical real time (2).

Therefore, in patients with septic shock, consensus guidelines recommend foregoing routine laboratory cortisol testing and proceeding directly to an empiric therapeutic trial of intravenous hydrocortisone when shock remains refractory to fluids and vasopressors (1).

#### Hydrocortisone Regimens and Tapering in Septic Shock</summary><div class="details-content">

The drug of choice for treating critical illness-related adrenal insufficiency is *intravenous hydrocortisone* administered at a dose of $200\text{–}300\text{ mg/day}$ (1,4). Standard delivery schedules include either $100\text{ mg}$ intravenously every 8 hours (or $50\text{ mg}$ IV every 6 hours), or a continuous intravenous infusion of $200\text{ mg}$ over 24 hours (1,4). Continuous infusion has the advantage of preventing acute glycemic spikes and erratic fluctuations in serum drug levels.

Because hydrocortisone provides substantial intrinsic mineralocorticoid activity (Table 51.1), oral fludrocortisone ($50\ \mu\text{g}$ once daily) is entirely optional; large randomized clinical trials have confirmed that adding fludrocortisone to hydrocortisone does not confer any survival advantage in septic shock (1,4).

Hydrocortisone should be discontinued once shock resolves—specifically when vasopressor infusions have been successfully weaned off and serum lactate levels have normalized (1,4). A gradual downward taper over several days is strongly advised: abrupt withdrawal can precipitate an acute rebound surge in circulating pro-inflammatory mediators and recurrent hemodynamic instability (4).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Corticosteroids as Anti-Inflammatory Agents</summary><div class="details-content">

Irrespective of baseline adrenal function, corticosteroids are widely prescribed for their potent anti-inflammatory properties in conditions such as severe acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), bacterial meningitis, and autoimmune exacerbations (1,4).

Methylprednisolone remains the most popular steroid for non-septic inflammatory conditions, despite the pharmacological fact that *dexamethasone is the most potent anti-inflammatory agent available* (Table 51.1). Dexamethasone is 5 times more potent than methylprednisolone and 25 times more potent than hydrocortisone, while possessing virtually zero mineralocorticoid effect (Table 51.1). In emergency situations where diagnostic ACTH testing is planned, dexamethasone ($4\text{–}6\text{ mg}$ IV) can be administered immediately without cross-reacting with commercial plasma cortisol assays (1,2).

## Evaluation of Thyroid Function in Critical Illness</summary><div class="details-content">

Laboratory tests of thyroid function are abnormal in up to 90% of all critically ill patients admitted to intensive care units (8). In the overwhelming majority of cases, these laboratory aberrations represent a physiological adaptation to nonthyroidal systemic illness—rather than primary pathology of the thyroid gland itself (8,9).

Disentangling benign nonthyroidal illness from true endocrinopathy requires an understanding of thyroid hormone economy, binding proteins, and the hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid feedback axis (8–10).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Thyroid Physiology: T4, T3, and TSH Assays</summary><div class="details-content">

The thyroid gland secretes predominantly thyroxine ($\text{T}_4$), a prohormone with minimal biological activity. The biologically active hormone is triiodothyronine ($\text{T}_3$), which is generated primarily in peripheral tissues through enzymatic outer-ring $5^\prime$-deiodination of $\text{T}_4$ by hepatic and renal iodothyronine deiodinases (8,10).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Protein Binding and Superiority of Free T4</summary><div class="details-content">

Both $\text{T}_4$ and $\text{T}_3$ circulate almost entirely bound to plasma carrier proteins: approximately 70% to thyroxine-binding globulin (TBG), 15% to 20% to transthyretin (prealbumin), and 10% to albumin (10). Less than 0.03% of total circulating $\text{T}_4$ and less than 0.3% of total $\text{T}_3$ circulate in the unbound, biologically active "free" state (10).

Because critical illness induces drastic decreases in serum albumin and TBG concentrations, total $\text{T}_4$ and total $\text{T}_3$ assays are profoundly misleading in the ICU (8,10). Direct measurement of *free $\text{T}_4$* is therefore mandatory for clinical evaluation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Diagnostic Interpretation of Serum TSH</summary><div class="details-content">

Serum thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), secreted by the anterior pituitary under the control of hypothalamic thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), remains the premier screening parameter for thyroid disease (10):

1. **Primary Thyroidal Disease:** TSH shifts in the *opposite* direction to free thyroid hormone levels (e.g., elevated free $\text{T}_4$ suppresses TSH to undetectable levels; low free $\text{T}_4$ drives a compensatory surge in TSH) (10).
2. **Secondary (Central) Thyroidal Disease:** Defects in the hypothalamic-pituitary axis cause TSH and free thyroid hormones to move in the *same* direction (e.g., pituitary necrosis yields low TSH accompanied by low free $\text{T}_4$) (10).
3. **Nonthyroidal Illness:** TSH concentrations usually remain within or near the normal reference range ($0.3\text{–}4.5\text{ mU/mL}$) despite marked perturbations in circulating peripheral hormone levels (8,10).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### The Euthyroid Sick Syndrome (Nonthyroidal Illness)</summary><div class="details-content">

The euthyroid sick syndrome (also termed nonthyroidal illness syndrome) is observed in 30% to 50% of all ICU patients (8–10).

The initial and most common manifestation is a dramatic fall in total and free $\text{T}_3$, driven by inflammatory cytokine suppression of hepatic $5^\prime$-deiodinase. As a result, $\text{T}_4$ is preferentially converted into metabolically inactive reverse $\text{T}_3$ ($\text{rT}_3$) via 5-deiodination (8). As illness progresses and systemic severity escalates, free $\text{T}_4$ concentrations also decline. Yet, despite subnormal levels of active thyroid hormones, serum TSH remains characteristically normal (or transiently mildly depressed), differentiating this condition from true primary hypothyroidism (Table 51.2) (8,10).

Current endocrinology consensus guidelines strongly emphasize that the euthyroid sick syndrome represents an adaptive, energy-conserving down-regulation of basal metabolic rate in response to catastrophic illness (8,23). Multiple randomized clinical trials have conclusively demonstrated that administering levothyroxine or liothyronine to patients with euthyroid sick syndrome fails to improve clinical outcomes and may worsen cardiac ischemia and nitrogen loss; therefore, *thyroid replacement therapy is strictly contraindicated in euthyroid sick syndrome* (8,23).

## Thyrotoxicosis and Thyroid Storm</summary><div class="details-content">

Thyrotoxicosis describes the clinical syndrome resulting from excessive circulating thyroid hormone concentrations, regardless of the underlying source (11,12). It is most commonly provoked by primary autoimmune hyperthyroidism (Graves disease, toxic multinodular goiter, or toxic solitary adenoma) (11,12).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Clinical Presentation and High-Risk Features</summary><div class="details-content">

The classical manifestations of thyrotoxicosis stem from generalized hypermetabolism and hyperadrenergic signaling: intense psychomotor agitation, fine kinetic tremors, heat intolerance, diaphoresis, hyperreflexia, weight loss, and marked cardiovascular hyperkinesis (sinus tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, and high-output cardiac states) (11–13).

In elderly patients, hyperthyroidism frequently presents with an atypical, misleading constellation designated *apathetic thyrotoxicosis* (13). Rather than presenting with manic agitation, these patients exhibit profound apathy, severe lethargy, depression, muscle wasting, and unexplained weight loss. The classic presentation of apathetic thyrotoxicosis in an elderly patient is new-onset atrial fibrillation accompanied by lethargy and cognitive decline (13).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Thyroid Storm: Diagnosis and Life-Threatening Manifestations</summary><div class="details-content">

Thyroid storm (thyrotoxic crisis) represents an extreme, life-threatening exacerbation of thyrotoxicosis triggered by acute physiological stress, such as infection, sepsis, non-thyroidal surgery, trauma, myocardial infarction, parturition, or sudden cessation of antithyroid pharmacotherapy (11,16).

Key diagnostic hallmarks of thyroid storm include (11):

- **Severe Hyperpyrexia:** Disproportionate hyperthermia with core body temperatures frequently exceeding $104^\circ\text{F}$ ($40.0^\circ\text{C}$).
- **Central Nervous System Decompensation:** Extreme agitation, delirium, frank psychosis, seizures, progressive stupor, and coma.
- **Cardiovascular Collapse:** Disproportionate sinus tachycardia ($> 140\text{ beats/min}$), rapid-ventricular-response atrial fibrillation, congestive high-output heart failure, and cardiogenic shock.
- **Gastrointestinal and Hepatic Dysfunction:** Nausea, profuse vomiting, secretory diarrhea, severe abdominal pain, and ischemic or cholestatic hepatic jaundice.

Even with aggressive contemporary intensive care management, the mortality rate of thyroid storm remains formidable at 10% to 25% (11,16).

#### Multimodal Management of Thyroid Storm</summary><div class="details-content">

Definitive therapy for thyroid storm follows an uncompromising sequential bundle (11,16):

1. **Adrenergic Control:** Propranolol is the standard agent due to its dual ability to control hyperadrenergic symptoms and block peripheral $5^\prime$-deiodinase (Table 51.3). In hemodynamically fragile patients or those with severe pulmonary disease, esmolol infusion provides rapid control with minimal risk (11).
2. **Thionamide Inhibition:** Propylthiouracil (PTU) is administered immediately via nasogastric tube ($500\text{–}1{,}000\text{ mg}$ loading dose). PTU is preferred over methimazole during thyroid storm because it blocks both thyroidal hormone synthesis and peripheral conversion of $\text{T}_4$ to active $\text{T}_3$ (Table 51.4) (11).
3. **Inorganic Iodine Blockade:** Saturated solution of potassium iodide (SSKI or Lugol’s solution) delivers concentrated inorganic iodine to trigger the acute Wolff-Chaikoff effect, abruptly halting thyroid hormone release (11). *Crucial timing:* Iodine must be administered at least 1 hour *after* the initial thionamide dose; premature iodine administration provides substrate that fuels accelerated de novo hormone synthesis (11,16). In patients with severe iodine anaphylaxis, oral lithium carbonate ($300\text{ mg}$ every 8 hours) serves as a substitute (16).
4. **Corticosteroid Coverage:** Intravenous hydrocortisone ($300\text{ mg}$ IV bolus, then $100\text{ mg}$ IV every 8 hours) protects against concurrent relative adrenal exhaustion and further inhibits peripheral conversion of $\text{T}_4$ to $\text{T}_3$ (1,11).
5. **Supportive Resuscitation and Trigger Eradication:** Aggressive intravenous volume repletion to correct severe dehydration; active cooling measures (cooling blankets and acetaminophen, strictly avoiding aspirin, which displaces thyroid hormone from TBG); and aggressive treatment of the underlying precipitating infection or stressor (11,16).

## Hypothyroidism in the Intensive Care Unit</summary><div class="details-content">

Symptomatic primary hypothyroidism occurs in 3% to 7% of the adult population (17). The vast majority of cases stem from autoimmune destruction of the thyroid gland (Hashimoto’s thyroiditis) or previous therapeutic ablation (radioiodine therapy or surgical thyroidectomy) (17,18). Less common secondary causes include pituitary apoplexy, postpartum pituitary necrosis (Sheehan’s syndrome), and pharmacological agents such as amiodarone, lithium, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors (17,18).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Clinical Manifestations and Organ Dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinical manifestations of chronic hypothyroidism are indolent and subtle: coarse dry skin, cold intolerance, profound fatigue, constipation, periorbital puffiness, and delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes (17,18). Contrary to widespread public perception, clinically significant obesity is not caused by hypothyroidism; associated weight gain rarely exceeds 2 to $4\text{ kg}$, consisting primarily of water and extracellular fluid retention (18).

In advanced cases, multisystem organ dysfunction emerges (19,20):

- **Cardiovascular Dysfunction:** Hypothyroidism impairs myocardial diastolic relaxation, causing diastolic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, reduced stroke volume, and bradycardia (20). Increased capillary permeability promotes large, protein-rich pericardial effusions. In addition, hypothyroidism significantly prolongs the myocardial $\text{Q-T}$ interval, heightening susceptibility to torsades de pointes ventricular tachycardia (20).
- **Renal and Skeletal Muscle Derangements:** Hypothyroid myopathy produces marked muscle stiffness, cramps, and elevations in serum creatine phosphokinase (CPK) (19). Furthermore, serum creatinine concentrations frequently rise in profound hypothyroidism due to accelerated release of creatine from damaged skeletal myocytes combined with reduced renal blood flow, mimicking acute kidney injury in the absence of intrinsic parenchymal damage (19).
- **Metabolic Alterations:** Impaired free water excretion induces hypoosmolar hyponatremia, driven by elevated circulating vasopressin levels and diminished distal tubule delivery (17,18).

### Myxedema Coma: Acute Management Protocol</summary><div class="details-content">

Myxedema coma represents the most severe, life-threatening expression of decompensated hypothyroidism (21,22). The term is historically misleading: true coma is present in fewer than 30% of cases, and non-pitting myxedematous skin edema (caused by dermal glycosaminoglycan deposition) is not universal (21).

Myxedema coma is typically precipitated in an elderly individual with long-standing occult hypothyroidism exposed to an acute stressor—such as sepsis, cold exposure, stroke, trauma, or the administration of sedatives, opioids, or anesthetics (21,22).

Cardinal clinical hallmarks include profound hypothermia (core temperatures frequently dropping below $90^\circ\text{F}$ or $32.2^\circ\text{C}$), severe central nervous system depression (obtundation, stupor), hypercapnic respiratory failure resulting from blunted ventilatory responses to hypoxia and hypercapnia, bradycardia, refractory hypotension, and severe hyponatremia (21,22). Even with rapid intensive care admission, mortality remains exceptionally high, ranging from 30% to 60% (22).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Resuscitation and Hormone Replacement Bundle</summary><div class="details-content">

Immediate, aggressive resuscitation is mandatory upon clinical suspicion without awaiting confirmatory laboratory results (22,23):

1. **Preemptive Hydrocortisone Coverage:** Myxedema coma is frequently accompanied by secondary adrenal suppression or exhaustion of adrenal reserve. Accelerating baseline metabolic clearance with thyroid hormone in the absence of corticosteroids can precipitate fatal circulatory collapse. Therefore, *intravenous hydrocortisone ($100\text{ mg}$ IV every 8 hours) must be administered immediately prior to giving the first dose of thyroid hormone* (1,22,23).
2. **Intravenous Levothyroxine ($\text{T}_4$):** Levothyroxine is initiated with a slow intravenous loading dose of $200\text{–}400\ \mu\text{g}$ administered over 5 to 10 minutes (to rapidly replenish exhausted circulating and tissue pools), followed by a daily intravenous maintenance dose of $50\text{–}100\ \mu\text{g/day}$ (or $1.6\ \mu\text{g/kg/day}$, utilizing 75% of the calculated oral dose) (22,23).
3. **Intravenous Liothyronine ($\text{T}_3$):** Because the peripheral enzymatic conversion of $\text{T}_4$ to active $\text{T}_3$ is severely impaired in hypothermic, critically ill patients, adding intravenous liothyronine is strongly advocated in profound coma. An intravenous loading dose of $5\text{–}20\ \mu\text{g}$ is administered, followed by $2.5\text{–}10\ \mu\text{g}$ IV every 8 hours until the patient demonstrates neurological recovery and hemodynamic stability (22,23). In patients with known severe coronary artery disease, lower doses are chosen to avoid provoking myocardial infarction.
4. **Active Supportive Care:** Passive external rewarming with warm blankets (avoiding rapid active rewarming, which induces massive peripheral vasodilation and shock); cautious fluid resuscitation with isotonic saline (supplemented with hypertonic $3\%$ saline only for life-threatening hyponatremic seizures); endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation for ventilatory failure; and intravenous broad-spectrum empirical antibiotics directed against occult sepsis (21–23).

## A Final Word: Clinical Significance</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Much Ado About Not Much</summary><div class="details-content">

The opening of this chapter noted the prevailing clinical apprehension that critical illness exerts a deleterious impact on adrenal and thyroid function, and that these endocrine perturbations negatively dictate clinical survival.

Rigorous examination of clinical evidence thoroughly refutes that presumption:

1. **CIRCI:** Although critical illness-related corticosteroid insufficiency is demonstrable in up to 60% of septic shock cohorts, multiple large-scale multinational randomized clinical trials (CORTICUS, ADRENAL, APROCCHSS) have conclusively confirmed that corticosteroid administration does not confer an unambiguous survival benefit; its primary clinical efficacy lies strictly in shortening the duration of vasopressor support and accelerating shock resolution (1,4).
2. **Thyroid Axis:** Perturbations in plasma thyroid hormones (the euthyroid sick syndrome) are ubiquitous across intensive care units, yet this biochemical state represents an adaptive physiological down-regulation rather than true pathological endocrinopathy, and hormonal supplementation confers zero therapeutic benefit (8,23).

Viewed objectively through the lens of evidence-based critical care, the interaction between acute systemic illness and adrenal or thyroid function carries far less prognostic importance than commonly assumed.

As Euripides observed twenty-five centuries ago:

> *Judge a tree from its fruit, not from the leaves.*

In the intensive care unit, endocrine laboratories generate abundant leafy changes, but the harvest of clinically meaningful, outcome-altering therapies remains sparse.
