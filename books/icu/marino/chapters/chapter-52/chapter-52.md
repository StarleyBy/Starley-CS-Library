# Pharmaceutical Drug Overdoses

> *A desire to take medicine is, perhaps, the great feature which distinguishes man from other animals.*
> 
> — Sir William Osler (1849–1919)

Prescription and over-the-counter medication use in the United States is virtually ubiquitous across all age cohorts (Figure 52.1) (1). This profound societal affinity for pharmacotherapy is mirrored by clinical prescribing patterns, providing a partial explanation for the dramatic surge in drug overdose fatalities: deaths from drug overdoses escalated more than 5-fold over the past two decades, rising from approximately $20{,}000$ deaths in the year 2000 to over $108{,}000$ deaths in 2022 (1).

Critically ill patients suffering from acute pharmaceutical intoxications constitute a substantial, highly resource-intensive population in modern intensive care units (1–4). 

This chapter examines the clinical pharmacology, toxidromes, evidence-based diagnostic algorithms, and targeted antidotal therapies for five major classes of pharmaceutical overdoses: acetaminophen, benzodiazepines, $\beta$-receptor antagonists, opioids, and salicylates (1–4,11,14,20,33,41).

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Key Principles and Clinical Recommendations</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Epidemiology and Unintentional Poisoning:** Pharmaceutical overdoses represent a leading cause of preventable ICU admissions and acute liver failure (1,2). Approximately 50% of acetaminophen-induced acute liver failure cases result from unintentional supratherapeutic ingestion rather than suicidal intent, underscoring the narrow therapeutic index of common over-the-counter formulations (2–4).
- **Acetaminophen Hepatotoxicity and N-Acetylcysteine (NAC):** Toxicity is mediated by the cytochrome P450 (CYP2E1) reactive metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI). When hepatic glutathione is depleted by $\ge 70\%$, NAPQI covalently binds hepatocellular proteins, provoking centrilobular hepatic necrosis (2,3). In acute ingestions ($4\text{–}24\text{ hours}$), serum acetaminophen concentrations plotted on the Rumack-Matthew nomogram determine antidotal therapy (treatment line begins at $150\ \mu\text{g/mL}$ at 4 hours) (14). Intravenous NAC ($300\text{ mg/kg}$ infused over 21 hours) provides a cysteine precursor for glutathione synthesis and is virtually 100% effective when initiated within 8 hours of ingestion (2,14). NAC should be continued beyond 21 hours if stopping criteria (normalizing transaminases, $\text{INR} \lt 2.0$, undetectable acetaminophen) are not satisfied (14).
- **Benzodiazepine Overdose and Flumazenil Constraints:** Pure benzodiazepine overdose causes central nervous system depression with preserved hemodynamics and mild respiratory depression; fatalities almost invariably involve co-ingestion of respiratory depressants (especially opioids or ethanol) (17,18). Flumazenil, a competitive $\text{GABA}_\text{A}$ receptor antagonist, rapidly reverses sedation but carries significant risks of precipitating refractory withdrawal seizures and ventricular arrhythmias in chronic users or co-ingestions with proconvulsant agents (e.g., tricyclic antidepressants) (20,23). Flumazenil is contraindicated in undifferentiated coma and is restricted primarily to procedural sedation reversal (23).
- **$\beta$-Receptor Antagonist (Beta-Blocker) Toxicity:** Manifests clinically with bradycardia, hypotension, and cardiogenic shock (24). Lipophilic agents (propranolol) exert quinidine-like membrane-stabilizing effects (cardiac sodium channel blockade causing QRS/PR prolongation and AV block) and severe neurotoxicity (coma, seizures in up to 60%) (25,26). Glucagon ($3\text{–}5\text{ mg}$ IV bolus, followed by $5\text{ mg/hr}$ infusion) bypasses adrenergic receptors by directly activating adenylate cyclase, augmenting intracellular cyclic AMP and restoring myocardial contractility (27,28). Adjunctive therapies for refractory toxicity include high-dose insulin euglycemia (HIE), phosphodiesterase inhibitors (milrinone), intravenous lipid emulsion (ILE), and mechanical circulatory support (ECMO) (27,31).
- **Opioid Overdose and Naloxone Titration:** The classical opioid toxidrome comprises central nervous system depression, pinpoint pupils (miosis), and hypoventilation/apnea (33). Naloxone, a pure $\mu$-opioid receptor antagonist, reverses respiratory depression rapidly via intranasal ($2\text{–}4\text{ mg}$) or intravenous ($0.4\text{–}2\text{ mg}$) administration (33–35). Because naloxone’s duration of action ($30\text{–}90\text{ minutes}$) is substantially shorter than that of most prescription opioids and synthetic fentanyl derivatives, recurrence of life-threatening respiratory arrest is common, necessitating prolonged observation and continuous intravenous naloxone infusion (two-thirds the effective waking dose per hour) (36).
- **Salicylate Toxicity and Urinary Alkalinization:** Acute or chronic aspirin poisoning uncouples oxidative phosphorylation, producing a pathognomonic early mixed respiratory alkalosis (direct medullary center stimulation) and severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis (lactic and ketoacid accumulation) (41,42). Systemic and urinary alkalinization using intravenous sodium bicarbonate (maintaining urine $\text{pH} \ge 7.5$) traps ionized salicylate within the renal tubules to accelerate urinary clearance (42,43). Aggressive potassium repletion is mandatory to prevent paradoxical aciduria (42). Hemodialysis is the definitive therapy for serum salicylate concentrations $> 90\text{ mg/dL}$ ($> 6.5\text{ mmol/L}$), severe acidemia, volume-overload pulmonary edema, or neurological deterioration (41,44).

## Acetaminophen Toxicity</summary><div class="details-content">

Acetaminophen (paracetamol, N-acetyl-p-aminophenol, APAP) is the most widely consumed analgesic and antipyretic medication in the world, present in more than 600 commercial prescription and over-the-counter preparations (2).

Despite its widespread perception as benign, acetaminophen is a potent dose-dependent intrinsic hepatotoxin and represents *the leading cause of acute liver failure in North America, Europe, and Australia* (2,3). In the United States, acetaminophen accounts for nearly 50% of all acute liver failure cases admitted to intensive care units (Figure 39.1) (3). Crucially, approximately half of all acetaminophen-induced acute liver failure episodes are entirely unintentional, resulting from repeated supratherapeutic self-medication for pain or febrile illness using combination over-the-counter remedies (3,4).

In 2011, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) mandated prominent boxed warnings and limited the acetaminophen content of prescription combination products to $325\text{ mg}$ per tablet (5). However, uncounted over-the-counter single-ingredient and cough/cold preparations continue to be sold without prescription in $500\text{ mg}$ and $650\text{ mg}$ strengths, sustaining high rates of hepatotoxicity (5).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Hepatic Metabolism and Toxic Mechanisms</summary><div class="details-content">

The pathophysiology of acetaminophen hepatotoxicity is intimately tied to its hepatic biotransformation pathways (Figure 52.2) (2,3).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Biotransformation and NAPQI Generation</summary><div class="details-content">

Under normal physiological conditions and therapeutic dosing, approximately 85% to 90% of an ingested acetaminophen dose is metabolized via Phase II conjugation pathways in hepatocytes: glucuronidation (via UDP-glucuronosyltransferases) and sulfation (via sulfotransferases) (2). The resulting glucuronide and sulfate conjugates are water-soluble, non-toxic, and rapidly excreted into the urine (Figure 52.2). Approximately 2% to 5% of the parent drug is excreted unchanged in the urine.

A minor fraction (approximately 5% to 10%) is metabolized via the hepatic cytochrome P450 mixed-function oxidase system (predominantly the CYP2E1 isoenzyme, with minor contributions from CYP1A2 and CYP3A4) into a highly reactive, electrophilic, toxic arylating intermediate: *N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI)* (Figure 52.2) (2,3). Under normal circumstances, NAPQI is immediately neutralized by rapid conjugation with the sulfhydryl group of intracellular glutathione, forming non-toxic mercapturic acid and cysteine conjugates that are eliminated renally (Figure 52.2).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Glutathione Depletion and Centrilobular Necrosis</summary><div class="details-content">

When a massive acute overdose or prolonged supratherapeutic ingestion occurs, the primary non-toxic glucuronidation and sulfation pathways become saturated (2,3). Consequently, a significantly larger fraction of acetaminophen is shunted through the CYP2E1 pathway, precipitating a massive surge in NAPQI production.

As NAPQI generation accelerates, hepatocellular glutathione reserves are rapidly consumed. When total hepatic glutathione stores fall by $\ge 70\%$, endogenous detoxification is overwhelmed (2,3). Excess unneutralized NAPQI binds covalently to nucleophilic cysteinyl sulfhydryl groups on vital hepatocyte intracellular proteins and mitochondrial macromolecules. This protein adduction triggers mitochondrial membrane permeability transition, ATP depletion, profound oxidative stress, calcium homeostasis collapse, and centrilobular (Zone III) hepatic necrosis (2,3).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Toxic Doses and Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

Susceptibility to acetaminophen hepatotoxicity varies substantially based on individual metabolic factors, nutritional state, and baseline glutathione reserves (6–8):

- **Acute Toxic Threshold in Adults:** In healthy adults, the acute toxic threshold is generally between $7.5\text{ g}$ and $15\text{ g}$ of ingested acetaminophen ($150\text{–}200\text{ mg/kg}$) (6,7). Poison control centers utilize an acute ingestion threshold of $10\text{ g}$ (or $\ge 150\text{ mg/kg}$, whichever is lower) as an absolute trigger for urgent medical evaluation and antidotal intervention (8).
- **Therapeutic Ceilings:** The FDA-approved maximum daily therapeutic dosage in adults is $4\text{ g/day}$ ($4{,}000\text{ mg/24 hr}$) (9). However, many clinical practice guidelines and expert hepatology panels recommend capping chronic daily intake at $\le 3\text{ g/day}$ ($\le 3{,}000\text{ mg/day}$) to avoid occult hepatic injury in ambulatory patients (10).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Predisposing High-Risk Conditions</summary><div class="details-content">

Several pathological states significantly heighten individual susceptibility to acetaminophen toxicity, rendering even therapeutic or mildly supratherapeutic dosages ($> 4\text{ g/day}$, and occasionally even $\le 4\text{ g/day}$) potentially lethal (8,11,12):

- **Chronic Alcohol Use Disorder:** Chronic heavy ethanol consumption potently induces hepatic CYP2E1 enzyme activity while simultaneously depleting hepatic glutathione stores due to poor dietary intake (11). Consequently, patients with chronic alcohol abuse can develop fulminant liver failure at dosages well below $4\text{ g/day}$ (11).
- **Malnutrition and Prolonged Fasting:** Depletion of endogenous glutathione precursors (cysteine, methionine) during anorexia nervosa, protein-calorie malnutrition, or prolonged starvation dramatically lowers the threshold for NAPQI-induced cellular necrosis (8).
- **Cytochrome P450 Induction:** Co-ingestion of medications that induce CYP2E1, CYP3A4, or other CYP enzymes—such as anticonvulsants (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital), antituberculosis agents (rifampin, isoniazid), and chronic St. John’s wort—accelerates the rate and magnitude of NAPQI formation (12).
- **Chronic Underlying Liver Disease:** Advanced cirrhosis and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis impair baseline hepatic antioxidant reserves, predisposing to injury, although baseline clearance changes remain variable (8,12).

</div></details>
</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Clinical Presentation and Staging</summary><div class="details-content">

Acetaminophen-induced hepatotoxicity progresses through four distinct, sequential clinical stages over several days (2,12,13):

1. **Stage 1 (0 to 24 Hours Post-Ingestion):** Patients are frequently completely asymptomatic or report only mild, nonspecific gastrointestinal symptoms, including anorexia, nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis, and generalized malaise. Serum aminotransferases (AST, ALT), total bilirubin, and international normalized ratio (INR) are characteristically normal during this initial latent window. The deceptive absence of early distress frequently delays medical presentation.
2. **Stage 2 (24 to 72 Hours Post-Ingestion):** Hepatic aminotransferases begin to rise dramatically, often exceeding $1{,}000\text{ IU/L}$ by 36 to 48 hours and climbing to extreme values ($> 10{,}000\text{ IU/L}$) in severe toxicity (13). Right upper quadrant tenderness, hepatomegaly, progressive jaundice, and coagulopathy (prolongation of prothrombin time and INR) emerge. Oliguria and rising serum creatinine indicate concurrent acute tubular necrosis, mediated either by direct toxic NAPQI generation in renal tubules or hepatorenal physiology.
3. **Stage 3 (72 to 96 Hours Post-Ingestion):** Represents the zenith of hepatic injury and fulminant hepatic failure (12). Characterized by severe jaundice, profound coagulopathy ($\text{INR} > 5.0$), hepatic encephalopathy (progressing from confusion to coma), hyperammonemia, severe lactic acidosis, and acute oliguric kidney injury. Multiorgan dysfunction syndrome (refractory vasoplegic shock, adult respiratory distress syndrome, cerebral edema with uncal herniation, and severe sepsis) peaks during this period, accounting for the vast majority of fatal outcomes.
4. **Stage 4 (4 to 14 Days Post-Ingestion):** Patients who survive the critical third stage enter a clinical recovery phase. Complete histological and functional hepatic regeneration typically occurs over several weeks to months, remarkably without chronic portal hypertension or post-necrotic cirrhosis (2).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Risk Assessment and the Rumack-Matthew Nomogram</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinical evaluation of acute acetaminophen poisoning requires immediate determination of five core variables: (*a*) the precise time of ingestion, (*b*) the total estimated dose, (*c*) the formulation ingested (immediate-release versus extended-release), (*d*) the presence of predisposing risk factors, and (*e*) baseline hepatic and renal function (2,14).

In patients presenting between 4 and 24 hours following an acute single ingestion of an immediate-release preparation, the serum acetaminophen concentration plotted against time post-ingestion serves as the definitive predictor of hepatotoxicity (Figure 52.3) (14).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Predictive Nomogram Interpretation</summary><div class="details-content">

The Rumack-Matthew nomogram utilizes a semi-logarithmic plot of plasma acetaminophen concentration versus time post-ingestion (Figure 52.3) (14):

- **Treatment Line (150-Line):** Extends from $150\ \mu\text{g/mL}$ ($993\ \mu\text{mol/L}$) at 4 hours post-ingestion down to $4.7\ \mu\text{g/mL}$ ($31\ \mu\text{mol/L}$) at 24 hours. Any measured concentration falling on or above this line warrants prompt initiation of antidotal therapy with N-acetylcysteine (14).
- **High-Risk Line (300-Line):** Represents the original investigational threshold extending from $300\ \mu\text{g/mL}$ at 4 hours down to $10\ \mu\text{g/mL}$ at 24 hours (14). Concentrations above this line confer a greater than 90% probability of severe hepatotoxicity in the absence of treatment.

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

##### Clinical Limitations and Atypical Ingestions</summary><div class="details-content">

The nomogram is strictly valid *only* for acute, single-point-in-time ingestions of immediate-release acetaminophen presenting between 4 and 24 hours post-ingestion (14).

The nomogram *cannot* be used to assess:
1. Ingestions occurring $< 4\text{ hours}$ previously (absorption and distribution are incomplete; levels drawn before 4 hours cannot predict toxicity and must be repeated at 4 hours).
2. Extended-release formulations (e.g., Tylenol ER), which exhibit erratic, prolonged absorption; in these cases, an initial level between 4 and 12 hours must be followed by a repeat level 4 to 6 hours later (14).
3. Repeated supratherapeutic ingestions over hours or days, where time-concentration kinetics are disrupted; in such cases, any detectable acetaminophen accompanied by elevated aminotransferases warrants immediate antidotal treatment (14).
4. Late presentations ($> 24\text{ hours}$ post-ingestion), where clinical decision-making relies entirely on aminotransferases and clinical status (14).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Antidotal Therapy: N-Acetylcysteine (NAC)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Mechanism of Action and Glutathione Resynthesis</summary><div class="details-content">

Exogenous glutathione cannot be administered therapeutically because it does not cross mammalian cell membranes in pharmacologically effective quantities (2,14).

However, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) functions as an ideal synthetic precursor: it readily crosses the hepatocyte cell membrane, where it is deacetylated into L-cysteine, directly supplying the rate-limiting substrate for de novo intracellular glutathione synthesis (Figure 52.2) (2). In addition, NAC can act directly as an alternative sulfhydryl electron donor to conjugate unneutralized NAPQI, promotes the non-toxic sulfation pathway, improves microcirculatory perfusion, and scavenges oxygen free radicals (2,14).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Clinical Indications and Timing of Administration</summary><div class="details-content">

Antidotal therapy with NAC is indicated for: (*a*) any patient whose serum acetaminophen concentration falls on or above the Rumack-Matthew treatment line; (*b*) an acute ingestion $> 10\text{ g}$ (or $\ge 150\text{ mg/kg}$) presenting with an unknown or delayed level; or (*c*) any suspected or documented acetaminophen exposure presenting with elevated serum aminotransferases (14).

When initiated within 8 hours of an acute ingestion, NAC provides virtually 100% hepatoprotection, reducing the incidence of severe hepatotoxicity to $< 5\%$ and mortality to near zero (2,14). However, NAC provides significant survival and microcirculatory benefits even when started late (beyond 24 hours) in patients with established fulminant liver failure (2,14).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Oral NAC Regimen and Adverse Reactions</summary><div class="details-content">

Although intravenous administration is the standard of care in intensive care units, an oral NAC regimen exists and exhibits equivalent clinical efficacy when fully absorbed (15). The oral protocol comprises a loading dose of $140\text{ mg/kg}$ (as a 5% solution diluted in juice or carbonated beverage to mask the noxious rotten-egg sulfur odor), followed by $70\text{ mg/kg}$ every 4 hours for 17 maintenance doses (total dose $1{,}330\text{ mg/kg}$ administered over 72 hours) (15). Severe persistent nausea and vomiting make completing the oral regimen difficult in up to 30% of patients.

Non-immune anaphylactoid reactions (erythematous flushing, pruritus, urticaria, and occasional bronchospasm) occur in 10% to 20% of patients receiving intravenous NAC, predominantly during the high-concentration loading dose in the first hour (16). True IgE-mediated anaphylaxis does not occur. Management consists of temporarily pausing the infusion, administering intravenous diphenhydramine and hydrocortisone, and resuming the NAC infusion at a 50% slower rate once cutaneous symptoms resolve (14,16).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Hemodialysis and Liver Transplantation Criteria</summary><div class="details-content">

Extracorporeal clearance of acetaminophen is highly effective due to the molecule's low molecular weight ($151\text{ Da}$), low plasma protein binding (approximately 10% to 25%), and small volume of distribution ($0.8\text{–}1.0\text{ L/kg}$) (14).

High-flux hemodialysis is indicated in extreme overdoses where the serum acetaminophen concentration exceeds $900\ \mu\text{g/mL}$ ($5{,}950\ \mu\text{mol/L}$), or in patients with early metabolic acidosis or altered mental status prior to the onset of transaminitis (14). Because hemodialysis also clears NAC, the intravenous NAC infusion rate must be increased to at least $12.5\text{ mg/kg/hr}$ during dialysis (14).

When acute liver failure progresses despite timely NAC therapy, emergent evaluation for orthotopic liver transplantation is lifesaving. The widely utilized King's College Hospital criteria identify patients with a $> 85\%$ predicted mortality who require immediate liver transplantation listing (2,3):
- Arterial $\text{pH} \lt 7.30$ after adequate fluid resuscitation (irrespective of the degree of encephalopathy); **OR**
- All three of the following within a 24-hour window:
  1. Grade III or IV hepatic encephalopathy;
  2. Prothrombin time $> 100\text{ seconds}$ ($\text{INR} > 6.5$);
  3. Serum creatinine $> 3.4\text{ mg/dL}$ ($> 300\ \mu\text{mol/L}$).

## Benzodiazepine Overdose</summary><div class="details-content">

Benzodiazepines are among the most frequently prescribed psychotropic agents worldwide and rank second only to opioids in medication-related overdose encounters (17).

However, pure isolated benzodiazepine ingestions have an extraordinarily high therapeutic index and are rarely, if ever, fatal (17,18). Fatalities involving benzodiazepines almost universally involve co-ingestion of other central nervous system or respiratory depressants—predominantly opioids, ethanol, barbiturates, or tricyclic antidepressants (17,18).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Clinical Features and Diagnostic Challenges</summary><div class="details-content">

Benzodiazepines bind stereospecifically to the $\gamma$-subunit of the pentameric $\text{GABA}_\text{A}$ receptor complex in the central nervous system, enhancing the affinity of the inhibitory neurotransmitter $\gamma$-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and increasing the opening frequency of the integral chloride channel (20). The resulting intracellular chloride influx hyperpolarizes the neuronal membrane, producing generalized central nervous system depression.

In isolated overdose, patients present with ataxia, slurred speech (dysarthria), anterograde amnesia, and somnolence (18). True coma is exceedingly rare in single-agent ingestions (18). Significant hypoventilation (occurring in 2% to 12% of cases), bradycardia (1% to 2%), and arterial hypotension (5% to 7%) are uncommon unless massive doses are ingested or secondary respiratory depressants are present (18). Occasionally, paradoxical excitation, delirium, and visual hallucinations occur, which may be misdiagnosed as acute delirium tremens (18).

Routine qualitative urine immunoassay drug screens frequently produce false-negative results for benzodiazepines: most commercial immunoassays detect oxazepam (the common metabolite of diazepam and chlordiazepoxide) but have low cross-reactivity for popular synthetic benzodiazepines such as lorazepam, clonazepam, alprazolam, and midazolam (19). Quantitative serum assays are not available in emergency settings. Consequently, diagnosis rests predominantly on clinical history and physical examination.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Antidotal Management: Flumazenil</summary><div class="details-content">

Flumazenil is a synthetic imidazobenzodiazepine derivative that functions as a competitive antagonist at the benzodiazepine recognition site on the $\text{GABA}_\text{A}$ receptor complex, possessing virtually zero intrinsic agonist activity (20).

Flumazenil is highly effective at reversing benzodiazepine-induced sedation and cognitive impairment within 1 to 2 minutes of intravenous injection, with peak clinical response achieved at 6 to 10 minutes (20). However, its ability to reverse benzodiazepine-induced respiratory depression is unpredictable and inconsistent (21,22).

The initial dose is $0.2\text{ mg}$ administered intravenously over 30 seconds. If adequate consciousness is not restored within 1 minute, a second dose of $0.3\text{ mg}$ can be given, followed by repeated $0.5\text{ mg}$ boluses every minute up to a cumulative ceiling dose of $1.0\text{ mg}$ (or $3.0\text{ mg}$ in severe cases) (20). Because flumazenil's elimination half-life is remarkably brief (approximately 45 to 60 minutes), resedation commonly occurs within 1 to 2 hours when longer-acting benzodiazepines have been ingested; a continuous maintenance infusion of $0.3\text{–}0.4\text{ mg/hr}$ can be titrated to sustain alertness (23).

#### Contraindications and Clinical Role</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinical utility of flumazenil in the ICU is strictly circumscribed by safety concerns (Table 52.2) (23).

Flumazenil abruptly strips the central nervous system of inhibitory GABAergic tone. In patients with chronic benzodiazepine tolerance or physical dependency, flumazenil precipitates acute, severe benzodiazepine withdrawal, manifesting as intractable generalized tonic-clonic seizures and autonomic storm (20,23). Furthermore, in mixed overdoses involving proconvulsant cardiotoxic drugs—specifically tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)—benzodiazepines provide protective anticonvulsant activity; antagonizing this protection with flumazenil frequently unleashes status epilepticus and fatal ventricular arrhythmias (20,23).

Consequently, flumazenil is *strictly contraindicated* in patients presenting with coma of undetermined etiology, those with suspected mixed pharmaceutical ingestion, and individuals with underlying epilepsy or chronic benzodiazepine therapy (23). In contemporary critical care, flumazenil is reserved almost exclusively for the rapid reversal of intentional procedural sedation or isolated pediatric accidental ingestion (23).

## Beta-Receptor Antagonist (Beta-Blocker) Toxicity</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute intoxication with $\beta$-adrenergic receptor antagonists ($\beta$-blockers) represents an uncommon but lethal toxicological emergency in intensive care (24). Overdoses can be life-threatening due to profound negative inotropic and chronotropic collapse, often complicated by systemic vasoplegia (24).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Toxic Manifestations and Membrane-Stabilizing Effects</summary><div class="details-content">

The hallmark cardiovascular signs of $\beta$-blocker toxicity are *bradycardia* (sinus bradycardia, junctional escape, or idioventricular rhythm) and *arterial hypotension* (24). Hypotension stems from a combination of direct negative myocardial inotropy (diminished stroke volume) and peripheral vasodilation caused by blockade of renal $\beta_1$-mediated renin release (24).

In massive overdoses, lipophilic $\beta$-blockers (most notably propranolol, carvedilol, and acebutolol) exert a quinidine-like *membrane-stabilizing effect* that is entirely independent of competitive $\beta$-receptor antagonism (24–26). These agents block fast inward cardiac sodium channels (analogous to Class IA antiarrhythmics), prolonging the PR interval, widening the QRS complex, and precipitating high-degree or complete atrioventricular (AV) dissociation, asystole, and refractory ventricular arrhythmias (25).

Furthermore, lipophilic $\beta$-blockers cross the blood-brain barrier in high concentrations, inducing profound central nervous system depression (somnolence, stupor, and coma) and generalized epileptic seizures (reported in up to 60% of propranolol overdoses) (26). Neurotoxicity and ventricular conduction delays represent direct consequences of sodium-channel membrane stabilization, rather than $\beta$-receptor blockade (25,26).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Glucagon Therapy</summary><div class="details-content">

Glucagon is a $29\text{-amino-acid}$ pancreatic polypeptide hormone whose primary physiological function is the stimulation of hepatic glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis (27). In high pharmacological concentrations, glucagon serves as a premier, life-saving antidote for $\beta$-blocker cardiotoxicity (27,28).

As illustrated in Figure 52.4, both $\beta$-adrenergic receptors and specific G-protein-coupled glucagon receptors reside on the sarcolemma of myocardial cells, independently linked to the enzyme adenylate cyclase. Activation of either receptor hydrolyzes intracellular ATP to cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) (27). In turn, cAMP activates protein kinase A (PKA), which phosphorylates voltage-sensitive L-type calcium channels, promoting inward calcium flux into the cytoplasm and triggering calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (Figure 52.4).

Because glucagon stimulates adenylate cyclase through its own independent receptor, *its positive inotropic and chronotropic actions completely bypass blocked $\beta$-adrenergic receptors* (Figure 52.4) (27,28).

#### Clinical Application and Dosing Regimens</summary><div class="details-content">

Glucagon is indicated for patients with $\beta$-blocker toxicity exhibiting symptomatic bradycardia, high-degree heart block, or hypotension refractory to initial crystalloid resuscitation and atropine (27). A favorable hemodynamic response occurs in approximately 90% of patients when adequate dosages are administered (27,28). However, glucagon *does not* reverse widened QRS complexes, prolonged AV conduction, or central nervous system seizures, because those manifestations are mediated by sodium-channel membrane stabilization (25,26).

Glucagon is initiated with an intravenous bolus of $3\text{–}5\text{ mg}$ (or $0.05\text{ mg/kg}$) administered over 1 to 2 minutes (28,29). Clinical improvement (increased heart rate and mean arterial pressure) typically appears within 3 minutes (29). If no response is observed after 5 to 10 minutes, a second intravenous bolus of $5\text{–}10\text{ mg}$ (or $0.07\text{ mg/kg}$) should be given (28). Because glucagon has an extremely short elimination half-life ($5\text{–}10\text{ minutes}$), a successful bolus must be immediately followed by a continuous intravenous infusion at $2\text{–}5\text{ mg/hr}$ (titrated to maintain hemodynamic stability) (27,28).

To maximize the chronotropic efficacy of glucagon, serum ionized calcium must be maintained within the normal range ($1.15\text{–}1.30\text{ mmol/L}$) via intravenous calcium chloride or gluconate administration (30). The primary adverse effects of high-dose glucagon therapy are intractable nausea and vomiting (which require prophylactic 5-HT3 antagonists, although ondansetron is often ineffective at high glucagon rates), hyperglycemia, secondary hypokalemia, and occasional hypertension from catecholamine release (27).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Adjunctive Inotropes: Phosphodiesterase Inhibitors</summary><div class="details-content">

Phosphodiesterase (PDE) inhibitors (such as milrinone) prevent the enzymatic breakdown of intracellular cyclic AMP to 5'-AMP by inhibiting PDE-3 (Figure 52.4) (31). By sustaining intracellular cAMP levels, PDE inhibitors exert synergistic inotropic effects with glucagon. However, because milrinone causes prominent systemic and pulmonary vasodilation, it can aggravate arterial hypotension and should be combined with an $\alpha_1$-adrenergic vasopressor (such as norepinephrine) (31).

For profound, refractory cardiotoxicity, additional salvage modalities include:
- **High-Dose Insulin Euglycemia (HIE):** High-dose regular insulin ($1\text{ unit/kg}$ IV bolus followed by $1\text{ unit/kg/hr}$, co-infused with concentrated dextrose) switches stressed myocardial metabolism from free fatty acids to glucose, dramatically increasing cardiac output and survival (24).
- **Intravenous Lipid Emulsion (ILE):** 20% lipid emulsion serves as a "lipid sink" to sequester lipophilic drugs (propranolol) while providing high-energy fatty acid substrate to ischemic myocytes (24).
- **Venoarterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (VA-ECMO):** Provides definitive mechanical hemodynamic bridge to drug clearance (24).

## Opioid Overdose</summary><div class="details-content">

The epidemic of opioid-related morbidity and mortality in the United States and worldwide represents an unprecedented public health crisis (32,33). Fatalities encompass not only synthetic street narcotics (illicitly manufactured fentanyl and its ultra-potent analogues) but also widely prescribed therapeutic opioids, including oxycodone, hydrocodone, morphine, methadone, and hydromorphone (32).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Clinical Presentation: The Opioid Triad</summary><div class="details-content">

The classic physical presentation of acute opioid intoxication is the diagnostic triad of:
1. **Central Nervous System Depression:** Stupor, lethargy, or unarousable coma.
2. **Miosis:** Pinpoint pupils (symmetric pupillary constriction down to $1\text{–}2\text{ mm}$), driven by stimulation of the parasympathetic Edinger-Westphal nucleus.
3. **Severe Hypoventilation:** Marked bradypnea (respiratory rate $< 8\text{ breaths/min}$), shallow tidal breathing, and frank apnea.

However, clinical diagnostic accuracy based on physical examination alone is deceptively poor (33). Severe cerebral anoxia, hypothermia, co-ingestion of anticholinergics or sympathomimetics, or ingestion of specific atypical opioids (such as meperidine or propoxyphene) can produce normal or dilated pupils (mydriasis) despite fatal opioid toxicity (33). Consequently, a prompt clinical response to the specific opioid antagonist naloxone remains the most definitive diagnostic and therapeutic test (33).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Naloxone Pharmacotherapy</summary><div class="details-content">

Naloxone is a pure competitive opioid antagonist that binds with high affinity to $\mu$-, $\kappa$-, and $\delta$-opioid receptors, completely lacking intrinsic agonist activity (33). It is exceptionally potent at displacing opioids from the $\mu$-receptor, which mediates the life-threatening clinical effects of analgesia, euphoria, and respiratory depression (33).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### Intranasal Naloxone Delivery</summary><div class="details-content">

Intranasal administration of naloxone has become the cornerstone of pre-hospital and community emergency resuscitation because it is needleless, eliminates the risk of bloodborne needle-stick injuries, and exhibits clinical efficacy equivalent to intravenous administration (34,35).

Commercial intranasal naloxone is available as a pre-packaged single-dose nasal spray delivering either $2\text{ mg}$ or $4\text{ mg}$ of naloxone hydrochloride in $0.1\text{ mL}$ of aqueous solution (35). The nozzle is placed into one nostril and the plunger depressed. The mean time to clinical arousal and restoration of spontaneous ventilation is approximately 3 minutes (34). Peak serum naloxone concentrations are attained at approximately 20 to 30 minutes (Figure 52.5) (35).

Clinical pharmacokinetic data demonstrate that administering a total dose of $4\text{ mg}$ divided as $2\text{ mg}$ in each nostril yields significantly higher peak plasma concentrations than delivering a single $4\text{ mg}$ puff into one nostril, owing to greater mucosal absorption surface area (Figure 52.5) (35).

#### Intravenous Naloxone Protocols and Adverse Reactions</summary><div class="details-content">

In hospital and intensive care unit environments, intravenous administration remains the gold standard, offering 100% bioavailability and immediate circulatory distribution (33,37):

- **Depressed Sensorium with Adequate Ventilation:** In patients with somnolence but normal ventilatory rates and oxygenation, an initial conservative intravenous dose of $0.04\text{–}0.4\text{ mg}$ should be administered, titrating slowly every 2 minutes to restore airway protective reflexes without precipitating acute withdrawal (38).
- **Severe Hypoventilation or Apnea:** In patients presenting with life-threatening bradypnea, cyanosis, or respiratory arrest, an initial dose of $1\text{–}2\text{ mg}$ IV push is mandatory (33). If no improvement in ventilatory effort occurs within 2 to 3 minutes, the dose should be doubled to $4\text{ mg}$. In suspected synthetic fentanyl analogue exposure (e.g., carfentanil), cumulative doses up to $10\text{–}15\text{ mg}$ may be necessary; failure to elicit spontaneous ventilation after $15\text{ mg}$ of naloxone virtually excludes pure opioid toxicity as the sole cause of respiratory arrest (33).
- **Continuous Infusion Strategy:** Naloxone has an elimination half-life of only 60 to 90 minutes (35). Because almost all pharmaceutical and illicit opioids have half-lives ranging from 3 to $> 24\text{ hours}$ (e.g., methadone), recurrent hypoventilation, coma, and apnea occur frequently as naloxone concentrations decline (36). Therefore, once successful waking is achieved, a continuous intravenous infusion should be initiated at an hourly dose equal to *two-thirds of the initial successful bolus dose* (compounded in $250\text{–}500\text{ mL}$ normal saline and infused over 6 hours), continuously titrated to spontaneous ventilation (36).

Naloxone itself possesses negligible direct toxicity. Its primary adverse reaction is the rapid precipitation of severe acute opioid withdrawal (rhinorrhea, piloerection, severe cramping, violent agitation, and emesis) (39). Rarely, high-dose naloxone precipitates acute noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, driven by an explosive surge in endogenous catecholamines causing massive pulmonary venoconstriction and capillary hydrostatic disruption (39).

## Salicylate Intoxication</summary><div class="details-content">

Salicylate poisoning (acetylsalicylic acid, aspirin) remains a treacherous and underappreciated clinical emergency, ranking second only to acetaminophen among over-the-counter analgesic fatalities (40).

Ingestion of $10\text{–}30\text{ g}$ ($150\text{–}300\text{ mg/kg}$) of aspirin in adults causes severe, life-threatening systemic intoxication, while ingestions $> 500\text{ mg/kg}$ are frequently fatal without immediate extracorporeal elimination (40,41).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Pathophysiological Hallmarks: Mixed Acid-Base Disorder</summary><div class="details-content">

Upon ingestion, acetylsalicylic acid is rapidly deacetylated by blood and gastric esterases into its active, toxic moiety: *salicylic acid* (41). Salicylic acid is a weak acid with a $\text{p}K_a$ of 3.0. At normal physiological blood $\text{pH}$ (7.40), greater than 99.9% of circulating salicylate is ionized, remaining trapped in the extracellular compartment and unable to penetrate the lipophilic blood-brain barrier (41). However, systemic acidemia shifts the equilibrium toward the non-ionized, lipid-soluble form, allowing salicylic acid to cross cell membranes into the brain, directly causing cerebral edema and death (41,42).

The hallmark of salicylate intoxication is a pathognomonic *mixed acid-base disturbance: acute respiratory alkalosis combined with high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis* (41):

- **Primary Respiratory Alkalosis:** Salicylate directly stimulates the medullary respiratory center in the brainstem, driving an intense hyperventilation (hyperpnea and tachypnea). The resulting profound decline in arterial $\text{PCO}_2$ produces an early respiratory alkalosis (41). This hyperpneic respiratory alkalosis is among the earliest physical signs of acute toxicity.
- **High-Anion-Gap Metabolic Acidosis:** Salicylate uncouples oxidative phosphorylation in the inner mitochondrial membrane, interrupting the synthesis of ATP while oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide generation accelerate (41,42). To sustain cellular survival, cells shift to anaerobic glycolysis, leading to massive lactic acid accumulation. Concurrently, uncoupling stimulates lipolysis, accelerating hepatic fatty acid oxidation and generating substantial acetoacetic and $\beta$-hydroxybutyric ketoacids. The accumulation of lactic acid, ketoacids, and unexcreted organic acids produces a severe high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis (41,42).
- **Hyperthermia and Dehydration:** Uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation dissipates energy as heat rather than storing it as ATP, generating prominent hyperpyrexia. In combination with tachypneic water loss and profuse diaphoresis, patients rapidly develop severe hypovolemic dehydration (41).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Clinical Manifestations and Diagnostic Levels</summary><div class="details-content">

Early signs of acute salicylate poisoning emerge within 1 to 3 hours: marked hyperpnea, tinnitus, subjective hearing loss, vertigo, nausea, vomiting, and diaphoresis (41).

As tissue salicylate accumulation accelerates, severe toxicity manifests: hyperpyrexia, agitation, confusion, delirium, seizures, cerebral edema, coma, and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema (ARDS) resulting from increased pulmonary vascular permeability (41). Laboratory analysis reveals mixed alkalosis/acidosis, hypernatremic dehydration, hypokalemia, and acute tubular necrosis (41,42).

Serum salicylate concentrations must be obtained immediately and repeated every 2 hours until levels are demonstrably declining (41):
- **Therapeutic Range:** $10\text{–}30\text{ mg/dL}$ ($0.7\text{–}2.2\text{ mmol/L}$).
- **Toxic Range:** $> 40\text{ mg/dL}$ ($> 2.9\text{ mmol/L}$).
- **Severe / Potentially Lethal Intoxication:** $> 75\text{–}80\text{ mg/dL}$ ($> 5.4\text{–}5.8\text{ mmol/L}$) in acute overdose (41).

In chronic salicylate toxicity (common in elderly patients self-medicating for arthritis), severe encephalopathy and noncardiogenic pulmonary edema occur at significantly lower serum concentrations ($30\text{–}50\text{ mg/dL}$) due to prolonged intracellular penetration (41).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Management Protocols and Urine Alkalinization</summary><div class="details-content">

Management combines aggressive crystalloid volume resuscitation, gastrointestinal decontamination, and urinary alkalinization (41,42).

Oral activated charcoal ($1\text{ g/kg}$, maximum dose $100\text{ g}$) should be administered to all patients presenting within several hours of ingestion (41). Because salicylates delay gastric emptying and can form concrete gastric pharmacobezoars, repeated doses of activated charcoal ($0.5\text{ g/kg}$ every 4 hours) are useful for ongoing absorption, especially with enteric-coated or sustained-release preparations (41).

Urinary alkalinization represents the cornerstone of non-dialytic salicylate elimination (Table 52.3) (42,43). By raising the urinary $\text{pH} \ge 7.5$, salicylic acid in the renal tubular lumen is converted into its ionized, charged salicylate conjugate ($\text{p}K_a = 3.0$), which cannot be reabsorbed across tubular epithelial cells ("ion trapping") (41,42). Increasing urine $\text{pH}$ from 5.0 to 7.5 accelerates renal clearance of salicylate by more than 10-fold to 20-fold (41,43).

#### Potassium Management and Hemodialysis Criteria</summary><div class="details-content">

Bicarbonate infusion drives extracellular potassium into cells, aggravating hypokalemia (42). In the presence of hypokalemia, the cortical collecting duct exchanger attempts to conserve potassium by excreting hydrogen ions into the urine, resulting in *paradoxical aciduria* (an acidic urine $\text{pH} \lt 6.0$ despite systemic alkalemia) (41,42). This acidic urine completely defeats ion trapping. Therefore, aggressive potassium chloride repletion (maintaining serum potassium $4.0\text{–}4.5\text{ mEq/L}$) is an absolute physiological prerequisite for successful urine alkalinization (Table 52.3) (42).

Hemodialysis is the definitive extracorporeal elimination technique for severe salicylate toxicity (41,44). Intermittent hemodialysis rapidly corrects acid-base derangements, removes salicylate, and restores fluid-electrolyte balance.

Absolute clinical indications for emergent hemodialysis include (41,44):
- Acute serum salicylate concentration $> 90\text{ mg/dL}$ ($> 6.5\text{ mmol/L}$) in acute overdose, regardless of clinical status;
- Chronic salicylate concentration $> 80\text{ mg/dL}$ ($> 5.8\text{ mmol/L}$) or $> 50\text{–}60\text{ mg/dL}$ in elderly patients with altered sensorium;
- Evidence of end-organ toxicity: cerebral edema, seizures, coma, or noncardiogenic pulmonary edema (ARDS);
- Refractory systemic acidemia ($\text{pH} \le 7.20$) or progressive clinical deterioration despite optimal urinary alkalinization;
- Oliguric acute kidney injury or fluid overload preventing safe bicarbonate administration.

## A Final Word: Public Health and Regulation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

### Regulating Acetaminophen: The Paradox of Safety</summary><div class="details-content">

If any over-the-counter pharmaceutical agent deserves strict legislative and regulatory reform, it is acetaminophen (2–5).

It remains an astonishing paradox of modern medicine that the leading cause of acute liver failure in North America and Western Europe is an over-the-counter pain reliever that can be purchased in lethal quantities without a prescription.

The fact that fully half of all cases of acetaminophen-induced acute liver failure are completely unintentional—arising from ordinary patients attempting to alleviate headaches, musculoskeletal aches, or fever with multiple over-the-counter cough and cold preparations—demonstrates a profound public failure to appreciate its narrow therapeutic index (3,4).

<details class="med-details" open><summary>

#### The Public Health Imperative for Prescription Reform</summary><div class="details-content">

The FDA's mandate requiring stronger warning labels on packaging has done little to mitigate the carnage, for few patients in pain read the fine print on a medication box (5). Restricting high-dose formulations to prescription-only status, capping unit package sizes, and requiring clear prescription guidance represent obvious, evidence-based steps to safeguard human life.

Acetaminophen attained its massive worldwide popularity in the 1970's largely because of widespread medical concern regarding the gastric and metabolic toxicities of aspirin. Looking back across five decades of critical care experience, it appears we replaced a potentially toxic drug with a potentially lethal one.
