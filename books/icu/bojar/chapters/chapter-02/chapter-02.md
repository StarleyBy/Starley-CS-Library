# Diagnostic Techniques in Cardiac Surgery

Although the general nature of a patient's cardiac disease can usually be ascertained from a thorough history and physical examination, diagnostic tests are essential to define the pathology and extent of cardiac disease more precisely. A variety of noninvasive and invasive modalities can be used to identify the presence and severity of cardiovascular abnormalities that may require surgery. This chapter briefly reviews the basic types of diagnostic tests available to the clinician and defines their role in preoperative evaluation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Chest Radiography
- **PA and lateral chest x-ray** is mandatory before cardiac surgery. It confirms the clinical diagnosis (LV enlargement in volume overload, LVH in AS/hypertension, enlarged cardiac silhouette in pericardial effusion, large left atrium in MS, wide mediastinum in aortic dissection/aneurysm) and identifies additional pathology (pulmonary disease, pleural effusions, prior surgical changes).
- **Surgical implications:** Ascending aortic calcification increases stroke risk and may necessitate alternative cannulation sites (femoral/axillary), avoidance of aortic cross-clamping, or transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Mitral annular calcification complicates valve repair/replacement. In reoperations, lateral film shows proximity of RV, aorta, or ITA grafts to the sternum, guiding cannulation strategy.

### Electrocardiography
- **Sinus tachycardia** indicates inadequate β-blockade, increasing risk of preoperative ischemia and postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF).
- **Sinus bradycardia** predicts need for temporary pacing; patients may not tolerate β-blockers or amiodarone.
- **Atrial fibrillation** with rapid ventricular response must be controlled; longstanding persistent AF requires postoperative anticoagulation; Maze procedure may be added.
- **Left bundle branch block (LBBB)** increases risk of asystole during Swan-Ganz insertion → defer until after sternotomy.
- **Right bundle branch block (RBBB) + first-degree AV block** increases risk of complete heart block after TAVR.
- **New conduction abnormalities in aortic endocarditis** suggest annular extension → urgent surgery.
- **Permanent pacemakers:** electrocautery may disrupt function; magnet must be available. ICDs must be inactivated preoperatively.

### Stress Testing and Myocardial Perfusion Imaging
- **Exercise ECG (ETT)** is most useful in patients with intermediate pretest probability of CAD. Findings predicting adverse prognosis at low workload (<6 METs): anginal symptoms, >2 mm ST depression in multiple leads persisting >5 min into recovery, ST elevation, hypotensive response, sustained VT.
- **Radionuclide MPI (SPECT/PET)** is more sensitive/specific than ETT, especially with baseline ECG abnormalities. Pharmacologic stress with regadenoson (adenosine A₂A agonist) is preferred when exercise is not possible. Rest/stress protocols assess viability and ischemia.
- **Stress MRI** has excellent resolution; dobutamine cine CMR has >80% sensitivity/specificity for significant coronary stenosis.
- **Stress echocardiography** identifies stress-induced regional wall motion abnormalities; comparable to nuclear imaging.
- **Viability studies** (SPECT with Tc-99m or thallium, FDG-PET, dobutamine stress echo, delayed enhancement MRI) distinguish hibernating myocardium from necrosis. Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) >15% correlates with sudden death risk in HCM; MRI-defined diastolic wall thickness >5.5 mm predicts contractile recovery after CABG. STICH trial: viability associated with improved LV function but not survival.

### Stress Echocardiography in Valvular Heart Disease
- **Abnormal findings:** symptoms at low workload, exercise capacity <85% predicted, blood pressure fall or <20 mm Hg rise, RVSP >60 mm Hg, worsening regurgitation, EF fall, regional wall motion abnormalities, sustained VT.
- **Aortic stenosis:** Symptom-limited ESE in asymptomatic severe AS identifies 8× higher MACE risk. DSE distinguishes true from pseudo-severe AS in low-flow, low-gradient AS (stage D2). Projected AVA based on normal flow rate may help.
- **Aortic regurgitation:** ESE elicits symptoms, assesses contractile reserve; exercise-induced EF fall prompts early surgery.
- **Mitral stenosis:** Nearly 50% of asymptomatic moderate-severe MS develop symptoms with exercise. High-risk: mean gradient >18 mm Hg (DSE) or >15 mm Hg (exercise), RVSP >60 mm Hg.
- **Mitral regurgitation:** Primary MR: RVSP >60 mm Hg, exercise capacity <85% predicted, latent LV dysfunction, AF, abnormal heart rate recovery. Secondary MR: pulmonary edema, ischemic wall motion abnormalities, EROA increase >13 mm².

### Cardiac Catheterization
- **Gold standard** for most cardiac diagnoses, except acute type A dissection (surgical emergency) and aortic endocarditis with vegetations (embolization risk).
- **Right-heart catheterization:** Swan-Ganz for pressures, oxygen saturations (shunt detection), thermodilution cardiac output.
- **Left-heart catheterization:** LVEDP measurement, ventriculogram (EF calculation, MR detection), aortic gradient. Crossing stenotic aortic valve in severe AS may increase stroke risk.
- **Fluoroscopy** identifies ascending aortic calcification (noncontrast CT for confirmation), coronary calcification, mitral annular calcification, prosthetic valve rocking (endocarditis), or restricted leaflet motion (thrombosis/pannus).

### Coronary Angiography
- **Gold standard for CAD.** Determines dominance, location/extent/degree of stenoses. SYNTAX scores guide PCI vs. CABG decisions.
- **Limitations:** Diffuse disease underestimates stenosis severity. **IVUS** measures minimal luminal area (MLA): left main MLA <7 mm² (symptomatic) or <5.5–6 mm² (asymptomatic) is significant; proximal arteries MLA <4 mm² significant. **OCT** has 10× higher resolution for stent assessment.
- **Fractional flow reserve (FFR)** ≤0.8 indicates functionally significant lesion. **iFR** ≤0.89 correlates with FFR ≤0.8. FFR-guided revascularization reduces grafting, improves patency, reduces MI/mortality.
- **Indications:** Suspected CAD requiring intervention, age >40 for open-heart surgery, annual transplant follow-up.
- **Contrast-induced AKI prevention:** Hemodynamically guided hydration (POSEIDON protocol) based on LVEDP: pre-procedure 3.5 mL/kg/h ×4 h; intra-procedure 5/3/1.5 mL/kg/h based on LVEDP <13/13–18/>18 mm Hg; post-procedure 1.5 mL/kg/h ×1 h.

### Echocardiography
- **Transthoracic (TTE):** initial assessment of LV/RV size/function, valve function, gradients, regurgitation.
- **Transesophageal (TEE):** superior imaging for mitral valve pathology (prolapse, torn chordae, papillary muscle rupture, annular dilatation), endocarditis (vegetations, periannular abscesses), aortic dissection (intimal flap, AR, pericardial effusion), cardiac masses, pericardial effusion/tamponade.
- **3D imaging** critical for MitraClip planning and precise pathology localization.
- **Preoperative echo** (Table 2.3) and **postoperative echo** (Table 2.4) provide comprehensive information across all clinical scenarios.

### Noninvasive Coronary Angiography
- **Coronary CT angiography (CCTA):** 64-slice or higher, heart rate <70 bpm. Sensitivity/specificity ~80–90% for >50% stenosis. Limitations: irregular rhythm, severe calcification, stents, small vessels (<1.5 mm). FFR-CT derived from CCTA superior to standard CTA, SPECT, PET.
- **Cardiac MRI (CMR):** Avoids radiation, less interference from calcification. Indications: coronary anomalies (young patients), coronary aneurysms, graft stenosis. Stress CMR with dobutamine or vasodilators identifies ischemia. Newer pacemakers/ICDs are MRI-conditional; legacy devices safe at ≤1.5 tesla.

### CT and MR for Aortic and Cardiac Disease
- **CT scanning:** Primary for aortic disease. Indications: aortic dissection (type A/B), aortic size assessment (centerline analysis for true diameter), ascending aortic calcification (noncontrast), reoperative planning (RV/aorta/graft proximity to sternum), iliofemoral evaluation pre-TAVR (size, calcification, tortuosity), retroperitoneal hematoma, pericardial/pleural effusions, intracardiac masses.
- **CMR:** Most sensitive/specific for aortic dissection. **Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE)** patterns differentiate ischemic (subendocardial/transmural) from nonischemic cardiomyopathies (midwall, epicardial). **MRA** with gadolinium defines aortic arch aneurysms. **Cine CMR** differentiates blood from tissue for ventricular function, valvular pathology, masses. Distinguishes restrictive from constrictive pericarditis.

## I. Chest Radiography</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### A. PA and lateral chest x-ray</summary><div class="details-content">

PA and lateral chest x-ray should be obtained on all patients before surgery. It should be consistent with the patient's cardiac diagnosis and can provide a wealth of potential information to the surgeon (Figure 2.1).

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Findings Compatible with Clinical Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Left ventricular (LV) enlargement** — volume overload (aortic regurgitation or mitral regurgitation) or dilated cardiomyopathy
- **Left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)** — aortic stenosis, hypertension
- **Enlarged cardiac silhouette** — pericardial effusion
- **Large left atrium or calcified mitral valve/annulus** — mitral valve disease
- **Wide superior mediastinum** — aortic dissection or aortic aneurysm
- **Pulmonary vascular redistribution** — congestive heart failure

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Other Potentially Relevant Abnormalities</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Pulmonary:** emphysema, pneumonia, parenchymal nodules, interstitial disease, previous pulmonary resection
- **Pleural space:** effusions, pneumothorax
- **Mediastinum:** tumors or widened mediastinum consistent with aortic disease
- **Bone:** pectus excavatum, rib resection from previous thoracotomy
- **Foreign bodies:** sternal wires from previous sternotomy, type of prosthetic heart valve, pacemaker wires, central venous catheters, position of intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### B. Surgical Implications</summary><div class="details-content">

The chest x-ray provides important information that may influence operability or operative technique.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Ascending Aortic Calcification</summary><div class="details-content">

Calcification of the ascending aorta or arch increases the risk of stroke. This may require:
- Alternative cannulation site (femoral or axillary artery)
- Avoidance of aortic cross-clamping (beating or fibrillating heart surgery)
- Avoidance of both cannulation and cross-clamping (off-pump surgery)
- Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest for aortic valve replacement or ascending aorta resection

Significant ascending aortic calcification in a patient with symptomatic severe AS is usually an indication for transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). Epiaortic echocardiography may be indicated during surgery to evaluate for ascending aortic atherosclerosis. Suspicion of ascending aortic calcification by chest x-ray (usually on the lateral film) or fluoroscopy during cardiac catheterization may be further defined by noncontrast CT scanning (see Figure 2.10). Calcification of the aortic knob alone is a common finding but is usually of little significance.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Elevated Hemidiaphragm</summary><div class="details-content">

An elevated hemidiaphragm on one side might deter the surgeon from using the contralateral internal thoracic artery (ITA), especially in diabetic patients, who arguably are more prone to phrenic nerve paresis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Mitral Annular Calcification</summary><div class="details-content">

Mitral annular calcification in patients with MR makes mitral valve repair or replacement more difficult and may necessitate creative surgical techniques.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Reoperative Surgery Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

For patients undergoing reoperation, PA and lateral films are essential:
- **PA film:** may identify proximity of the ITA pedicle (metallic clips) to the midline, although selective ITA angiogram is usually required for true course
- **Lateral film:** demonstrates proximity of aortic aneurysm, right ventricle (RV), and occasionally ITA to the posterior sternal table

If concern exists about potential damage to these structures with sternotomy, femoral or axillary cannulation and even initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) prior to sternotomy may be indicated. Alternatively, a thoracotomy incision should be considered, especially for redo mitral valve surgery (right chest) or redo coronary surgery to left-sided vessels (left chest).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. Minimally Invasive Surgery Planning</summary><div class="details-content">

The location and orientation of the heart (craniocaudal and relationship to midline) should be considered when selecting the appropriate incision. For example, in thin patients and those with emphysema, the heart has a vertical orientation and lies caudal in the chest. For aortic valve surgery, a more distal intercostal space incision may be necessary with either a partial upper sternotomy or anterior thoracotomy approach.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 6. Congestive Heart Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

The patient with congestive heart failure may benefit from aggressive diuresis prior to surgery or hemofiltration during CPB.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## II. Electrocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

#### A. Overview

A 12-lead ECG must be reviewed prior to surgery because it can yield valuable information about the nature of the patient's disease, the urgency of surgery, the appropriate management of arrhythmias, and other considerations in perioperative management.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### B. Common Disorders of Rate and Rhythm</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Sinus Tachycardia</summary><div class="details-content">

The presence of sinus tachycardia in a patient with coronary disease suggests inadequate β-blockade. This may predispose the patient to preoperative ischemia and postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF). Most patients undergoing CABG should be managed preoperatively with β-blockers.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Sinus Bradycardia</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with sinus bradycardia usually require temporary postoperative pacing and may not tolerate β-blockers or amiodarone that might otherwise be used prophylactically to prevent postoperative AF. Patients with sick sinus or tachycardia/bradycardia syndrome are more likely to require a permanent pacemaker postoperatively.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Atrial Fibrillation</summary><div class="details-content">

A rapid ventricular response to AF must be controlled pharmacologically. It can precipitate myocardial ischemia in patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) and can compromise cardiac output in patients with significant LVH. If longstanding persistent AF is present:
- Prophylactic medications to prevent postoperative AF are usually of little benefit
- Postoperative anticoagulation is indicated to prevent thromboembolic stroke
- A Maze procedure may be considered as an adjunct to other cardiac operations

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Ventricular Tachyarrhythmias</summary><div class="details-content">

Ventricular tachyarrhythmias may result from active ischemia or remote myocardial infarction. If revascularization is indicated, a postoperative electrophysiology study should be considered in patients with sustained ventricular tachycardia (VT). An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) should be inserted if VT is inducible or if the ejection fraction (EF) remains <35% 90 days after revascularization.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### C. Conduction Problems</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Bundle Branch Blocks</summary><div class="details-content">

Ischemic ECG changes are difficult to assess in patients with a bundle branch block. Early catheterization may be indicated in patients with acute coronary syndromes considered at high risk based on clinical criteria.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Left Bundle Branch Block (LBBB)</summary><div class="details-content">

The presence of LBBB increases the risk of asystole during insertion of a Swan-Ganz pulmonary artery (PA) catheter. This should be deferred until after sternotomy has been performed so that immediate epicardial pacing can be achieved.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Right Bundle Branch Block (RBBB) with First-Degree Block</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with RBBB and first-degree block are more prone to the development of complete heart block after TAVR and may require placement of a permanent pacemaker.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Conduction Abnormalities in Aortic Endocarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

The development of conduction abnormalities in a patient with aortic valve endocarditis suggests annular extension of the infection, which is an indication for urgent surgery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. Inferior Infarction</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients sustaining an inferior infarction may develop heart block and require temporary pacing preoperatively.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### D. Evidence of Ischemia and Infarction</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. ST and T Wave Changes</summary><div class="details-content">

ST and T wave changes consistent with ischemia require aggressive management. This may entail:
- Intravenous nitrates
- Antiplatelet agents (aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors [clopidogrel, ticagrelor, prasugrel], and/or glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors)
- Unfractionated heparin or low molecular weight heparin
- Placement of an IABP

An early invasive strategy with urgent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or surgery is usually indicated. Evidence of recent infarction may influence the timing and risk of surgery. Waiting several days may lower the surgical risk in stable patients, but is inadvisable if the patient develops recurrent chest pain, ischemia, cardiogenic shock, or has threatening coronary anatomy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Q Waves and Viability</summary><div class="details-content">

In patients with depressed LV function, an ECG with Q waves is more consistent with transmural infarction, but the absence of Q waves suggests that the myocardium may be chronically ischemic and hibernating, rather than infarcted. Further evaluation with viability studies may be indicated to determine whether surgery will prove beneficial in improving LV function and survival.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### E. Transvenous Pacemaker and ICD Issues</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Permanent Pacemakers</summary><div class="details-content">

The function of a permanent transvenous pacing system can be disrupted by electrocautery at the time of surgery. A magnet must be available to convert the pacemaker to a fixed pacing mode.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. ICD Units</summary><div class="details-content">

ICD units should be inactivated prior to surgery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Postoperative Interrogation</summary><div class="details-content">

Because the right atrial lead of a dual-chamber pacemaker may be displaced during surgery or the pulse generator may be damaged by electrocautery, all permanent pacemaker systems must be interrogated immediately after surgery to ensure appropriate sensing and pacing.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## III. Stress Testing and Myocardial Perfusion Imaging in Coronary Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

#### A. Overview

Stress testing plays a major role in detecting the potential presence and functional significance of CAD. In addition to basic exercise stress testing, rest and stress myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) have greater sensitivity and specificity in identifying viable and ischemic myocardium that will benefit from an interventional procedure.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### B. Exercise ECG (Exercise Tolerance Test or ETT)</summary><div class="details-content">

In patients who can exercise, stress testing to maximal exercise (or at least >85% of maximal predicted heart rate to optimize sensitivity) is performed on a treadmill with a graded protocol (Figure 2.2). ECG changes of ischemia can be difficult to detect in patients with LBBB, ventricular-paced rhythms, significant LVH, or ST changes on baseline ECG; for these patients, an imaging study is preferable. The ECG leads that demonstrate ST depressions during exercise testing do not necessarily correlate with the site of ischemia, but this can be determined more precisely with imaging studies.

![](_page_6_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2.2 •** Positive exercise stress test. (Left) baseline ECG before exercise (leads II, III, V5, V6). (Right) exercise ECG after four minutes of stage 2. At a heart rate of 157 bpm, note the presence of 3 mm of ST depression in these leads, suggesting inferolateral ischemia.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Interpretation Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The sensitivity and specificity of ETTs are not ideal, and proper interpretation depends on the pretest probability of CAD:
- A positive ETT in an asymptomatic nondiabetic patient may be a false positive
- A negative ETT in a patient with symptoms and multiple risk factors does not exclude CAD

Therefore, exercise ECG testing is most useful for the patient with an intermediate probability of disease based on clinical criteria.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Clinical Value</summary><div class="details-content">

Exercise testing mimics the clinical circumstances during which the patient becomes symptomatic and determines the workload level at which symptoms occur. The latter correlates with the likelihood of more severe coronary disease and with prognosis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. High-Risk Findings</summary><div class="details-content">

The following findings are consistent with multivessel coronary disease and an adverse prognosis, especially when they occur at a low workload (<6 METs):
- Development of anginal symptoms
- >2 mm ST depression in multiple leads persisting >5 minutes into recovery
- ST segment elevation
- Failure to increase blood pressure to >120 mm Hg or sustained decrease >10 mm Hg or below rest levels
- Sustained ventricular tachycardia

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### C. Radionuclide Myocardial Perfusion Imaging (rMPI)</summary><div class="details-content">

Rest and stress imaging can assess myocardial perfusion, viability, global myocardial function, and regional wall motion abnormalities. Stress may be induced as part of an exercise protocol, pharmacologically with a coronary vasodilator, or with an inotrope (dobutamine) to increase myocardial oxygen demand with mild vasodilatation.

Perfusion distal to a stenosis is maintained at rest by autoregulatory dilatation of resistance vessels. Exercise or vasodilators elicit regional differences in coronary flow reserve: blood flow increases three- to fivefold in normal arteries but much less beyond coronary stenoses. This discrepancy identifies ischemic zones and, inferentially, coronary stenosis.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Techniques and Tracers</summary><div class="details-content">

rMPI is more sensitive and specific than exercise stress testing in detecting CAD and is more effective in determining severity and location of ischemia. It is most useful when baseline ECG changes preclude adequate interpretation or when the patient cannot adequately exercise.

- **SPECT imaging** is more widely available than PET
- **Tc-99m tetrofosmin (Myoview)** and **Tc-99m sestamibi (Cardiolite)** have essentially supplanted thallium-201

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Exercise Protocols</summary><div class="details-content">

If the patient can exercise, a treadmill or bicycle is used. At peak exercise or when symptoms develop, a radioisotope is injected, exercise continues for one minute, and SPECT imaging is obtained. Low-level treadmill exercise combined with vasodilator stress imaging gives an assessment of exercise capacity, provides excellent images, and is associated with fewer noncardiac side effects.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Pharmacologic Stress (Vasodilators)</summary><div class="details-content">

If the patient cannot exercise, pharmacologic stress is generally induced with:
- **Regadenoson (Lexiscan)** — selective adenosine A₂A receptor agonist (most common)
- Less commonly: adenosine or dipyridamole

Regadenoson 400 μg is given intravenously (IV), flushed with saline, followed 10–20 seconds later by radioisotope injection; imaging is obtained 15–45 minutes later. These medications produce coronary vasodilation with a fourfold increase in myocardial blood flow above baseline in normal arteries (coronary flow reserve = hyperemic/baseline flow). With flow-limiting coronary stenosis, tracer uptake is reduced in the myocardium subtended by those vessels. At peak stress, lack of tracer uptake (“cold spot”) may be consistent with either irreversible infarction or ischemia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Dobutamine Stress</summary><div class="details-content">

Contraindications to vasodilators include hypotension, sick sinus syndrome, high-degree AV block, bronchospastic lung disease, or theophylline use. Dobutamine can be used for MPI in these patients:

- Administered in five three-minute stages: 5, 10, 20, 30, and 40 μg/kg/min
- Increases heart rate (chronotropy) and contractility (inotropy), increasing oxygen demand and myocardial blood flow
- Once target heart rate is achieved (occasionally with atropine) or symptoms develop, radionuclide is injected over 10 seconds, allowed to circulate for one minute, and imaging obtained

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. Rest/Stress Protocols</summary><div class="details-content">

Rest/stress protocols are most commonly used because they can assess viability and ischemia. A common protocol involves:
- Infusion of radioactive tracer for rest images (e.g., 9 μg of tetrofosmin)
- Vasodilator injection
- Re-administration of a three-times-higher dose of tracer
- Imaging obtained 15–60 minutes later (Figure 2.3)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 6. Stress/Redistribution Studies (Thallium-201)</summary><div class="details-content">

Stress/redistribution studies may be performed using thallium-201, either after exercise or after dipyridamole administration:
- Initial stress imaging within 10 minutes
- Redistribution imaging 2.5–4 hours later
- If residual defect persists, delayed reimaging 18–24 hours later after small reinjection of thallium-201 (improves sensitivity for viability)
- Alternatively, a rest-only image may be obtained with initial and delayed redistribution images

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### D. Stress MRI Scanning</summary><div class="details-content">

Stress MRI has excellent spatial and temporal resolution and is very effective in assessing regional wall motion abnormalities and detecting, localizing, and quantifying ischemia. Various applications include:
- Rest/stress perfusion imaging
- Dobutamine cine stress cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) — has >80% sensitivity and specificity for identifying significant coronary stenosis

Some consider nuclear imaging the gold standard for assessing myocardial perfusion and recommend it as a noninvasive approach for intermediate- to high-risk patients with equivocal stress tests: cardiac CT to assess anatomic stenosis, then stress MRI to assess hemodynamic significance. Delayed gadolinium enhancement on cardiac MR identifies areas of viability and nonviability (see section F.3.e).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### E. Stress Echocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Stress echocardiography is based on the principle that stress-induced ischemia from coronary artery stenosis produces regional wall motion abnormalities. This may be performed using:
- **Exercise stress echo (ESE)** — bicycle or treadmill
- **Dobutamine stress echo (DSE)** — increases myocardial oxygen demand

If demand cannot be met by increased blood flow, ischemia produces regional wall motion abnormalities in the distribution of the stenotic coronary artery, determining location and extent of ischemia. Stress echoes are more sensitive and specific than exercise ECG and provide comparable results to exercise nuclear imaging.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### F. Viability Studies</summary><div class="details-content">

Myocardial viability studies are useful in patients with severe LV dysfunction to distinguish myocardial necrosis from “hibernating myocardium” that may recover function after revascularization. The clinical utility of these studies has been called into question.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Clinical Impact of Revascularization</summary><div class="details-content">

Revascularization of viable areas generally improves LV function and symptoms in patients with CHF with or without angina, although in some patients symptoms improve without improvement in global or regional function. Extensive remodeling and high end-systolic volumes are associated with less improvement in LV function.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Evidence from STICH Trial</summary><div class="details-content">

Numerous meta-analyses have reported a strong association between viability and improved survival with CABG. However, in the 10-year follow-up of the STICH (Surgical Treatment for Ischemic Heart Failure) trial:
- Presence of viability was associated with improved LV function with both medical and surgical therapy
- This did not translate into improved survival
- Another analysis indicated viability was predictive of survival benefit in patients with moderate LV dysfunction but lost prognostic benefit when LV dysfunction became severe

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Modalities for Detecting Viability</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### a. SPECT Imaging with Tc-99m Tracers (Tetrofosmin or Sestamibi)</summary><div class="details-content">

Based on intact mitochondrial function reflecting viability. Gated SPECT scanning allows assessment of ventricular function as well as viability using routine rest/stress imaging.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### b. SPECT Imaging with Thallium</summary><div class="details-content">

Thallium uptake reflects cell membrane integrity; viable zones will perfuse and retain thallium at rest.
- **Rest-redistribution imaging:** nonischemic viable tissue retains thallium at rest; rest ischemia shows initial defect with redistribution four hours later
- **Stress-redistribution imaging:** ischemia produces defect at stress that may demonstrate redistribution with reinjection or delayed imaging up to 18–24 hours later

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### c. ¹⁸F-Deoxyglucose (FDG) Uptake (PET or SPECT)</summary><div class="details-content">

FDG is a marker of glucose uptake, assessing metabolism and cell viability.
- Perfusion assessed with ¹³N-ammonia or rubidium-82
- ¹⁸FDG injected to assess metabolism
- Matching perfusion and metabolism = nonischemic or infarcted
- Preserved metabolic activity in zones of reduced perfusion = viable, hibernating myocardium
- May detect viability in zones considered nonviable by thallium imaging

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### d. Dobutamine Stress Echo (DSE)</summary><div class="details-content">

Identifies contractile reserve, suggesting viability. Low-dose dobutamine assesses changes in global and regional wall motion. A biphasic response (improvement at low dose, worsening at peak stress) is highly predictive of recovery of regional contractile function in patients with LV dysfunction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### e. Delayed Enhancement Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)</summary><div class="details-content">

Based on cell membrane integrity using gadolinium-based contrast:
- Normal myocardium: low gadolinium concentration → appears black
- Abnormal myocardial tissue: accumulates gadolinium → hyper-enhanced and bright
- **Late gadolinium enhancement (LGE)** in regions of transmural nonviability
- LGE >15% correlates with sudden death risk in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy
- MRI-defined diastolic wall thickness >5.5 mm predicts contractile recovery after CABG
- MRI provides excellent image quality with precise epicardial and endocardial borders for wall motion and thickening assessment

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## IV. Stress Echocardiography in Valvular Heart Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

#### A. General Comments

Exercise stress echo (ESE) is very useful in assessing the physiologic and hemodynamic severity of valvular heart disease in patients without significant symptoms and can be used to assess prognosis (see section VII for non-stress echo imaging). The patient should have resting and stress echocardiogram for assessment of:
- Valve gradients
- Degree of regurgitation
- Stroke volume
- PA and right ventricular systolic pressures (RVSP)
- RV and LV size and function

**The test is abnormal if:**
- Symptoms occur at lower than expected workload
- Exercise capacity <85% of predicted
- Blood pressure falls or increases <20 mm Hg
- RVSP rises to >60 mm Hg
- Degree of valvular regurgitation worsens
- EF falls or regional wall motion abnormalities develop
- Sustained ventricular arrhythmias occur

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### B. Aortic Stenosis (AS)</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic stenosis is a progressive process, and patients often limit activities and deny symptoms. Because of the adverse impact of symptoms on survival (mean survival only 1–2 years), stress testing is recommended in “asymptomatic” stage C patients with severe AS.

Studies show that an abnormal symptom-limited ESE is associated with:
- Eightfold higher risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE)
- 5.5 times higher risk of sudden death
- Increase in mean gradient >20 mm Hg or exercise-induced pulmonary hypertension associated with higher MACE risk

Both ACC/AHA and European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines recommend AVR for patients who develop symptoms (class I) or an abnormal blood pressure response (class IIa) with exercise stress testing.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Dobutamine Stress Echo in Low-Flow, Low-Gradient AS</summary><div class="details-content">

DSE is very useful in stage D2 patients (low-flow, low-gradient AS with depressed LV function). DSE differentiates true AS from pseudo-severe AS (AVA increases with dobutamine due to increased cardiac output without gradient increase).

**a. Stage D2 patients:**
- Diagnosis of severe AS confirmed by mean gradient increase to >40 mm Hg with dobutamine
- AVA may calculate <1.0 cm² even if gradient does not reach that level (AVA-gradient discordance)
- Discordance may occur when inadequate contractile reserve present (stroke volume <20% or EF falls), suggesting intrinsic myocardial dysfunction, not just afterload mismatch
- AVR results (surgical or TAVR) are suboptimal with higher mortality, but AVR still improves upon poor natural history if untreated

**b. Poor contractile reserve:**
- May be difficult to differentiate true from pseudo-severe AS
- “Projected AVA” based on normal transvalvular flow rate may more appropriately assess severity

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Paradoxical Low-Flow, Low-Gradient AS (Stage D3)</summary><div class="details-content">

The role of DSE is not clear-cut in stage D3 AS (normal EF). Diagnosis is usually made because of low stroke volume index <35 mL/m². If that is not present, severe AS is unlikely. Projected AVA may be helpful, but aortic valve calcium assessment by CT scanning may be more suggestive of severe AS.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### C. Aortic Regurgitation (AR)</summary><div class="details-content">

Progression to symptomatic AR is slow, and patients often remain asymptomatic despite progressive LV dysfunction. ESE can:
- Elicit symptoms
- Assess contractile reserve (absence correlates with potentially irreversible LV dysfunction even after AVR)
- Exercise-induced fall in EF prompts early consideration of surgery

Other subtle changes considered high-risk:
- Early RV systolic dysfunction
- Measurements approaching guideline recommendations for surgery (EF 50–55% or LVESD approaching 50 mm)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### D. Mitral Stenosis (MS)</summary><div class="details-content">

ESE or DSE is useful in assessing physiologic significance of MS when discordance exists between clinical symptoms and hemodynamics. Some patients appear asymptomatic with severe MS, while others are symptomatic with moderate MS.

With exercise:
- Heart rate increases → less diastolic filling time
- Mitral gradient increases along with LA and PA pressures

Nearly 50% of patients with asymptomatic moderate–severe MS develop symptoms during exercise.

**High-risk features:**
- Mean gradient >18 mm Hg with DSE or >15 mm Hg with exercise
- RVSP >60 mm Hg at peak exercise
- Rapid rise in RV systolic pressure with less exercise → more likely to develop dyspnea

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### E. Mitral Regurgitation (MR)</summary><div class="details-content">

Stress echo is helpful in assessing exercise-induced changes in MR when discordance exists between symptoms and severity.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Primary (Degenerative) MR</summary><div class="details-content">

High-risk features:
- Increase in RVSP to >60 mm Hg
- Exercise capacity <85% of predicted
- Latent LV dysfunction
- AF
- Abnormal heart rate recovery

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Secondary (Functional or Ischemic) MR</summary><div class="details-content">

ESE may reveal:
- Subclinical LV dysfunction due to ischemia
- Severe MR suggested by:
  - Pulmonary edema
  - Ischemic wall motion abnormalities
  - Increase in effective regurgitant orifice area >13 mm²

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## V. Cardiac Catheterization</summary><div class="details-content">

#### A. Overview

The gold standard for diagnosis of most forms of cardiac disease remains cardiac catheterization. It is indicated in most patients whose clinical presentation and diagnostic testing suggest an interventional procedure (PCI or cardiac surgery) may be indicated. **Exceptions:**
- Acute type A aortic dissections (surgical emergencies)
- Aortic valve endocarditis with vegetations (catheter manipulation may cause embolization)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### B. Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Right-Heart Catheterization</summary><div class="details-content">

Performed in patients with valve disease and those with coronary disease and LV dysfunction. Involves placement of a Swan-Ganz PA catheter to:
- Obtain intracardiac pressures
- Measure oxygen saturations from each chamber to detect intracardiac shunts (ASD, VSD)
- Mixed venous oxygen saturation from PA port indirectly reflects cardiac output
- Thermodilution cardiac output obtained
- Used with pressure gradients to calculate valve areas using the Gorlin formula

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Left-Heart Catheterization</summary><div class="details-content">

Involves advancing a catheter from the aorta through the aortic valve into the LV, allowing:
- Measurement of left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP)
- Left ventriculogram to assess EF (EDV − ESV / EDV) (Figure 2.7)
- Identification of MR (Figure 2.8)
- Fick cardiac output calculation
- Aortic valve gradient measurement during pullback

**Caution:** In patients with AS, crossing the valve increases stroke risk. This should not be performed if severe AS is confirmed by echocardiography.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Combined Pressure Evaluation</summary><div class="details-content">

Evaluation of both left- and right-heart pressures is invaluable in diagnosing constrictive pericarditis (see Figure 1.32).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Aortogram (“Root Shot”)</summary><div class="details-content">

May be performed in patients with aortic valve disease to assess degree of AR (Figure 2.9). Excessive whip of an angiographic catheter may suggest dilated aortic root. Aortogram provides an estimate of aortic size that might necessitate ascending aorta replacement, but CT or echocardiogram assesses size more precisely.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. Fluoroscopy</summary><div class="details-content">

Fluoroscopy yields valuable information, including:

**a. Ascending aortic calcification** — may require further evaluation by noncontrast CT scanning (Figure 2.10)

**b. Severe coronary calcification or extensive coronary stenting** — may make bypass grafting virtually impossible

**c. Location of intravascular catheters or assist devices** — or position of intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP)

**d. Severe mitral annular calcification** — can make mitral valve repair or replacement technically challenging (Figure 2.11)

**e. “Rocking” of a prosthetic valve** — suggestive of endocarditis with annular invasion and possible dehiscence

**f. Limitation of movement of prosthetic valve discs** — consistent with valve thrombosis or restriction by pannus ingrowth (Figure 2.12)

</div></details>

</div></details>

## VI. Coronary Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### A. Basic Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Coronary angiography is performed as part of the cardiac catheterization procedure by placing special preformed catheters directly into the coronary ostia and injecting contrast into the coronary arteries (Figures 2.13 and 2.14). Angiography assesses:
- Whether circulation is right or left dominant (posterior descending artery origin)
- Location, extent, and degree of coronary stenoses (Figure 2.15)

This information determines if an interventional procedure is indicated and whether PCI or CABG is preferable. SYNTAX and SYNTAX II scores are often used to make that determination. Anatomic factors for surgery include quality and bypassability of target vessels based on size and extent of distal disease.

(C) LAO 30° caudal 30° projection provides a good view of the LAD system and the origin of the circumflex. </div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

Anteroposterior (AP), right anterior oblique (RAO), and left anterior oblique (LAO) views at varying cranial and caudal angulation optimize visualization. For reoperative procedures:
- **True AP projection** — identifies whether ITA pedicle is close to the midline (Figure 2.16)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### C. Limitations of Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Coronary angiography is still considered the gold standard for CAD identification, but it has limitations.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Diameter Stenosis and Cross-Sectional Area</summary><div class="details-content">

For a purely concentric stenosis:
- 50% diameter = 75% loss of cross-sectional area (CSA)
- 67% diameter = 90% loss of CSA
- Thus, >50% diameter stenosis is considered significant.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Diffuse Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

Calculation of % diameter stenosis compares narrowed segment to the largest segment of an artery. In patients with diffuse CAD, the larger segment may not reflect normal vessel diameter if the entire vessel is diseased, thus underestimating disease extent.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Intravascular Ultrasound (IVUS)</summary><div class="details-content">

IVUS visualizes the entire vessel wall and gives more precise calculation of narrowing, measured as “minimal luminal area” (MLA) in mm² (Figure 2.18). Indications:
- Identify stenosis that may benefit from stenting
- Particularly helpful in indeterminate left main stenosis

**Significant MLA thresholds:**
- Left main: <7 mm² (symptomatic) or <5.5–6 mm² (asymptomatic)
- Other proximal arteries: <4 mm²

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT)</summary><div class="details-content">

OCT is an optical analog of IVUS with tenfold higher resolution. It is beneficial for:
- Assessing plaque burden in native arteries
- Assessing results of coronary stenting (identifies dissections, thrombus, incomplete stent apposition)

</div></details>

![](_page_23_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2.18 •** Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) study of the left main coronary artery. A 2 Fr echo probe is introduced into the vessel, producing images of the lumen and the surrounding vessel wall. This allows for the calculation of a minimal luminal area (MLA). In this case, the MLA was <4 mm², consistent with severe left main stenosis.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. Fractional Flow Reserve (FFR)</summary><div class="details-content">

The correlation of stenosis degree and functional significance is imprecise. FFR measurements using a pressure wire help determine if intervention is indicated. FFR assesses maximal myocardial flow beyond a stenosis relative to normal artery. Hyperemic flow decreases significantly with >50% stenosis.

**Technique:**
- Catheter placed through stenosis
- Proximal and distal pressure measurements after inducing hyperemic response (intracoronary or IV adenosine)
- Distal/proximal pressure ratio (FFR) ≤0.8 = functionally significant → revascularization provides clinical benefit
- Also beneficial for sorting out left main lesion significance

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 6. Instantaneous Wave-Free Ratio (iFR)</summary><div class="details-content">

iFR is a non-hyperemic pressure-based index of stenosis severity using the wave-free period of diastole. An iFR ≤0.89 is considered functionally significant, has excellent correlation with FFR ≤0.8, and provides comparable outcome in guiding PCI.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 7. FFR-Guided Surgical Revascularization</summary><div class="details-content">

FFR-guided approach to surgical revascularization is uncommon. However, studies show this approach results in:
- Less grafting
- Higher graft patency rate
- Lower rate of angina
- Significant reduction in myocardial infarction and mortality out to six years

This information could help decide which patients should undergo surgery and which vessels need bypass.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 8. Occluded Vessels</summary><div class="details-content">

Occluded vessels that are underfilled via collateral flow may represent large vessels that are good surgical targets (but not always). Symptomatic patients with viable myocardium subtended by occluded vessels may benefit from PCI of the chronically occluded vessel or from CABG.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### D. Important Indications for Coronary Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Suspected CAD Requiring Intervention</summary><div class="details-content">

Any patient with suspected CAD in whom an interventional procedure might be indicated on a clinical basis, including:
- ST-elevation myocardial infarctions (STEMIs)
- Acute coronary syndromes (non-STEMI or unstable angina)
- Stable ischemic heart disease with positive stress tests
- Ischemic pulmonary edema

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Age >40 Requiring Open-Heart Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients >age 40 who require open-heart surgery for other reasons. Angiography should also be considered in younger patients with multiple risk factors for premature CAD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Cardiac Transplant Follow-Up</summary><div class="details-content">

Annual follow-up of cardiac transplant patients to detect silent allograft CAD.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### E. Procedural Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Metformin</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients taking metformin for diabetes should withhold this medication starting the day before catheterization.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Contrast-Induced Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) Prevention</summary><div class="details-content">

The efficacy of numerous medications to reduce contrast-induced AKI has not been consistently demonstrated. Most protocols involve hydration prior to, during, and following catheterization to dilute contrast in the kidneys. However, excessive hydration can contribute to heart failure and venous hypertension, deleterious to renal function.

**Hemodynamically-guided hydration** based on central venous pressure or LVEDP reduces contrast-induced AKI, especially important in patients with HF and chronic kidney disease.

**POSEIDON study protocol:**
- **Pre-procedure:** 3.5 mL/kg/h × 4 h
- **Intra-procedure (based on LVEDP):**
  - 5 mL/kg/h for LVEDP <13 mm Hg
  - 3 mL/kg/h for LVEDP 13–18 mm Hg
  - 1.5 mL/kg/h for LVEDP >18 mm Hg
- **Post-procedure:** 1.5 mL/kg/h × 1 h

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## VII. Echocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

#### A. General Principles

Echocardiography provides real-time two- and three-dimensional (2D and 3D) imaging of the thoracic aorta and cardiac structures. It is an invaluable noninvasive means of evaluating RV and LV size and function, with color, continuous wave, and pulsed Doppler recordings to assess valve function before, during, and after surgery.

Although adequate information can be ascertained from a transthoracic study in the preoperative patient (Figures 2.19 and 2.20), transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provides superior imaging because of the probe's proximity to the heart. Both 2D and 3D imaging are extremely helpful in assessing mitral valve pathology and results of surgical or percutaneous mitral valve repair.

TEE is very important in the preoperative evaluation of MR because it provides assessment of the nature and degree of MR, which may differ from intraoperative evaluation due to altered loading conditions.

#### 3. Aortic Dissection</summary><div class="details-content">

Although most patients with suspected dissections undergo CT scan with contrast as initial imaging, TEE has very high sensitivity and specificity in identifying dissections, noting:
- Intimal flap and its origin
- Potential differential flow in true and false lumens
- Degree of AR
- Presence of pericardial effusion

The study is usually performed intraoperatively but may be performed preoperatively in indeterminate cases (Figure 2.27).

</div></details>

#### 4. Cardiac Masses</summary><div class="details-content">

TEE is usually the best technique to identify location and site of attachment of a cardiac tumor (Figures 2.28 and 2.29).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. Pericardial Effusion and Tamponade</summary><div class="details-content">

TEE is invaluable in identifying pericardial effusions and tamponade when transthoracic imaging is not adequate.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### C. Stress Echocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

DSE can be used to:
- Identify ischemia and myocardial viability
- Determine contractile reserve
- Assess severity of AS in patients with low-flow, low-gradient AS (primarily stage D2, occasionally stage D3)

</div></details>

## VIII. Noninvasive Coronary Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

#### A. General Comments

Technological advances in noninvasive imaging have led to CT and MRI scanning for coronary anatomy evaluation. Temporal and spatial resolution is good for evaluating proximal coronary arteries, though limitations exist. They are generally recommended for:
- Patients with equivocal stress tests
- Intermediate coronary risk
- Atypical symptoms

Not recommended as screening in asymptomatic patients or for patients with typical symptoms that may warrant PCI or CABG.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### B. Coronary CT Angiography (CCTA)</summary><div class="details-content">

Multidetector row or multislice CT scanning (MDCT or MSCT) with at least 64 slices per gantry rotation (preferably 256 or 320 slices) provides adequate imaging with little motion artifact when heart rate is slowed to <70 bpm with β-blockers.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Diagnostic Performance</summary><div class="details-content">

CCTA provides excellent imaging of native coronary arteries and bypass grafts with sensitivity and specificity of about 80–90% (Figure 2.30). It accurately detects clinically significant lesions (>50% stenosis).

**Limitations:**
- Requires contrast and radiation
- Suboptimal imaging with:
  - Irregular heart rhythm
  - Severe coronary calcification or stents
  - Very small vessels <1.5 mm

Stents interfere with imaging, but CCTA is quite accurate for in-stent stenosis. Contraindicated in renal dysfunction or contrast allergy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Coronary Anomalies</summary><div class="details-content">

CCTA is beneficial for identifying proximal course of coronary arteries near great vessels in patients with coronary anomalies. However, for younger patients, cardiac MRI is preferable to avoid radiation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Physiologic Assessment</summary><div class="details-content">

The ability to assess physiologic significance simultaneously with anatomic findings can be achieved using combined technologies:
- CCTA combined with SPECT MPI improves accuracy
- **FFR derived from CCTA** using special software is very accurate for detecting hemodynamically significant lesions in multivessel disease, superior to standard CTA, SPECT, or PET scanning

</div></details>

![](_page_34_Figure_1.jpeg)

**Figure 2.30 •** (A) Coronary CT angiogram (CCTA) obtained on the Toshiba Aquilion scanner. This patient with prior surgery had a patent LITA to the LAD and a patent saphenous vein graft (SVG) to the right coronary artery (RCA). A nubbin of occluded SVG is noted on the aorta. (B) Native coronary arteries can be evaluated more extensively in one view using multiplanar reconstruction. Here, the LAD has diffuse calcific disease. (C) Tight stenosis of the LAD on CCTA. (D) Corresponding stenosis noted on invasive coronary angiography. Adapted with permission from Cury, et al. *J Am Coll Cardiol Img* 2016;9:1099-113 (C & D).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### C. Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (CMR) Imaging</summary><div class="details-content">

CMR visualizes proximal coronary arteries but is not quite as sensitive and specific as CCTA; therefore, not recommended for routine CAD identification (Figure 2.31A and B).

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Advantages</summary><div class="details-content">

- No irradiation or contrast
- Less interference from coronary calcification
- Less motion artifact at higher heart rates

**Limitations:**
- Suboptimal images in patients with stents, arrhythmias, or irregular breathing patterns

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Primary Indications for CMR in CAD Assessment</summary><div class="details-content">

**a. Coronary anomalies** — identifying proximal course (especially in young patients) (Figure 2.31C)

**b. Coronary aneurysms**

**c. Saphenous vein graft stenosis or internal thoracic artery graft stenosis** — CMR has sensitivity and specificity >90% for significant lesions. However, if patient is symptomatic and may require intervention, CMR is usually not indicated unless graft cannot be selectively cannulated by routine angiography. CMR and CCTA have similar accuracy for bypass grafts.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Functional Assessment</summary><div class="details-content">

CMR provides important information about myocardial flow and function, preferentially used for functional assessment of coronary disease (see section IX).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Safety in Patients with Cardiac Devices</summary><div class="details-content">

- Newer pacemakers and ICDs are considered MRI-conditional
- Older systems (“legacy devices”) initially considered contraindicated have been shown safe using field strength up to 1.5 tesla

</div></details>

## IX. Evaluation of Aortic and Cardiac Disease by CT and MR Scanning</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### A. CT Scanning</summary><div class="details-content">

Computerized tomography (CT) scanning is used primarily for aortic disease evaluation but has several other potential uses in identifying chest pathology in addition to CCTA. Sixty-four or greater slice CT angiography with color volume-rendered multiplanar reformation provides excellent angiographic images.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Aortic Dissection</summary><div class="details-content">

Identification of aortic dissection in patients with typical or atypical presentation (Figures 2.32–2.34). Requires contrast CT scan; can simultaneously rule out pulmonary embolism with appropriate gating. CT scans can also be used for postoperative follow-up to identify distal aneurysmal disease.

![](_page_37_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2.32 •** CT scan with contrast demonstrating a type A aortic dissection visualized in (A) coronal, (B) sagittal, and (C) transverse cuts across the arch and (D) at the mid-heart level. Note the intimal flap separating the true lumen (TL) and false lumen (FL), with opacification of the TL, which is significantly compressed by the FL as the dissection extends into the descending aorta.

![](_page_38_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2.33 •** CT scan with contrast of a type B dissection which starts just distal to the left subclavian artery without any involvement of the ascending aorta. Note the intimal flap separating the true and false lumens. AAo, ascending aorta; DAo, descending aorta; PA, pulmonary artery.

![](_page_38_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 2.34 •** Volume-rendered image of a type A aortic dissection shown in (A) coronal and (B) sagittal cuts.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Assessment of Aortic Size</summary><div class="details-content">

**a. Contrast CT with 3D reconstruction** provides transverse, sagittal, and coronal cuts as well as rotational 3D image (Figure 2.35). These provide exact measurement of aortic diameter, as obliquely coursing structures (tortuous or ectatic aorta segments) give spurious measurements on transverse cuts. **Centerline analysis** software overcomes these shortcomings, typically applied to descending thoracic aneurysms for endografting planning. Volume-rendered images give better assessment of distal aneurysm extent, helping decide if distal anastomosis requires circulatory arrest.

**b. Descending thoracic aneurysms** (Figure 2.36). Proximal and distal extent and vessel tortuosity are important for thoracic endografting (TEVAR) strategy.

(A) The axial slice may not provide an accurate size assessment if the measurement is not coaxial. (B) The coronal (and sagittal) slices are helpful in defining the precise diameter of the aorta from the root to the arch. (C) A 3D volume-rendered reconstruction image of the same patient can be rotated to give a better appreciation of the aortic diameter near the arch.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Ascending Aortic Calcification</summary><div class="details-content">

Evaluation of ascending aortic calcification suggested by chest x-ray or fluoroscopy (see Figure 2.10). Should be done without contrast.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Reoperative and Minimally Invasive Surgery Planning</summary><div class="details-content">

Location of RV, aorta, saphenous vein grafts, or ITA graft relative to sternum for reoperative planning. Also applicable to:
- Selecting proper incision site for minimally invasive surgery
- Transaortic TAVR planning

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. Pericardial and Pleural Evaluation</summary><div class="details-content">

Identification of pericardial (and pleural) effusions or pericardial thickness in constrictive pericarditis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 6. Intracardiac Masses</summary><div class="details-content">

Identification of intracardiac masses (see Figure 2.28B).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 7. Iliofemoral Evaluation</summary><div class="details-content">

Evaluation of abdominal aorta and iliofemoral systems essential prior to minimally invasive cases with femoral arterial cannulation. Diffusely diseased vessels may dissect during retrograde perfusion; atherosclerotic disease can embolize retrograde to brain. Prior to TAVR, iliofemoral systems assessed for size, calcification, and tortuosity; if markedly abnormal, alternate access site may be mandated (Figure 2.37).

</div></details>

#### 8. Retroperitoneal Hematoma</summary><div class="details-content">

Evaluation for retroperitoneal hematoma after percutaneous femoral vessel cannulation (Figure 2.38).

![](_page_41_Figure_4.jpeg)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 9. Pulmonary and Mediastinal Abnormalities</summary><div class="details-content">

Identification of pulmonary or mediastinal abnormalities noted on preoperative chest x-ray. Baseline CT prior to cardiac surgery eliminates potential distortion of pulmonary pathology by postoperative changes.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### B. Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (CMR) Imaging</summary><div class="details-content">

CMR is useful in identifying ischemic and nonischemic myocardial disease, imaging aorta and branches, analyzing congenital heart disease, and providing images of coronary arteries and bypass grafts.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. Safety with Cardiac Devices</summary><div class="details-content">

- CMR can be performed in patients with coronary stents (fields <3 tesla)
- Current-generation mechanical prosthetic heart valves (most bileaflet valves are carbon and silicone, not metal)
- Retained epicardial pacing wires
- Safe for legacy pacemakers and defibrillators using field <1.5 tesla

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Radiation Avoidance</summary><div class="details-content">

Major advantage of MRI is avoidance of radiation, preferable in young patients.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Stress CMR for Ischemia and Viability</summary><div class="details-content">

Pharmacologic stress CMR with dobutamine or coronary vasodilators identifies areas of myocardial ischemia and may be more sensitive than DSE for detecting coronary disease and predicting functional recovery after revascularization.

Myocardial viability evaluation:
- **Gadolinium:** distinguishes viable from infarcted myocardium
- **Dobutamine:** systolic wall thickening >2 mm predictive of functional recovery

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. Aortic Dissection</summary><div class="details-content">

CMR is the most sensitive and specific test for aortic dissection detection (Figure 2.39). It demonstrates:
- Differential flow velocities in true and false channels
- Branch artery compromise

Not generally performed in unstable patients requiring monitoring and IV infusions. Consider when clinical suspicion high but other tests inconclusive. Commonly used for follow-up after dissection repair to assess false channel status or distal aneurysmal expansion.

![](_page_42_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 2.39 •** MRI scan demonstrating the intimal flap separating the true and false lumens in a patient with an extensive aortic dissection involving both the ascending and the descending thoracic aorta.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. Late Gadolinium Enhancement (LGE)</summary><div class="details-content">

LGE identifies areas of focal myocardial fibrosis and infarction, assessing viability and likelihood of contractility improvement after revascularization. Greater LGE correlates with higher sudden death risk in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). CMR identifies variations in LV pathology in HOCM and is very useful for identifying nonischemic cardiomyopathies (Figure 2.40).

![](_page_43_Figure_5.jpeg)

**Figure 2.40 •** The various patterns of late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) seen with different types of cardiomyopathies. Typical patterns include transmural, subendocardial (regional and global), epicardial, and midwall LGE. These may differentiate between ischemic (subendocardial and transmural) and nonischemic cardiomyopathies, HOCM, myocarditis, sarcoidosis, and amyloidosis. (Image courtesy of Silva Marques et al., *Heart* 2015;101:565-72.)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 6. Magnetic Resonance Angiography (MRA)</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic and cerebrovascular diseases defined by:
- Time-of-flight (TOF) MRA
- Gadolinium-enhanced MRA (Figure 2.41)

Spin-echo and cine steady-state free precession (SSFP) CMR provide excellent 3D images of aortic disease, including true and false aneurysms, periaortic abscesses, and perigraft fluid collections.

![](_page_44_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2.41 •** MRA with gadolinium demonstrating aneurysms of the aortic arch on a rotational image that were poorly delineated in transverse images.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 7. Cine CMR</summary><div class="details-content">

Cine CMR allows differentiation of blood (bright) from cardiac tissue (dark). Useful for:
- Ventricular function assessment
- Valvular pathology
- Intracardiac masses (see Figure 2.28C)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 8. Pericardial Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

CMR is useful in assessing pericarditis or tumor invasion into pericardium. It can differentiate restrictive from constrictive pathology.

</div></details>
