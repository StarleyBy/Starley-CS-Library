#### **Abstract**

The cardiac pericardium is a multilayer tissue that envelops the heart. Physiologic roles of the pericardium include: (1) protecting the heart, (2) creating a compartment for containing the pericardial fuid, and (3) providing this compartment for optimizing cardiac movements and functionality within the thoracic cavity. Compared to other cardiothoracic tissues, the pericardium exhibits material properties that vary based on location within the greater pericardial sac. These properties are a result of varied collagen fber alignments, collagen densities throughout the pericardial sac, and overall cardiac health. Initiatives to better understand the tensile properties of human and large mammalian pericardium have driven both therapies and medical device manufacturing. Recent therapeutic approaches have been directed to exploit the space that exists between the pericardium and the epicardial surface of the heart. ### **13.1 Introduction**

The pericardium is a double-walled, fbroserous, and conical sac structure encasing the heart and proximal roots of the great cardiac vessels. In humans, it is located within the mediastinal cavity posterior to the sternum and cartilages of the third through seventh ribs of the left thorax and is separated from the anterior wall of the thorax. It is encompassed from the posterior resting against the bronchi, the esophagus, the descending thoracic aorta, and the posterior regions of the mediastinal surface of each lung. Laterally, the pericardium is covered by the pleurae and lies along the mediastinal surfaces of the lung. It can come into direct contact with the chest wall near the ventricular apical region, but this varies with the dimensions of the long axis of the heart or with

## **13.2 Pericardial Anatomy**

In humans, the 1–3 mm thick fbrous pericardium is commonly described as a *fask*-*shaped bag*. The neck of the pericardium (superior aspect) is closed by its extensions surrounding the great cardiac vessels, while the base is attached to the central tendon and to the muscular fbers of the left side of the diaphragm (Fig. 13.2). Much of the pericardium's diaphragmatic attachment consists of loose fbrous tissue that can be readily separated and/or isolated, but there is a small area over the central tendon where the diaphragm and the pericardium are completely fused (see also Chaps. 3 and 4).

Examination of the pericardium reveals that it is composed of two interconnected structures: the serous pericardium and the fbrous pericardium. The serous pericardium is one continuous sac with a large enfold that contains the heart (Fig. 13.3). An appropriate analogy would be a fst (representing the heart) pushed into the side of a defated balloon (representing the serous pericardium), therefore enveloped by two individual layers of material. The interior surface of the pericardium is intimately connected to the surface of the heart and is known as the *visceral pericardium* or the *epicardium*. The exterior surface of the serous pericardium is known as the *parietal pericardium* and is fused with the thick lining of the fbrous pericardium. The pericardial space, where the pericardial fuid resides, is bounded on either side by the parietal and visceral pericardium. To the naked eye, however, the visceral pericardium cannot be distinguished from the surface of the heart, and the parietal pericardium cannot be distinguished from the fbrous pericardium. For this reason, the general use of the term *pericardium* refers to the composite of the parietal and fbrous pericardium, which appears to be a single sac that surrounds the heart. Yet, more accurately, the pericardium should be described as three total layers, with fuid lining between two of them (Fig. 13.3).

The inferior vena cava enters the pericardium through the central tendon of the diaphragm where there exists a small area of fusion between the pericardium and the central tendon, but it receives no covering from this fbrous layer. Between the left pulmonary artery and subjacent pulmonary vein is a triangular fold of the serous pericardium known as the *ligament of the left vena cava* (or vestigial fold of Marshall). ### **13.3 Physiology of the Normal Pericardium**

### **13.3.1 Pericardial Fluid**

In normal hearts, the pericardium should be considered as only a potential space. It contains 20–60 mL of pericardial fuid, most of which resides in the major pericardial sinuses and the atrioventricular grooves [1]. The fuid is an ultrafltrate of plasma and therefore has many similarities to plasma in its electrolyte composition; pericardial fuid, however, contains about half the total protein concentration, one-third the triglyceride and cholesterol contents, and one-ffth the amount of white blood cells [2]. A more complete comparison of plasma and pericardial fuid composition is provided in Table 13.1.

The details of the formation, clearance, and turnover of pericardial fuid have not yet been fully explained. Yet, it is generally agreed that pericardial fuid is derived from plasma leakage from myocardial capillaries [3], and this fltrate is

During situations of high pericardial fuid pressure, such as in cardiac tamponade, investigators have found that fuid may pass through the pericardium and enter the pleural space [4]. The turnover time of pericardial fuid in humans has not been established, but in sheep, it is observed to be every 5.4 h [5].

As mentioned previously, pericardial fuid distribution is not uniform. The majority of the fuid is found in the major sinuses and grooves of the heart and makes up the pericardial reserve volume. The fuid is considered to be well mixed due to the motion of the heart, and agents injected into the pericardial space quickly and evenly disperse throughout [5]. Too much pericardial fuid, either due to disease or an intervention, may cause increased pericardial pressure and compromise cardiac performance, a syndrome called *cardiac tamponade*. ## **13.3.2 Mechanical Efects of the Pericardium**

The degree to which the pericardium alters heart wall movement(s) varies depending on the ratio of cardiac to pericardial size, loading conditions, and the degree of active and passive flling. Closure of the pericardial sac following openheart surgery has been proposed to (1): avoid possible postoperative complications, (2) reduce the frequency of ventricular hypertrophy, and/or (3) facilitate future potential reoperations by reducing fbrosis [6]. Furthermore, reported differences in ventricular performance, dependent on the presence of the pericardium, have been observed following cardiac surgery [7, 8].

In general, the presence of a pericardium physically constrains the heart, often resulting in a depressive hemodynamic infuence limiting cardiac output by restraining diastolic ventricular flling [9, 10] (see Video 13.1 in the online supplemental material). The physical constraint by the pericardium is translated into direct external mechanical forces that can also alter patterns in myocardial and systemic blood fow [10, 11]. Direct primary and indirect secondary effects are observed as additional forces through the chamber-free walls. Because both the left and right side atria and the left and right side ventricles are bound by a common septum, geometrical changes from chamber interaction(s) are dynamic, depending on the different flling rates and ejection rates of each of the four chambers [12, 13]. Thus, it is important to note that chamber-to-chamber interactions through the interventricular septum and by the pericardium further promote direct mechanical chamber interactions [14–16].

The effects of the pericardium on mechanical measures of cardiac performance are generally not evident until ventricular and atrial flling limitations are reached, changing geometrical and mechanical properties through factors such as maximum chamber volume and elasticity. These effects become more evident as the pericardial limitations become extended [17, 18]. With the known force-length dependence of cardiac muscle, variation of chamber volumes through the removal of the pericardium will, in turn, alter isometric tensions and therefore directly impact systolic ejection. On the other hand, in specifc cases where the restrictive role of the pericardium greatly increases, such as during cardiac tamponade, an increased intrapericardial fuid volume may result in critical restriction by the pericardium that then reduces cardiac performance (Fig. 13.5) [19, 20].

It should also be noted that increases in intrathoracic pressure will create an additional interaction between the ventricles, as well as between the heart and lungs in a closed chest. Thus, studying cardiac function in situ (with an opened chest) or in vitro allows for the elimination of these infuences of intrathoracic pressure and for more direct identifcation and quantifcation of pericardial infuences on cardiac performance and ejection [21]. Furthermore, such isolation of these pericardial effects from diastolic flling is an important consideration, since normal ventricular output is dependent on diastolic pressure and independent of the presence of the pericardium [22].

### **13.5.2 Comparative Biomechanics of Human and Porcine Pericardium**

Across large mammalian species, the biomechanical properties of the pericardium are varied and the result of: (1) the aggregate collagen fber alignments, (2) varied collagen fber densities, (3) varied pericardium thicknesses, and (4) the given age of the animal at time of tissue harvest. Though bioprosthetic heart valves commonly use pericardium ranging between 0.15 and 0.40 mm in thickness there is a clinical push for valve size reductions. These initiatives can take the form of changes to valve frames, inorganic valve materials, and leafet geometries. However, it remains crucial to balance leafet sizes, longevities, and manufacturing feasibilities. Developing comparative biomechanical models for commonly employed pericardial tissues is imperative for ultimately improving ft, function, and long-term performance of developed bioprosthetic heart valves. Planar uniaxial and planar biaxial tensile testing are two modalities that preclinical researchers use to comparatively measure the biomechanical properties of human and large mammalian pericardium. While both methods are commonly employed to study the biomechanical properties of human and large mammalian tissues, planar uniaxial and planar biaxial testing differ on sample destruction and measured parameters. During planar uniaxial testing, samples are laser or die cut into an American Society for Testing Materials (ASTM) standard shape and then displaced along a single loading vector, parallel to a gauge area on the sample, until a peak tensile strain is reached at the avulsion threshold. While periodic loading protocols can be utilized, samples are often distended or destroyed after a short period of time. Planar biaxial testing, on the other hand, is intended to be a nondestructive testing method. During planar biaxial testing samples are dissected into either cruciform or square samples depending on tissue availability. Samples are then displaced along both the vertical and horizontal axes using user-defned waveforms to measure properties such as axial load versus displacement, strain, and sheer. Compared to uniaxial testing, planar biaxial testing allows for intrinsic tissue properties such as anisotropy to be assessed; that is, where fber alignments and orientation are determinants of underlying biomechanical properties.

Preliminary studies performed at the University of Minnesota's Visible Heart® Laboratories (VHL) have employed planar biaxial testing to comparatively measure the axial load and strain properties of both viable human and porcine pericardium. Human parietal pericardial specimens were recovered from non-transplantable human hearts or human heart-lung blocs that were donated through LifeSource, an organ procurement agency, local to the VHL. Following cardiac arrest, the donor's heart, or heartlung bloc, was removed by dissecting at the aortic trunk, inferior vena cava (IVC), and superior vena cava (SVC). Isolated hearts and heart-lung blocs were then sealed in iced saline and transported to the VHL. For this particular study, samples had to be received within ten hours post aortic cross clamp to be included for testing. Donor information was deidentifed and documentation was reviewed for serologies. Donations were rejected if there was excessive damage to the heart during extraction or if documentation showed a positive status of MRSA, prions, hepatitis, or meningitis. Age, gender, and health histories were then documented. Hearts and heart-lung blocs were removed from iced saline and rinsed in room temperature 0.9% saline. The pulmonary vasculature was dissected to remove the lungs and to isolate the heart. Any trachea and esophagus left behind during the initial organ procurement process were also removed and utilized if possible. The isolated heart and any remaining portions of the pericardium were rinsed again with room temperature 0.9% saline. During the organ recovery process, human organs and tissues are dissected, and consequently, we were unable to control how much, or what part, of the pericardial sac remained. Next, the remaining pericardial sac was dissected from the heart. As much superfcial adipose tissue was removed from the isolated parietal pericardium using a series of blunt dissections and room temperature saline rinses. Adipose tissue continued to be removed so long as the pericardium was not damaged or considered to be biomechanically altered. However, in some instances, adipose tissue would fuse tightly to the pericardium and could not be removed without damaging the sample. Pericardial samples were excluded if extensive damage were observed. Following removal of adipose tissue, each pericardial sample was sectioned into squares measuring approximately 2–3 cm along each side. All prepared specimens were then placed in a carbogen perfused Krebs- Ringer buffer solution in preparation for subsequent planar biaxial testing.

For these comparative studies, porcine pericardium was recovered immediately prior to cardioplegia of the given animal's heart prior to in vitro reanimation cardiac investigations using protocols approved by the University of Minnesota Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Porcine were initially sedated using an intramuscular injection of 500 mg of Telazol (Zoeitsus, USA), containing 1:1 ratio of tiletamine and zolazepam. This was followed by an intravenous injection of 1–200 mg of Methohexital (Brevital, USA). Sedated porcine were then intubated and ventilated. Anesthesia was maintained with isofurane at >1.5 MAC. A median sternotomy was then performed to expose the cardiothoracic cavity. The heart was often pretreated with Omegaven or Smofipid via pericardial delivery 60 min prior to heart extraction. Immediately prior to cardioplegia, the pericardium was extracted and immediately placed into oxygenated saline. The isolated pericardial samples were then dissected following the same procedure outlined above. However, additional rinsing steps were added to remove any pretreatment solutions. Additional porcine pericardial samples and hearts, were also obtained minutes postmortem from the University of Minnesota Department of Animal Sciences Andrew Boss Laboratory of Meat Science (ABLMS). These research animals were electrically stunned and slaughtered using a jugular and/or carotid artery puncture. The visceral organs were then removed from the carcass and checked for disease. The heart was then dissected from the heart-lung bloc at the pulmonary vasculature, rinsed, and immediately transferred to a container of prepared cardioplegic solution. The pericardium was then isolated as previously described within 20–30 minutes of being dissected from the carcass. Following recovery, these pericardial samples were rinsed using room temperature 0.9% saline until coagulated blood and mucosal membranes were removed. If the entire sac could be recovered the sac was sectioned into large squares. Pericardium at the periphery was discarded. As with human pericardium, any observable surface defect or damage was grounds for excluding a specifc area of the pericardial sac from biaxial testing. Pericardial areas that passed initial screening for surface defects and damage were sectioned into squares measuring approximately 3-4 cm on each side. Samples were then stored in room temperature carbogen perfused Krebs- Ringer solution prior to testing.

Human and porcine parietal pericardial samples were tested within ten hours of the given post aortic cross clamp and 20–30 minutes post isolation, respectively. The biomechanical properties of pericardium were measured using a TA Instruments Planar Biaxial Testbench (TA Instruments, USA). Both human and porcine tissues were biaxially tested using the same protocol. Following dissection and cleaning processes, pericardial samples were removed from the Krebs- Ringer buffer and attached to the planar biaxial testing system using threaded tissue clamps coupled to 225 N load cells oriented along the vertical and horizontal axes of the sample. The pericardial sample was then adjusted to zero passive tension by moving individual motors fore and aft along the attached tracks. Load cell preload was monitored to ensure no bias along a specifc axis. New Krebs- Ringer buffer, prewarmed to 37 °C was added to the water jacketed bath and an oxygenating stone with carbogen was submerged to allow for continuous gassing. The outer chamber was flled and warmed to 37 °C using the system's own heating element. Following the initial set up, the axial load cells were balanced with particular attention paid to the tare values. Each pericardial sample was removed from oxygenated storage and blotted dry. Five fducial markers, used by the equipped digital video extensometer (DVE) to track strain, were painted on the superfcial surface of the given pericardium sample using oil based paint. Markers were spaced depending on the relative size of the given sample. The DVE camera was positioned such that the carefully prepared pericardial sample was positioned as close to center as possible in the image plane. The image threshold was adjusted to remove any background or surface defects and maximize resolution of the fve markers. Tracking boxes were then cantered over each marker.

For these VHL analyses, 2000 data points were taken following the initial mechanical preconditioning step. Peak axial strain and axial load (N) were measured along the vertical and horizontal axes of the pericardial sample at 3 mm displacement. Axial strain and load were then normalized to the surface area of the superfcial surface to accommodate for the variability and unique anatomy between samples. In some instances, porcine pericardium below 0.25 mm in thickness would rapidly tear along the clamp- tissue interface. Depending on when this was observed, whole experiments or sections of experimental data would be discarded.

During both human and porcine biaxial studies, axial loads and axial strains were assessed against displacement (mm). By comparing these metrics to displacement data collection could focus on peak displacements values throughout the force-controlled test sequence. Sample integrity through the study was assessed by monitoring axial load values in real time. Drastic decreases in axial load values would be observed if the sample slipped within the claps, as was very common with human tissues, or pericardial tearing. In our observations, pericardium below 0.25 mm in thickness would be especially prone to damage during biaxial testing often tearing at the edge of the tissue clamps. Strain was also actively monitored to check for sample mounting and positioning. While slight negative strain values were considered to be normal hysteresis during the loading cycle, negative strain values at peak displacements suggested incorrect tissue mounting or testing related damage. If negative strains were observed in the vicinity of peak displacements, the test was stopped, and then the motor positioning was readjusted to ensure better zero passive tension at the start of the experiment. In both human and porcine pericardial studies, sample slippage was not frequently observed, but was considered a case of user error. Krebs- Ringer solution around a given sample was changed every 2–3 hours.

In both human and porcine pericardial studies, high levels of data instability were observed within the initial 50 seconds and within the terminal 5 minutes of a given study. Instability at the start of the experiment was attributed to initial tissue preconditioning. In contrast, terminal instability was attributed to either a loss of clamping force due to sample degradation or sample damage at the tissue-clamp interface. As a result, data analyses within these two windows were excluded. Statistical analyses of human and porcine axial data were performed by calculating the mean axial strains and loads at 3 mm displacement over 2000 data points. Further, to accommodate for variabilities in sample sizes, the axial means were normalized to the surface areas of the superfcial faces. The differences in the axial strains and loads were then compared between sample types. Statistical signifcance of the difference between human and porcine axial load and strain values was studied using a single factor ANOVA test at 0.05 signifcance. Preliminary studies did not show a statistically signifcant difference between the axial strain or axial load properties of human and porcine pericardium. However, common to both human and porcine pericardia data was a high degree of variability when normalized. This was attributed to the inconsistent collagen fber alignment and density intrinsic to pericardium that is responsible for its functionality within the cardiothoracic tissue.

Both human and porcine pericardial tissues are, in our opinion, one of the most complicated and inconsistent cardiothoracic tissues to investigate yet they are also perhaps the most relevant cardiothoracic tissue we have studied using biaxial testing methods. As previously stated, animal pericardium is routinely used in medical devices such as bioprosthetic heart valves. Notably, due to the irregular natures of animal pericardium and factors that cannot be controlled, yields of pericardial based products are in general very low and can even be sometimes cost prohibitive. The pericardial biomechanical properties are also highly variable causing some devices to fail early. To improve device yields and performances, a few preclinical studies are beginning to explore the use of vena cava as a more stable alternative.

Future biomechanical studies on physiologically viable pericardium should look to expand human tissue studies and account for the effects of regional thickness differences. Further work will be required to determine how the biaxial properties of human pericardium will vary with gender, age and/or disease states and how swine samples vary with breed and age.

## **13.6 Surgical Uses of the Pericardium**

The durability and anisotropic properties of large mammalian fbrous pericardium make it especially useful in loadbearing surgical applications such as grafts and/or bioprostietic heart valves; that is, tissues that experience strain and deformation across multiple axes [30]. In both such applications, today, both bovine and porcine pericardium are most commonly used. During cardiac surgery, fresh autografts, cryopreserved homografts, or glutaraldehydefxed xenografts can be used as patches during reconstructive repairs in both congenital and acquired heart diseases. For example, in congenitally malformed hearts, pericardial patches are used during surgical repairs of the right ventricular outfow tracts and/or during repair of torn aortic leafets. Pericardial patches have also been used to repair the mitral and aortic valves as well as ventricular walls in acquired diseases [31].

Porcine and bovine pericardial tissues have also proven useful in the manufacturing of bioprosthetic heart valve leaflets and full valve assemblies. The search for an alternative to mechanical valves has led people to investigate many types of bioprosthetic valves. These bioprostheses have consisted of homografts (preserved cadaveric valves), autografts (transplant of a patient's pulmonic valve to the aortic position—the Ross procedure), and xenografts. Xenograft valves have included preserved porcine aortic tissue as well as preserved bovine pericardium. More specifcally, glutaraldehydefxed bovine or porcine pericardium has been used in the design of aortic and mitral bioprosthetic valves and has realized wide clinical success. Such pericardium is prepared and fxed using specifc processes (which typically vary slightly between manufacturers) and then cut to resemble a tri-leafet semilunar valve and assembled into a stent with a sewing cuff.

With ubiquitous use in heart valves, the biomechanics of glutaraldehyde-fxed pericardium have been carefully studied. Biomechanical properties have been shown to be dependent on species, anatomic orientation, and fxation process. For example, there are signifcant differences in uniaxial mechanical properties of bovine, porcine, and ostrich pericardium [32]. It has also been shown that collagen fber orientation, and hence mechanical stiffness, is dependent on the location and orientation of the tissue within the pericardial sac [33]. Finally, uniaxial properties of glutaraldehyde-fxed bovine pericardium can be modulated depending on the stress applied during fxation [34].

### **13.7 Intrapericardial Therapeutics**

### **13.7.1 Clinical Pericardial Access**

Traditionally, pericardial access has been limited to patients with pericardial effusions. The effusion gives physician a buffer between the fbrous pericardium and the epicardium during pericardiocentesis or creating a pericardial window, thus preventing damage to the myocardium and coronary vessels. Recently, there has been an interest in accessing the healthy pericardium for therapeutic procedures and the delivery of therapeutic agents. The challenge of accessing a healthy pericardium is to puncture and catheterize the pericardium with minimal risk to the heart. This is a diffcult task considering the almost negligible layer of pericardial fuid in a healthy pericardial sac. The Tuohy needle, a hypodermic needle originally developed for insertion of an epidural catheter, is commonly used for pericardial access. This needle has an anti-coring curve at its tip to prevent puncture of the myocardium.

In an effort to improve the ease of access, several unique biomedical devices or tools have been (or are being) developed with novel catheter designs that allow controlled myocardial penetration during fuoroscopic visualization. ### **13.7.2 Intrapericardial Therapies**

For quite some time, nonsurgical intrapericardial therapy has been employed in patients with suffcient fuid in the pericardial space allowing a needle to be safely placed within the space [1]. This methodology has been used for patients with clinical indications such as, but not limited to, malignancies, recurrent effusions, uremic pericarditis, and connective tissue disease. As mentioned above, instrumenting the pericardium has been made possible by numerous techniques that allow for the study of intrapericardial therapeutics and diagnostics by clinicians and investigators alike. In addition, with recent advances in minimally invasive cardiac surgical procedures, it is likely that the integrity of the pericardium will be preserved more often during cardiac surgery.

Intrapericardial access has become the foundation of many novel minimally invasive cardiac procedures. This includes epicardial mapping and ablation for both ventricular [39] and atrial [40] arrhythmias in patients where endocardial approaches are challenging or ineffective. Intrapericardial echocardiography, where an intracardiac ultrasound probe is introduced percutaneously into the pericardium, has been shown to aid in complex ablation procedures [41]. Epicardial lead placement can also be performed through minimally invasive access to the pericardium in patients where traditional venous lead implantation has failed [42]. The LARIAT procedure and device (SentreHEART, Redwood City, CA, USA) is a novel approach to percutaneous ligation of the left atrial appendage that relies on both pericardial access and transseptal left atrial access. In this procedure, a device in the pericardium interacts magnetically with a device in the left atrium to position the system and perform the ligation. Initial clinical results of the procedure have shown promising results [43].

Beyond device-based interventions, intrapericardial access has been used to introduce therapeutic agents to treat cardiac disease. Specifcally, the endoluminal delivery of various agents has been found to be clinically limited due to short residence time, highly variable deposited agent concentration, inconsistency in delivery concentrations, and relatively rapid washout of agents from the target vessel [44]. A desired example of targeted application includes infusion of concentrated nitric oxide donors, which could present undesirable effects if systemically delivered. Further, one is allowed increased site specifcity and the delivery of labelspecifc therapeutic agents to target cells, receptors, and channels. A great deal of interest has been focused on delivery of angiogenic agents and various growth factors into the intrapericardial space [45–47]. In particular, research has concentrated on administration in patients with ischemic heart disease [48, 49]. Early results indicate several benefts associated with the delivery of angiogenic agents that include increased collateral vessel development, regional myocardial blood fow, myocardial function in the ischemic region, and myocardial vascularity.

In our lab, we have shown that intrapericardial delivery of omega-3 fatty acids can drastically reduce infarct size and lower the occurrence of ventricular arrhythmias. In one study [50], 23 swine were treated with an infusion of either omega-3 fatty acids or saline in the pericardial sac prior to occluding the left anterior descending artery. Prior to, during, and after the occlusion, hemodynamic and electrophysiological data were recorded. Upon sacrifcing the animal, the heart was sectioned and stained to determine infarct size. We found that both infarct size and arrhythmia scores were reduced by half in animals treated with omega-3 fatty acids. Furthermore, the treatment had a minimal effect on hemodynamics, which is in contrast to many antiarrhythmic drugs. Current research is underway to determine if this therapy would be feasible in a clinical setting.

## **13.7.3 Pericardial Pharmacokinetics**

As described previously, the pericardium in humans is generally believed to contain 20–60 mL of physiologic fuid (0.25 ± 15 mL/kg) situated within the cavity space [51]. Yet, dye studies suggest that pericardial fuid is not uniformly distributed over the myocardium, with the majority of pericardial fuid residing within the atrioventricular and interventricular grooves as well as the superior-transverse sinuses. Although the pericardial fuid is not uniformly distributed, pharmacokinetic studies suggest that there is complete mixing of the fuid so that pericardial fuid content is spatially uniform [52–54]. Hence, sampling pericardial fuid content should not vary functionally by sampling location [53].

Tissue distribution and drug clearance clearly affect all drug response. Because specifc pericardial pharmacokinetic data remain unknown for the majority of compounds, pericardial drug disposition must be gleaned from physical chemical properties based upon a few select studies. Pericardial fuid is cleared via lymphatics and epicardial vasculature, with the former being a very slow process [55]. In addition to these passive clearance mechanisms, the epicardial tissues contain metabolic enzymes that may clear compounds via a biotransformation process. This is likely to occur with certain labile peptides and small molecules such as nitric oxide. Unfortunately, there is very little known today about pericardial drug metabolism. In general, it is considered that whether or not a compound residing in the pericardial space is cleared via lymphatic drainage, passive diffusion or biotransformation will depend on its molecular size, tissue affnity, water solubility, and enzymatic stability. Thus, compounds such as large proteins do not rapidly diffuse into the vascular space and are slowly cleared from the pericardial space perhaps via lymphatics unless, of course, they are biotransformed [53, 56]. Importantly, this yields a pericardial fuid clearance and residence time longer than the corresponding plasma half-life. For example, administering atrial naturetic peptide into the pericardial fuid space had a fvefold longer clearance and residence time within the pericardial fuid space, as compared to plasma clearance of an intravenous dose [56]. Similarly, small water-insoluble compounds may also have very prolonged pericardial fuid residual times.

One case report documented that the pericardial fuid half-life of 5-fuorouracil (sparingly soluble in water) was approximately tenfold longer than plasma half-life (168 versus 16 min); it should be noted that the patient in this investigation had metastatic breast carcinoma with pericardial involvement [52]. The patient had received a relatively large pericardial 5-fuorouracil dose (200 mg) to manage recurrent pericardial effusion. This large dose, however, was associated with nearly undetectable plasma levels, indicating minimal spillover from pericardial fuid into the systemic circulation. While it was expected that 5-fuorouracil would have a longer pericardial residual time because it is water insoluble, it is unknown if these fndings would occur in a healthy pericardial fuid space.

On the other hand, small water-soluble compounds have up to fve- to eightfold shorter pericardial fuid clearance and residence times as compared to plasma [52]. For example, procainamide is a water-soluble compound that has a pericardial fuid half-life ranging from 30 to 41 ± 2.1 min as compared to the 180-min plasma half-life; it has been reported that the procainamide rapidly diffused out of the pericardial space with a terminal elimination half-life approximately 5–7 times shorter than plasma [57]. However, procainamide spillover from pericardial fuid into plasma was considered not to produce measurable plasma concentrations because of the relatively low pericardial doses (0.5–2 mg/kg). Similarly, it is not surprising that the converse was also true, that intravenously administered procainamide rapidly diffused into the pericardial space, across the plasma to pericardial fuid concentration gradient, such that pericardial fuid procainamide concentrations were similar to plasma approximately 20–30 min following an intravenous injection. The likely explanation for these fndings is that the vast ventricular epicardial blood supply served as a clearing system (pericardial administration) or a delivery system (intravenous administration) according to drug concentration diffusion gradient. Importantly, the diffusion of pericardialadministered procainamide into the vascular space will likely prevent drug accumulation in ventricular tissue and a global pharmacologic response.

In addition to pericardial drug residence and clearance times, the determination of distribution volume may be of considerable importance, particularly to achieve desired peak drug concentrations. There is a direct and inverse relationship between peak drug concentrations and drug distribution volumes, such that a low drug distribution volume achieves higher peak concentrations. Perhaps of clinical importance, with the very small pericardial fuid volume, it is likely that pericardial drug doses can be substantially reduced to achieve therapeutic concentrations. This was evident whereby sequential pericardial procainamide doses of 0.5, 1, and 2 mg/kg produced peak pericardial fuid concentrations that ranged from 250 to 900 μg/mL; these concentrations were nearly 1000-fold greater than peak plasma concentrations of procainamide following the administration of a 2 mg/kg intravenous dose. In a follow-up study in which a single procainamide dose was employed, similar fndings were documented; it was also reported that a pericardial fuid volume distribution of 1.6 ± 0.16 mL/kg was observed, which is approximately 1000-fold smaller than the plasma procainamide volume distribution of 2000 mL/kg. While pericardial procainamide dosing produced very large pericardial fuid concentrations, procainamide could not be detected in the plasma given the very small doses. With such a powerful diffusion gradient, it is likely that pericardial procainamide delivery can achieve very high atrial tissue concentrations. Indirect evidence of tissue distribution is a procainamide distribution volume that is larger (40–50 mL) than the estimated pericardial fuid volume of 20–30 mL. Since the procainamide pericardial volume of distribution exceeded the expected pericardial volume, there was some tissue distribution. These pharmacodynamic data suggest that tissue distribution mainly occurs in the atrium, likely because the atrium is a very thin structure with a low blood supply. Thus, this tissue architecture is ideal for specialized therapeutic drug diffusion and therefore differs from that of the ventricle(s).

Unfortunately, most pericardial procainamide pharmacokinetic studies performed to date have not directly measured tissue concentrations following infusion. However, in one study which evaluated the pharmacodynamic effects of pericardial amiodarone delivery, the amiodarone tissue distribution was quantifed at several myocardial locations [58]. Not surprisingly, it was reported that atrial and epicardial ventricular tissue had the highest amiodarone tissue concentration, while ventricular endocardial amiodarone tissue concentrations were approximately tenfold lower. Importantly, the amiodarone levels were likely still within a therapeutic range. This was supported by the fact that pericardial amiodarone delivery prolonged endocardial ventricular refractory periods by up to 13%, which was equivalent to epicardial ventricular refractory period measurements and the magnitude of atrial refractory period prolongation. The similar refractory response between epicardial and endocardial measurements, with very large differences in amiodarone tissue concentrations, indicates that amiodarone effects are maximal at low tissue concentrations. Unlike pericardial amiodarone administration, pericardial procainamide had no effect on endocardial ventricular refractory periods [54]. It is likely that such a benefcial ventricular tissue distribution does not occur with more water-soluble compounds such as procainamide. On the other hand, it is not surprising that amiodarone, when administered into the pericardial space, could penetrate ventricular tissue and affect global ventricular electrophysiology because it is highly lipophilic and has a huge tissue distribution including the intracellular space [58]. A more recent study comparing intrapericardial and intravenous administration of amiodarone in goats [59] showed similar results; plasma drug concentrations were signifcantly lower when amiodarone was administered into the pericardium, yet the drug's antiarrhythmic effects were in many measures improved over intravenous administration. The study also confrmed a high gradient of amiodarone tissue concentration from epicardium to endocardium during intrapericardial administration. We have also shown similar benefcial effects in our lab when comparing intrapericardial and intravenous delivery of metoprolol in swine [60]. Intrapericardial delivery showed lower plasma concentrations, more sustained antitachycardic effects, and less negative effects on contractility and mean arterial pressure when compared with intravenous delivery.

Lastly, perhaps it is possible to modify molecules to achieve an optimal pericardial fuid residence time and thus therapeutic outcomes. More specifcally, for some agents, it may be desirable to have a short residence time. For example, pericardial drug delivery to cardiovert atrial fbrillation may require very high drug concentrations for only a brief duration, given the acute nature of the therapy. On the other hand, the ability to manage chronic conditions such as ischemic heart disease or heart failure may necessitate longer pericardial residual times. In this regard, Baek et al. recently showed that a derivatized nitric oxide donor molecule, diazeniumdiolate, with bovine serum albumin resulted in a fvefold increase in pericardial fuid clearance and residence time versus a small molecule nitric oxide donor (diethylenetriamine/NO) [61]. This group went on to show that it may be possible that a single pericardial dose of the nitric oxide donor could inhibit in-stent restenosis. Unlike patients with any type of effusion, the normal pericardium is a very thin layer, bringing it closer to the heart and subsequently increasing the risk of harm to the patient.

The ability to access the pericardial space has created new opportunities to further understand the pharmacokinetics of intrapericardial therapeutics, as well as the role of the pericardium under normal cardiac function and/or following cardiac disease. Despite the growing literature establishing the feasibility of intrapericardial therapeutics and diagnostics, the results of clinical trials employing pericardially delivered agents directed toward arrhythmias, angiogenesis, restenosis, and/or other coronary and myocardial indications are currently lacking. See also Chap. 26 for target pericardial delivery studies.

# **13.8 Summary**

The pericardium is a unique structure that surrounds the heart and serves several important physiological roles. The removal of the pericardium, certain pericardial disorders, or the buildup of fuids within this space will ultimately alter hemodynamic performance. Recent therapeutic approaches have been directed to exploit the space that exists between the pericardium and the epicardial surface of the heart. New devices and techniques are being developed to access this space in a minimally invasive fashion. An important consideration when utilizing animal models to study such devices is that the pericardium in humans is much thicker and there is more pericardial fuid than in commonly employed animal models. The pharmacokinetics of many drugs may be greatly enhanced if the drug is delivered into the pericardium. As more is learned about the pericardium, it may play a signifcant role in cardiac therapies.
