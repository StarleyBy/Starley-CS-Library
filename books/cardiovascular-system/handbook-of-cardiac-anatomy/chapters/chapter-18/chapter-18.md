# **18.2 Echo Probes**

There are two primary types of ultrasound probes used for cardiac imaging: transthoracic (TTE) and transesophageal (TEE). As these names suggest, TTE is performed by scanning the skin surface through the chest wall to acquire images; meanwhile, TEE is performed by introducing the probe, a gastroscope with an ultrasound unit on its tip, into the patient's upper gastrointestinal tract. Discussion of other types of echo, such as intracardiac echo (ICE), is beyond the scope of this chapter, as it relates primarily to interventional cardiology.

Both forms of echo have major advantages and disadvantages. TTE is non-invasive, relatively simple to perform and can provide real-time clinical information about cardiac anatomy and function. The major disadvantage is that patient factors play more substantial roles in acquiring high quality images, including (1) body habitus, (2) previous surgical interventions, (3) rib shadowing, and (4) lung tissue. Additionally, in certain patients, more applied probe pressure on the chest and abdomen may be required to achieve clinically signifcant images, which can lead to patient discomfort.

The use of TEE is signifcantly more invasive than TTE and requires a more specialized skillset. Patients typically require some degree of sedation for these procedures and some patients may require general anesthesia, depending on pertinent patient factors. However, the image qualities are signifcantly better since the esophagus is in close proximity to the heart. The probe used for TEE is also more powerful than a TTE probe, allowing for higher resolution imaging and 3D acquisitions. While posterior and inferior structures are better imaged with TEE, anterior and superior images are better visualized with TTE.

In recent years, as technology advances, echo image qualities have improved dramatically. TTE probes are starting to have the same functionality as TEE, including 3D echo capabilities. Furthermore, as ultrasound technologies improve, the sizes of the echo machines have also begun to shrink. While it was once commonplace to see large mobile computer units or laptops as echo machines, more companies are offering pocket-sized and phone-based ultrasound units. These mobile and affordable machines have made bedside ultrasound ubiquitous in the United States among medical schools and residency programs, which makes understanding image acquisitions and interpretations even more important. As the level of entry for physicians using this equipment lowers, it is very important that those using these technologies are adequately trained.

# **18.3 The Basics of Ultrasound Imaging**

In order to better understand how to interpret the images obtained with echocardiography, one must have a basic understanding of ultrasound physics and how the echo machine forms images. In this section, we will discuss how the echo machine creates images in 2D and 3D, as well as color fow Doppler (CFD) and Spectral Doppler. Lastly, we will discuss how to interpret these obtained images correctly and the clinically useful calculations that can be obtained from these measurements [1].

# **18.3.1 Ultrasound Physics**

In its most basic form, the ultrasound probe transmits a sound wave of a known frequencies into tissue and receives the returning sound frequencies. Based on the changes in frequencies of these sound waves and the time it takes to return to the receiver, the machine determines how dense and how far away the object of interest is relative to the probe. Sound waves propagate through different tissues at different speeds, with air having the slowest propagation velocity (330 m/s) and bone having the greatest (4080 m/s); however, blood and most soft tissues have the same propagation speeds of 1540 m/s. For this reason, the ultrasound machine assumes that all sound waves travel at this speed. By knowing this, and the amount of time it takes for the wave to bounce back to the receiver, the machine can calculate how far away from the probe an object is located. It assumes that half of the travel time was towards the object and half was back to the probe, therefore, the machine can plot the object at that distance from the probe on the display [1, 2].

The strength of a given sound wave depends on its amplitude, which can change as the sound wave travels though tissue. The tissue itself absorbs some of the energy, while some energy is scattered or refected from surfaces of differing acoustic impedance. This creates changes in the amplitudes of the returning sound waves, which the machine will use to assign a certain amount of brightness to the object [1, 2].

There are two primary modes of producing the echo images used in clinical practice: M-mode and B-mode (Fig. 18.1). M-mode is an older form of ultrasound and will only give a single scan line of the picture being investigated. Using the track ball, the cursor should be aligned through the object of interest. The image formed will have time on the x-axis and depth on the y-axis. The density will appear by the shades of white and gray on the screen. M-mode uses a high frame rate and therefore is useful for fast moving objects, such as tracking valve leafet motions or the interatrial septum. B-mode, also known as 2D imaging, produces an echo image with which people are more familiar. With this imaging, the x-axis is depth, the z-axis is amplitude and there is no y-axis. A line of B-mode data is transmitted in an arc back and forth through the tissue. The images are generated very quickly to give the appearance of a continuous moving picture. As frequency of the sound waves increase, the resolution increases at the expense of decreased tissue penetration. However, if imaging deeper structures, the frequency can be reduced to improve tissue penetration at the expense of the image resolution [1, 2].

3D echo utilize the same principles as 2D echo, but adds an extra plane (Fig. 18.2). While a 2D echo probe crystal contains around 64 to 128 elements per row, a 3D probe has 3000 elements per row. Instead of a triangular dataset that is then converted to a 2D image, a pyramidal dataset is created and converted to a 3D image. Shading is then used to convey depth on 3D echo. Color fow Doppler can also be added to the image to demonstrate fow dynamics in the 3D dataset [1, 3].

#### **18.3.2 Doppler Physics**

The Doppler Effect is the change in frequency that occurs when a sound wave bounces off a moving object. The Doppler Shift is the *amount* of change in frequency that occurs when the sound wave bounces off the moving object. For the purposes of understanding clinical echocardiography, there are two types of relevant Doppler: Spectral Doppler and color fow Doppler (CFD). Calculating the Doppler Shift allows us to determine the speed and direction of a moving object, such as blood fow or valve leafets [1, 2].

The major forms of spectral Doppler are continuous wave Doppler (CWD) and pulse wave Doppler (PWD) (Fig. 18.3). For CWD the echo probe uses two different crystals to continuously transmit and receive sound waves. By continuously transmitting and receiving sound waves, all velocities can be measured through the cursor line, but locations of the highest velocities cannot be determined; this is known as range ambiguity. CWD is used for assessing stenotic lesions, because we assume that the highest velocities will be in the narrowest locations, in the line of the cursor. PWD uses only a single crystal that transmits a sound wave and waits for it to return before sending out the next pulse of sound. By placing the cursor at a known location, velocities can be measured at that one spot: this is known as range resolution. PWD is not ideal for measuring higher velocities and is subject to aliasing, a phenomenon whereby the velocities within an object are greater than the rates that the PWD can record, resulting in the tracing appearing on the opposite side of the baseline. Aliasing is also known as the "wagon wheel effect"—when a wagon wheel in old Western movies moved faster than the frame rate of the camera and therefore appeared to be rotating in the opposite direction. For both CWD and PWD, the cursor must be appropriately aligned with the direction of fow. If the angle is off by more than 20 degrees, it will create signifcant underestimation in measurement [1, 2].

CFD is a form of PWD that utilizes multi-gated acquisitions to measure Doppler shifts by assigning color to the direction of fow, transposing a semi-quantitative measure of fow velocities onto a 2D echo image (Fig. 18.4). The standard format is to assign blue color to blood fowing away from the probe and red to blood fowing toward the probe (BART = Blue Away, Red Toward). Since this is a form of PWD, it is also subject to aliasing, which can be seen at high velocities. Aliasing on CFD will appear as blue fow toward the probe and red fow away from the probe. The amount of aliasing can be adjusted by changing the color scale, also known as the Nyquist limit. The Nyquist limit is typically set between 60 and 70 cm/s—decreasing this value will permit more aliasing to occur at lower blood fow velocities while increasing the Nyquist limit will allow less aliasing [2]. These concepts are important for recognizing laminar and turbulent fows, and for the calculation of PISA. A more quantitative assessment of cardiac function using echo is beyond the scope of this chapter [1].

# **18.5.3 Left Atrium**

The left atrium (LA) is the chamber superior to the left ventricle. It receives blood from the pulmonary veins during systole and deposits it into the LV during diastole through the mitral valve (MV). An important structure within the LA is the left atrial appendage (LAA), which must be examined for thrombus in low-fow settings, such as in the patient presenting with atrial fbrillation. By convention, the atrioventricular valves (in this case, the MV) are considered part of their respective ventricle, not the atrium. # **18.6.1 Aortic Valve**

In a typical clinical TTE exam, the AV and LVOT should be evaluated together. The AV can be visualized in the PLAX view, which can be zoomed in on the AV. This can allow for measurement of the LVOT and aortic annulus during systole. Additionally, CFD can be used to evaluate for turbulence or regurgitation of the AV [4].

To examine at the AV in short axis, the parasternal short axis view at the level of the great vessels with zooming in on the AV is most ideal. In this view, one can evaluate each of the coronary cusps in detail and look for any abnormalities such as a bicuspid AV [4].

Lastly, to evaluate the gradients across the AV, one will need good Doppler alignment across the LVOT and AV. With TTE, this can best be obtained from the apical 5C or apical LAX views. Spectral Doppler will allow for evaluations and gradings of valvular stenosis and/or regurgitation [4].

The standard TEE exam of the aortic valves includes images from the ME and TG views. These assessments should allow for visualizations of all three cusps as well as the measurements of gradients across the aortic valve and LVOT. The standard ME views, include the 5C, LAX, AV LAX, AV SAX, TG LAX and the dTG 5C views (Fig. 18.8). ## **18.6.2 Mitral Valve**

The MV is the atrioventricular valve separating the LA and the LV. The MV anatomy consists of the mitral annulus, the anterior leafet (AMVL) and posterior leafet (PMVL) and the submitral apparatus. The sub-valvular structures include the anterolateral and posteromedial papillary muscles with corresponding chordae tendinae which can be classifed as

The mitral annulus is in fbrous continuity with the AV via the aortomitral curtain or aortomitral fbrosa, located between the right and left fbrous trigones within the fbrous skeleton of the heart valve complex [5]. Primary MV chordae attach to the anterior and posterior MV leafet free margins, while secondary chordae attach to the ventricular side of each leafet. The "stay chordae" are secondary chordae that helps prevent leafet prolapse and maintain valve geometry by inserting into the ventricular side of the leafet body. Lastly, tertiary chordae are only found on the PMVL and attach to the ventricular wall [6]. A properly functioning MV has a large area of leaflet coaptation with minimal regurgitation [1].

The MV apparatus as described above, is a complex, multidimensional structure that is often diffcult to image using 2D echocardiography. Unlike the mostly one-dimensional TV, the MV is saddle-shaped with its peaks at the center points of the AMVL and PMVL and its troughs at the anterior and posterior commissures. The AMVL makes up approximately 2/3 of the area of the mitral valve while the PMVL makes up approximately 2/3 of the annular circumference [12]. The normal valve area is 4–5 cm2 , which smaller than that of the TV [1, 2].

Due to the complex nature of the MV and surrounding structures, two systems of nomenclature were developed to describe the MV and associated pathologies: the Duran Classifcation and the Carpentier Classifcation. The Duran Classifcation is not as widely used in the United States and is therefore beyond the scope of this chapter [13]. The Carpentier Classifcation divides the AMVL and PMVL into three portions based on the normal embryological development of each of the three PMVL scallops. From lateral to medial, starting from the anterior commissure, the PMVL is divided into P1, P2 and P3 portions. The corresponding AMVL is then divided into A1, A2 and A3 counterparts (Fig. 18.18). The ASE and SCA recommend using the Carpentier system for describing the MV and associated pathology to ensure clear and accurate communication within and between specialties [1, 14].

Transthoracic evaluation of the mitral valve includes evaluation of both leafets with visualization of each segment in addition to evaluation of the submitral apparatus. Due to the position of the MV relative to the esophagus, the TEE exam will be superior to the TTE exam for evaluating the MV. The primary views to evaluate MV function from the TTE exam will be from the PLAX, PSAX and apical views [4]. ## **18.6.3 Tricuspid Valve**

The TV is the atrioventricular valve between the RA and RV, with a normal area of 5–8 cm2 . Similar to the MV, the TV has an incomplete fbrous annulus and leafets which are attached to chordae and papillary muscles. Typically, the TV has ventricularly displaced annulus and three asymmetric leafets (septal, anterior and posterior). Unlike the MV, the TV annulus is not in fbrous continuity with its ventricular outfow valve (the pulmonic valve in this case) [6]. Due to its anterior location and orientation in the chest, the TV is not easily imaged or assessed by Doppler using TEE [1].

Compared to TEE, the TTE exam is better positioned for detailed assessments of the TV due to its anterior location in the chest. The PLAX view with a focus on the RV infow can be found by tilting the transducer inferiorly toward the patient's right hip [4]. From this view, one can visualize the TV and RV with excellent Doppler alignment across the valve. Additionally, the apical 4C with RV focus provides good alignment for CFD evaluations of the TV but less ideal alignment for spectral Doppler. The PSAX view with focus on the TV provides good imaging of the TV, specifcally the anterior leafet. The apical 4C with posterior angulation shows the coronary sinus as it enters the RA, just superior to the septal leafet of the TV. Lastly, the subcostal 4C view shows the TV in the near feld and can provide excellent resolutions of the TV [4].

A comprehensive TEE examination of the TV includes the ME 4C, ME 5C, ME coronary sinus, ME RV infowoutfow and ME modifed bicaval 2D views with and without CFD (Fig. 18.8) [5]. When assessing fow velocities and gradients across the TV with TEE, the modifed bicaval view provides the most reliable Doppler alignment. The TG RV infow and TG RV infow-outfow views can be used to assess the sub-tricuspid apparatus [5]. Hepatic venous fows and fow reversals from TV regurgitation can be assessed using the TG hepatic vein view [1, 14].

# **18.6.4 Pulmonic Valve**

The pulmonic valve (PV) is the right heart equivalent of the AV; however, there are several key structural differences between these two valves. The pulmonary root includes the RVOT, pulmonary annulus, PV and the main PA. Unlike the AV, which is in fbrous continuity with the MV, the PV and the TV are not an intricately related complex as the RVOT occupies the infundibulum of the RV [14]. The PV tends to be slightly larger in area than the AV (3.5–4.5 cm2 versus 3–4 cm2 ) [2]. Additionally, the PV is more anteriorly positioned compared to the AV, with thinner cusps. The PV has three semi-lunar cusps, known as the anterior, left and right pulmonic cusps [14]. The pulmonic root also has a sinotubular junction, though it is not as pronounced as its aortic counterpart and does not have coronary sinuses [1, 6].

Similar to the TV, the PV anterior location makes TTE the ideal imaging modality for this valve's assessment. The PV can be seen in the PLAX view with focus on the PV. To acquire this image, one must tilt and rotate the probe toward the RVOT. In this image the PV is seen in long axis and in the near feld, allowing for annular measurements of the valve and proximal PA. The PSAX views at the level of the great vessels with focus on the PV and PA may allow for spectral Doppler alignment across the PV to evaluate for stenosis or insuffciency. It can also allow for measurements of the proximal and distal PA as well as the right and left pulmonary arteries. To achieve this image, one may need to tilt the probe anteriorly. Lastly, the apical 5C view with focus on the RVOT shows the PV in the far feld [4]. Though the resolution of the valve is worse than in the other views, this view provides the best Doppler alignments across the PV.

#### **18.7.1 LVADs**

Though the gold standard for treatment of end-stage heart failure is ultimately an orthotopic heart transplant, the primary alternative treatment has become ventricular assist devices (VADs). These can be devices specifcally to support the LV (LVAD), RV (RVAD) or both (BiVAD). As the technology continues to improve, there are newer and better designed implantable devices. For the purposes of this discussion, we will divide these into temporary devices, such as the Impella (Abiomed; Danver, MA) and intraaortic balloon pumps (IABP), and durable LVAD devices (also see Chap. 46).

Many of these devices are being placed in the interventional cardiology laboratory/clinic under fuoroscopy, but other placements are also being guided by TEE. These devices require precise deployments to ensure optimal performances as well as to prevent harm from a malpositioned device.

The Impella device utilizes microaxial fow, thereby augmenting left ventricular ejection. It is inserted either from the axillary artery or the femoral artery and threaded retrograde through the AV. For TMVR procedures, the TEE guidance and views are the same as for the MitraClip. Again, TEE guidance can be used to guide septal puncture and 3D echo can be used to position the device. In the case of treating mitral stenosis, the patient may require a balloon valvuloplasty prior to deployment of the TMVR. This can lead to a period of extreme hemodynamic instability until the new valve is deployed. Similar to the TAVR, a TMVR must be evaluated for properly functioning leafets and a stable nonrocking valve in the MV annulus. Additionally, LVOT obstruction must also be ruled out after implantation. Trace to mild residual central mitral regurgitation is common and not a concern; however, a large regurgitant jet and/or PVLs must be addressed [23]. These can be evaluated in the ME views of the MV and from the 3D view of the MV with CFD.

Currently, compared to the left-sided valves, right-sided valve procedures are less commonly performed. Although transcatheter tricuspid valves are in development, they are not regularly encountered in the clinical arena [16]. A major hurdle is compression of the AV node due to its proximity to the septal leafet of the TV. However, edge-to-edge repairs are being performed on the TV, similar to the MV. These procedures are typically performed under general anesthesia due to their prolonged procedure time; as they tend to be more technically diffcult than a MitraClip [16]. No septal puncture is performed but the procedure is still guided by TEE. The views most useful for this procedure are the ME 4C, RV infow-outfow, and modifed bicaval views. Again, the valve should be evaluated after the procedure for residual tricuspid regurgitation as well as new tricuspid stenosis. 3D image guidance may be used; however, due to the anterior position of the TV, 3D TEE is not always straightforward for the TV [16, 20].

Transcatheter pulmonic valve interventions are indicated in patients with severe pulmonic insuffciency and resultant RV volume or pressure overload. This is primarily seen in patients with congenital heart disease or acquired from prior PV interventions (such as a Ross procedure). As a result, these patients tend to be younger and may require multiple interventions. It is typically performed under general anesthesia and therefore utilizes TEE [16]. This can be challenging, as the PV is anterior and diffcult to image from the esophagus; however, a skilled echocardiographer using standard and non-standard views can usually obtain good images [24] (Fig. 18.30). Possible complications that can be viewed by echo from this procedure include: (1) coronary artery compression with new regional wall motion abnormalities, (2) aortic regurgitation, (3) valve embolization, and (4) RVOT rupture and valve migration, which can lead to pulmonary arterial occlusion [24].

## **18.7.4 Extracorporeal Mechanical Circulatory Support**

Though there are many different types of extracorporeal mechanical circulatory support systems, for the purposes of this discussion, we will be examining the different types of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) and RVADs.

The main types of ECMO are venovenous (VV), venoarterial (VA), and venoarteriovenous (VAV). Simply, VV ECMO is used when the cardiac function is preserved, but when pulmonary function is poor, such as in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) or COVID pneumonia. In this confguration, the blood is drained from the venous side, oxygenated, and returned back to the venous circulation. VA ECMO is used to support cardiac function and can offload the work of a failing heart. A typical situation where VA ECMO would be utilized is in the setting of cardiac arrest or post-cardiac surgery complications. From an echo guidance standpoint, again the ME bicaval view is best to identify proper venous cannula positioning. VAV ECMO is a combination of both VV and VA ECMO. In this configuration, deoxygenated blood is drained from the RA, sent to an oxygenator and pump, and circulated to not only the arterial system but also back into the venous system. This is used to combat the North-South Syndrome (also known as Harlequin Syndrome). This occurs in patients who have partially recovered cardiac function but poor pulmonary function such that their heart is pumping deoxygenated blood through the aortic valve. This creates a mixing zone in the aortic arch where the oxygenated blood from the femoral artery mixes with deoxygenated blood from the heart and lungs. In such a case, this hypoxic mixture then perfuses the coronary arteries and brain, leading to myocardial and cerebral ischemic injury. To combat this, oxygenated blood is returned to the RA so the ejected blood is not hypoxic. Again, the ME bicaval view is the best for visualizing the proper cannula locations [26].

Finally, the RVAD device is a form of ECMO in which only the RV is bypassed and can be configured with or without an oxygenator. This device, which is typically placed under fluoroscopy, is somewhat similar to a large pulmonary artery catheter. It is inserted from the right internal jugular vein and placed through the TV and PV, terminating in the main PA. It drains deoxygenated blood from the RA, circulates it to a pump with or without an oxygenator, and returns it to the patient distal to the PV. Proper positioning can be confirmed with the ME bicaval view, to ensure the drainage port is in the RA. The ME RV inflow-outflow view can follow the course of the device through the RV and distal to the PV (Fig. 18.32). One can also evaluate for pulmonic insufficiency, as a result of the device from this view. Lastly, the distal positioning can be confirmed from the UE ascending aorta short-axis view. From this view, one can confirm that the distal cannula tip is in fact in the main PA and has not migrated into the right or left pulmonary arteries [25].

## **18.8 Summary**

Echocardiography is an advanced imaging technology with many practical clinical applications. Not only can it evaluate accurately a given patient's cardiac anatomy and function, but it can also guide cardiac procedures. TTE has become ubiquitous in the clinical setting as a valuable non-invasive method to diagnose cardiac disease while transesophageal echo, though more invasive, provides higher defnition detail about cardiac function. As ultrasound technologies continue to improve and become more widely available, profciency in echocardiography will become increasingly important for the modern clinician to master.
