# **9 Detailed Anatomical and Functional Features of the Cardiac Valves**

Michael A. Bielecki, Michael G. Bateman, Jason L. Quill, Alexander J. Hill, and Paul A. Iaizzo

#### **Abstract**

The use of high-resolution noninvasive imaging in modern cardiology to collect detailed images of valve function has dramatically accelerated the understanding of functional human heart anatomy. In the healthy human, the cardiac valves determine the passage of blood through the heart. The atrioventricular valves open during diastole to allow the flling of the ventricles and close during systole (ventricular contraction), directing blood through the semilunar valves to the body; these valves, in turn, close during diastole to prevent the fow of blood back into the ventricle. By presenting a comprehensive review of the histology, functional anatomy, and morphology of the cardiac valves, this chapter promotes an understanding of the valve features required for valvar repair or replacement via either surgical or minimally invasive (transcatheter) means.

# **9.1 Introduction**

A critical understanding of cardiac anatomy is essential for both engineers and clinicians with the intent of developing and/or employing improved or novel technologies or therapies for treating a diseased or ineffcient cardiac valve. Likewise, such knowledge is required for directing translational research, including initiating preclinical investigations, assessing the feasibility of clinical trials, and performing frst-in-man procedures. There are two atrioventricular valves in the human heart, namely, the right or *tricuspid* and left or *mitral*, and two semilunar valves, specifcally the *pulmonary on the right* and *aortic on the left*. All four valves are complex anatomical structures which can vary greatly among individuals and/or become modifed by disease processes. In this review, we discuss the anatomy, pathology, and issues related to transcatheter and surgical repairs of the atrioventricular and arterial valves in a translational manner.

The high prevalence of aortic valvular pathologies in the growing elderly population, coupled with poor clinical outcomes for patients who go untreated, has resulted in prolifc research and development efforts associated with designing more effective and less traumatic therapies. The development of therapies designed to treat the semilunar valves has been guided by anatomical information gathered from highresolution imaging technologies, which in turn focused attention on the need for a complete understanding of both the valves and their surrounding anatomies. Today, the introduction of transcatheter therapies to repair or replace the atrioventricular valves has, through the same anatomically guided design principles, provided clinicians with the ability to percutaneously treat valves with even greater anatomical complexity (see Chap. 39).

#### **9.2 The Cardiac Skeleton**

Before describing the specifc anatomies of the cardiac valves, it is important to understand the anatomical framework that holds these valves in position and thus consequently the relationships of each valve to one another. Figure 9.1 shows an anatomical plate of a human heart with the atria and great arteries removed, highlighting the close proximity of all four cardiac valves to each other. Traditionally, the four valves of the heart have been described as being supported by a fbrous framework or *cardiac skeleton* made of dense connective tissue passing transversely through the base of the heart between the atria and the ventricles. Bateman et al. [1] describe the strongest part of the skeleton as the area of fbrous continuity between the leafets of the mitral and aortic valves. This fbrous strap, thickened at both its ends by the fbrous trigones, anchors the aorticmitral valvar unit within the base of the left ventricle (Fig. 9.2). The coronet-like support of the aortic valvar leaflets extends antero-cranially from the region of fbrous continuity and is often considered to represent an aortic valvar annulus, but there are no anatomical structures supporting the semilunar hinges of the aortic valvar leafets (Fig. 9.3).

However, the extent of the skeleton is often greatly exaggerated. The so-called annular components of the atrioventricular valves then extend inferiorly and posteriorly from the central fbrous body and the left fbrous trigone, respectively (Fig. 9.3) [3, 4]. It is the exception rather than the rule, however, for these fbrous cords to extend throughout the full circumference of the left and right atrioventricular junctions. The annuluses of the atrioventricular valves, as such, are better formed in the mitral as opposed to the tricuspid junction. In the mitral junction, it is normal to fnd segments of the valvar leafets hinged from the fbroadipose tissue of the atrioventricular junction, rather than from a frm fbrous annulus [5]. Likewise, in the tricuspid junction, the valvar leafets are normally hinged from fbroadipose tissue [6]. The annuluses, as part of the atrioventricular junctions and rarely being complete fbrous rings, are highly dynamic and change dramatically in shape and size throughout the cardiac cycle from systole to diastole [1]. It is also the fbroadipose tissue of the junctions that provides the greatest part of the required electrical insulation between the atrial and ventricular muscular masses, with the atrioventricular bundle of the conduction system being the only structure in the normal heart that crosses the insulating plane through the atrioventricular component of the membranous septum.

Uniquely, the leafets of the pulmonary valve have no direct fbrous support other than that provided by the valvar sinuses. The basal components of each leafet are supported by the right ventricular infundibulum. The separation of the pulmonary root away from the other valvar structures makes surgical removal during the Ross procedure possible, with the presence of the supporting skirt of infundibular musculature facilitating its use as an autograft to replace the aortic valve [7].

# **9.3 The Atrioventricular Valves**

In the most basic anatomical sense, the atrioventricular valves are made up of three main components:

- Valve leafets attached to the respective annulus.
- Tendinous cords attaching the leafets to the ventricular myocardium.
- Papillary muscles providing the anchoring points for the tendinous cords to the ventricular wall.

The leafets of the atrioventricular valves can be thought as forming a *skirt* that hangs from the annulus; leafets are divided into a series of sections that constitute the distinct leafets of each valve. Due to the extent of variations between individuals with regard to leafet morphologies, there has been much debate relative to nomenclature on the number of leafets of both the mitral and tricuspid valves [8–10]. Traditionally, the division of the leafets has been determined by the presence of commissures, which are defned as an area within the valve complex, located between two adjacent leaflets. Recently, there has been more research into the complexity of these valves, particularly in the tricuspid, where the number of leafets is more heterogenous, ranging from two to four leafets associated with the tricuspid or right atrioventricular valve [8].

The leafets themselves are attached to the ventricles via the sub-valvar apparatus of each valve. In general, each apparatus consists of the tendinous cords and the papillary muscle complexes of each valve. The tendinous cords are usually categorized by (1) those that support the free edges of the valves, (2) those that support the rough zones (the region between the free edge and each annulus), and/or (3) those that attach to the leafets near to the annulus. Typically, the cords supporting the free edges of the leafets are known as *fan cords* due to the presence of multiple fenestrations. Those that attach to the rough zone of the leafets are distinguished by their larger size and are commonly defned as *strut cords*. Finally, those that attach near the annulus are known as *basal cords*. The strut cords are of specifc importance, as they bear the highest mechanical loads during systole [11]. Furthermore, the number and distribution of the tendinous cords across a given valve are critical to its function; it is well documented that dysfunction of these structures can lead to prolapse of the valves [12–14]. In general, the cords attach to the heads of the papillary muscles, which themselves play an important role in the function of each valve by contracting during systole to cushion the valve closure and help prevent the valve from prolapsing into the atrium.

Due to their role in the systolic closure of the atrioventricular valves, papillary muscle dysfunction can lead to regurgitation of blood through the atrioventricular valves. Dysfunction of the papillary muscles often results from a cardiac event, such as a myocardial infarction, or following an infection of the ventricular musculature (endocarditis). In rare cases, such damage can lead to full rupture of the papillary muscle and subsequent catastrophic regurgitation of the associated atrioventricular valve which, in turn, can result in life-threatening complications such as cardiogenic shock [12, 13].

#### **9.3.1 Atrioventricular Valve Function**

During systole, when the ventricles are contracting, the subvalvar apparatus of each valve prevents the leafets from prolapsing into the atria and aids in ventricular ejection by effectively drawing the apex of the ventricle toward the basal ring. Additionally, it has been shown that the sub-valvar apparatus plays a crucial role during diastole, while the ventricle is flling, by moderating wall tensions and improving the effciency of the ventricular myocardium [15]. During systole in normal/healthy cardiac function, the valve leafets, which bulge toward the atrium, can be considered to stay pressed together throughout the contraction and therefore do not prolapse. During diastole, when the ventricles are relaxing and the chambers are flling through the open atrioventricular valves, eddy currents that form behind the leafets and tension in the sub-valvar apparatus help to control diastolic valve function by moderating the motion of the open leafets.

These sequences display the normal cardiac function of the mitral and tricuspid valves, respectively [16, 17]; the images were obtained from the atria (above the valve) and from the ventricular apexes (below the valve).

Dysfunction of the atrioventricular valves is usually characterized by one of two symptoms: (1) failure of a valve to successfully close or (2) failure of a valve to successfully open. Dysfunction of the valves during systole (i.e., failure of the valve to successfully close) is known as valvar *incompetence* and results in the *regurgitation* of blood back in a retrograde direction through the atrioventricular junction. Such dysfunction results in a decrease in cardiac output and increases the pressure within the atria during systole leading to compensatory myocardial hypertrophy and in some cases heart failure or atrial fbrillation. Dysfunction of the valves in diastole (failure of the valve to fully open and allow blood to fll the expanding ventricles) is termed *stenosis*. This decrease in the effective orifce area of the open valve is often due to stiffening or calcifcation of the valve leafets and can initiate chronic cardiopulmonary diseases such as hypertension, heart failure as the ventricles works to compensate for the reduced fow. #### **9.4 The Semilunar Valves**

A healthy semilunar valve is composed of three valve leaflets, each attached to its respective sinus. These valves lie between the ventricular outfow tracts and the arterial trunks, the main arteries carrying blood away from the heart. This elegant structure is much simpler than that of the atrioventricular valves described previously, in that the semilunar valve leafets do not require a tension apparatus to maintain competency. When closed, the three leafets of each valve coapt along zones of apposition, or *commissures,* which are fbrous zones some distance from the free edge of the leaflets. At the center of the valve where all three leafets coapt, a distinct fbrous nodule can be found. The valve leafet margins are attached to the arterial wall in the shape of a halfmoon, hence the *semilunar* moniker. Normally, the regions of the valves where the commissures meet the arterial wall are considerably higher than the seats of the leafets, thereby giving the valve a crown-like shape. These three points, particularly in the aortic valve, are used to defne the sinotubular junctions (Fig. 9.6). Although we have discussed the positioning of the valves in the heart by referring to their respective annuluses, many anatomists contest the idea that there are single defned annuluses for both the pulmonary and aortic valves [20]. Interestingly, there is a defned annulus where the respective arteries are attached to the ventricular outfow tract; however, due to the crown-like structure of the valve, the hemodynamic junction of the valves spans this annulus. This structural shape results in part of the arterial wall being considered a ventricular structure (in a hemodynamic sense) and, in turn, part of the ventricular wall an arterial structure. Just distal to the valves are the *arterial sinuses* that are represented by dilations of the artery positioned above each leafet and additionally, for the aortic valve, house the coronary artery ostium. The sinus also provides a recess for the valve leafets to retract into, allowing for unrestricted fow from the ventricle to the artery. ## **9.4.1 The Functioning of the Semilunar Valves**

When a semilunar valve is functioning correctly, the leafets are pushed into the sinus during myocardial contraction (systole) to allow blood to leave the ventricles. As the myocardium relaxes and the pressure within the ventricle drops below the pressure distal to the valve in the arterial system (the aorta or pulmonary artery), the valve snaps shut. This usually happens soon after ventricular systole but before the heart has completely relaxed, so that during diastole, when the chambers are flling through the atrioventricular valves, the leafets of the semilunar valves remain tightly closed. A positive pressure difference between the aorta and the coronary sinus, which lies within the right atrium, allows for the fow of blood through the coronary vasculature during diastole. Thus, it should be noted that the heart muscle is perfused with blood when the semilunar valves are closed and the cardiac myocytes are relaxing.

Figures 9.7 and 9.8 show image sequences of the functional movements of the pulmonary and aortic valves, respectively; these images were obtained from reanimated human hearts employing Visible Heart® methodologies [16, 17]. The images include views of semilunar valves from above (i.e., from videoscopes within the pulmonary artery and the aorta) and from below (with videoscopes within the right and left ventricular outfow tracts).

In general, dysfunction of the semilunar valves is usually characterized by one of two symptoms: failure of the valves to successfully close or failure of the valves to successfully open. Dysfunction of the valves during systole, that is, failure of the valve to successfully open, is defned as *stenosis* of the valve. This pathology is characterized by a reduction in the effective orifce area of the valve (the size of the opening that allows blood to pass), which in turn forces the ventricles to work harder to move blood to the body or lungs potentially leading to ventricular hypertrophy and/or mitral valve failure. Dysfunction of the semilunar valves during diastole, when the ventricles are relaxing, results in *regurgitation*; this occurs when blood is allowed back into the ventricle from the arterial system, overloading the ventricles and potentially causing chronic heart failure.

# **9.5 Valve Histologies**

Interestingly, the atrioventricular valves share very similar leafet histology. The atrial sides of the leafets consist of spongy tissue (lamina spongiosa) comprised of fbrocytes, histiocytes, and collagen fbers [23]. It is these collagen fbers that supply the mechanical strength required of the atrioventricular valves. The ventricular sides consist of fbrous tissue (lamina fbrosa), and both layers are surrounded by endothelial cells. Additionally, the valve leafets have been shown to incorporate both primary sensory and autonomic innervation with the anterior leafet of the mitral valve demonstrating twice the innervation of the posterior leafet [24]. These nerves are typically situated in the lamina spongiosa and extend over the proximal and medial portions of the leafet [23]. Fibroblasts [25], smooth muscle cells, and myocardial cells are also commonly located within the leaflet tissue [26].

Cells within the leafets have been shown to elicit two types of contractile activity: (1) a brief contraction or twitch at the beginning of each heartbeat (refecting contraction of myocytes in the leafet in communication with, and excited by the adjacent atrial muscle) which has relaxed by midsystole and whose contractile activity is eliminated with β-receptor blockade, and (2) sustained tonic contractions (or tone) during isovolumic relaxation, which has been shown to be insensitive to β-blockade but doubled by stimulation of the neurally rich region of aortic-mitral continuity [27]. These contractile activities within the leafets are hypothesized to aid in the maintenance of the atrioventricular valve leafet shape. This, in turn, could help prevent mechanical shock to the leafets upon valve closure and also aid in optimizing the leafet shape for funneling blood into the ventricular outfow tracts [27].

The tendinous cords are composed of a collagen core, surrounded by elastin fbers interwoven in layers of loose collagen. Similar to the valve leafets, they also have an outer layer of endothelial cells, but it is the collagen cores that support the greatest degree of mechanical load during systole and restore the wavy confguration during diastole. The elastin fbers are normally arranged in a parallel fashion relative to the collagen fbers, and as the cords are stretched during systole, the elastin fbers are also stretched, straightening the collagen. It is hypothesized that it is this composite confguration of elastin and collagen that provides a smooth mechanism for the transmission of cordal forces from the leafets to the papillary muscles and facilitates the wavy diastolic confguration of the cords. The relative amount of collagen and elastin within the given cord varies according to their relative types, as does the relative amount of contained DNA and their degree of vascularization. Normally the vascularization of the tendinous cords is located between their collagen cores and the elastin fbers and is further considered to supply nutrients to the leafets. It has been reported that a higher DNA content within both the anterior and posterior marginal cords relates to inherently higher rates of collagen synthesis in order to prevent mechanical deterioration [10].

The papillary muscles can be considered part of the ventricular myocardium and hence are composed of aggregated myocytes. The cells exhibit complex junctions, called *intercalated discs*, allowing multiple cells to form long cellular networks. Within the papillary muscles, these muscle fbers run parallel to each other along the length of the muscle to increase contractile force and effciency. The papillary muscles are extensively innervated and have complex vascular systems in order to maintain coordinated contractions with the continuum of the ventricular myocardium [14].

It was Gross who frst drew attention to the specifc histological structures of the arterial valves, his account then being endorsed by others such as Misfeld and colleagues [23, 28]. Each leafet of the semilunar valve was described to have a fbrous core, or *fbrosa*, with an endothelial lining containing delicate sheets of elastin on its arterial and ventricular aspects. This so-called fbrous "backbone" is represented by a dense collagenous layer, which gives way to a much looser structure, or *spongiosa*, toward the ventricular aspects of the leafet cusps. The zone of apposition of the leafets consists of an abrupt thickening of the fbrous layer made up of closely packed vertically directed fbers and builds at the central portion of the free edge, creating a node termed the *nodulusArantii* [23, 28]. Figure 9.9 displays a cross section of an aortic valve leafet displaying the varying tissue types [31].

The mechanisms for stenosis of the arterial valves in noncongenital cases involve a complex cascade of events comprised of several steps [31]. Degeneration of the valve leafets, particularly in the aortic valve, causes outfow obstruction, and can in some cases also cause regurgitation [30]. One method of degeneration is characterized by fbrocalcifc remodeling whereby the valve interstitial cells fbrose, a process defned by the overgrowth and scarring of the tissues attributed to excess deposition of extracellular matrix components on the surface of the leafets. Collagen is normally created by the valve interstitial cells to serve as a scaffold, however the disorganized growth and excess production of collagen during fbro-calcifc remodelling contributes signifcantly to valve stenosis. Subsequently, osteogenic differentiation of the damaged interstitial cells into calcifc phenotypes is triggered by hemodynamic shear stress, reactive oxygen species, infammatory cytokines, and higher lipid accumulation on the valve [31]. This degenerative process leads to stiffening of the valve leafets and, coupled with an infammatory response to the damaged tissue, accelerates the remodeling of calcifc aortic valves. Congenital defects can also lead to arterial valve stenosis with abnormalities such as bicuspid aortic valves commonly presenting as the cause for aortic stenosis in younger patients. Treatments for stenosis in the semilunar valves involve either surgical or transcatheter valve replacement. More information on these solutions is further discussed in Chap. 36.

Yet, it should be emphasized that, as with other anatomical studies, these data do not account for all anatomical variations. For example, it has also been reported that the cordal attachments to the mural leafet may extend simply from the ventricular myocardium to the leafet without a papillary muscle attachment. Furthermore, it is well known that the tendinous cords themselves may elicit highly variably anatomies and various subpopulations of tendinous cords have been classifed by both function [43] and type [44]. ## **9.7 The Tricuspid Valve**

The right atrioventricular valve, or tricuspid valve, is situated within the right atrioventricular junction and modulates the fow of blood between the right atrium and right ventricle. This valve is typically defned by three leafets suspended from the muscular atrioventricular junction and connected to the ventricular wall via three distinct papillary muscle complexes (as seen in Fig. 9.10). When defned using attitudinally correct nomenclature, these leafets are located in septal, anterosuperior (traditionally anterior), and inferior (traditionally posterior) positions (Figs. 9.16 and 9.17) [46]. Interestingly, recent anatomical studies investigating variability in the leafet morphology of the tricuspid valve have shown that more than 40% of patients may present with right atrioventricular valves exhibiting four distinct leafets [Holda et al. JACC 2019 PMID: 30678795]. These fndings reinforce the fact that anatomic variation must be accounted for when planning both surgical and transcatheter valve interventions.

The anterosuperior leafet is the largest of the three and extends from the medial border of the ventricular septum to the acute margin of the atrioventricular junction (Fig. 9.15). The leafet is hinged from the undersurface of the supraventricular crest and provides a curtain between the infow and outfow tracts of the right ventricle. The inferior leafet is hinged from the diaphragmatic aspect of the atrioventricular junction. The septal leafet is then hinged from the ventricular border of the triangle of Koch, with the hinge crossing the right-sided aspect of the membranous septum, dividing it into atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial components [47]. The septal leafet is often cleft as it crosses the membranous septum. When viewed in closed position, the trifoliate zones of apposition between the leafets extend to their peripheral ends. It is these ends that are typically considered to represent the valvar commissures, which can be named as being anteroseptal, anteroinferior, and infero-septal [1].

The smaller inferior muscle complex supports the zone of apposition between the inferior and septal leafets. This is a differentiating feature between the tricuspid and mitral valves, the leafets of the latter valve lacking any septal attachments.

# **9.8 The Aortic Valve**

As previously mentioned, due to its location in the center of the heart between the mitral valve and the tricuspid valve, the aortic valve is considered the "centerpiece" of the heart and is often the most important cardiac valve with respect to normal cardiac function. An artist's rendition of the opened aortic root showing the features of the aortic valve can be seen in Fig. 9.20.

#### **9.8.1 The Aortic Root**

The aortic root contains three circular rings and one crownlike ring (Fig. 9.6) [4, 20]. The connection of the leafets to the arterial wall mimics the shape of a crown, whose base forms a virtual ring known as the basal plane of the valve. This plane represents the inlet from the left ventricular outfow tract into the aortic root. The top of the crown can be considered as a true ring, the sinotubular junction, defned by the sinus ridge and the related sites of attachment of the peripheral zones of apposition, between the aortic valve leaflets [32]. Hence the sinotubular junction dictates the transition from the aortic root into the ascending aorta. The semilunar hinges then cross another defned "ring" known as the anatomic *ventriculoarterial* junction. This overall anatomic arrangement was illustrated previously in Fig. 9.6, but can be readily observed when the aortic root is opened linearly as seen in Fig. 9.19.

The normal aortic root elicits a relatively consistent shape between patients, but can vary dramatically in size (Table 9.5) [52, 53]. Kunzelman et al. demonstrated a defnable mathematical relationship between root diameter and clinically measurable leafet dimensions [54]. In general, the diameter at the level of the sinotubular junction typically exceeds that at the level of the basal plane by a factor of 1:1.6 [54, 55]. The valvar complex is a dynamic structure with its geometric parameters changing continuously throughout the phases of the cardiac cycle and relative to the associated changes in pressure that will occur within the aortic root [56]. For example, from diastole to systole, the relative change in diameter at the level of the sinotubular junction and at the *ventriculoarterial* junction has been noted to increase by ~12% and decrease by ~16%, respectively [56–58]. Additionally, the orientation of the left ventricular outfow tract and the aortic root (i.e., the angle between the two) is known to vary from patient to patient. It is also understood that this angle becomes more acute with age. Middelhof et al. reported that hearts from individuals aged >60 years exhibited angles between 90° and 120°, whereas individuals aged <20 years presented with angles between 135° and 180°.

It is important to note that one of the most critical functions of the aortic root is to facilitate coronary artery perfusion during ventricular diastole. This is achieved by directing 3–5% of the circulating blood through both the left and right coronary arteries while the aortic valve itself is closed. In general, the orifces of the coronary arteries arise within the two anterior sinuses of Valsalva, usually positioned just below the sinotubular junctions [55, 59, 60]. However, it is not unusual to fnd these arteries positioned superiorly relative to the sinotubular junction. Cavalcanti et al. reported the mean distance measured from the orifce of the left coronary artery to the basal attachments of the corresponding leafets was 12.6 ± 2.61 mm, and for the right coronary artery, it was 13.2 ± 2.64 mm in 51 normal postmortem hearts [61]. In addition, the right coronary artery ostium tends to be more eccentric in the cusp, with roughly 30% of the populations ostium presiding 20° or more from the centreline of the right coronary cusp, compared to the left, where this was true for only 5% with the left ostium [62]. A graphical representation of this distribution can be seen in Figure 9.21. Variations in coronary arterial origin, nonetheless, can occur with some of these confgurations posing as risk factors in sudden cardiac death [63, 64]. It should also be recalled that it is the location of these coronary arteries that dictates the naming of the aortic valve leafets/cusps—the left coronary, the right coronary, and the nonadjacent (or non-coronary).

# **9.8.2 The Aortic Leafets**

As noted above, the leafets of the aortic valve are named for the branching coronary arteries that feed the left and right sides of the heart (Fig. 9.20). More specifcally, both the right and left leafets attach to the aortic root in the predominantly muscular region of the left ventricular outfow tract, whereas the nonadjacent leafet is chiefy attached to the fbrous region above the membranous septum (Fig. 9.19). This fbrous continuity connects the aortic valve to the anterior (aortic) leafet of the mitral valve, forming the aorticmitral curtain. The zone of apposition of the right leafet to the nonadjacent leafet is positioned above the membranous part of the ventricular septum. The zone of apposition of the nonadjacent leafet with the left coronary aortic leafet is adjacent to the anterior wall of the left atrium. The left leafet then continues toward the right leafet, again achieving support from the muscular part of the ventricular septum. As previously mentioned, the zones of apposition themselves ascend as they extend to be attached peripherally at the sinotubular junction; below each peripheral attachment, there is a fbrous interleafet triangle that forms part of the ventricular outfow tract [65].

# **9.10 Valve Co-location with Other Cardiac Structures**

When performing cardiac valve surgeries and/or contemplating novel percutaneous approaches to valvar repairs, it is vital to have a strong anatomical appreciation of the associated structures, that is, those cardiac structures that surround the valves. The anatomical features surrounding the mitral and tricuspid valves are displayed in Figs. 9.23 and 9.24. The position and course of the coronary vasculature is key to the clinical anatomy of both the mitral and tricuspid valves. The great coronary vein, continuing as the coronary sinus, circles the mural leafet of the mitral valve [35, 70]. The circumfex artery, having branched from the main stem of the left coronary artery, courses in concert with the venous channel, usually running much closer to the hinge of the mural leafet. The atrioventricular node and its zones of transitional cells are closely related to the septal hinge of the tricuspid valve, with the slow pathway into the node being a constituent part of the vestibular atrial myocardium in this region. At the apex of the triangle of Koch [4, 21], the specialized cardiomyocytes of the atrioventricular node bundle themselves together and pierce the central fbrous body to become the penetrating atrioventricular bundle, or the bundle of His (Fig. 9.24). Although closely related again to the hinge of the septal leafet of the tricuspid valve, and at potential risk whenever surgery is performed within the right atrium or through the tricuspid valve, the atrioventricular node should be suffciently distant not to pose a threat to those operating on the mitral valve. The bundle of His, having passed through the membranous septum, divides on the crest of the muscular ventricular septum into the left and right bundle branches. These components are at greater risk during procedures on the aortic rather than the atrioventricular valves.

The most important anatomical structure related to the pulmonary root is the frst perforating branch of the anterior interventricular artery; this artery is avoided during the Ross procedure. Note should also be taken of anomalous coronary arteries either coursing between the arterial roots or extending across the right ventricular infundibulum. Being located in the most anterior aspect of the heart, the pulmonary root is also directly adjacent to the sternum; thus, sternal compression may alter the performance of any prosthesis placed in the pulmonary annulus [1].

The anatomical orientation of the aortic valve is considered much more challenging; thus, the remainder of this section will focus on this valve. One of the most important and complex structures in proximity to the aortic valve is the *cardiac conduction system*. As mentioned previously, the atrioventricular node is located within the *triangle of Koch*, situating the atrioventricular node in close proximity to the subaortic region of the left ventricular outfow tract, helping to explain why the treatment of pathologies involving the aortic valve may lead to either a complete heart block or to an intraventricular conduction abnormality [29]. # **9.11 Common Clinical Imaging of the Cardiac Valves**

Due to its relatively high availability, ease of use, and minimal side effects to the patient, the standard 2D cross-sectional Doppler echocardiogram is the most common imaging modality applied to assess the relative function and anatomical position of the cardiac valves [43, 73]. Recently, improvements in 3D echocardiographic imaging have added the ability to assess valve function in 3D thereby greatly improving the resources available to clinicians when diagnosing functional and fow-based valve diseases. Clinically, both the mitral and tricuspid valves are commonly imaged via the parasternal long-axis view, allowing the echocardiographer to assess the following valve criteria [73]:

- Size and shape of the annulus.
- Mobility of the leafets, in particular, whether they prolapse or show fail or restricted motion (assessment will also include exclusion of thickening calcifcation, myxomatous degeneration, clefts, fusions along zones of apposition, perforations, vegetations, or abnormal shelves or membranes).
- Length and thickness of the tendinous cords and whether they are fused or ruptured.
- Number, structure, and function of the papillary muscles.
- Whether the function of the left ventricle is normal, is globally deranged, or shows evidence of regional abnormalities of motion of the walls.

# **9.12 Summary**

The atrioventricular and semilunar valves are highly complex anatomical structures. Their overall function and/or subsequent dysfunction can be due to abnormalities or failures within any of their respective subcomponents. Today, the detailed assessment of their anatomy and function via high resolution medical imaging modalities provides critical information required by cardiologists, internationalists, and/ or cardiac surgeons required to determine optimal treatments approaches.

Although the anatomies of each valve type are similar, it should be noted that unique pathological changes can affect each valve resulting in differing approaches to disease assessment and treatment. Ultimately, a detailed understanding of the atrioventricular and semilunar valve anatomy will aid physicians and engineers alike in the development and deployment of future clinical therapies for the treatment of congenital and degenerative diseases affecting these valves. Furthermore, the use of common anatomical descriptions of these anatomical structures (defned as attitudinally correct anatomy) by anatomists, clinicians, and medical device designers is recommended for the successful and expedient communication of ideas and information in the modern world of medicine.
