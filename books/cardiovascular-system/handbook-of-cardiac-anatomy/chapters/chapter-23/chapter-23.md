We describe the integration of these technologies to create a more optimal clinical workfow, which in turn empower healthcare teams, enhances clinical value as well as guides the education and training of healthcare professionals and the given patient's families.

#### **23.1 Introduction**

Worldwide, cardiac defects occur in about 8–10 per 1000 live births. Congenital heart disease (CHD) encompasses an array of distinct malformations and defects present within the heart at birth. CHD is the most common type of birth defect, with an incidence of almost 1 in 100 live births, representing the leading cause of infant morbidity and mortality. This is furthered by the fact that the incidence of CHD in stillbirth is higher than that of live births. Patients with CHD often present with highly variable and unique abnormalities, which despite advancements in imaging and diagnosis, remain diffcult to understand. Therefore, communicating a visual understanding of these anatomical defects is especially challenging for educators in CHD. See also Chap. 41 for more discussion of CHDs.

Cardiac imaging plays a crucial role in the evaluation and management of patients with CHD. Advancements in cardiac imaging techniques will continue to fnd rapid clinical applications in the assessment of such patients and provide high degrees of necessary information to the providers, prior to and/or after performed interventions [1]. Recent years have witnessed exponential growths in an array of cardiac imaging technologies, allowing better visualizations of CHD complex anatomies as well as improved assessments of associated physiologies. These advances have become increasingly important as more complex surgical and catheter-based procedures are evolving to address the needs of a growing congenital heart disease population. This state-of-the-art chapter presents advances in echocardiography, cardiac magnetic resonance, cardiac computed tomography, and 3-dimensional modeling; as shown in Fig. 23.1.

#### **23.2 Imaging Modalities**

Echocardiography has indeed become a cornerstone in the noninvasive imaging toolkit for diagnosing and monitoring patients with CHD and/or acquired heart diseases in infants, children, and adolescents. This versatile imaging technique plays a pivotal role in providing detailed information relative to cardiovascular anatomies, ventricular functions, and valvular functions. Understanding basic cardiac anatomy is fundamental to comprehending the structure and function of the heart. This overview aims to elucidate key aspects such as the position of the apex, cardiac situs, ventricular characteristics, great arteries, atrial features, and the anatomical and physiological signifcance observed through different imaging views.

#### **23.2.1 Basic Cardiac Anatomy**

When one starts examining a patient with a CHD, there are a few basic anatomic paraments that one such frst assess.

• First, where is the position of the heart's apex. This initial step should be performed utilizing a subcostal window, seeking visualization from a four-chamber view; levocardia is established if the apex of the heart is directed to the left, and dextrocardia if directed to the right.

- Situs, identifcation of the abdominal situs is possible from the subcostal window; with slight rotation and caudad angulation of the probe, the aorta and inferior vena cava should be visualized as they course into the abdomen. Situs solitus is established with visualization of the aorta to the left of the inferior vena cava, with reversal of position indicating situs inversus.
- Ventricles, they may be distinguished from one another as follows:
  - 1. Identifcation of the atrioventricular tricuspid and mitral valves.
  - 2. The right ventricle demonstrates apical displacement of its associated atrioventricular (tricuspid) valve.
  - 3. The tricuspid valve is trileafet, with one leafet having a chordal attachment to the septum.
  - 4. The left ventricle is associated with a bileafet mitral valve. Anterior and posterior leafets, with no septal insertions.
  - 5. The most reliably distinguishing feature of the right ventricle is the moderator band, which also typically demonstrates an irregular endocardial outline, triangular shape, and the presence of an infundibulum.
  - 6. The left ventricle demonstrates a truncated ellipsoidal shape with two separate groups of papillary muscle and a smooth endocardial surface.
- Great Arteries—a cephalad angulation of the probe, from a subcostal or apical window, will allow differentiation of the two discrete branches of the pulmonary trunk in contrast to the more diminutive coronary arteries branching from the aortic root.
- Atria—right atrium (RA) has a wide triangular appendage, and is anteriorly located; left atrium (LA) has a thin, long appendage, and is posteriorly located.
- Atrial Septum—Right has a Eustachian Valve; left has a foramen ovale fap.
- The tricuspid valve—associated with the RV, has three leafets, is located toward the apex.
- Mitral valve—associated with the LV, has two leafets, and is attached to the lateral wall by papillary muscles.
- Aorta—will disperse into the coronaries.
- Pulmonary artery—will branch to the right and left.

#### **23.2.3 The Importance of Echocardiography in Managing CHD and Acquired Heart Disease**

Here are some key points highlighting the importance of echocardiography in managing CHD and acquired heart disease:

- *Noninvasive Imaging Techniques:* Echocardiography is a noninvasive imaging modality, eliminating the need for invasive imaging procedures, commonly used for initial assessment and follow-up evaluations.
- *Comprehensive Assessment:* This allows for the comprehensive assessments of various aspects of the heart, including anatomical structures, physiological functions, and hemodynamics. It provides real-time imaging of the heart's structures and blood fow.

- *Transthoracic Echocardiography (TTE):* With its radiation-free, portable, widely available, and noninvasive nature, TTE is particularly emphasized as the frst-line tool for the diagnosis and follow-up of pediatric and young adult patients with CHD [2]. This approach involves placing the transducer on the chest (transthoracic) to obtain images of the heart. Echocardiography stands as the predominant cardiac imaging test for assessing cardiovascular diseases linked to structural, functional, or hemodynamic abnormalities in the heart or major vessels. Offering real-time ultrasonic visualizations from various angles. TTE can also include Doppler and color fow imaging, enabling a dependable evaluation of cardiac hemodynamics and blood fow. TTE, being noninvasive, portable, and effcient, excels in providing comprehensive anatomic, hemodynamic, and physiologic insights into the pediatric heart. The TTE examination initiates with real-time two-dimensional (2D) echocardiography, delivering high-resolution images of cardiac structures and their movements [3]. Note that over time, TTE techniques have evolved from simple M-mode tracings to a suite of technologies encompassing 2D imaging, pulsed and continuous wave spectral Doppler, color fow Doppler, tissue Doppler, 3-dimensional (3D) imaging, and myocardial strain imaging [1, 3]. These technologies continue to improve at very high rates which will only improve the ease of such assessments in the future: uses of artifcial intelligence algorithms will also be helpful.
- *Anatomical, Physiological, and Hemodynamic Abnormalities:* Echocardiography is crucial for evaluating a wide range of abnormalities, including anatomical defects, physiological variations, and hemodynamic conditions associated with congenital heart conditions.
- *Real-Time Monitoring:* Echocardiography enables realtime monitoring, allowing healthcare professionals to visualize the dynamic aspects of the given patient's cardiac function and detect abnormalities promptly.
- *Doppler Echocardiography:* Doppler echocardiography is an integral part of these techniques, providing valuable information relative to blood fow patterns, velocities, and pressure gradients across different cardiac structures [2–4].
- *Pediatric and Young Adult Patients:* Echocardiography is particularly valuable in the pediatric population due to its noninvasiveness and the abilities to obtain detailed information without exposing young patients to radiation or other potential risks [2].
- *Follow-Up Assessments:* Echocardiography is routinely used for follow-up assessments, allowing healthcare providers to monitor changes in specifc cardiac anatomies and functions over time, assess the effectiveness of per-

- formed interventions, and make informed next step clinical decisions [4–6].
- *Need for an Echocardiogram*: Indications for acquiring a pediatric echocardiograms cover a broad spectrum of symptoms and signs: encompassing cyanosis, failure to thrive, exercise-induced chest pain or syncope, respiratory distress, murmurs, congestive heart failure, abnormal arterial pulses, and/or cardiomegaly. These indications may point toward structural congenital heart diseases, such as intracardiac shunts, obstructive or regurgitant lesions, transposition physiology, abnormal venous connections, conotruncal anomalies, coronary artery anomalies, functionally univentricular hearts, and/or complex lesions like abnormal laterality (heterotaxy/isomerism). Additionally, specifc syndromes, family histories, and extracardiac abnormalities associated with congenital heart diseases, all necessitate echocardiography, even in the absence of cardiac symptoms. Echocardiograms are also crucial for assessing acquired heart diseases in children, including Kawasaki disease, infective endocarditis, various cardiomyopathies, rheumatic fever and carditis, systemic lupus erythematosus, myocarditis, pericarditis, HIV infection, and/or exposures to cardiotoxic drugs.

## **23.5 Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE)**

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) in children and patients with congenital heart disease (CHD) represents a distinct and crucial discipline. In the past decade, the evolution of pediatric transesophageal ultrasound imaging has signifcantly advanced the diagnoses and treatments of congenital heart diseases in children [11]. While primary diagnostic transesophageal studies are rarely indicated in infants and on unoperated children, the safety and clinical utilities of TEE for various pediatric and adult congenital cardiac surgeries and interventions are well-established [12].

In children, TEE is commonly employed to assess anatomies and functions during cardiac surgery, particularly for those with a known CHD. Nevertheless, preoperative transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is recommended for all children undergoing cardiac surgery, complementing intraoperative TEE by providing additional information unattainable with TEE alone [2, 11]. Intraoperative TEE, performed just before surgery, aids the operative team in reviewing preoperative fndings, identifying any potential new issues, and assessing hemodynamics and myocardial functions just prior to the procedure. TEE has also proven benefcial in catheter placements, real-time monitoring of volume status, and evaluating myocardial functions.

During cardiopulmonary bypass, TEE is instrumental in assessing cannula positions and venous drainage, aiding intracardiac deairing, and characterizing rhythm disturbances. Post-intervention TEE plays a vital role in evaluating the adequacy of surgical repair as well as subsequently assessing ventricular function. However, it is crucial to recognize that postoperative TEE fndings may be infuenced by factors like anesthetic depth, hemodynamic status, and vasoactive drug administrations. When making decisions to return to bypass, the care team should consider not only TEE fndings, but also other clinical information, hemodynamics, and the risk-beneft ratio of re-intervention.

Today, TEE is increasingly being employed in the cardiac catheterization laboratory, providing precise identifcations of septal closure device locations, cardiac geometries, and number of a given patient's defects. This aids in minimizing fuoroscopy times, decreasing contrast loads, and enhancing overall procedural safeties. In the intensive care unit, TEE serves diagnostic purposes for postoperative patients, those with suboptimal transthoracic windows or an open sternum, as well as in managing patients undergoing mechanical circulatory support.

Importantly performing TEE necessitates an understanding of the patient's oropharyngeal anatomy, various endoscopic techniques, as well as awarenesses of indications, contraindications, and associated risks. For example, in small children, appropriate probe selection and supervision of intubation are crucial to mitigate risks.

Today, for TEE probe selections for infants and children, one primarily considers the patient's weight and the probe size [13]. Note, the pediatric mini-multiplane and micromultiplane probes are specifcally designed for neonates, infants, and small children. While manufacturers suggest weight limits, in the hands of the experienced clinician, these probes have been safely used below these recommendations. The adult 2D multiplane TEE probe should be considered for a patient who's weight is >25 kg. The advantage of using an adult sized probe when possible is better image qualities, due to having a greater density of piezoelectric crystals, and the larger size may allow for improved contact with the esophagus. The risk/beneft ratio of oversizing the TEE probe should be considered on a case-by-case basis but may be safe when placed with minimal resistance; see Fig. 23.4.

Echocardiographic evaluations in children and patients with CHD commonly focus on structures rather than views. Given the diversity of CHD, TEE views may need modifcations to discern precise cardiac anatomies. ## **23.6 The Epicardial Echocardiogram**

Today, epicardial echocardiography has emerged as an indispensable tool in the realm of congenital heart surgery; providing crucial visualizations of cardiovascular structures beyond the reach of TEE. Further, intraoperative epicardial echocardiography serves as a valuable alternative when TEE imaging faces challenges, such as: patient size constraints, contraindications to esophageal instrumentation, and/or when patients cannot tolerate TEE imaging due to concerns relative to potential hemodynamic or respiratory compromise [13, 14]. Epicardial imaging becomes particularly essential for overcoming TEE limitations, and also for offering superior imaging of vascular structures like the pulmonary arteries, the aorta, and shunts [15]. Its advantages extend to visualizing anterior structures, such as the right ventricular outfow tract, which may be challenging to capture adequately through the esophagus. It can also prove crucial in patients undergoing interventions for muscular ventricular septal defects through either direct surgical closure or periventricular transcatheter interventions. #### **23.7 Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (CMR) and Computed Tomography (CT)**

Today, cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) and computed tomography (CT) serve as valuable adjuncts to echocardiography in assessing CHD. While both imaging modalities offer whole heart imaging and detailed visualizations of extracardiac vasculatures, each has its own advantages and considerations.

#### **23.7.1 Cardiovascular Magnetic Resonance (CMR): Clinical Factors**

*Listed below are some of the clinical factors one should consider relative to utilizing CMR for the CHD patient:*

- *Advantages:* CMR provides detailed visualizations of thoracic vessels and structures. Also it provides effective illustrations of arch patterns and their relationships to the patient's airways [16]. This imaging modality is not dependent on TTE acoustic windows [2]. The clinician is able to interrogate the heart in any geometry, provides highly accurate assessments of ventricular volumes and fows [17].
- *Radiation-Free:* CMR is free of ionizing radiation, making it a preferred choice, especially in pediatric patients [2].
- *Versatility:* CMR can be employed to interrogate the heart in any geometry, providing comprehensive evaluations of cardiovascular anatomies, functions, and/or tissue characteristics.
- *Accurate and Reproducible:* CMR allows highly accurate and reproducible assessments of ventricular volumes and fows.
- *Considerations:* Provides suboptimal imaging for arrhythmias. Best for patients with an inability to use gadoliniumbased contract agents, due to renal disease [2]. Note, CMR techniques are too slow to image the heart in real time [18].
- *Patient Compliance:* CMR studies require patients to lie still for the study duration; which may be challenging for infants, small children, or those unable to comply, necessitating sedation or general anesthesia.
- *Breath-Hold Challenges:* Some sequences require patient breath-holds, which can be challenging for certain individuals.
- *Noise and Ear Protection:* CMR image acquisition can be noisy, requiring ear protection, and patients are often provided with headphones.

- *Clinical Applications:* CMR perfusion imaging can be used to identify perfusion defects in patients before/after a given surgical intervention. CMR pharmacological stress imaging can be used to assess coronary artery anomalies that are associated with increased risks for myocardial ischemia [2].
- *Comprehensive Assessments:* CMR enables noninvasive assessments of cardiovascular anatomies, ventricular functions, myocardial perfusion, myocardial viabilities, quantitative fow analyses, and tissue imaging.
- *Advances in Imaging:* Rapid high-resolution imaging, 3D spatial assessments, and quantitative evaluations have been facilitated by advances in hardware designs, pulse sequences, and image reconstruction techniques.

#### **23.7.2 Computed Tomography (CT): Clinical Factors**

*Listed below are some of the clinical factors one should consider relative to utilizing CT for the CHD patient:*

- *Advantages:* Today CT provides a higher spatial resolution than MRI, can be used as an alternative imaging tool for other contraindicated imaging. CT can provide information on intracardiac and extracardiac anatomies, coronary arteries, and vascular structures [19]. CTs are also more effective in gathering data relating to muscular structures and blood/tissue interfaces than ultrasounds [20].
- *Spatial Resolutions:* Contrast-enhanced cardiac CT provides high spatial resolutions, aiding to delineate small structures such as coronary arteries effectively.
- *Acquisition Speed:* CT is fast and effcient in producing detailed images.
- *Considerations: With CT there are several noted parameter to consider.*
- *Radiation Exposure:* CT requires ionizing radiation exposure, and thus decisions on modality choice should consider factors like the clinical questions, patient characteristics, and imaging tool availability.
- *Contrast Agent Use:* Contrast agents, often iodine-based, are used for vascular enhancement during CT scans.
- *Clinical Applications: Several to consider with CT in CHD.*
- *Whole Heart Imaging:* CT serves well in providing whole heart imaging and detailed visualization of extracardiac vasculature.
- *Tissue Characterization:* CT can provide valuable information on cardiac function, tissue characterization, and circulatory hemodynamics.

#### **23.7.3 CMR and CT Uses**

In conclusion, the choice between CMR and CT as adjuncts to echocardiography in assessing CHD depends on many factors, such as: the clinical question, patient factors, and/or the specifc diagnostic information required [19]. While CMR offers radiation-free versatility and detailed assessments, CT excels in spatial resolution and speed. Both modalities contribute signifcantly to the comprehensive evaluation of congenital heart conditions.

#### **23.8 High-Resolution 3D Computational Heart Models and Printing**

The integration of 3D printing technology into the feld of cardiology, particularly for congenital heart diseases (CHD), has revolutionized healthcare practices. This innovative approach enables clinicians and surgeons to plan and simulate interventions with unparalleled precision, offering numerous benefts in education, preoperative planning, and patient communication [20].

The process of 3D printing involves several key steps, starting with image acquisition through either employed computed tomography (CT) or cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging. The obtained medical image datasets are commonly stored in DICOM format, are then loaded into one of the available segmentation software for further processing.

#### **23.8.1 Digital Segmentation**

Digital Segmentation is the process referred to, for creating a 3D object based on two-dimensional images separated by a grey-scale thresholds. During such a segmentation, specifc structures of interest within the heart are identifed and isolated using edge-based algorithms. This step results in the creation of fles in Standard Triangle Language (.stl) or Object fle (.obj), which saves the digital 3D model. Current available software so perform segmentation varies from desktop-based to virtual-reality (VR) based applications.

*Mesh Optimization:* After segmentation, the mesh (3D model) may undergo optimization to correct inaccuracies derived from image artifacts or low spatial resolution. Postprocessing software contains tools such as smoothing, noise reduction, shells, etc., that help ensure that the model accurately represents the patient's anatomy.

*Slicing (optional):* Using the post-processing software, the healthcare team can decide to slice the 3D models to optimize the visualization of certain structures (e.g., long axis cut over right ventricle to visualize a Ventricular Septal Defect). Slicing also simplifes the printing process.

#### **23.8.2 Various 3D Printing Techniques**

Today, the two most widely used 3D printing techniques in cardiology are Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) and Polyjet technologies:

#### **Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM):**

- In FDM, thermoplastic flaments are heated to their melting point and extruded layer by layer to create the 3D model.
- FDM is known for producing highly detailed models and prototypes, making it valuable in cardiology.
- The cost-effectiveness and ease of use makes FDM suitable for fast prototyping.

#### **Polyjet Technology:**

- Polyjet involves solidifying liquid polymer layers using ultraviolet (UV) light. It offers the capability to create smooth, accurate, multi-colored, and multi-material models.
- Polyjet is ideal for producing patient-specifc cardiovascular models due to its fexibility and complexity.
- Printing in fexible, and transparent materials allow for 3D orientation.

## **23.8.3 Applications of 3D Printing in Cardiology**

Today, 3D printing has diverse applications in cardiology, including:

- *Educational Tools:* 3D printed models aid in teaching students about cardiac anatomy and pathologies more effciently [20].
- *Preoperative Planning:* Surgeons use 3D printed models to simulate interventions, reducing operative time, anesthesia exposure, and intraoperative blood loss.
- *Patient-Specifc Models:* Customized 3D printed heart models help clinicians communicate effectively with patients and their families, and enhance understanding of cardiovascular conditions.

## **23.8.4 Advantages and Future Implications in 3D Printing for CHD**

Currently, for the clinical applications of 3D printing for the CHD patient, there as several know advantages, including:

- *Tactile Feedback:* 3D printed models provide tactile feedback, allowing realistic anatomical navigation and improving procedural planning. Various types of materials can be used for such prints which will alter degrees of tactile feedback.
- *Optimized Surgical Procedures:* Currently employed 3D printing technologies have been described to contribute to optimized surgical procedures and/or transplants: by providing an in-depth understanding of heart anatomy and disease pathologies.
- *Multimodal Image Acquisition:* Combining multiple imaging modalities can be employed to enhance image resolutions and ultimate model accuracies.
- *Open-Source Tools:* Today, utilizing various available open-source computational modeling tools can be employed to better refne the meshed geometries obtained during high-resolution image segmentations.

In conclusion, the incorporation of 3D printing in cardiology, especially for CHD patients, marks a transformative leap in medical practice. Such technologies' versatility in education, preoperative planning, and patient communication positions them as powerful tools for both improving clinical care and advancing cardiovascular treatments: see Fig. 23.6.

This segmented digital library of patient-specifc models serves as a valuable resource for teaching cardiac morphology, heart development, and CHD.

In conclusion, the incorporation of virtual reality into the feld of congenital heart diseases has the potential to revolutionize medical practice, education, and patient understanding. As technology continues to advance, the impact of VR on diagnostic imaging tools and our comprehension of complex cardiac pathologies is expected to grow, leading to more robust, patient-centered, and safe clinical care [22]. For additional information on this topic see Chap. 48.

#### **23.10 Summary**

Recent and ongoing technological advancements of employed imaging modalities for assessing CHD, including (1) fetal echocardiography, (2) the transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), (3) transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE), (4) epicardial echocardiography, (5) cardiac computed tomography (CT), (6) cardiac MRI, (7) 3-dimensional (3D) computational cardiac modeling and printing and (8) more recently, virtual reality (VR). These provide expanding capabilities to better characterize each patient's unique presentation of CHD. This information can in turn be integrated into more precise diagnoses and management of CHD patients. The above-discussed clinical examples of integrating multimodality imaging for such patients provide the readers with important insights into how such technologies can be employed. We also described the integrations of these technologies to create more optimal clinical workfows, which in turn empowers healthcare teams, enhances clinical value as well as guides the education and training of healthcare professionals and the given patient and families.
