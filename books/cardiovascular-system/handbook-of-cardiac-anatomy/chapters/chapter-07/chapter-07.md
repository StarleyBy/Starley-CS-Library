#### **Abstract**

The blood pressure generated by the heart is an essential feature of life; beating ~2–3 billion times during an average lifetime, the heart is a wonder of evolution. The overall contractile performance of the heart depends upon: (1) the delivery of blood containing carbon substrates and oxygen through the coronary circulation to cardiomyocytes; (2) the ability of cardiomyocytes to effciently extract these substrates from the extracellular space, and (3) the pathways via which the chemical energy stored within the carbon substrates is converted into mechanical energy, which pressurizes and moves blood through the arterial circulation. Importantly, demands placed on the heart are highly dynamic, and, thus, the mechanisms of energy conversion must be tightly regulated to maintain cytosolic and intraorganelle homeostasis. The latter characteristic is crucial if the performances of the contractile apparatus and intracellular organelles are to remain optimal over the broad range of cardiac work states required by a physically active human. Critically, in contrast to skeletal muscles that undergo fatigue and decreased contractile performance after strenuous work, the heart, even at a high rate of myocardial energy expenditure, must not become fatigued as declines in cardiac contractile performances can be lifethreatening. This chapter describes the ways in which the chemical energy stored in ingested carbon substrates (primarily glucose and fatty acids) is converted into adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and reviews some of the regulatory systems that integrate the function of these pathways and provide responsiveness to changes in ATP demand in proportion to changes in cardiac workload without destabilizing the intracellular milieu. #### **7.1 Introduction**

In the interest of putting the subject of *myocardial metabolism* into a broader perspective, the heart, and all other organs, can be thought of as ultimately *solar-powered*. Virtually all energy-driven biological processes in our biosphere are ultimately dependent upon the energy contained in the photons radiated by the sun. Photons are captured by plants, and their energy is used to synthesize energy-rich carbon molecules and oxygen that are critical for many biological processes (Fig. 7.1). In green plants, a portion of the solar photon energy is frst trapped by chlorophyll and then stored in the form of highly energetic chemical bonds, which are used to break the bonds of water and carbon dioxide (CO2) to generate molecular oxygen and carbohydrates such as glucose (C6H12O6).

Animals are unable to directly convert solar photon energy into a storage form of chemical energy, so simple and complex animal life is ultimately powered by photosynthesis in plants and some algae and bacteria. The ingestion of plants and/or animals containing energy-rich molecules supplies animals with carbohydrates, fatty acids, and amino acids that can be metabolized to support the generation of ATP, which, in turn, powers nearly all cellular functions.

#### **7.2.1 Regulation of Myocardial Blood Flow**

Coronary blood fow is highly regulated and either falls or rises appropriately in response to subtle or large changes in myocardial energy expenditure. The principal function of the heart is to generate needed pressures that propel blood from the ventricles into the pulmonary and systemic circulations. Elevations of the cardiac rate of ATP expenditure during exercise or other stresses increase myocardial demand for oxygen and carbon substrates, which necessitates increases in blood fow. In clinical practice, assessing the effects of changes of the rates of myocardial energy expenditure on cardiac performance (e.g., during exercise stress testing) is a means to test the relative regulation of cardiac blood fow when workload increases.

The coronary circulation operates on the principle of "just-in-time" delivery of oxygen and carbon substrates. In other words, coronary blood fow is regulated to be only minimally greater than required to meet the instantaneous metabolic demands of the heart. Furthermore, the heart extracts 70–80% of O2 from the blood as it fows through the coronary capillaries. Because of this high level of basal oxygen extraction, there is little ability to increase oxygen uptake by means of increased extraction of oxygen from the blood. As a result, increases in myocardial energy requirement during exercise or other stresses must be satisfed by concomitant increases in coronary blood fow. As noted, ATP and oxygen levels in the myocardium are relatively small. Therefore, the response times for increases in coronary fow following an increase in cardiac work must be rapid (i.e., a few seconds). The coupling of myocardial demand with circulatory supply is achieved by a responsive signaling system that links the rate of myocardial energy expenditure to vasomotor activities in the resistance vessels that control coronary blood fow. While many of the major components have been identifed, signifcant questions remain regarding this highly integrated system.

# **7.2.3 Blood Flow in the Diseased Heart**

Coronary blood fow in the diseased heart can be limited by: (1) partial or complete obstruction of the large coronary arteries (e.g., atherosclerotic disease); (2) decreased responsiveness of the signaling systems relating blood fow to energy requirements; and/or (3) increases in extravascular mechanical forces acting to compress the small vessels in the wall of the left ventricle. Partial or complete obstructions are generally caused by atherosclerotic disease of the epicardial coronary arteries. Atherosclerosis is described as an infammatory lesion that frequently develops in arteries with signifcant turbulent blood fow and is particularly damaging to the coronary and carotid arteries. A moderately narrowed vessel may not cause problems at rest but only restricts blood fow during periods of increased blood fow demand, as relaxation of arterioles can compensate for obstruction in larger arteries. However, when the capacity for vasodilation of the arterioles is exhausted, any further increase in cardiac work cannot induce an increase in blood fow and the myocardium supplied by the narrowed epicardial vessel will become ischemic. A suffciently severe narrowing of a coronary vessel may limit blood fow even when the subject is at rest. Coronary atherosclerosis is most damaging when the endothelium covering the lesion is breached, leading to the formation of an intravascular embolism that can break free and resulting in the catastrophic complete blockage of downstream arteries and in the cessation of blood fow to large portions of the myocardium.

As noted earlier, in the heart, blood fow to the inner layers of the left ventricle occurs principally during diastole. This is because tissue pressures in the wall of the left ventricle during systole are so great that inner layer arterioles are squeezed shut by the extravascular compressive forces produced by cardiac contraction. Diastolic left ventricular tissue pressures are also greatest in the subendocardium. When the heart fails and/or becomes hypertrophied, these extravascular compressive forces increase as left ventricular flling pressure increases. In the hypertrophied or failing heart, slowing of myocyte relaxation also shortens the duration of the diastolic interval and thus limits coronary fow reserve in the inner cardiac layers. In the normal heart, autoregulatory (i.e., metabolic vasodilatation) processes cause enough arteriolar vasodilation in the subendocardium to compensate for systolic underperfusion. However, increased left ventricular diastolic pressure (thereby, myocardial tissue pressure) in the failing heart may compress the arteriolar bed in the inner myocardial layers suffciently to overwhelm autoregulatory mechanisms, particularly those that normally maintain adequate subendocardial blood fow. Since subendocardial blood fow occurs almost exclusively during diastole, tachycardia also acts to impede blood fow in the subendocardium by shortening the diastolic interval. Thus, even in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease or intrinsic arteriolar abnormalities, increased arteriolar compression can limit blood fow to the inner myocardial layers of the diseased heart. Importantly, if these abnormalities limit substrate delivery to the inner myocardial layers, they will disrupt the balance between ATP synthetic capacity and ATP utilization in this region of the ventricular wall. Thus, the extravascular forces cause the subendocardium to be the region of the ventricular wall that is most vulnerable to hypoperfusion and ischemia.

## **7.3 Intermediary Metabolism and Bioenergetics in the Normal Heart**

This section will review the metabolic pathways that ultimately supply the heart with ATP (Fig. 7.4). Glucose and fatty acids are the main substrates consumed by the heart, with fatty acid consumption predominating under most circumstances.

### **7.3.1 Glucose Metabolism**

Glucose enters cardiomyocytes via the sarcolemmal glucose transporter protein GLUT1 (which is insulin-independent) or GLUT4 (which is insulin-dependent). Once in the cell, glucose is phosphorylated to glucose 6-phosphate by the enzyme hexokinase, and, from here, glucose can take several diverse metabolic paths. Glucose 6-phosphate can enter the glycogen synthesis pathway to form glycogen, a macromolecular polymeric storage form of glucose that is not only abundant in the liver and skeletal muscles but is also present in the heart. Another fate is the pentose monophosphate shunt that generates nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) and ribose that are necessary for many important cellular processes. Finally, and central to our current discussion, it can undergo molecular rearrangement via the enzyme phosphohexose isomerase to form fructose 6-phosphate and continue through a series of enzymatic reactions that make up glycolysis (Fig. 7.4). A second phosphorylation of fructose 6-phosphate via phosphofructokinase generates fructose 1,6-bisphosphate. Each of these phosphorylation steps consumes one molecule of ATP. Fructose 1,6-bisphosphate is next split into glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate by the enzyme aldolase. These two molecules are in a constant state of exchange through a reversible reaction catalyzed by the enzyme triosephosphate isomerase. Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate is then phosphorylated to form 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate by the enzyme glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase. This reaction also simultaneously reduces NAD+ to NADH as the aldehyde group is oxidized to carboxylic acid. In the next cytosolic reaction, the high-energy phosphate bond in 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate is transferred to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) to form ATP and 3-phosphoglycerate via the enzyme phosphoglycerate kinase. The latter molecule is converted into 2-phosphoglycerate by the enzyme phosphoglycerate mutase. Enolase, another cytosolic enzyme, then converts 2-phosphoglycerate into phosphoenolpyruvate. The latter is converted to pyruvate by the enzyme pyruvate kinase. During this reaction, the ~P in phosphoenolpyruvate is transferred to ADP to form another ATP molecule. Pyruvate can then enter the mitochondrial matrix to be further metabolized by the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex of enzymes. The PDH complex converts pyruvate to acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA), releasing a CO2 molecule and reducing NAD+ to NADH. Acetyl-CoA then enters the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which is also located within the mitochondrial matrix (Fig. 7.5).

There are instances in which the mitochondrial metabolism of pyruvate is limited, for example under low oxygen conditions. This causes a backup in the glycolytic pathways, resulting in the depletion of cytosolic NAD+. Under these circumstances, pyruvate can be converted into lactic acid by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). Lactate and a proton (H+) are then exported from the myocyte via the monocarboxylic acid transporter. This pyruvate to lactic acid reaction is associated with the oxidation of NADH to NAD+ and is critical to maintaining glycolysis when the availability of oxygen to cardiomyocytes is limited. Conversely, under aerobic conditions, lactate in the blood can be transported into cardiomyocytes by the same monocarboxylic acid transporter and converted into pyruvate by LDH. This LDH-catalyzed reaction reduces NAD+ to NADH.

## **7.3.2 Fatty Acid Metabolism**

Figure 7.4 also presents a fowchart for fatty acid oxidation, also known as β-oxidation. Dietary long-chain, non-esterifed free fatty acids (NEFAs) are generally the predominant carbon substrate in the normal myocardium. Lipids are transported in the blood in the form of NEFAs or triglycerides bound to plasma albumin or are incorporated into lipoproteins. The latter can be broken down to release NEFAs by the lipoprotein lipase present at the surface of the capillaries and cardiomyocytes. NEFAs are transported into cardiomyocytes by sarcolemmal fatty acid transport proteins (e.g., CD36). Within the cell, NEFAs are bound to fatty acid-binding proteins, which provide solubility and intracellular transport. Once in the cell, NEFAs are either re-esterifed and stored as triglycerides in lipid droplets or sent directly to mitochondria to be metabolized. Fatty acids are metabolized by β-oxidation, which begins when the enzyme acyl-CoA synthetase uses the energy from ATP hydrolysis to attach a coenzyme A (CoA) molecule via a thioester linkage to the fatty acid to form a long-chain acyl-CoA. Because the long-chain acyl-CoA cannot readily diffuse through the mitochondrial inner membrane and thus requires a transport mechanism, a complex of proteins transport the acyl-CoA into the mitochondria through a carnitine-bound intermediate. The long-chain acyl-CoA is regenerated within the mitochondrial matrix. It is then processed by a sequence of enzyme-catalyzed reactions that progressively oxidize the third carbon in the acyl chain. With each cycle, two carbons are released in the form of acetyl-CoA. In addition, this process produces a reduced NADH and ubiquinol, both of which contribute to ATP production. If the long-chain acyl-CoA has an even number of carbon atoms, then the fnal products of the last cycle will be two acetyl-CoA molecules. However, if it has an odd number of carbon atoms, then the fnal products of the last cycle will be one acetyl-CoA and one propionyl-CoA. Unlike acetyl-CoA, propionyl-CoA cannot enter the TCA cycle directly but is readily converted into succinyl-CoA, a TCA cycle intermediate, allowing it to contribute to energy production by maintaining a TCA cycle intermediate pool size. The products of complete β-oxidation of a fatty acid are acetyl-CoA and the occasional propionyl-CoA if the fatty acid has an odd number of carbons, NADH, and ubiquinol. However, in the absence of oxygen, both NADH and ubiquinol will accumulate, causing the whole process of β-oxidation to come to a halt. As such, β-oxidation cannot occur under anoxic or ischemic conditions, and a markedly ischemic myocardium does not support either pyruvate- or fatty acidderived ATP synthesis. Hence, during ischemia, the only remaining source of ATP synthesis is anaerobic glycolysis.

## **7.3.3 The Tricarboxylic Acid (TCA) Cycle**

Acetyl-CoA is the carbon substrate consumed by the TCA cycle with both carbons released as fully oxidized CO2. As already discussed, when oxygen is available, acetyl-CoA is produced in the mitochondrial matrix through pyruvatederived glycolysis or β-oxidation of fatty acids. Figure 7.5 shows the individual reactions of the TCA cycle. In the frst step of the cycle, acetyl-CoA is combined with oxaloacetate to form citrate and releases CoA; this reaction is catalyzed by the enzyme citrate synthase. Citrate then enters a sequence of reactions that ultimately generate two molecules of CO2 and four reducing equivalents in the form of three molecules of NADH and one direct synthesis of ubiquinol. One highenergy phosphate molecule (guanosine triphosphate, GTP) is also produced, which can be directly utilized or converted into ATP. During this process, the six-carbon citrate is stepwise decarboxylated and ultimately converted back into oxaloacetate, a four-carbon molecule, which, when condensed with a new acetyl-CoA, reinitiates the sequence of reactions just described. The NADH delivers its electrons to complex I of the electron transport chain (ETC).

#### **7.3.4 The Mitochondrion**

Mitochondria are central to the metabolism of most mammalian cells, and this is especially true for cardiomyocytes, in which mitochondria account for about a third of the total cellular volume. Mitochondria contain the β-oxidation pathway enzymes, the TCA cycle enzymes, the electron transport chain, and ATP synthase (also called F1F0-ATPase). In cardiomyocytes, mitochondria are located in rows intertwined between the bundles of myoflaments and in close approximation to the membranes of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR). This positioning is ideal for providing ATP to subcellular domains that drive much of the energy demand of the cell. As a result of an evolutionarily defned symbiosis, mitochondria are housed within an outer membrane, which provides a degree of separation from the cytosolic compartment but allows for the free fow of metabolites. The inner mitochondrial membrane is a remarkably complex membranous system containing the components of the electron transport chain, ATP synthase, the adenine nucleotide translocator (ANT), and other transporters. In cells with high energy demands, the inner mitochondrial membrane is highly folded to form cristae structures that function to maximize the surface area available for ATP synthase. The mitochondrial matrix contains the TCA cycle enzymes, β-oxidation enzymes, and many other enzymes and reactants. The primary function of these matrix proteins is to provide electron equivalents to the electron transport chain and offer protection against reactive side reactions of respiration. Between the inner and outer mitochondrial membranes is the intermembrane space containing creatine kinase, which is important for high-energy phosphate transport out of the mitochondria, and cytochrome *c*, a component of the electron transport chain. The importance of the intermembrane space to oxidative ATP synthesis will be discussed subsequently. See also Chap. 6 for additional discussions on cardiomyocyte cell biology.

This means that if the rate of fatty acid utilization is slower than the uptake rate, cardiomyocytes will accumulate fatty acids. The latter is mainly stored as triglycerides in lipid droplets. Excess lipid accumulation can be damaging to cardiomyocytes and other cells as well. A second regulatory site is at the level of long-chain fatty acid transport into mitochondria. As discussed above, long-chain acyl-CoA must be converted into long-chain acyl carnitine at the outer mitochondrial membrane by the enzyme carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1. This enzyme is inhibited by malonyl-CoA derived from cytosolic acetyl-CoA, which is synthesized from citrate exported from the mitochondrial matrix. Citrate export occurs in conditions corresponding to periods of plentiful carbon substrates in the matrix. Cytosolic citrate also accelerates the conversion of acetyl-CoA into malonyl-CoA via allosteric activation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase. The net effect of increased malonyl-CoA synthesis is to limit fatty acid uptake and utilization by mitochondria. This explains why lactate and exogenously supplied pyruvate can compete successfully with fatty acids for oxidation by mitochondria. An increased cardiac work state or ischemia often results in increased AMP levels, leading to activation of AMPK, which can directly inhibit acetyl-CoA carboxylase and thereby reduce malonyl-CoA levels. This relieves inhibition of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 and facilitates fatty acid entry into mitochondria. The rate of mitochondrial fatty acid or pyruvate oxidation, assuming no limitation of transport into mitochondria, is ultimately controlled by the rate of consumption of the products of these metabolic pathways. # **7.3.7 Myocardial Carbon Substrate Selection**

Although the primary carbon substrates taken up and metabolized by the myocardium are free fatty acids and glucose (Fig. 7.4), the heart also readily takes up and metabolizes pyruvate, lactate, ketone bodies, and amino acids. However, the relatively low blood concentrations of these substrates and the normal availability of glucose and fatty acids limit their utilization. In contrast, during intense exercise that is associated with a marked elevation of blood lactate content or during periods when blood levels of ketone bodies are elevated, the utilization of these substrates increases markedly, whereas that of glucose and fatty acids is decreased. A more detailed discussion of the regulation of blood levels of these substrates exceeds the scope of this chapter, yet a few orienting comments are appropriate regarding glucose and fatty acid utilization patterns. Blood glucose levels are maintained within a narrow range (~4–5 mM) in nondiabetic subjects. Glucose homeostasis is a balance between the alimentary uptake of glucose, its release from the liver, in which it can be synthesized de novo or liberated from the glycogen storage pool, and its removal from the blood by various organs. As noted, glucose uptake is strongly infuenced by insulin, a hormone secreted by specialized cells in the pancreas that enhances glucose transport into the cells of some tissues, notably skeletal and cardiac muscle.

The heart has often been called an *omnivore* because of its capacity to consume virtually any available carbon substrate, either directly or following processing. As already noted, glucose is transported into cardiomyocytes by a family of sarcolemmal glucose transport proteins; of these, the predominant transporter (GLUT4) is insulin-dependent. Fatty acids enter myocytes via sarcolemmal fatty acid transport proteins, while lactate and pyruvate are taken up by the sarcolemmal monocarboxylic acid transporter. Utilization of glucose by the heart is largely regulated by the availabilities of fatty acids, glucose, and insulin in the blood. In the fasted state, glucose levels are generally normal, but insulin secretion is modest and blood fatty acid levels, released from the liver and adipose tissue, are high. Hence, fatty acids are the predominant cardiac substrate despite normal blood glucose levels. In contrast, during vigorous exercise, blood lactate levels can rise markedly, as lactate is a by-product of skeletal muscle glycolysis, which is markedly enhanced by exercise. Lactate competes favorably with the myocardial metabolism of fatty acids and glucose, despite the presence of substantial blood levels of the latter substrates, and much more lactate is consumed by the heart.

In contrast, after a high-carbohydrate meal, blood glucose levels rise, eliciting insulin secretion. Increased insulin levels stimulate glucose uptake by the heart, skeletal muscles, and certain other tissues and also cause blood fatty acid levels to decrease by stimulating their uptake by the liver and adipose tissue. As a result, myocardial glucose consumption increases and fatty acid consumption decreases. Of interest, lactate and pyruvate are the most favored substrates when the relative blood concentrations of all the substrates are experimentally equalized. Of major importance, under physiological conditions, the switching of carbon substrates *does not* affect myocardial performance.

A recent study using metabolomics to quantify cardiac uptake and release of metabolites in humans has found that the heart obtained most carbons from free fatty acids, as expected. Notably, ~15% of the carbon uptake consisted of ketones; this proportion increased in patients with heart failure with a reduced ejection fraction, proportionally to circulating levels. These data suggest that the consumption of ketones is driven by substrate availability, which is encouraging given the recent interest in ketones as a fuel source for the failing heart.

## **7.3.8 Regulation of the TCA Cycle, Electron Transport Chain, and Oxidative Phosphorylation**

The rate of ATP production is dependent on the free energy of ATP hydrolysis, which is defned by the local concentrations of ADP, phosphate, and ATP. In the simplest form, this dependence on the equilibrium provides a means to increase ATP production in response to changes in ATP hydrolysis. In the in vivo animal heart, the overall ADP levels remain fairly constant, even when increased cardiac workload drives increased rates of ATP utilization, leading to substantial increases in myocardial oxygen consumption. However, this does not mean that ADP availability is not crucial to the rate of ATP synthesis. When the rate of ATP expenditure is increased in the normal myocardium, there is an obligatory demand to increase the rate of ADP delivery to mitochondria to maintain a steady state. The important concept here is that the rates of ADP and phosphate delivery to the mitochondrial matrix defne the local equilibrium conditions that facilitate increased ATP synthesis. Thus, the availability of ADP remains a critical regulator of ATP synthesis during periods of increased workload, despite the fact that changes in *steady-state* ADP levels are not observed.

Coupling ATP production with ATP utilization is essential for the heart to perform its contractile function over a wide range of workloads. The regulatory processes matching ATP supply and demand must have extremely rapid response times to ensure suffcient ATP synthesis in response to increased workload. This is necessary because the low concentrations of ATP relative to its rate of consumption mean that delays in ATP synthesis can quickly result in declines in ATP concentrations and in the energy liberated by ATP hydrolysis, which can impair cardiac performance. An important aspect of quickly matching supply and demand is to provide for rapid transit of chemical energy from the mitochondria to the sites of ATP hydrolysis. This is accomplished through the transfer of the chemical energy in the terminal phosphate of ATP to a molecule of creatine, resulting in the formation of creatine phosphate. The reaction converting cytosolic ATP to phosphocreatine is rapid and near equilibrium via the enzyme creatine kinase, which is located both within the mitochondrial intermembrane space and near the ATPases in the cytosol. The phosphocreatine shuttle (Fig. 7.7) is used in tissues that are subject to rapid changes in ATP consumption, namely, the brain, skeletal muscles, and the heart. The primary advantage of transferring this chemical energy to creatine is that creatine phosphate diffuses an order of magnitude faster than ATP in water. This difference is likely magnifed within the densely packed cytosol, where ATP's highly charged triple phosphate tail and adenosine head are likely to interact with a large number of proteins. Figure 7.7 provides a brief overview of the ~P and ADP transport function of phosphocreatine.

The transient increase in cytosolic Ca2+ is central to myocardial contraction, as detailed elsewhere in this book and briefy in Fig. 7.9. The elevation of Ca2+ initiates a series of events that result in increased ATP hydrolysis, and the nature of the transient increase in Ca2+ is dynamic and changes in response to exercise. During exercise, increased norepinephrine release from sympathetic nerve fbers and increased epinephrine from the adrenal glands activate β-adrenergic receptors in cardiomyocytes. This receptor activation causes an increase in Ca2+ entry into the cytosol with every beat by increasing the open probability of sarcolemmal voltagedependent Ca2+ channels. Cytosolic Ca2+ levels are further augmented by the increased heart rate, thus increasing the number of Ca2+ channel openings/minute. In addition to increased Ca2+ infux, β-adrenergic receptor stimulation increases the rate of Ca2+ sequestration by the sarcoplasmic/ endoplasmic reticular Ca2+ ATPase (SERCA) pump by removing the inhibition of the protein phospholamban. The more fully loaded sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR), the predominant source of "contraction activation" Ca2+, can then release more Ca2+ per beat via the ryanodine receptor into the cytosol. The larger systolic cytosolic Ca2+ transients that follow adrenergic stimulation and/or increased heart rate then generate increased force and sarcomere shortening.

The central role of Ca2+ in mediating cardiac contractions makes it a key component in the matching of ATP supply to the heart's highly variable demand. The release of SR Ca2+ into the cytosol initiates ATPase activities associated with both contraction and relaxation. Concurrently, in microdomains where the SR and mitochondria are adjacent, some of this released Ca2+ enters the mitochondrial matrix, where it, in turn, stimulates greater ATP production. This feed-forward mechanism elegantly posits that the signal to consume ATP and the signal to produce it are the same molecule, i.e., Ca2+. In response to higher average cytosolic Ca2+ levels, mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake increases, resulting in increased mitochondrial matrix Ca2+, which has multiple effects on oxidative phosphorylation. The TCA cycle has three ratelimiting enzymes (PDH, isocitrate dehydrogenase, and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase), and the activities of several of these enzymes are enhanced by increased mitochondrial matrix Ca2+ levels (Fig. 7.10). Cytosolic Ca2+ reaches the intermembrane space through a voltage-dependent anion channel (VDAC) and is then transported into the mitochondrial matrix through the mitochondrial Ca2+ uniporter (MCU), a multi-protein complex in the inner mitochondrial membrane (Fig. 7.11). Mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake has long been observed, but the molecular identity of MCU, the poreforming component of the uniporter, was revealed in 2011. Subsequent studies have identifed a large number of proteins that regulate mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, including MCUb, EMRE, and the MICU family of Ca2+-sensing proteins in the intermembrane space (MICU1, MICU2, and MICU3). The relative stoichiometry of these proteins determines the kinetics of mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake, including the minimum cytosolic Ca2+ concentration threshold required for the uniporter to become active. In terms of substrate levels, the Ca2+-mediated acceleration of TCA cycle enzymatic reaction rates, in turn, increases the rates of acetyl-CoA entry into and fux through the TCA cycle. This acceleration occurs without requiring pyruvate, acetyl-CoA levels, or immediate substrate levels of the TCA cycle pool to rise, provided that the rates of delivery of acetyl-CoA are increased suffciently to support the increased TCA cycle fux rate. A major consequence of an increased TCA cycle fux is the more rapid generation of mitochondrial NADH, resulting in direct and indirect syntheses of ubiquinol. Thus, the steady-state levels of electron donors are maintained or increased despite the elevated rate of electron transfer into the electron transport chain. Hence, stimulation of the electron transport chain fuxes is facilitated by an increased rate of reducing equivalent generation. Increased rates of electron transport chain fuxes will then result in an increased rate of H+ pumping by the electron transport chain. This maintains the electrochemical gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane at a level adequate to support the increased rate of H+ fux through ATP synthase, which is required to increase ATP production. A direct activating effect of mitochondrial matrix Ca2+ on ATP synthase has been proposed, though the exact mechanism remains debated. The maintenance of the electrochemical potential across the inner mitochondrial membrane during periods of increased ATP demand allows ATP synthase to increase the rate of ATP production without requiring ADP levels to rise. Hence, so long as the rate of ADP delivery to mitochondria increases appropriately, ADP levels can remain reasonably stable. The electrical potential difference across the inner mitochondrial membrane further facilitates the electrogenic exchange of ATP, which has a −4 charge, with ADP, which has a −3 charge; thus, the mitochondrial membrane potential favors delivery of ADP into the matrix while increasing ATP effux. Maintaining ADP concentration is crucial because elevated ADP levels decrease the free energy stored in ATP, resulting in direct effects on contractile protein function and the ability of ion pumps to generate gradients that are essential for cellular function. Matching ATP supply and demand requires that the activation of carbon substrate metabolic pathways, the TCA cycle, the electron transport chain, and ATP synthase is coordinated and that the control of respiration is driven by both *the rate of ATP utilization and the rate of delivery of ADP* to the mitochondrial matrix.

To summarize, these observations support the concept that increases in average cardiomyocyte cytosolic Ca2+ levels, such as those seen during exercise, act as an important feed-forward signal for oxidative phosphorylation. This signal stimulates, in a parallel manner, ATP utilization by the contractile processes and ATP synthesis by the mitochondria. The net effects of these regulatory mechanisms facilitate fuxes of basic food-derived carbon substrates through the metabolic sequences without requiring the cytosolic concentrations of the initial and intermediate substrates (including ADP) to increase. The rapid response times of this entire system also allow ATP levels to remain relatively constant despite large and rapid fuctuations in the rate of ATP utilization. Critically, these mechanisms ensure that the ADP and ATP levels and the ATP/ADP ratio remain stable during periods of increased ATP demands, thus maintaining the free energy (ΔGATP) released by hydrolysis of the terminal highenergy phosphate bond of ATP in the face of increased rates of ATP use. Metabolic control theory, as applied to the regulation of oxidative phosphorylation, glycolysis, β-oxidation, and the TCA cycle, has been the subject of detailed study. For further discussion of this topic, refer to the references listed at the end of this chapter.

## **7.3.9 Toxic By-products Generated by Mitochondria and Other Cellular Moieties That Impact Energy Generation and Contraction**

Carbon substrate metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, and a number of other cellular processes produce potentially toxic by-products (Fig. 7.12). The descriptor "potentially" is employed because a number of these by-products (when generated at low levels) can exert useful infuences on intracellular signaling pathways; in contrast, when generated at high levels, they are highly toxic. As a generalization, these moieties can induce modifcations of proteins, lipids, and carbohydrates, which can alter the function of these molecules in diverse ways, including the stimulation and/or disruption of their normal function. Some examples of the "toxic by-products" included in this category are highly reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (ROS and RNS, respectively), hydrogen sulfde (H2S), carbon monoxide (CO), nitric oxide (NO), and others. Consequently, the heart and all other tissues have evolved strong defense systems at sites of generation of these reactive species (i.e., in the cytosol, mitochondria, and other organelles). The single electron reduction of oxygen forms a superoxide radical (O2 <sup>−</sup>), arguably the most common ROS species. O2 <sup>−</sup> is produced by the mitochondrial electron transport chain and by NADPH oxidases located in the plasma membrane, SR, and peroxisomes as well as other intracellular sources. Although O2 <sup>−</sup> is highly reactive and oxidizes the iron–sulfur (Fe-S) centers in proteins, it does not cross lipid membranes and is relatively unstable. If not quickly metabolized, the latter can then be rapidly converted into the hydroxyl radical, a highly toxic entity capable of oxidizing C–H bonds, resulting in lipid peroxidation. Additionally, O2 − interacts with NO to form peroxynitrite, another highly toxic molecule. Somewhat paradoxically, low concentrations of H2O2 and other reactive molecules can act to modulate a number of intracellular molecular signaling pathways, and their absence can result in a reductive stress that leads to cardiac dysfunction (Fig. 7.12). However, at high concentrations, these reactive species are quite toxic and interfere in many important processes required for cellular homeostasis. For example, ROS are generated in huge quantities during the postischemic reperfusion period and substantially add to the injury accrued during the ischemic period (see later discussion of the effects of ischemia on the myocardium).

A large variety of mechanisms to protect against the chemical damage inficted by ROS exist in nearly all cell types, but are especially important in cardiomyocytes and skeletal muscle cells, which have the potential to generate excessive levels of O2 − and its toxic derivatives. As mentioned above, the superoxide dismutase isoforms, strategically located in the interstitial space, cytoplasm, and within mitochondria, convert O2 − into H2O2. In turn, H2O2 is converted to water by catalase, another protective enzyme that is widely distributed within cells, and by members of the peroxiredoxin and glutathione peroxidase families within the mitochondria. The rapid degradation of H2O2 limits the formation of the highly reactive hydroxyl radical. In experimental models, augmenting ROS defenses by transgenic techniques or by administration of small-molecule scavengers of ROS has been shown to protect against ROS-induced damage occurring during the postischemic reperfusion period. However, it should be noted that many animal studies and large clinical trials involving ingestion of substantial doses of antioxidants (e.g., vitamin E, *N*-acetyl cysteine, etc.) have been negative, possibly because the antioxidant molecules do not reach the intracellular site of ROS generation and/or because they may ablate the effects of benefcial signaling induced by low levels of ROS generation. There are also small molecules present in the cytosol and mitochondria, which can nonenzymatically scavenge the reactive species.

Another defense against the deleterious effects of reactive species is to reverse the chemical "damage" they induce. There are a number of coupled enzyme/substrate systems that combat oxidative stress. One cogent example of ROSinduced damage is the sequential oxidation of the sulfur atoms contained in the amino acid cysteine, which often plays important structural and/or catalytic roles in proteins. Under these circumstances, another enzyme, glutaredoxin, uses glutathione, an SH (sulfhydryl) group-containing molecule that is abundant in many cells including cardiomyocytes, as a cofactor and reduces the oxidized sulfur atoms on the protein through oxidation of glutathione. The oxidized glutathione is then reduced back to its native form by another enzyme, glutathione reductase. Notably, if the sulfur atom undergoes multistage oxidation due to interaction with ROS, the process may then not be reversible by antioxidative defenses.

Within limits, irreversibly modifed proteins and other molecules can be replaced by cellular synthetic processes. However, if large numbers of these molecules are damaged by an oxidative insult, then repair capacity will be exceeded, potentially leading to cell death. Taken together, the oxidant defenses of the *normal* cardiomyocyte are extremely effective and are highly protective over a wide range of myocardial work states. However, they are clearly inadequate to protect the diseased myocardium, which is often subjected to quite severe oxidant stresses induced by various types of pathological processes.

# **7.4 Metabolism in the Diseased Myocardium**

### **7.4.1 The Ischemic Myocardium**

The most common cause of inadequate myocardial ATP synthesis is ischemia, and the most frequent cause of myocardial ischemia is occlusive disease of the major coronary arteries. *Demand ischemia* occurs when narrowed coronary arteries conduct suffcient blood to sustain aerobic metabolism in the myocardium during basal levels of physical activity, but the degree of narrowing limits the increases in blood fow that normally occur during the increased demands for ATP production during exercise or other stress. This phenomenon is the basis of exercise- or stress-induced angina pectoris (i.e., heart-associated chest pain). Patients with this pathophysiology are asymptomatic at rest or during mild exertion, but when the work of the heart exceeds the ability of a narrowed coronary artery to meet the increased blood fow required to support the increased rate of ATP demand, ischemia ensues and the patient experiences chest pain. When this happens, the patient will typically discontinue exercise, and during the rest period, the cardiac work and ATP synthetic needs fall to levels at which the narrowed coronary artery can meet the blood fow demand; hence, ischemia is relieved. See also Chap. 40 for a discussion on the treatment of coronary artery disease.

In contrast, *supply ischemia* occurs when the coronary artery narrowing is so severe that it limits blood fow under baseline ATP demand. Supply ischemia is generally caused by an acute narrowing or abrupt complete closure of coronary vessels and may cause myocardial infarction. Less commonly, supply ischemia can occur as the result of coronary artery spasm, in which inappropriate (pathological) vasoconstriction of a major coronary artery causes transient total (or near total) occlusion of blood fow. Although the nature of myocardial ischemia is qualitatively similar in supply and demand ischemia, supply ischemia is generally more clinically severe. Because of the greater vulnerability of the inner myocardial layers of the left ventricle to blood fow reduction, mild ischemia is usually confned to the subendocardium. However, as the coronary obstruction becomes more severe, ischemia proceeds as a wavefront from the subendocardium to the subepicardium until the full myocardial wall is involved.

When myocardial blood fow is inadequate, the limited availability of both oxygen and carbon substrates may limit the ATP synthetic rate, but because of the local stores of glycogen and lipid droplets, oxygen defciency is most critical. Consequently, when coronary blood fow is limited, the severity of the induced oxygen defcit determines the extent of limitation of oxidative phosphorylation and secondarily enhances the rate of glycogen breakdown and glycolysis. Depending on the severity of the oxygen defcit, the movement of electrons within the electron transport chain slows or stops. This is because electrons cannot be transferred from cytochrome oxidase to molecular oxygen (the terminal electron acceptor), resulting in the accumulation of reduced cytochrome *c*. The unavailability of oxidized cytochrome *c* to accept electrons causes a backup within complex III, increasing the concentrations of ubiquinone within the inner mitochondrial membrane. This blocks the activity of both complex I and succinate dehydrogenase (complex II), leading to the accumulations of NADH and succinate, respectively. The pumping of H+ from the mitochondrial matrix into the intermembrane space stops because it depends on the energy liberated as electrons pass through the electron transport chain on their way to reducing oxygen within cytochrome oxidase. As a result, the protonmotive force across the inner mitochondrial membrane begins to dissipate and mitochondrial ATP synthesis fails. If the oxygen defciency is severe, dissipation of the H+ gradient is signifcant, as ATP synthase begins to operate in the reverse direction, using the energy liberated by ATP hydrolysis to pump H+ back into the intermembrane space. Because the H+ electrochemical potential is also used to energize other mitochondrial functions in addition to supporting ATP synthesis, mitochondrial degradation of ATP during periods of oxygen limitation may serve to preserve nonoxidative mitochondrial function and prolong the duration that mitochondria can persist before resumption of blood fow. However, this mechanism of mitochondrial preservation occurs at the expense of hastening the fall in cytoplasmic ATP levels, and the latter limits other cardiomyocyte functions that depend on ATP.

Glycolysis can, in principle, proceed normally in the absence of oxygen, but when blood fow is reduced or stops, glycolysis is also limited due to buildup of metabolic byproducts that inhibit glycolysis (Fig. 7.8). Without oxygen, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation ceases and the fnal product of glycolysis, pyruvate, can be converted to lactate as mentioned in Sect. 7.3.1. Cytosolic accumulation of lactate and H+ can inhibit glycolysis in conditions of low blood fow, as the rapid export of lactate and H+ during normal blood fow is no longer possible. When the myocardium is ischemic, the capacity to export lactate and H+ decreases in proportion to the severity of the blood fow reduction as these substances accumulate in the extracellular space. Another factor contributing to inhibition of glycolysis is the limited availability of cytosolic NAD+, which is a substrate of glyceraldehyde phosphate dehydrogenase (Figs. 7.4 and 7.8), a key enzyme of the glycolytic sequence. Oxygen limitation causes cessation of forward electron transport chain function and thereby stops the mitochondrial oxidation of ubiquinol and NADH generated by the TCA cycle and/or transferred from cytosolic NADH into mitochondria. If the NADH is not oxidized back to NAD+, the rate of glycolysis will slow signifcantly. Although NADH can no longer be oxidized in mitochondria, it can be oxidized to NAD+ during the conversion of cytosolic pyruvate to lactate by LDH. However, the accumulation of cytosolic lactate during ischemia can inhibit this reaction, further decreasing the concurrent regeneration of NAD+ and limiting NAD+ availability to the glyceraldehyde phosphate dehydrogenase step in glycolysis.

These concepts have specifc importance in patients with coronary artery disease because they typically defne the temporal boundary of viability of the ischemic myocardium. For example, in the severely ischemic myocardium, limited oxygen delivery may halt oxidative phosphorylation and lead to the accumulation of metabolic by-products, including H+, all of which dramatically reduce the contractile function and viability of the affected myocardium. However, if residual coronary blood fow during ischemia is suffcient to permit some glycolysis, by allowing the export of some lactate and H+ and the import of some glucose, then this modest glycolytic ATP production may preserve tissue viability for some time. In other words, a level of coronary blood fow that is insuffcient to maintain oxidative phosphorylation and contractile function may support glycolytic ATP production at a rate suffcient to sustain myocardial viability until appropriate medical or surgical treatment can restore blood fow to the ischemic myocardium.

During ischemia when ATP is generated by glycolysis, the accumulation of lactate, with a pKa of ≈3.9, leads to acidosis within the cytosol. At a lower pH, the increase in H+ stimulates exchange with extracellular Na+ via the plasma membrane Na+-H+ exchanger (NHE). Elevations in cytosolic Na+ increase the activity of the plasma membrane Na+-Ca2+ exchanger (NCX), resulting in elevation of cytosolic Ca2+. Under these conditions, Ca2+ can enter mitochondria, presumably through the MCU, although this has been questioned. Increased mitochondrial matrix Ca2+ above a somewhat undefned threshold triggers opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), which renders the mitochondrial inner membrane permeable to solutes as large as 1.5 kD, collapses any remaining mitochondrial membrane potential, and can lead to cell death. Because mPTP opening leads to cell death, it is considered a critical contributor to tissue injury during reperfusion. Notably, mPTP opening is reportedly inhibited by acidic conditions, e.g., during ischemia, and, thus, only during reperfusion does it become a critical contributor to cell death and tissue injury. Hence, a therapeutic window exists in which drugs to block mPTP opening in patients with myocardial infarction could be administered just prior to reperfusion. To date, however, the protein composition of mPTP remains heavily debated, with evidence suggesting that ATP synthase and ANT are each and perhaps both capable of serving as mPTP. Inhibiting mPTP opening to prevent reperfusion injury may be an effective therapeutic strategy, but this still an area of active research.

Another adverse biochemical event that occurs during the ischemic period, especially during a subsequent period of reperfusion, is a markedly enhanced generation of highly reactive chemical species (ROS and others) by mitochondria and extra-mitochondrial enzymes. The reaction of these species with myocardial lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates further damages the structural and functional capabilities of cardiomyocytes. Cardiomyocyte endogenous protective mechanisms (see earlier discussion) offer a partial defense against the effects of the high levels of reactive species generated during and following a relatively short period of ischemia, but they are inadequate to forestall the injury consequent to a long period of ischemia. A major source of ROS upon reperfusion has been shown to be reverse electron transport, driven by the oxidation of ubiquinol and succinate, which accumulate during ischemia. This burst of ROS upon reperfusion, synergistically with high matrix Ca2+ levels, can lead to the opening of mPTP and subsequent cell death. Reactive species-induced damage contributes to both ischemic and postischemic cardiomyocyte loss. In experimental models of cardiac ischemia/reperfusion, augmentation of defenses against reactive species has been shown to decrease cardiomyocyte loss.

### **7.4.2 Metabolism in the Hypertrophied and Failing Hearts**

A chronic mechanical overload produced by hypertension, heart valve abnormalities, and/or ischemic destruction of a portion of the left ventricle can result in left ventricular dilation as well as cellular hypertrophy. The severity of the dilation is dependent on the excess stress placed upon the viable myocardium. In general, left ventricular wall tension (systolic and diastolic) increases as ventricular dimensions increase; this is a consequence of the law of Laplace that relates the internal chamber pressure and chamber radius (and wall thickness) to the level of chamber wall tension. The increased left ventricular wall tension is sensed by the cardiomyocytes and can activate changes in the expression patterns of a number of genes involved in control of myocyte growth (hypertrophy), energy metabolism, and many of the processes that support cardiomyocyte contraction. It does this, in part, by directly activating sarcolemmal receptors (e.g., angiotensin-II type 1 receptors) that are responsive to stretch. Among the adverse consequences of activation of these receptors is the stimulation of ROS generation and increased levels of reactive species that may overwhelm antioxidant capacity and cause oxidant damage to many important structural and functional molecules. This damage then limits a variety of cardiomyocyte functions (i.e., metabolism, ion pumping, and contraction).

Initially, the stress-activated growth stimulation pathways stimulate cardiomyocytes to grow (i.e., hypertrophy), and this can increase chamber wall thickness. Because increased wall thickness can reduce systolic wall stress to normal (via the Laplace relationship), limiting stress on myocytes results in a prolonged period of stable compensated myocardial hypertrophy. However, chronic (*pathological, not exercise induced*) myocardial hypertrophy is usually associated with important changes in energy metabolism, including reductions of ATP and creatine phosphate levels. Subsequently, reductions also occur in the level of molecules participating in energetic processes such as oxidative phosphorylation. These diseased hearts have increased glucose metabolism and decreased fatty acid metabolism, which may have short-term energetic benefts, perhaps by modestly decreasing the oxygen cost of ATP synthesis. However, the overall ATP synthetic capacity of the severely hypertrophied and failing myocardium is signifcantly reduced. The period of compensated hypertrophy in the chronically overloaded heart is often followed by a signifcant contractile dysfunction of the ventricular myocytes with ultimate progressive left heart dilation and dysfunction and mild-to-severe symptoms such as marked fatigue and shortness of breath. At this point, the clinical syndrome known as heart failure is present.

The question arises as to whether the decreased ATP synthetic capacity observed in hypertrophied and/or failing cardiomyocytes *induces* the decompensated state. It has been shown that all the operational subsystems in the failing cardiomyocyte (i.e., energetic, contractile, and electrical) are severely dysfunctional and that this dysfunction occurs in a parallel manner. However, whether contractile dysfunction in the failing heart is caused primarily by a limited ability of the abnormal contractile apparatus to consume ATP or reduced ability to produce ATP (i.e., the concept of the energy-starved heart) is still unclear. In line with the latter, some studies show that pressure overload-induced heart failure is associated with depletion of mitochondrial Ca2+, limiting electron supply for the electron transport chain and impairing bioenergetics. Correspondingly, MCU overexpression or small-molecule inhibition of mitochondrial Ca2+ effux through NCLX have benefcial effects in these heart failure models. It should be noted, however, that in other studies, mitochondrial Ca2+ overload, for instance induced by deletion of NCLX or via SR Ca2+ leak, worsened postmyocardial infarction heart failure phenotypes in mice. Hence, enhancing mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake as a therapeutic strategy to support the matching of energy supply to demand in heart failure may have context-dependent benefts and drawbacks.

In animal models, molecular and other interventions directed toward energetic systems (i.e., increasing the function of the sarcoplasmic reticulum or modifying contractile protein function) have both effectively attenuated the development of contractile and energetic dysfunction. As discussed in Chap. 43, interventions that block the adverse effects of the neurohumoral activation present in patients and animals with heart failure are also benefcial. This is because neurohumoral activation is associated with stimulation of many of the adverse signaling pathways that are activated by stretch of the myocardium.

Lastly, signifcant abnormalities of left ventricular chamber function can add an ischemic component to the intrinsic metabolic abnormalities of the hypertrophied and failing myocardium, even when occlusive coronary artery disease is not present. Increased ventricular flling pressures, impaired diastolic relaxation, and tachycardia present in the abnormal heart can limit the ability of myocardial blood fow (especially in the subendocardium) to increase normally in response to an increased metabolic rate. Hence, in the chronically overloaded heart, acquired intrinsic abnormalities of the cardiomyocytes and superimposed limitations of blood fow can act together to compromise cardiomyocyte ATP synthetic capacity and thus further impair the overall cardiac function.

### **7.4.3 Primary (Genetic) Myocardial Metabolic Abnormalities**

As described above, changes in gene expression in the hypertrophied or failing cardiomyocyte can cause various abnormalities of myocardial metabolism that, in turn, can act to constrain contractile function. However, "primary" (genetically determined) abnormalities of carbon substrate metabolism or oxidative phosphorylation can also cause myocyte hypertrophy and/or failure. Interested readers can refer to the references at the end of this chapter for more information on such defects. A dramatic example of one abnormality and a successful therapeutic approach to treat this metabolic defect will be discussed below.

An inherited defciency of the enzyme very long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase (VLCAD) is known to be associated with cardiomyopathy and skeletal muscle myopathy. This enzyme is the frst component of the β-oxidation sequence. Because most dietary fatty acids are of the longchain-type, the defciency of VLCAD results in a markedly increased cardiac dependence on other carbon substrates (e.g., lactate, ketones, or glucose) for ATP generation. The fact that even upregulation of these alternative metabolic pathways cannot adequately support cardiac function is indicated by the development of progressive myopathy. The cytosolic accumulation of long-chain fatty acids, which cannot be fully metabolized, likely also contributes to the myopathy. The classical treatment for this condition has been to replace the long-chain fatty acids in an individual's diet with even-numbered short- or medium-chain fatty acids such as octanoate or decanoate. Importantly, the short-chain fatty acids bypass the metabolic roadblock because the medium- and short-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenases are normal in these patients. This therapeutic strategy induces increased fatty acid utilization and causes clinical improvement, but, unfortunately, cardiac and skeletal muscle myopathies may persist in many patients treated in this manner, due to complexities in other substrate availability. Notably, most inborn abnormalities of energy-generating systems in the heart and other organs are not amenable to such a simple dietary manipulation. Over the long term, work in the molecular biology of genetic manipulation shows promise in remedying these diseases.

## **7.5 Summary**

This chapter summarizes how chemical energy stored in ingested carbon substrates is converted into ATP as well as some of the regulatory systems that match ATP production with ATP demand. Derangements of these processes during disease, and the role of toxic by-products generated by metabolic processes, have also been presented. Myocardial energetic regulation is an essential feature of normal cardiac physiology and is frequently impaired in common heart diseases. Thus, having a good understanding of these processes is necessary for developing effective therapeutic strategies.
