Physical examination is paramount in the clinical assessment of pathophysiology. Two key elements of a physical examination are (1) proper measurement of blood pressure and (2) careful auscultation of heart sounds.

# 16.1.1 Physiology of Blood Pressure

Blood pressure is the force applied on arterial walls as the heart pumps blood through the circulatory system. The rhythmic contractions of the left ventricle result in cyclical changes in arterial blood pressure. During ventricular systole, the heart pumps blood into the circulatory system, and the pressure within the arteries reaches its highest level—a point known as *systolic blood pressure*. During diastole, the pressure within the arterial system falls to its lowest level, a point called *diastolic blood pressure*.

Mean blood pressure, often used clinically, represents the time-weighted average of the arterial pressures recorded during one cardiac cycle. The alternating systolic and diastolic pressures create an outward and inward movement of the arterial walls, perceived as arterial pulsation or arterial pulse. Pulse pressure is another clinical term that represents the difference between systolic and diastolic blood pressure.

Blood pressure is measured and expressed in millimeters of mercury (mmHg). A normal systolic blood pressure (SBP) is less than 130 mmHg, and a normal diastolic blood pressure (DBP) is less than 85 mmHg<sup>A</sup>. These guidelines have changed over time and depend on the measurement setting such as office or home. Blood pressure higher than normal signifies *hypertension*, and one lower than normal is called *hypotension*. Normal mean arterial pressure is between 60 and 90 mmHg. The mean arterial pressure (MAP) has significance in that it will drive tissue perfusion and can be measured directly using an automated blood pressure cuff or estimated using the following formulas where pulse pressure (PP) is the difference between SBP and DBP:

$$MAP = DBP + \frac{PP}{3}$$
 or  $MAP = \frac{SBP + 2 \times DBP}{3}$ 

Blood flow throughout the circulatory system follows pressure gradients. By the time blood reaches the right atrium, representing the end-point of the venous system, pressure will decrease to near zero. The two major determinants of arterial blood pressure are: (1) cardiac output, representing the volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute, and (2) systemic vascular resistance, which is the resistance to blood flow created by the vascular bed. Cardiac output depends on a combination of factors including preload, contractility, afterload, heart rate, and heart rhythm. Systemic vascular resistance is determined by multiple factors including the vasomotor tone of arterioles, terminal arterioles, and/ or precapillary sphincters. Blood pressure as a function of the cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance can be expressed with the following formula, modeled after Ohm's law.

$$V = I \times R$$
$$BP = CO \times SVR$$

The separate components can be further broken down and derived in a clinically useful manner to aid in the diagnosis and treatment of hypotension and hypertension.

$$V = I \times R$$

$$MAP - RAP = CO \times SVR$$

$$MAP = HR \times SV \times SVR + RAP$$

$$MAP = HR \times EDV \times EF \times SVR + RAP$$

V = voltage, I = current, R = resistance, MAP = mean arterial pressure

RAP = right atrial pressure, CO = cardiac output, EDV = end diastolic volume

SVR = systemic vascular resistance, HR = heart rate, SV = stroke volume, EF = ejection fraction

Clinical examples of each blood pressure determinate, include: (1) pain increasing HR and SVR, (2) arrhythmias affecting HR, (3) hemorrhage reducing EDV, (4) myocardial infarction decreasing EF, or (5) sepsis and anesthetics reducing SVR.

It is important to note that these variables are not mutually exclusive. For instance, if a patient with atrial fibrillation presenting with rapid ventricular rate, has a heart rate of 150 bpm the blood pressure may actually decrease instead of increase, as this formula might predict. This is because both the end-diastolic volumes and ejection fractions will together decrease more than the heart rate will increase secondary to reduced diastolic filling times and decreased contractility, via the Frank–Starling effect.

Blood pressure is not constant throughout circulation. Systolic blood pressure decreases by 3–5 mmHg in arteries 3 mm in diameter and then reaches approximately 85 mmHg when entering arterioles; this accounts for approx. 50% of the resistance of the entire systemic circulation. Blood pressure is further reduced to around 30–40 mmHg at the point of entry into capillaries and then becomes approximately 10 mmHg at the venous end of the capillaries.

The speed of the advancing pressure wave during each cardiac cycle far exceeds the actual blood flow velocity. In the aorta, the pressure wave speed maybe 15 times faster than the flow of blood. In an end artery, the pressure wave velocity may be as much as 100 times the speed of the forward blood flow.

As the pressure wave moves peripherally through the arterial tree, wave reflection, refraction, and interference distort the pressure waveform, causing an exaggeration of systolic and pulse pressures. This enhancement of the peripheral pulse pressure can cause the systolic and diastolic blood pressure in the radial artery to be 20–30% higher and 10–15% lower than the aortic blood pressure, respectively. Importantly, the mean blood pressure in major peripheral arteries will closely correspond to the mean aortic arterial pressure.

# **16.2 Methods of Measuring Blood Pressure**

Arterial blood pressure can be measured both noninvasively and invasively, as described in the following sections.

# **16.2.1 Noninvasive Methods**

# **16.2.1.1 Palpation**

Palpation as a method of measuring blood pressure dates back to 2600 BC. Hypertension was known as hard pulse disease, and patients were treated with acupuncture, bleeding, and leeches. Palpation is a relatively simple and easy method for assessing systolic blood pressure. For example, a blood pressure cuff containing an infatable bladder is applied to the arm and infated until the arterial pulse felt distal to the cuff disappears. Pressure in the cuff is then released at a speed of approx. 3 mmHg per heartbeat until an arterial pulse is felt again. The pressure at which the arterial pulsation can be detected is the systolic blood pressure; diastolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure cannot be readily estimated using this method. Furthermore, the systolic blood pressure measured using the palpation method is typically an underestimation of the true arterial systolic blood pressure, that is, because of the insensitivity of one's sense of touch and the relative delay between blood fow below the cuff and the appearance of arterial pulsations distal to the cuff.

#### **16.2.1.2 Doppler Method**

The Doppler method for blood pressure measurement is a modifcation and improvement of the palpation method. It uses a Doppler probe to determine blood fow distal to the blood pressure cuff. The *Doppler effect* represents a shift in the frequency of a sound wave when the transmitted sound is refected back from a moving medium, such as blood. The sound wave shift from an artery is detected by a Doppler monitor and presented as a periodic swishing sound representing systolic blood fow. The pressure in the cuff, at which blood fow is detected by the Doppler probe, is the systolic arterial blood pressure. Doppler-assisted blood pressure measurement is more accurate, precise, and less subjective in estimating systolic blood pressure, compared to the palpation method. Using Doppler for blood pressure assessment can be quite useful for patients in shock and patients in low-fow states, those with ventricular assist devices, as well as obese patients and neonates. Disadvantages of the Doppler method include an inability to detect diastolic blood pressure as well as motion artifacts. Further, poor signal quality results if the probe is not applied directly over an artery with adequate angular (parallel) alignment.

## **16.2.1.3 Auscultation (Riva-Rocci Method)**

The auscultation method uses a blood pressure cuff placed around an extremity (usually an upper extremity) and a stethoscope placed above a major artery just distal to the blood pressure cuff (e.g., the brachial artery if using the cuff on the upper extremity). Infation of the blood pressure cuff above the patient's systolic blood pressure fattens the artery and stops blood fow distal to the cuff. Pressure in the cuff is slowly released. As the artery becomes partially patent, turbulent blood fow within the artery produces the so-called *Korotkoff* sounds, named after the individual who frst described them. Dr. Nikolai Korotkoff frst described these sounds which are caused by the vibrations created when blood fow in the partially fattened artery transforms from laminar into turbulent, and this state persists as long as there is a turbulent fow within the vessel. Systolic blood pressure is determined by the pressure of the infated cuff at which Korotkoff sounds are frst detected. Diastolic blood pressure is determined by the cuff pressure at which Korotkoff sounds become muffed or disappear.

In patients with chronic hypertension, there can be an auscultatory gap that represents the disappearance of the normal Korotkoff sounds in a wide pressure range between the systolic and diastolic blood pressures. This condition will lead to an inaccurately low diastolic blood pressure measurement. Korotkoff sounds can also be diffcult to detect in patients who are in shock, in low cardiac output states, or in those with marked peripheral vasoconstriction. High vascular resistance decreases vibration and decreases sound formation. Low systemic vascular resistance can also lead to inaccurate readings. The use of microphones and electronic sound amplifcation can greatly increase the sensitivity of this method although motion artifacts and electrocautery can produce interference.

#### **16.2.1.4 Oscillometry**

Oscillometry is a noted method for blood pressure measurement which employs an automated blood pressure cuff. Arterial pulsations cause pressure oscillations in a cuff placed over an extremity. These oscillations are at their maximum when the cuff pressure equals the mean arterial pressure and decrease signifcantly when the cuff pressure is above the systolic blood pressure or below the diastolic blood pressure. Importantly, the systolic and diastolic blood pressures are calculated values based on proprietary algorithms. Advantages of this approach include the ease and reliability of use; some of the potential technical problems include motion artifacts, electrocautery interference, and unreliable accuracy in patients with arrhythmias.

When selecting a blood pressure cuff for noninvasive blood pressure measurement, it is important to select a cuff in accordance with the patient's size. An appropriate size means that the cuff's bladder length is at least 80% and the cuff width is at least 40% of the patient's arm circumference. If the cuff is too small, the measured pressure can be falsely elevated. On the other hand, if the cuff is too large, the measured pressure will likely be falsely low.

Blood pressure is most commonly taken while the patient is seated with the arm resting on a table and slightly bent, which will typically position the patient's arm at the level of his/her heart. This same principle should be applied if the patient is in a supine position; the blood pressure cuff should be level with the patient's heart. If the location of the blood pressure cuff during blood pressure measurement is above or below the patient's heart level, measured blood pressure will be either falsely lower or higher than the actual pressure, respectively. This difference can be represented as the height of a column of water, which is almost identical to an equalheight column of blood, interposed between the level of the blood pressure cuff and the level of the patient's heart. To convert centimeters of water to millimeters of mercury, the measured height of the water column should be multiplied by a conversion factor based on the relative density of water and mercury of 0.74 (10 cm H2O = 7.4 mmHg).

All of the aforementioned methods for assessing blood pressure do so indirectly, by registering blood fow below a blood pressure cuff. Other noninvasive methods include plethysmography and arterial tonometry.

# **16.2.1.5 Arterial Tonometry**

Tonometry devices can be used to determine beat-to-beat arterial blood pressure by adjusting the pressure required to partially fatten a superfcial artery located between a tonometer and a bony surface (e.g., radial artery). These devices commonly consist of an electronic unit and a pressuresensing head. The system includes an adjustable air chamber and an array of independent pressure sensors that, when placed directly over the artery, assess intraluminal arterial pressures. The resultant pressure records resemble invasive arterial blood pressure waveforms. Limitations of this method include improper placement, motion artifacts, and the need for frequent calibration. Additionally, arteries must be continuously compressed during recording.

#### **16.2.1.6 Plethysmography**

The plethysmographic method for blood pressure assessment employs the principle that arterial pulsations cause a transient increase in blood volume. In general, photoplethysmography (PPG) measures the amounts of optical absorption at a region of interest over time. Measurements can be via transmission (most commonly measured at the ear, toe, or fnger), or refections (commonly measured at the forehead, forearm, or leg). Plethysmography is commonly employed in oxygen saturation probes to determine heart rates and O2

saturations based on differentiating arterial and venous absorption patterns (which requires pulsatile fow). However, these absorption patterns can also be used to estimate the relative volume of blood with each pulsation. Given certain assumptions such as arterial elastance, transformation, and waveform analyses these data can be used to predict the patient's blood pressure. Sophisticated computational models combine these data with simultaneous measurement of additional modalities at multiple sites to improve the clinical predictive value of blood pressures as well as stroke volumes and dynamic variables such as stroke volume variations.

A common modality that utilizes PPG is the volume clamp or *Penaz* method of blood pressure measurement. Continuous plethysmography can also be used to determine the minimum pressure needed by a fnger cuff to maintain constant fnger blood volume. For such, a light-emitting diode and a photoelectric cell are used to detect changes in the relative fnger volumes; this information is used to rapidly adjust the cuff pressure needed to maintain a constant volume. In healthy patients, the cuff pressure will correspond to the arterial blood pressure. Pressure data can be displayed as a beat-to-beat tracing or as a continuous waveform. Thus, this method allows for continuous noninvasive blood pressure measurement. Importantly, this relationship does not hold true for patients with low peripheral perfusion, such as those with peripheral artery disease, hypothermia, and/or patients in low-fow states, and/or patients on high-dose vasopressors. Thus alone, this method of BP measurement is often inadequate in critically ill patients. Additionally, the necessity of periodic calibrations and prolonged applications can induce venous congestion and pain.

# **16.2.1.7 Pulse Propagation: Pulse Wave Velocity and Pulse Transit Time**

Pulse wave velocity (PWV) refers to the time (pulse transit time (PTT)) it takes for a pressure wave to propagate through the arterial tree, at distance (D). It can be defned as:

$$PWV = D / PTT$$

PPG can be used to calculate the PPT if recorded simultaneously at two distinct locations at distance D along the arterial tree. The pulse arrival time (PAT) is an alternative interval measure that uses the R-Wave of the ECG in combination with a single distal signal, such as PPG. The PTT can be calculated from the PAT using the ECG if the pre-ejection delay (electromechanical delay and isovolumetric contraction time) is taken into effect.

$$PAT = PTT + pre - ejection time.$$

A variety of combinations of different modalities (electrocardiography, ballistocardiography, phonocardiography, videoplethysmography, impedance plethysmography, and photoplethysmography (PPG)) have been used to calculate the pulse transit time (PTT), pulse arrival time (PAT) and pulse wave velocity (PWV)(Ref\*\*\*\*). PTT, PAT, and PWV have been used to predict real-time BP. In general, distance (proximal and distal or multiple measurements) has required multiple regions of interest, although there is emerging interest in developing models to predict BP from a single site for incorporation into devices such as smartphones, watches, and rings. The potential for remote, noninvasive, continuous blood pressure monitoring would provide signifcant clinical value. Utilization of existing technology such as accelerometers, light sources, and cameras in smartphones as well as skin contacts on wearables have provided numerous combinations of coordinated signals that can be used to more accurately predict BP. Single-site PPG utilizing specifc wavelengths to target depths corresponding to proximal and distal aspects of the arteriolar tree (arteries, arterioles, and capillaries) may obviate the need for multiple sampling sites. Each model has strengths and weaknesses. Motion and electrophysiologic artifacts as well as calibration remain universal challenges. Algorithms used to predict blood pressure are often based on machine learning models of sample populations which have limited generalization and application.

# **16.2.2 Invasive Methods of Blood Pressure Measurement**

# **16.2.2.1 Indications**

Invasive arterial blood pressure involves cannulation of an artery and direct measurement of the blood pressure relative to atmospheric pressure. This allows for continuous, beat-tobeat measurement of blood pressure as well as a method to access arterial blood supply. Indications for arterial cannulation include hemodynamic instability, surgical procedures with risk for hemodynamic instability, use of vasoactive drugs such as epinephrine, norepinephrine, or vasopressin, the need for frequent arterial blood sample measurements, or situations where blood fow is not pulsatile such as venousarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or cardiopulmonary bypass.

# **16.2.2.2 Cannulation Sites**

Arteries most often selected for direct cannulation are the radial, ulnar, brachial, femoral, dorsalis pedis, or axillary. Cannulation of an artery should be avoided if there is: (1) a lack of appropriate collateral circulation, (2) infection at the desired site of cannulation, or (3) damage to the artery such as dissection. Preexisting vascular defciency (Raynaud's disease) or prior arterial cannulation such as a recent heart catheterization may also be reasons to avoid cannulating an artery. The radial artery is the most often selected artery for invasive blood pressure monitoring because the superfcial and distal location makes it easy to access and the good collateral blood fow provided by the ulnar artery makes ischemic injury to the hand a rare event. More central arteries such as femoral or axillary arteries are desirable if uses of high-dose vasoactive medications are anticipated. For instance, during a liver transplant or some cardiothoracic surgery, the radial MAP may become more damped than an axillary or femoral artery. If the anesthesiologist monitoring the pressure wishes to optimally dose vasoactive medications, a more central arterial line may provide a more clinically relevant measurement. Axillary and femoral arterial lines are also, anecdotally, more reliable. The larger vessel size and often longer catheter length make it less likely that small amounts of thrombosis or extension and fexion of the extremity will interfere with the measurement. In some clinical instances, there may be a requirement for two separate arterial lines. For instance, dissection of the aortic arch or descending aorta can lead to very different blood pressures between the right upper extremity and the left upper or lower extremities. In this situation, dual simultaneous monitoring, in addition to other monitoring and diagnostic modalities, may help determine the extent of aortic dissection. Lastly, in the setting of complex aortic surgery, various cardiopulmonary bypass or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation cannulation sites may lead to differential oxygenation of the arterial system. In these settings, a right upper extremity arterial line is often the best place to measure the partial pressure of oxygen due to the proximate origin of the innominate artery.

#### **16.2.2.3 Techniques**

There are two general clinical categories for performing arterial cannulation. One being the catheter over a needle technique or the *Seldinger's* technique. Such can be performed with either palpation or real-time ultrasound guidance. When using the catheter over a needle technique, the operator enters a selected artery with a needle over which a catheter has been placed. After free blood fow through the needle, the catheter is advanced over the needle and into the artery, after which the needle is withdrawn. The catheter is then connected to a pressure-transducing system. When using Seldinger's technique, the operator frst enters the artery with a needle. After confrmation of free blood fow through the needle, the operator places a steel wire into the artery and withdraws the needle. A plastic catheter is then advanced into the artery over the wire, and then the wire is removed and the catheter is connected to a transducer system. The second method, the catheter over a needle technique requires additional skill and experience because the tip of the catheter must completely enter the lumen of the vessel. Note, that the Seldinger technique only requires a smaller needle and wire to enter the lumen of the vessel. Arteries can be located by palpation or by ultrasound. Real-time ultrasound guidance requires the use of a high-frequency transducer to visualize the artery and needle in either the short or long axis. One can use either the catheter over the needle or Seldinger's technique with either palpation or ultrasound during the placement of the catheter. Both methods require sterile or semi-sterile techniques and skilled operators.

### **16.2.2.4 Devices and the Physics of Transduction Systems**

Invasive arterial pressure monitoring systems include a catheter (20 gauge for adults and 20–24 gauge for pediatric patients), tubing, a transducer, and an electronic monitor for signal amplifcation, fltering, and analysis. Such pressure transducers are commonly based on the strain gauge principle—stretching a wire of silicone crystal changes its electrical resistance. The catheter, the connective tubing, and the transducer are preflled with saline, and the use of a pressure bag provides continuous saline fush of the system at a typical rate of 3–5 mL/hr. These systems should also allow for intermittent bolus fushes.

The quality of information gathered using invasive blood pressure monitoring depends on the dynamic characteristics of the whole system. For example, the complex waveform obtained from the arterial pulse can be expressed as a summation of simple sine and cosine waves using Fourier analysis. These simple pressure waves have the property of resonance and have the tendency to amplify if they occur at the fundamental or natural frequencies of the employed system. The fundamental frequency is described as that at which the system oscillates when disturbed and the natural frequencies as those that are multiples of the fundamental. The fundamental frequency of our blood pressure is a function of the heart rate and considering the range of 30–300 bpm the subsequent range of fundamental frequencies being measured is 0.5–5 Hz. To avoid such resonance, the frequency of the measurement system should be >10 times the frequency of that being measured. Most invasive blood pressure monitoring systems are designed to have natural frequencies of approx. 200 Hz, greatly exceeding the frequency of the arterial pulse waveform in order to reproduce features such as the dicrotic notch. Inherent to the natural frequency of a system is also the damping coeffcient of a system characterizing how quickly oscillations in the system will spontaneously decay. Both the.

natural frequency and the damping coeffcient are primarily determined by the length, size, and compliance of the catheter and tubing employed, as well as by the presence of any air bubbles or blood clots that may be trapped in the tubing. Shorter, stiffer, smaller diameter tubing and less viscous fuid increase the natural frequency and decrease the damping of a system. Bubbles and clots and an underflled pressure bag will all increase the damping of the system. An underdamped system can result in a signifcant overestimation of the systolic blood pressure and overdamping will blunt the response of the catheter-tubing-transducer system and lead to an underestimation of the systolic blood pressure. Diastolic blood pressure will also be affected by altering the above-mentioned factors but to a lesser degree. In general system response characteristics can be optimized by using short, low-compliance tubing and by avoiding air trapping by using a fushing system and keeping the pressure bag properly infated.

When an invasive blood pressure system is connected to a patient, it should be zero-referenced and calibrated. Zero referencing is performed by exposing the transducer to atmospheric pressure and defning the pressure as zero. The stopcock used for zero referencing must be at the precise height of the transducer and ideally should be attached directly to the transducer with no tubing in between. Subsequent measurement of blood pressure should be performed with the transducer at the level of the point of interest. This is generally the level of the patient's heart or the patient's phlebostatic axis. In certain cases, elevating the transducer to determine the pressure at a level above the heart may be desired, such as in neurosurgical procedures with the head raised where cerebral perfusion pressure may not be accurately refected by pressure monitored at the level of the heart. It is very important that the transducer is properly placed as the reading will vary 7.4 mm Hg for every 10 cm of change in elevation above or below the heart and misplacement could lead to inappropriate diagnosis and treatment of blood pressure. System calibration is a separate procedure and involves connecting the invasive blood pressure system to a mercury manometer, closing the system to the patient, and pressurizing it to certain predetermined pressures. The gain of the monitor amplifer is then adjusted until the displayed pressure equals the pressure in the mercury manometer. Historically, recommendations are to perform calibration at least once daily although with more contemporary transducer designs external calibration is no longer required in the clinical setting and only in the factory with the device. Zero referencing should be performed at least once a clinical shift or whenever there is concern about the accuracy of blood pressure.

#### **16.2.2.5 Arterial Waveform Analysis**

When connected to a patient, today's employed invasive blood pressure monitoring systems provide digital readings of systolic, diastolic, and mean blood pressures and pressure waveforms. Watching the trend of a waveform and its shape can provide other important information as well. More specifcally, the top of the waveform represents the peak systolic blood pressure, and the bottom is the diastolic blood pressure. The dicrotic notch very closely approximates the end of systole and is likely a result of pressure waves refected off a closed aortic valve and the compliance of the aorta. The anacrotic notch is rarely seen distal to aortic root cannulation and thus is not useful in invasive blood pressure interpretation in peripheral sites. The rate of the upstroke of the arterial blood pressure wave depends on the myocardial contractility as well as fxed and dynamic left ventricular outfow tract obstruction, aortic stenosis, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. The rate of the downstroke is affected by systemic vascular resistance, aortic insuffciency, and elastance. As the arterial waveform progresses down the arterial system, a steeper upstroke and decreased diastolic blood pressure are observed. This is due to the loss of kinetic energy as the wave travels down the arterial system as well as the change in elasticity in the arterial walls. Importantly, the mean arterial pressure at central and peripheral sites is highly correlated.

Many insights into a patient's physiology can be revealed using arterial waveform analyses. A common and clinically important method to aid in the diagnosis of hypovolemia is to determine pulse pressure variation (PPV) as a consequence of changes in end-diastolic volumes and stroke volumes as a result of changes in blood fow returning to the heart with respiration and altering intrathoracic pressures. A monitor can perform the calculation but a trained clinical eye can also make this determination with a glance at the tracing. Various invasive arterial line systems now perform more complex analyses of arterial tracings and in addition to PPV will utilize waveform analysis such as pulse power and pulse contour analysis. This can be used to determine stroke volume variation and volume responsiveness or even predict imminent hypotension. Despite these advances in arterial pressure waveform analyses, there are disadvantages such as additional calibration, high levels of invasiveness, and inaccuracies during common clinical scenarios such as arrhythmias, hemodynamic instability, or the presence of ventricular assist devices or balloon pumps. Volume status may seem simple on the surface but in truth is exceedingly complicated and no single algorithm or monitor alone has been shown to be suffcient.

#### **16.2.2.6 Complications**

Potential complications associated with arterial cannulation include bleeding, hematoma, infection, thrombosis, ischemia distal to the cannulation site, vasospasm, embolization with air bubbles or blood clots, nerve damage, pseudoaneurysm formation, atheroma, and the risk of inadvertent intraarterial drug injection.

# **16.3 Diagnoses**

## **16.3.1 Pulsus Paradoxus**

Normally, the arterial and venous blood pressures fuctuate throughout the respiratory cycle, decreasing with inspiration and rising with expiration. This fuctuation in blood pressure under normal conditions is less than 10 mmHg. During inspiration increased venous pulmonary venous capacitance limits loading of the left ventricular and associated contractilities, via the Frank-Startling law. Additionally left ventricular afterload increases with a relative decrease in intrathoracic pressure and left ventricular output is further reduced. The net effect is a transient decrease in left ventricular stroke volume. The right ventricle both increases and decreases preload to the left ventricle, but overall has a net contribution to LV preload and blood pressure support and limits the normal drop in blood pressure during inspiration to less than 10 mm Hg. This occurs because during inspiration the right ventricle sees increased flling and preload causing an increased right ventricular stroke volume. Albeit the same increased RV flling does cause interventricular septal shift toward the LV causing some degree of decreased LV preload. Regardless, the right ventricles' increased stroke volume and contribution toward limiting the left ventricle's decrease in stroke volume is not enough to overcome the decreased LV flling due to venous capacitance and septal deviation. This less than 10 mm Hg drop in blood pressure is normally welltolerated. However, some clinical conditions can cause an exaggerated blood pressure response to inspiration. *Pulsus paradoxus* is defned as an inspiratory decrease of systolic blood pressure by more than 10 mmHg. Conditions that drastically reduce the transmural or distending (flling) pressure of the right heart interfere with the ability of the right ventricle to compensate for the natural decrease in the flling of the left ventricle; the result is an exaggerated drop in the left ventricular flling, stroke volume, and systemic blood pressure. Disease processes that can cause pulsus paradoxus include: pericardial effusion, adhesive pericarditis, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary emphysema, severe asthma, paramediastinal effusion, endocardial fbrosis, myocardial amyloidosis, scleroderma, mitral stenosis with right heart failure, tricuspid stenosis, hypovolemia, and/or pulmonary embolism. Associated clinical signs include a palpable decrease in pulse with inspiration and a decrease in the inspiratory systolic blood pressure of more than 10 mmHg compared to the expiratory pressure. On clinical examination, one can detect extra beats on cardiac auscultation during inspiration, when compared to palpation of the peripheral pulse. This may also be observed on an invasive arterial waveform.

# **16.3.2 Pulsus Alternans**

*Pulsus alternans* is an alternating weak and strong peripheral pulse, caused by alternating weak and strong heart contractions. A weak contraction will decrease the ejection fraction, hence increasing the end-diastolic volume (the volume of blood remaining in the ventricle after a weak heart contraction). As a result, in the next cardiac cycle, the heart will be stretched more; according to the Frank–Starling mechanism, this will then generate higher pressures and a stronger perceived pulse. Pulsus alternans may be found in patients with severe heart failure, various degrees of heart block, and alterations in calcium. Pulsus alternans are characterized by a regular rhythm and must be distinguished from pulsus bigeminus, which is an irregular heart rhythm (see below). One may observe an alteration in the amplitude of the peak systolic pressure on the invasive arterial waveform.

# **16.3.3 Bigeminal Pulse**

A *bigeminal pulse* is caused by premature contractions after every other heartbeat, resulting in an alternation in the relative pulse strength. A bigeminal pulse can often be confused with pulsus alternans. However, in contrast to the latter in which the rhythm is regular, in pulsus bigeminus the weak beat always follows a shorter pulse interval; thus there is an arrhythmia.

# **16.3.4 Pulse Defcit**

*Pulse defcit* is the inability to detect some arterial pulsations when the heart beats, as can be observed in patients with atrial fbrillation, in states of shock, or with premature ventricular complexes. The easiest way to detect pulse defcit is to place a fnger over an artery while monitoring the QRS complexes on an electrocardiogram monitor. A QRS complex without a detected corresponding pulse represents a pulse defcit. In the presence of atrioventricular dissociation, when atrial activity is irregularly transmitted to the ventricles, the strength of the peripheral arterial pulse depends on the relative timing of the atrial and ventricular contractions. In a patient with rapid heartbeats, the presence of such variations suggests ventricular tachycardia. With an equally rapid rate, an absence of variation in pulse strength suggests a supraventricular mechanism. See Chaps. 13 and 28 for an additional description of arrhythmias.

### **16.3.5 Wide Pulse Pressure**

*Wide pulse pressure*, often called *water hammer pulse*, is observed in cases of severe aortic regurgitation and consists of a steep upstroke in the arterial blood pressure tracing (percussion wave) because the left ventricle receives increased blood volume; this is followed by a rapid descent later in systole with no dicrotic notch.

#### **16.3.6 Pulsus Parvus Et Tardus**

The phenomenon of *pulsus parvus (small) et tardus (late)* is observed in cases of aortic stenosis and is caused by a reduction in stroke volume and prolonged ejection phase, producing reductions and delays in the volume increments inside the aorta. "Tardus" refers to delayed or prolonged early systolic acceleration, while "parvus" refers to diminished amplitude and rounding of the systolic peak.

# **16.3.7 Bisferiens Pulse**

A *bisferiens (biphasic) pulse* is characterized by two systolic peaks—the percussion and tidal waves—separated by a distinct midsystolic dip. The peaks are often equal, or one may be larger. Bisferiens pulse occurs in conditions in which a large stroke volume is ejected rapidly from the left ventricle and is observed most commonly in patients with either pure aortic regurgitation or with a combination of aortic regurgitation and stenosis. A bisferiens pulse can also be elicited by patients with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. In these patients, the initial prominent percussion wave is associated with the rapid ejection of blood into the aorta during early systole, followed by a rapid decline as the obstruction becomes prominent in midsystole, followed by a tidal wave. Very rarely, bisferiens pulse occurs in individuals with a normal heart.

### **16.3.8 Dicrotic Pulse**

Not to be confused with a bisferiens pulse, in which both peaks occur in systole, the *dicrotic pulse* is characterized by a second peak positioned in early diastole immediately after the second heart sound. The normally small wave that follows aortic valve closure (dicrotic notch) is exaggerated and measures more than 50% of the pulse pressure on direct pressure recordings. A dicrotic pulse usually occurs in conditions such as cardiac tamponade, severe heart failure, and hypovolemic shock, in which a low stroke volume is ejected into a soft elastic aorta. Rarely, a dicrotic pulse can be noted in healthy adolescents or young adults.

# **16.4 Heart Tones**

# **16.4.1 Physiology and Normal Heart Sounds**

The primary heart tones are caused by vibrations created by pressure differentials during the closure of the heart valves. Normal valve opening makes little or no audible sound. In general, heart tones are brief and characterized by varying intensities (loudness), frequencies (pitch), and qualities (timbre). To understand heart tones better, let us briefy review the normal physiology of the cardiac cycle. The electric impulse for cardiac contraction starts from the sinoatrial node located in the right atrium and travels through Bachmann's bundle to the left atrium. Following atrial contraction, the electrical impulse travels through the atrioventricular junction and splits into left and right bundle branches. Contraction of the ventricles begins with the left ventricle, resulting in mitral valve closure slightly before closure of the tricuspid valve. Ejection, on the other hand, starts in the right ventricle, because the right ventricular ejection normally occurs at a much lower pressure than the left ventricular ejection (shorter isovolumic contraction time).

The frst heart sound (S1) arises from the closure of the mitral valve (M1) and shortly thereafter by the closure of the tricuspid valve (T1). There is some evidence that the frst heart sound (S1) is partially due to the movement of blood in early systole and is due to the peak rate of rise of left ventricular systolic pressure. The initial component of the frst heart sound (M1) is most prominent at the cardiac apex and occurs just before the upstroke of the carotid pulse. The second component (T1), when detected, normally presents at the left lower sternal border; it is less commonly heard at the apex and is seldom heard at the base. When the frst heart sound noticeably splits, like in Ebstein's anomaly (often associated with delayed right ventricular activation), its frst component (M1) is normally louder.

As mentioned previously, the intensity of heart sounds will increase with increases in pressure gradients across a particular valve. With an increasing pressure gradient, the blood velocity and the resultant force causing valve closure will increase, producing louder and more easily detectable sounds. Another factor affecting the intensity of the sound produced by an atrioventricular valve is the valvular position at the onset of systole. Heart sounds caused by premature contractions may be quieter, as a result of leafet position as well as reduced preload and pressure generation.

The second heart sound (S2) is caused by the closure of the aortic and the pulmonic valves. Normally the frst component of this second heart sound is caused by the aortic valve closure (A2), followed shortly thereafter by the pulmonic valve closure (P2).

A physiologically normal third heart sound (S3) (Fig. 16.1) can be heard shortly after S2 in children adolescents and young healthy adults. S3 is a low-pitched vibration caused by rapid early ventricular flling during diastole secondary to active ventricular relaxation. When detected later in life, S3 is referred to as a gallop and may be pathologic, most commonly from diastolic dysfunction.

The physiological fourth heart sound (S4) (Fig. 16.2) is typically soft and low-pitched and best heard in late diastole just before S1. S4 is generated by rapid ventricular flling during atrial systole, causing vibrations of the left ventricular wall and the mitral apparatus. Normally, S4 is primarily heard in infants, small children, and adults over the age of 50 years. A loud S4, which can be associated with shock, is considered a pathological sign and is referred to as a *S4 gallop*.

# **16.4.3 Abnormal Heart Sounds**

Changes in the S1 sound can also be observed in states causing variation in the velocity of atrioventricular valve closure, such as ventricular tachycardia, atrioventricular block, ventricular pacemakers, atrial fbrillation, and so on. An accentuated S2 is commonly detected in patients with: (1) diastolic or systolic hypertension, (2) aortic coarctation, (3) aortic dilation, (4) atherosclerosis of the aorta, and/or (5) pulmonary hypertension which is characterized by a loud pulmonary component of the second heart sound. A diminished S2 sound is detected most often in aortic valvular stenosis, pulmonic stenosis, and/or pulmonary emphysema.

An accentuated S2 is commonly detected in patients with (1) diastolic or systolic hypertension, (2) aortic coarctation, (3) aortic dilation, (4) atherosclerosis of the aorta, and/or (5) pulmonary hypertension which is characterized by a loud pulmonary component of the second heart sound. A diminished S2 sound is detected most often in aortic valvular stenosis, pulmonic stenosis, and/or pulmonary emphysema.

# **16.4.3.1 Splitting of S2**

In spontaneously breathing patients, physiologic *splitting* of S2 can be normally heard during *inspiration*. Negative intrathoracic pressure generated by spontaneous inspiration causes the pulmonary valve to close later in the cardiac cycle such that A2 and P2 can transiently be heard distinctly. Persistent splitting of S2 is heard in both inspiration and expiration and can be variable or fxed. Persistent splitting of S2 is abnormal and clinically associated with (1) delayed activation of the right ventricle; (2) prolonged right ventricular ejection time relative to left ventricular ejection time(ex: pulmonic stenosis), right heart failure and right ventricular volume overload such as in left-to-right intracardiac shunt (atrial or ventricular septal defect); and (3) increased impedance of the pulmonary vasculature, as in massive pulmonary embolism or pulmonary hypertension.

*Paradoxical splitting* of S2 is caused by a delay in the closure of the aortic valve (A2) such that physiologic changes associated with breathing result in splitting heard during expiration. A2 and P2 fuse to become a single sound (S2) during inspiration, as P2 moves later into the cardiac cycle. Pathologies that move the closure of the aortic valve (A2) later into the cardiac cycle cause paradoxical splitting of S2. Causes include: (1) left bundle branch block, (2) premature ventricular contractions (arising from the right ventricle), (3) ventricular tachycardia, (4) aortic stenosis, (5) left ventricular outfow obstruction, (6) hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, (7) coronary artery disease, (8) myocarditis, and/or (9) congestive cardiomyopathy.

Two other pathological sounds, heard when auscultating the heart, are *clicks* and *opening snaps*. Clicks are caused by the rapid movement of valvular structures. Systolic clicks are referred to as ejection or non-ejection clicks, depending on their timing relative to systole. Ejection clicks, occurring early in systole, commonly indicate semilunar valve anomalies and, in rare conditions, great vessel lesions. Non-ejection clicks are heard in mid-to-late systole and may represent mitral (more often) or tricuspid or mitral valve prolapses. In contrast, a tricuspid valve opening snap is often heard when there is tricuspid valve stenosis and in conditions associated with increased blood fow across the tricuspid valve (e.g., the presence of large atrial septal defect). Similarly, a mitral valve opening snap is caused by elevated left atrial pressure, mostly resulting in rapid valve opening to the point of maximum excursion. A mitral valve opening snap is often associated with mitral stenosis and leave off these other conditions, or which mitral opening snap is almost never observed. The mitral valve opening snap is similar in quality to a normal heart sound and can be often clinically confused with the splitting of S2.

#### **16.4.3.2 Murmurs**

Normally, blood fow in the heart is laminar and produces little sound. *Murmurs* are created by turbulent blood fow, which may be caused by shunts, stenosis, obstruction, or valvular regurgitation [12–14]. Murmurs are characterized based on:

Location:

- Aortic valve: Second intercostal space at the right sternal border.
- Pulmonary valve: Second intercostal space at the left sternal border.
- Tricuspid valve: Fourth intercostal space at the left sternal border.
- Mitral valve: Fifth intercostal space at the left midclavicular line.
- Radiation: Murmurs may radiate, allowing auscultation at remote locations.

Quality and Intensity (systolic murmurs grade I-V, diastolic grade I–VI):

- Grade I: Faint murmur, barely audible.
- Grade II: Soft murmur.
- Grade III: Easily audible but without a palpable thrill.
- Grade IV: Easily audible murmur with a palpable thrill.
- Grade V: Loud murmur, audible with stethoscope lightly touching the chest.
- Grade VI: Loudest murmur, audible with stethoscope not touching the chest.

Frequency (pitch):

- High or low frequency.
- Other descriptive terms: Blowing, harsh, musical, rumbling, squeaky.

Quality: Confguration/Profle:

- Crescendo: A murmur that increases in intensity.
- Decrescendo: A murmur that decreases in intensity.
- Crescendo–decrescendo: A murmur that initially increases in intensity, peaks, and then decreases in intensity.
- Plateau: Static intensity.

Duration and Timing:

- Systolic: Occurs at or after S1, fnishing before or at S2.
- Diastolic: Occurs at or after S2, fnishing before or at S1.
- Can be described as early, mid, or late.
- Systolic murmurs can be holosystolic (including S1 and throughout systole).
- Diastolic murmurs can be holodiastolic (including S2 and throughout diastole).
- Continuous murmurs (e.g. patent ductus arteriosus and LVAD).

Radiation Sites:

• See acoustic areas above.

#### **16.4.4 Dynamic Auscultation**

Dynamic auscultation refers to the use of provocative manipulation of physiologic parameters to elicit or change the character, timing, and/or intensity of murmurs. Changes in a given patient's BP, preload, afterload, contractility, and HR can amplify, reduce, or elicit murmurs to confrm underlying pathophysiology [13]. Diagnostic maneuvers frequently used include inspiration (reduces intrathoracic pressure and RV afterload and increases RV preload), prolonged valsalva (decreases both RV and LV preload), exercise (increases HR and contractility), changes in body position (e.g. standing up decreases preload, and squatting increases preload)), and hand grip (increases left ventricular afterload).

Spontaneous inspiration decreases intrathoracic pressure, which increases right ventricular preload and decreases pulmonary vascular resistance. Conversely, in the left heart, transient negative intrathoracic pressure with spontaneous inspiration causes a reduction in preload and a relative increase in systemic vascular resistance reducing fow. In contrast, spontaneous expiration causes just the opposite decreased venous returns (and right ventricular preload), increased pulmonary vascular resistance, and decreased right-sided fows; on the left, this increased intrathoracic pressure with expiration transiently increases ventricular preload and reduces afterload which increases stroke volume. Notably, prolonged measures that decrease RV fow, will ultimately reduce LV preload. For example, initially, a valsalva maneuver increases LV stroke volume, but during a prolonged valsalva, the reduced RV output will ultimately result in reduced LV preload, stroke volume and blood pressure. A valsalva maneuver tends to increase the murmur of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy but decrease the murmur of aortic stenosis. Whereas squatting, which increases preload tends to amplify the murmurs of aortic stenosis, and aortic and mitral regurgitation but decreases the murmur of HOCM.

An example of using pharmacodynamic provocation to differentiate murmurs is the administration of amyl nitrate, which decreases LV afterload and tends to amplify the systolic murmurs of aortic stenosis and hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy while decreasing the intensity of mitral regurgitation (also a systolic murmur).

In addition, changes in a patient's positioning can affect heart sound intensity; this is caused by relative changes in ventricular preload, ventricular size, and movement of the heart closer to or farther from the chest wall. A recumbent position typically accentuates murmurs of mitral and tricuspid stenosis. Similarly, a left semilateral position accentuates left-sided S3 and S4, mitral opening snaps, and mitral regurgitation murmurs. Standing will affect general hemodynamics by pooling blood in the lower extremities and decreasing heart-flling pressure and ventricular size. Thus, standing up is clinically used to accentuate mitral and tricuspid clicks. Sitting up accentuates tricuspid valve opening snaps, and sitting up and leaning forward are the best maneuvers for enhancing murmurs due to aortic and pulmonic regurgitation or aortic stenosis. Exercise causes increases in heart rate, shortens diastole, elevates left atrial pressure, and shortens the time for closure of the heart valves. Physiological changes during exercise increase the amplitude of S1, S2, S3, and S4, mitral opening snap, existing mitral regurgitation or stenosis, and/or patent ductus arteriosus murmur.

# **16.4.5 Specifc Murmurs**

#### **16.4.5.1 Aortic Stenosis**

Aortic stenosis (Fig. 16.4) is characterized as a crescendo– decrescendo murmur which is best heard at the aortic auscultation area. The high-velocity jet within the aortic root results in radiation of the murmur upward, to the right second intercostal space, and may be heard in the carotid arteries. Although this murmur when heard in the second right intercostal space is typically harsh, noisy, and impure, the same murmur heard when auscultating over the left apical area can be pure and often considered musical. The harsh *basal murmur* is believed to be caused by vibrations created when high-velocity blood jets are ejected through the aortic root. The musical second component of the aortic stenosis murmur originates from periodic high-frequency vibrations of the fbrocalcifc aortic cusps and can be quite loud and heard even from a distance without an auditory aid. Murmurs due to aortic stenosis are accentuated by expiration, sitting up, and/or leaning forward. The high-frequency apical midsystolic murmur of aortic stenosis should be distinguished from the high-frequency apical murmur of mitral regurgitation, a distinction that may be diffcult to detect, especially if the aortic component of the second heart sound is soft or absent. In such patients, echocardiography will likely be required to determine the relative cardiac pathophysiology. See Chap. 22 for more information on echocardiographic methods.

### **16.4.5.2 Pulmonic Stenosis Pulmonary**

Pulmonary stenosis is a characteristic midsystolic murmur originating in the right side of the heart and best auscultated over the pulmonic auscultation area. This murmur begins after the frst heart sound, rises to a peak in crescendo, and then decreases in slow decrescendo, fnishing before a delayed or soft pulmonary component of the second heart sound. In general, the length and the profle of this murmur depend on the severity of the pulmonary valve obstruction.

High pulmonary diastolic pressure generates high-velocity regurgitant fows resulting in a louder high-frequency blowing murmur that may last throughout diastole. #### **16.4.5.5 Mitral Stenosis**

Mitral stenosis (Fig. 16.6) is a crescendo–decrescendo holodiastolic murmur heard best at the mitral auscultation area and is typically accentuated both by exercise and assuming a recumbent position. Higher heart rates elevate the pressure gradient between the left atrium and ventricle. Mitral stenosis that lasts up to the frst heart sound, even after a long diastolic time (i.e., at low heart rates), is consistent with severe stenosis (enough to generate a persistent gradient even at the end of long diastole).

### **16.4.5.6 Tricuspid Stenosis**

The murmur caused by tricuspid stenosis is relatively middiastolic in onset and differs from the mitral stenosis in two important aspects: (1) the tricuspid stenosis murmur becomes louder with inspiration; and (2) is best heard over a relatively localized area along the left lower sternal border. Detectable inspiratory increases in loudness occur because of augmentation of the right ventricular volume, decreases in right ventricular diastolic pressure, and increases in the gradient and fow across the stenotic tricuspid valve.

### **16.4.5.7 Mitral Regurgitation**

Mitral regurgitation murmurs (Fig. 16.7) are systolic murmurs easily heard over the mitral auscultation area. These murmurs are accentuated by exercise and are heard best in the left semilateral position. Acute severe mitral regurgitation is often accompanied by an early systolic murmur or holosystolic murmur that has a decrescendo pattern, which diminishes or ends before the second heart sound. The physiological mechanism responsible for this early systolic decrescendo murmur is acute severe regurgitation into a relatively normal-sized left atrium with limited distensibility. In such patients, the regurgitant fow is typically maximal early in systole and reaches minimum by late systole.
