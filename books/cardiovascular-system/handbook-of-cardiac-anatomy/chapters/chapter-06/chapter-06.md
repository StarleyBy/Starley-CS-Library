#### **Abstract**

The function of the heart as a pump is ultimately dependent on the coordinated contractions of its chambers to move blood throughout the body. These contractions are produced by cardiac myocytes, the muscle cells of the heart. An understanding of the structure and function of these cells on an individual level provides insights into adaptations of the heart to normal and pathophysiological changes over the course of a lifetime.

# **6.1 General Cellular Morphology**

All human cells can be thought of as biological machines that are surrounded by a membrane bilayer (plasma membrane). The plasma membrane has a nominal thickness of ~5 nm (50 Å) and encloses an intracellular space whose environment is closely regulated for optimal performance. The average diameter of a non-muscle cell is on the order of 10–20 μm. The plasma membrane is primarily composed of a bilayer of phospholipids, bipolar molecules with hydrophilic head groups, and hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails (Fig. 6.1). The head groups face the aqueous extracellular and intracellular environments, while the lipid tails form an oily barrier that prevents the free diffusion of charged

## **6.2 Cardiac Muscle Cell Morphology**

Cardiac muscle cells (cardiac myocytes) are similar to other cells, in that they have many organelles in common, but they are also distinct, in that they also contain an elaborate contractile scaffold (Figs. 6.2 and 6.3) that is anchored to proteins in their membrane. Extracellular domains of the membrane-anchoring proteins connect to the extracellular matrix of connective tissue and facilitate the transmission of force from the contractile matrix. Force generation arising from protein–protein interactions within the internal protein lattice leads to contraction of the cardiac myocytes and, ultimately, to the pumping of blood by the heart. #### **6.3 Cardiac Cell Membranes**

The surface (or plasma) membranes of cardiac cells are punctuated by openings of membrane-lined channels called *transverse tubules* (or T-tubules), which pass through the cell and are flled with extracellular fuid (Fig. 6.4). As they traverse the cell, individual T-tubule passages branch and connect to other transverse tubule channels to form a reticular network that encircles the internal contractile structures known as myofbrils. Abutting the T-tubules, in the sarcoplasm (cytoplasm) of the myocytes, is the sarcoplasmic (endoplasmic) reticulum, a cytosolic organelle that specializes in storing calcium. T-tubules provide a pathway for conduction of action potentials through the entire thickness of a cardiac myocyte. Their close association with the sarcoplasmic reticulum helps couple cardiac action potentials with the release of calcium from internal stores. This arrangement of T-tubules and the sarcoplasmic reticulum is also found in skeletal muscles but not in smooth muscles.

## **6.4 Intercalated Disks**

A structure referred to as the *intercalated disk* forms strong mechanical links between myocytes (Fig. 6.5). The desmosomes stabilize the undulating membrane structure of the intercalated disk, which interdigitates with and binds to the desmosomes on the surface of the neighboring cardiomyocytes. The protein components of these membrane plaques include N-cadherin, desmin, vinculin, α- and β-catenin, desmoplakin-1, desmocollin-2, and plakoglobin-2 [1, 2]. The tight cell-to-cell coupling of the intercalated disks contributes to the structural integrity of the branches of myocardial cells. This connection of the cardiac myocytes facilitates some lateral shifting; however, longitudinal shifting of cardiac myocytes relative to one another is practically impossible (Fig. 6.5). Importantly, it is the structural integrity of the intercalated disks between individual cells that allows force to be transmitted across the myocardium.

#### **6.7 Thin Filaments**

The principle structural component of thin flaments is a double-stranded flament of the globular protein actin (Fig. 6.7). Thin flaments also incorporate the regulatory proteins tropomyosin (Tm) and troponin (Tn). Tropomyosin is a double-stranded α-helical coiled-coil protein that spans seven actin monomers (~35 nm). Troponin is a globular protein complex with three subunits: TnC, a calcium-binding subunit; TnI, a subunit that facilitates inhibition of muscle contraction; and TnT, a subunit that connects the troponin complex to tropomyosin and actin. Tropomyosin molecules are aligned end to end around the helical coil of the thin flament with one Tn complex attached to each Tm molecule. When a muscle is relaxed, the track traced by tropomyosin as it binds to actin on the thin flament impedes the binding of the myosin crossbridge domains (see below) to actin-binding sites [4]. However, upon myocyte activation and the subsequent increase in myoplasmic calcium concentrations, free calcium binds to TnC, inducing a conformational change of the entire troponin complex that is transmitted to tropomyosin. Tm then shifts its position on the actin thin flament, revealing the site on actin required for strong myosin binding. Myosin can then bind to the thin flament in a manner conducive to force production. This association of a tropomyosin–troponin complex over seven actin monomers represents a de facto regulatory subunit along the thin flament. The overlap of Tm molecules creates a mechanism for the communication of the activation signal along the thin flament, making the initiation of force generation via this mechanism highly cooperative [4].

This thin flament-based mechanism for the regulation of contraction is also used for the control of skeletal muscles. In contrast, in smooth muscles (e.g., the muscles of the vascular system, gut, and airways), while the regulation of contraction is also calcium-dependent, the regulatory protein troponin is absent. The rise in calcium concentration is sensed by the cytosolic protein calmodulin, and activation occurs via a different thick flament-based mechanism.

#### **6.9 Energy Metabolism**

ATP production in cardiac muscle is primarily accomplished via oxidative phosphorylation, a multistep enzymatic process that extracts energy from glucose, fatty acids, and other energy-rich compounds and converts it to ATP. The energy extraction and ATP production occur in the mitochondria found in the cytoplasmic space of cardiac myocytes. Intracellular concentrations of ATP hover in the 4–5 millimolar (mM) range with additional energy stored in creatine phosphate, which acts as a backup to the ATP supply. Creatine phosphate (~20 mM) can be used to regenerate ATP in a one-step enzymatic process catalyzed by the enzyme creatine kinase. There is an absolute requirement for oxygen in the ATP production mechanism, and this is the reason that blood fow to the myocardium is so critical. The amount of ATP and creatine phosphate normally present is insuffcient to power the contractile activities and other uses of ATP in heart cells for more than a few beats. Therefore, continuous delivery of nutrients and oxygen and removal of waste products are crucial processes for the normal functioning of the myocardium. For a more detailed description, see Chap. 7.

## **6.10 Force Production: The Crossbridge Cycle**

During diastole, myosin crossbridges bind ATP and hydrolyze it but cannot use the energy released during hydrolysis to produce force (Fig. 6.8) because of the inhibitory infuences of tropomyosin and troponin on the thin flament. The hydrolytic events (Fig. 6.8, step 1) induce conformational changes in myosin, which allow it to hold on to the products of ATP hydrolysis (inorganic phosphate (Pi) and adenosine diphosphate (ADP)). In this conformation, the myosin heads retain most of the energy released during the hydrolysis of the high-energy phosphate bond of ATP. The energized crossbridge domains of myosin are in constant motion due to thermal Brownian motion [8], but encounters with the thin flament are unproductive because of the steric blocking of Tm–Tn. During systole, calcium from the extracellular milieu and the sarcoplasmic reticulum foods the cytoplasm, raising the intracellular calcium concentration from micromolar to millimolar levels. As discussed above, the binding of calcium to TnC initiates conformational changes in the Tm–Tn complex that moves them from their blocking position on the thin flament, thus removing inhibition. The energized myosin crossbridges can then bind to the actin-binding sites and thus to the thin flament (Fig. 6.8, step 2). This association with actin catalyzes the release of Pi and ADP, and a concomitant forcegenerating conformational change of the myosin head occurs while it is bound to actin (Fig. 6.8, step 3). The conformational change pulls the thin flament past the thick flament. At the end of the force-generating transition, the vacant enzymatic active site of myosin can rebind ATP (Fig. 6.8, step 4), inducing a structural change that then reduces the affnity of the crossbridge for actin and causes crossbridge detachment. #### **6.11 The Length–Tension Relationship**

The myofbrils of a cardiac myocyte are tethered to the membranes and intercalated disks at each end of the cell via connective protein linkages. Cell length is a dynamic variable that shortens with each systolic contraction and stretches during each diastole as the chambers refll with blood. This means that the myofbrils and sarcomeres also shorten and lengthen during the cardiac cycle. It is important then to realize that there is a direct connection between the overlap of the thick and thin flaments and the resultant force output developed by cardiac muscle cells.

Sarcomere length is defned as the distance from one Z-line to the next along a myofbril. When the sarcomere length in a cardiac myocyte is approximately 2 μm, the overlap of actin and myosin is optimal and nearly all of the myosin crossbridge domains bind to the thin flaments. This confguration of the sarcomere leads to maximal isometric (isovolumic) force production (Fig. 6.9). When the myocyte is stretched, the potential force decreases because of the decrease in the overlap region between crossbridges on the thick flaments and binding sites on the thin flaments. Thus, the decreased force is the direct result of the reduced potential for possible crossbridge formations as the cell is stretched (Fig. 6.9). If the myocyte is shortened from the full overlap position and then activated, the subsequent force generated also decreases but for a different reason. We have already stated that the arrangement of the thick and thin flaments in the sarcomere is semicrystalline. When the cells become too short, several types of flament misalignments are possible, such as: (1) the thick flaments can run into the Z-disks and become disordered or (2) the thin flaments can cross the M-line and interact with each other or with myosin crossbridges from the other half-sarcomere. The disorder and/or interference that may occur in an overly shortened sarcomere is the cause for the decrease in myocyte tension. From the graph of the length–tension relationship (Fig. 6.9), we can notice a peak representing the non-compressed full overlap position. Shortening or lengthening the myocyte from this set point will decrease the resultant muscle tension. In a normal cardiac cycle, the cell shortens during systole and has its length reset by stretching of the vessel wall during diastole. Since adjustment of the cell length changes the amount of force (or pressure) that can be generated, it is sometimes referred to as the preload of the cell.

## **6.11.1 Practical Applications of the Length– Tension Relationship**

In general, the length of cardiac cells or myocytes is controlled in vivo through their shortening during systole and their stretching during diastole (Fig. 6.9). That the set point of the length–tension relationship can be tuned in this manner is, in part, the mechanical underpinning for Starling's law of the heart (e.g., stroke volume increases as cardiac flling increases). Furthermore, if cardiac flling adjusts the sarcomere length to a point closer to the plateau of the length–tension relationship (Fig. 6.9), this causes an alignment of the contractile proteins to result in greater force production during the next systolic period. In healthy hearts at rest, the set point is slightly short of the peak of the length–tension relationship (sarcomere length <2 μm). In this position, the contraction forces are suffcient to support blood fow throughout the body at rest. During normal physical exertion, skeletal muscles squeeze the veins and increase the volume of blood returning to the heart during each cardiac cycle. This additional blood stretches the myocardium and moves the length– tension relationship toward its peak. The increased force during the isovolumic (isometric) phase of systole will result in a greater stroke volume and more perfusion pressure for the muscles during exercise. However, in hypertrophic cardiomyopathies, flling pressures are less likely to stretch the myocytes and reset the length of the cells, leading to inadequate perfusion. In contrast, in dilated myopathies, myocytes may be overstretched during diastole, reducing the pressurebuilding capacity of ventricular muscles.

#### **6.12 Force and Velocity**

The velocity of muscle cell shortening depends on the load that it works against. In cardiac muscles, the load for the left ventricle is the systemic blood pressure, and it is referred to as the *afterload*. Importantly, as the afterload increases, the velocity of myocyte shortening decreases. This relationship can impact the ejection fraction as the ventricles are less effective in expelling blood against high systemic pressures.

#### **6.13 Myocyte Hypertrophy**

All muscle cells in the human body respond to the work they are required to do, and cardiac myocytes are no exception. The heart's continuous cycles of flling with blood, contraction, and ejection of blood throughout a person's lifetime maintain the healthy muscular tone of the myocytes. However, physiological and/or pathological conditions (exercise, pregnancy, hypertension, valvular insuffciency, etc.) place stresses on the heart that lead to remodeling. This remodeling of the myocardium, due to increased biomechanical stress, triggers activation of the biochemical and neurohormonal signaling pathways, depending on the manner in which stresses are applied to the heart.

Chronic high blood pressure forces the left ventricle to produce a higher internal pressure than normally required for the ejection of blood from the heart into the aorta. Over time, this *pressure overload* will result in *left ventricular hypertrophy*. As more and more effort is required by the heart to compensate for the hypertension, myocardial protein synthesis is stimulated and there is an increase in the production of sarcomeric proteins (myosin, actin, etc.). These myocardial proteins are then assembled to build additional myofbrils in myocytes. To accommodate the increase in the number of myofbrils, the cells also add phospholipids to the sarcolemma, expanding their diameter and ultimately increasing the thickness of the overall myocardium. This adaptation to hypertension has been termed "concentric hypertrophy" [5, 6].

Alternatively, conditions such as valvular insuffciency, pregnancy, or an arteriovenous shunt can cause the ventricles to experience a *volume overload*; these stresses also result in a structural adaptation of cardiac myocytes. The stimulation of protein synthesis of sarcomeric proteins, in these cases, results in an increase in the number of sarcomeres in existing myofbrils, which can increase the length of the myofbrils. This elongation is also accommodated by incorporation of additional phospholipids into the plasma membrane, resulting in an expansion of cardiac myocytes along their long axes; this is referred to as *eccentric hypertrophy* [5, 6].

An interesting aspect of these changes in cellular morphology is that concentric hypertrophy triggers an increase in protein synthesis during the early phases of the hypertrophic adaptation, while eccentric hypertrophy frst slows protein degradation, reducing myofbril turnover, and then increases protein synthesis to extend myofbrillar lengths.

As noted above, cells can have a variety of selective ion channels in their membranes. The term "gating" refers to the trigger required for opening a given channel. More specifically, voltage-gated ion channels respond to changes in the local membrane potential of the cell, and ligand-gated ion channels respond to specific circulating biochemical factors. Non-gated channels include: (1) spontaneously active ion channels that have a random frequency of opening and closing and (2) leak channels that seem to be constitutively open though at a low level. In addition to classification based on their control mechanisms, channels are also classified by their ion selectivity and/or the direction of ion passage that they facilitate.

The sinoatrial node is a specialized collection of cardiac myocytes in the right atrium. These cells have unstable resting potentials that lead to their spontaneous depolarization with a relatively rapid and regular repeat (i.e., more rapid than all other myocytes). Cardiac activation is governed by the principle of *overdrive suppression*. This principle states that the myocytes with the most rapid frequency of depolarization control the overall rhythm of the heart. Furthermore, the action potential of the sinoatrial node is referred to as a "slow response" because the upstroke of the depolarization is slower than that of the non-nodal cardiac cells that provide the contractile force during atrial or ventricular contraction. However, rapid repeat of this "slow-response" depolarization gives the sinoatrial node overall control of the heart rate. For additional details on this process, see Chap. 11.

#### **6.16 Summary**

The heart's function as a pump is dependent on the coordinated contractions of its chambers to move blood throughout the body. These contractions are produced by cardiac myocytes, the muscle cells of the heart. This chapter describes the structure and function of these cells and provides insights into adaptations of the heart to normal and pathophysiological changes over the course of a lifetime.
