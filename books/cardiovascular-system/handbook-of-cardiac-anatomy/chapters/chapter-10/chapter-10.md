# **10 The Coronary Vascular System and Associated Anatomical Features**

Amanda N. DeVos, Julianne H. Spencer, Sara E. Anderson, and Paul A. Iaizzo

#### **Abstract**

Even as recent as several hundred years ago, the general function of the coronary vascular system was largely unknown. Today, it is well established that the coronary system is a highly variable network of both arteries supplying and veins draining the myocardium of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood. Due to recent advances in therapeutic technologies, the coronary vascular system has been utilized as a conduit in a variety of biomedical applications, e.g., cardiac resynchronization therapy, valve repair, and/or drug delivery. Additionally, symptomatic diseases such as coronary artery disease can be alleviated with percutaneous intervention or coronary artery bypass grafts. It is well accepted that a comprehensive understanding of the geometric anatomical characteristics of the coronary system will allow for medical devices to be engineered to deliver novel therapies more successfully to a greater variety of cardiac patients.

## **10.1 Introduction**

While anatomical studies of the heart began centuries ago, Herophilus (c. 335-c. 280 BC) was the frst to observe and record differences between coronary arteries and veins, including the discovery that arteries were much thicker than veins; veins, in contrast to arteries, collapsed when emptied of blood [1]. More than a thousand years later, in 1628 AD, William Harvey, court physician to King James I and King Charles I, published "On the motion of the heart and blood in animals," the frst accurate description of the circulatory system [2]. Subsequently, in 1689, Scaramucci conjectured that the contraction of the heart displaced blood from deeper coronary vessels into coronary veins [3]. However, the exact details of coronary blood fow were not well understood until the technology capable of measuring arterial and venous fows was more recently developed [3].

Today, it is known that the coronary system is comprised of arteries, arterioles, capillaries, and cardiac venules and veins. The coronary arteries originate within the ascending aorta, just above the aortic valve where the right and left coronary ostia are located. These two arteries branch and spread over the surface of the heart (epicardium) as they traverse away from the aorta. These arteries taper and branch into progressively smaller branches that progress inward to penetrate the epicardium and supply blood to the transmural myocardium. These vessels then branch into arterioles, which then branch into innumerable capillaries that ultimately deliver oxygenated blood to all of the heart's cells. Blood continues through these capillary beds to begin the return back into the cardiac chambers via various drainage routes illustrated in Fig. 10.1. The majority of capillaries drain into venules, which then empty into the coronary venous system. The coronary veins can be categorized into two subgroups: the greater and smaller cardiac venous system. A corrosion cast of a human heart reveals the complexity and extensiveness of the coronary vascular system in Fig. 10.2.

Coronary blood fow is critical in supporting cardiac function. If coronary fow becomes obstructed, due to disease or acute events, consequences are commonly detrimental and sometimes fatal. Whenever coronary blood fow falls below the amount required to meet metabolic needs, the myocardium is considered ischemic; the pumping capability of the heart is impaired, and there are associated changes in electrical activity (e.g., increased risk of fbrillation). The effects of inadequate cardiac blood fow can be seen on a 12-lead electrocardiogram within beats (see also Chap. 17). Prolonged ischemia can lead to myocardial infarction, commonly called a *heart attack*, which can be fatal or cause irreversible myocardial cell death. Coronary artery disease (CAD), which is associated with obstruction of arterial blood fow, is the most common type of heart disease and is currently the leading cause of death in the United States in both males and females [4]. For more details on cardiac oxygen and nutrient delivery and its association with ischemia, refer to Chap. 7: Fueling Normal and Diseased Hearts— Myocardial Bioenergetics.

## **10.2 Coronary Arteries**

Critically, the coronary artery system supplies oxygenated blood to the myocardium; if these vessels are occluded, then ischemic damage and myocardial infarction may occur.

## **10.2.1 Coronary Arteries: Anatomical Description**

Oxygenated blood is pumped into the aorta from the left ventricle. As shown in Fig. 10.3, the ostia of the left and right coronary arteries are located just above the aortic valve in the left and right sinuses of Valsalva, respectively. In general, coronary arteries traverse the surface of the heart and are encased in varying amounts of epicardial fat. However, some branch arteries course directly into the myocardium [4]. ## **10.2.2 The Left Coronary Artery and Its Branches**

The left coronary artery and its branches supply the majority of oxygenated blood to the ventricular myocardium and additionally to the left atrium, left atrial appendage, pulmonary artery, and aortic root [5, 6]. The *left coronary artery main*, also called the *left main* for short, typically originates at the left sinus of Valsalva and courses in the left anterior direction. After approximately 1–2 cm, the left main bifurcates into the *left anterior descending artery* (LAD) and the *circumfex artery,* at a location between the left atrial appendage and the pulmonary trunk [7]. Some individuals possess a third artery that branches off the left main, between the LAD and the circumfex, called the *intermediate artery*. This vessel usually supplies the left margin of the heart [7, 8].

## **10.2.2.1 Left Anterior Descending Artery and Its Branches**

The left anterior descending (LAD) artery, also known as the anterior interventricular artery, originates at the end of the left main at an angle close to 180 degrees. The vessel curves around the pulmonary artery trunk and continues anteriorly down the interventricular septum to the apex of the heart. The left anterior descending artery typically branches into the diagonal arteries, deep septal perforators, and the left descending septal artery. Although there is a high amount of variation in the course of the diagonal arteries, they typically branch off at an angle and supply the free wall of the left ventricle. The deep septal perforators commonly branch at 90° angles into the interventricular septum. These arteries characteristically supply the anterior two-thirds of the septum and can also have high degrees of variation. Finally, the left descending septal artery is an important vessel that supplies blood to the moderator band and anterior papillary muscle [7].

#### **10.2.2.2 Circumfex Artery and Its Branches**

The circumfex artery, also referred to as the *left circumfex artery*, stems from the left main and courses along the left atrioventricular groove. The circumfex artery gives rise to small, arterial, marginal branches that supply the aortic root and myocardium near the atrioventricular groove. In patients who are right-dominant, the circumfex artery terminates into the left obtuse artery. The left obtuse artery courses down the left margin of the heart towards the apex. In patients who are left dominant (approximately one-tenth of individuals), the circumfex continues along the atrioventricular groove until it terminates at the cardiac crux, where it branches into the artery that supplies the atrioventricular node and the inferior interventricular artery, traditionally known as the *posterior interventricular artery* [7, 9, 10].

## **10.2.3 The Right Coronary Artery and Its Branches**

The right coronary artery originates in the right sinus of Valsalva and courses along the atrioventricular groove over the epicardial surface, adjacent to the tricuspid valve annulus. The infundibular artery, also known as the conal artery, conus artery, and third coronary artery, can also originate directly from the right sinus of Valsalva or as a branch off the right coronary artery. In three-ffths of the population, the next branch of the right coronary artery is the artery to the sinus node [7, 8]. This artery branches off the circumfex artery in the remaining two-ffths of the population [7, 8]. The right marginal artery is typically the largest branch of the right coronary artery. This major artery supplies the free wall of the right ventricle [7, 10]. In most individuals, the right coronary artery becomes the inferior interventricular artery when it reaches the diaphragmatic surface of the heart. Note that the right coronary artery does not typically taper in diameter until it gives rise to the inferior interventricular artery [4]. The inferior interventricular artery runs toward the apex of the left ventricle and supplies the diaphragmatic surface of the heart.

## **10.2.4 Abnormal Coronary Artery Anatomy**

Many anomalies of the coronary artery system are associated with the origin of the major arteries. The most common ostium abnormality happens when the left and/or right coronary artery originates at an opposite sinus, which has been found in 0.03% to 0.1% of patients [7, 11]. There have also been reports of the presence of a single coronary artery that supplies the entire heart [7, 12, 13]. There is evidence that both of these abnormalities may increase the risk of sudden death [7, 12–14]. The right coronary artery has also been reported to originate outside of the sinuses of Valsalva including branching from the brachiocephalic artery, the aorta, or the pulmonary trunk [7]. The risk of these anomalies is dependent on the subsequent course of the artery. For example, an artery that courses between the aortic and pulmonary roots has a higher chance of compression [7, 15, 16]. A left coronary artery originating at the pulmonary trunk, also known as Bland-White-Garland Syndrome, is a rarer (1 in 300,000) and more dangerous anomaly that can cause decreased blood fow and typically requires surgical treatment [7, 17–19]. Figure 10.5 summarizes examples of coronary ostia abnormalities.

Another known anatomical abnormality relative to this vasculature is the presence of coronary arterial fstulas. These are defned as a direct connection between a coronary artery and a cardiac chamber, superior vena cava, coronary sinus, pulmonary artery, or pulmonary vein [7, 19, 20]. The most common fstula sites are located in the right ventricle [7, 21]. It has been reported that these fstulas are benign for approximately half of the patients; the remaining half of the patients have reported endocarditis, myocardial ischemia, ruptured aneurysm, and/or heart failure associated with their anomaly [7, 22].

There have also been reports of duplication of the major arteries. The left anterior descending artery is the most commonly duplicated artery with an occurrence rate of approximately 1% [7, 23]. Most coronary artery duplications do not cause clinical complications; however, it is important to be aware of such duplications during surgical interventions.

#### **10.2.5 Coronary Arteries: Disease**

Coronary arterial functional anatomy changes throughout a person's lifespan; in the elderly, coronary arteries typically become more tortuous, their inner diameters expand, and more calcifc deposits can be observed [24]. While these changes are common with aging, certain disease states can signifcantly increase fow restrictions to the point of causing detrimental damage, myocardial ischemia, and/or death.

More specifcally, coronary artery disease (CAD) is generally defned as the gradual narrowing of the lumen of the coronary arteries due to atherosclerosis. A micro-computed tomography (CT) model of a human heart with CAD is shown in Fig. 10.6, and a histologic image of coronary atherosclerosis is shown in Fig. 10.7. Atherosclerosis is a condition that involves thickening of the arterial walls via cholesterol and fat deposits that build up along the endoluminal surface of the arteries. With severe disease, these plaques may become calcifed, increase in size, and eventually cause signifcant stenosis; a stenotic vessel has an increased vascular resistance relative to that of healthy vessels. A steady decrease in arterial cross-sectional area can eventually lead to complete blockage of the artery. As a result, oxygen and nutrient supply to the myocardium decreases below the level of demand, that is, during exercise and during rest (when more extreme). As the disease progresses, the myocardium downstream from the occluded artery becomes ischemic. Eventually, myocardial infarction may occur if the CAD is not detected and treated in a timely manner (see Chap. 26) for additional descriptions of cardiac ischemia.

Myocardial ischemia not only impairs the electrical and mechanical function of the heart but also commonly results in intense, debilitating chest pain known as *angina pectoris*. However, anginal pain can often be absent in individuals with CAD (or in individuals with early disease stages) at rest but induced during physical exertion or with emotional excitement. Such situations are associated with an increase in sympathetic tone that increases myocardial oxygen consumption (and subsequently ischemia) when blood fow cannot keep up with myocardial metabolic needs. To date, the typical treatment for angina resulting from coronary artery disease includes initial pharmacological approaches, such as the administration of (1) coronary vasodilator drugs (e.g., nitroglycerin); (2) nitrates to reduce myocardial demand by dilating systemic veins, thus reducing preloads; or (3) β-blockers (e.g., propranolol).

## **10.4 Coronary Veins**

Coronary venous fow occurs during diastole and systole, and the coronary venous system drains the myocardium of oxygen-depleted blood.

## **10.4.1 Coronary Veins: Anatomical Description**

The relative size of the coronary venous system dominates the arterial system; there are at least twice as many veins as arteries in human myocardial tissue [25, 26]. In general, veins are considered to be *low-resistance conduits* to the heart and can alter their capacity to maintain venous pressure [27]. As previously mentioned, the coronary veins are considered to be primarily organized into two subgroups—the greater and smaller cardiac venous systems. The greater cardiac venous system is comprised of the coronary sinus and its tributaries, as well as the anterior cardiac veins, atrial veins, and the veins of the ventricular septum [28, 29]. The smaller cardiac venous system, also known as the Thebesian vessels, is comprised of the arterio-luminal vessels and venoluminal vessels [28–30].

#### **10.4.1.1 The Greater Cardiac Venous System**

The greater cardiac venous system, also referred to as the *major cardiac venous system*, delivers the majority of deoxygenated blood from the outer layers of the myocardium to the chambers of the heart. These veins are responsible for approximately three-quarters of the myocardial drainage [31]. Similar to some of the major coronary arteries, there are several nomenclatures present in the literature for many of the major veins in the greater cardiac venous system. This chapter refers to the attitudinally correct venous names, although some of these names are currently less common in the literature and in practice.

#### **10.4.1.1.3 Lateral Veins**

The lateral veins, also known as the left marginal veins or the obtuse marginal veins, typically course along the left side of the heart and drain the left ventricular myocardium into the great cardiac vein or coronary sinus [31, 49]. They are commonly located in an inferior position at an obtuse angle of the heart and parallel the course of the left marginal branch of the left coronary artery [49, 53].

## **10.4.1.1.4 Inferior Veins**

The inferior veins of the left ventricle, previously known as the posterior veins of the left ventricle, typically originate from the lateral and inferior aspects of the left ventricle and course between the great cardiac vein and middle cardiac vein [31, 41, 51, 52]. The vessels generally drain into the coronary sinus. Similar to the lateral veins, the anatomy of the inferior veins is also highly variable [28].

#### **10.4.1.1.5 Middle Cardiac Vein**

The middle cardiac vein, also referred to as the posterior interventricular vein or more correctly, the inferior interventricular vein, is a major coronary vein that typically originates near the apex and usually ascends in or very near to the posterior interventricular sulcus [6, 28, 30, 31, 41, 45, 51, 52, 54]. The middle cardiac vein drains into the coronary sinus or directly into the right atrium [28]. The middle cardiac vein is also one of the most consistently present coronary veins [28].

#### **10.4.1.1.6 Small Cardiac Vein**

The small cardiac vein, also known as the right cardiac vein, commonly drains the inferior and lateral walls of the right ventricle [6]. A small vein in comparison to the previously mentioned veins, the small cardiac vein originates in the inferior part of the right coronary sulcus and courses the base of the right ventricle, paralleling the right coronary artery [31]. This vein typically empties into the coronary sinus but sometimes drains into the middle cardiac vein or directly into the right atrium. The small cardiac vein is not always present in the human cardiac venous system [31, 55].

#### **10.4.1.1.7 Oblique Vein of the Left Atrium**

The oblique vein of the left atrium, also referred to as Marshall's vein since John Marshall frst reported it, delivers deoxygenated blood from the lateral and inferior regions of the left atrium to the atrioventricular groove [28]. As mentioned earlier, the termination of this vein is an anatomical landmark for the origin of the coronary sinus and the end of the great cardiac vein.

#### **10.4.1.1.8 Right Marginal Vein**

The right marginal vein, also known as the vein of Galen, typically originates at or superior to the cardiac apex and then courses along the free wall of the right ventricle towards the right atrium. The right marginal vein has highly variable anatomy [29]. The vessel has been cited to drain into the small cardiac vein, the middle cardiac vein, or the right atrium directly [28, 31, 56, 57].

#### **10.4.1.1.9 Anterior Cardiac Veins**

The anterior cardiac veins, also known as the minor cardiac veins, the accessory cardiac veins, and the unnamed veins of Vieussens [29, 56], return deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle directly to the right atrium. These vessels typically course along the right margin of the heart [31]. There are usually one to three major anterior cardiac veins present in the human heart [29, 56].

### **10.4.1.1.10 Atrial Veins**

The atrial veins deliver deoxygenated blood from the atrial tissues back to the cavities of the atrial chambers. The left atrial veins consist of three categories based on the region of the atrium that they drain—the posterolateral veins, the posterosuperior veins, and the septal veins [29, 31]. The posterolateral veins, which include the oblique vein of the left atrium, course along the left atrial posteroinferior and lateral walls and then drain into the coronary sinus. The posterosuperior veins of the left atrium are present in approximately three-fourths of individuals. These vessels drain the posterosuperior region between the pulmonary veins and empty directly into the left atrium or pulmonary veins [31]. The left atrial septal veins, which can be further categorized into the anteroseptal veins and posteroseptal veins, typically drain directly into the right atrium. Anteroseptal veins have been found in almost all human hearts, while posteroseptal veins have been found in approximately one-third [31]. The right atrial veins are located in the intramural region of the right atrial wall and empty directly into the chamber [28, 29].

#### **10.4.1.2 The Smaller Cardiac Venous System**

The veins of the smaller cardiac venous system, also referred to as the Thebesian vessels, minor cardiac venous system, and lesser cardiac venous system, are typically smaller than 0.5 mm in diameter and originate from the endocardial layers of the myocardium [28, 29]. These vessels are responsible for draining approximately one-fourth of the deoxygenated blood used up by myocardium and drain directly into their respective heart chamber [31]. The majority of these small veins are present in the atria, although more Thebesian veins have been found in the right ventricle relative to the left ventricle [28, 58].

The main smaller cardiac veins consist of the arterioluminal and veno-luminal vessels, as displayed in Fig. 10.10 [31]. The arterio-luminal vessels originate from coronary arterioles or capillaries and empty directly into their respective heart chamber. Similarly, the veno-luminal vessels originate in coronary capillaries and venules, and then empty into their respective heart chambers. The vessels that drain straight from the capillaries to the heart chamber are also referred to as Thebesian vessels [58, 59].

A predominant valve can also be identifed at the delineation between the coronary sinus and the great cardiac vein [43–49, 52]. Venous valves within the major left ventricular veins have been observed to be comparable in terms of number of valves per vein, except in the left marginal vein, which has fewer valves per vein [63]. #### **10.4.1.3.1 Abnormal Venous Anatomy**

The human coronary venous anatomy is highly variable across patient populations. There have been reports of anatomic abnormalities associated with the cardiac veins. Fortunately, most abnormalities of the venous system do not cause clinical complications. The most common reported coronary venous abnormality is a persistent left superior caval vein, where the veins on the left superior side of the body drain into a left superior caval vein and then directly into the coronary sinus [28]. The persistent left superior caval vein on its own is a nonthreatening anatomical variation, but it has been associated with other congenital cardiac malfunctions. Specifcally, the abnormality is present in 0.3% of patients with normal hearts and in 4.5% of patients with congenital heart disease [64, 65].

Occlusion of the coronary sinus orifce is another reported anomaly of the coronary venous system. This abnormality is extremely rare but is well reported [66–68]. In these patients, the major cardiac veins are instead drained through the superior vena cava. As a result, therapies requiring access to the coronary sinus ostium must be delivered into the superior vena cava [28].

Similar to the coronary arteries, some of the major cardiac veins have also been reported to be duplicated [29]. For example, the anterior interventricular vein has been observed to be duplicated in 3% of patients. In these cases, two large veins run parallel ~1 mm apart along the anterior interventricular groove and merge to become one vessel at the atrioventricular groove [49]. The coronary sinus has also been reported to be duplicated with one coronary sinus in its normal location and another coronary sinus located intramurally [28, 69].

## **10.4.2 Coronary Veins: Disease**

Cardiac veins are less likely to develop clinically signifcant disease states such as atherosclerosis. This is probably due to the inherently lower pressure within the venous portion of coronary circulation and the higher distensibility of the walls of the cardiac veins. When diseases such as heart failure induce myocardial remodeling, the venous system adapts to maintain both blood volume and venous pressure. However, it is generally accepted that the coronary venous system is not affected by atherosclerotic disease, and its high collateralization allows blood to return to the heart via numerous pathways [70].

## **10.6 Anastomoses and Collaterals**

Connections between arteries that supply or veins that drain the same regions, known as *anastomoses*, provide alternate routes for blood to reach (via arteries) or leave (via veins) the same cardiac region. While numerous anastomoses have been observed on the epicardial surface of the heart, even more are considered present within the myocardium [5]. During fetal development, anastomoses are somewhat prominent; after birth, these channels decrease in both size and functionality [5].

In the general population, anterior interventricular and middle cardiac veins typically have the same origin and termination and follow the same course, while veins between the interventricular sulci are highly variable in number, size, course, and occurrence of anastomoses [31]. In addition, in general, coronary veins have a higher occurrence of anastomoses than coronary arteries [31], resulting in a highly interconnected venous network [6, 26, 70]. More specifcally, the apical region of the heart will typically elicit the greatest density of venous anastomoses [66, 73].

Interestingly, in hearts with atherosclerotic damage, small anastomoses develop into larger, more effcient collaterals due to hypertrophic remodeling [5]. Such collaterals may help to protect against ischemia by continuing to allow blood to fow to a given region of the heart when an original arterial route becomes obstructed [39, 73]. Collaterals protect against hypoxia and ischemia by continuing to supply oxygenated blood to a specifc region of myocardium [5, 74]. While collaterals increase in size and number with age, whether this increase is related to atherosclerosis is considered unknown [74, 75]. Nevertheless, large and signifcant collaterals can often be observed near an infarcted area, which may indicate a slow remodeling process [39, 76]. However, in severe stages of CAD, even extensive collaterals will not allow certain regions of the myocardium to be adequately perfused. To treat CAD patients, either the arterial branch can be reopened by coronary angioplasty and/or stenting, or a new pathway can be created via coronary artery bypass grafting. If such an obstruction is an acute event, collaterals will not have suffcient time to develop to adequately protect a given heart region from myocardial tissue damage.

Coronary magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) is another method that can noninvasively image the coronary arteries without exposure to radiation [86]. Coronary MRA can also assess cardiac function when combined with other magnetic resonance imaging methods [86, 88]. However, coronary MRA has a lower temporal resolution when compared to CT and traditional angiography methods [86]. Positron emission tomography (PET) and single-photon emission tomography (SPECT) are additional noninvasive methods to visualize the coronary arteries but are less common due to their relatively high costs [89, 90].

## **10.8 Medical Devices and the Coronary System**

There are many devices that are designed to interact with the coronary vasculature. Most of the coronary artery devices are utilized for treating and assessing for CAD. The venous system, however, has been used as a location for multiple cardiac therapies, such as drug delivery, lead implantation, and annuloplasty valve repair. See Chap. 40 for more devices associated with the coronary artery vasculature.

## **10.8.1 Devices and the Coronary Arteries**

There are many devices designed to be utilized within the coronary arteries. The vast majority of these devices are used to image the arteries for or to treat CAD. Delivery tools such as guide catheters, guide wires, and micro-catheters are needed to deliver treatment tools such as angioplasty balloons and stents. Additionally, lesion preparation devices have been developed in cases where balloons and stents cannot be advanced into the lesion. These devices are used to create a big enough space for stent delivery and to break up the calcifcation to allow for better stent expansion. Lesion preparation tools include cutting balloons, scoring balloons, rotational atherectomy, orbital atherectomy, laser angioplasty, and lithotripsy. Lastly imaging catheters and coronary pressure catheters are utilized for screening and assessing the coronary artery anatomy before and after percutaneous intervention procedures. All of these devices are described in detail in Chap. 40.

#### **10.8.2 Devices and the Coronary Veins**

The coronary venous system has been utilized as a delivery site for a variety of cardiac therapies due to the venous system's high collateralization and its relative immunity to atherosclerotic disease [63]. For example, local electrograms can be recorded from the coronary venous system to indicate various arrhythmias and potential ablation target sites for left-sided accessory pathways [34, 91, 92]. Additionally, defbrillation coils implanted within the middle cardiac or anterior interventricular veins can lower subsequent defbrillation thresholds signifcantly compared to other locations within the right ventricle and superior vena cava [93, 94]. These lower thresholds are likely attributable to more effcient transfer of the defbrillation current through the heart [93]. Furthermore, from the coronary venous system, the coronary fow reserve can be determined and coronary perfusion during percutaneous coronary intervention can be monitored [34, 95].

The coronary venous system is considered an effective conduit for drug delivery as it is not prone to the effects of atherosclerotic disease. For example, for decades, the distribution of cardioplegia through the coronary sinus has been proven to be safe and effective in myocardial protection, and even superior to the traditional method of antegrade cardioplegia, especially in patients with coronary artery disease [34, 96]. Restoration of coronary blood fow prior to an acute myocardial infarction can signifcantly reduce infarct size and improve myocardial function. Additionally, the administration of recombinant tissue-type plasminogen through the coronary venous system was shown to result in both shorter recovery times and signifcant reduction in infarct size when compared to intravenous administration [97]. Cell therapies can also be delivered directly to the myocardium as a potential treatment for heart failure [98].

Although, uncommon, the coronary venous system can potentially be used to bypass a CAD occlusion. In one case study, it was demonstrated that a catheter-based system allowed arterialization of a cardiac vein to bypass a totally occluded left anterior descending coronary artery [99].

The coronary venous system has also been used as a site for pacing lead implantation to allow for cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT). CRT involves pacing at least two ventricular sites (typically one in the right and one in the left) in order to minimize the required time for total ventricular activation. Ideally, CRT improves cardiac synchrony in certain patients with heart failure that elicits ventricular desynchrony [100, 101]. The coronary venous system is currently the most popular location for left ventricular pacing, which is accomplished by a transvenous approach [102]. Transvenous pacing leads are considered less invasive and allows for greater patient comfort [103]. It should also be noted that several studies have reported the benefcial results of pacing from the lateral or inferolateral region of the left ventricle; either the lateral vein or inferior vein are considered optimal implant sites [100, 102, 104–111]. CRT is generally successful in around two-thirds of patients [112]. For additional information on cardiac pacing, see Chap. 33: Pacing and Defbrillation.

Lead extraction from the coronary venous system, if required, can be particularly diffcult due to thin venous walls, but may be necessary in the event of an infection, complication, phrenic nerve stimulation, high pacing threshold, and/or lead dislodgement [113, 114]. Leads that are implanted in the coronary venous system also tend to be smaller in diameter and therefore have a higher risk of damage to the lead during extraction [115]. Unlike non-CS leads, the majority of these leads can be extracted with simple traction [115, 116]. Leads that have been implanted for a longer period of time are more likely to require more complex tools for extraction, such as locking stylets and/or laser sheaths [116]. Also, the extraction of active fxation leads from the coronary venous system have been reported to be more challenging regardless of implant duration [115, 117, 118]. For additional details on lead extraction, see Chap. 33.

The coronary sinus and great cardiac vein have specifcally been used for a number of cardiac therapies. For example, the coronary sinus has been used as a means to deliver ablation therapy to the left atrium to treat atrial fbrillation [119–122]. In another therapy, it has been reported that a reducer stent can be deployed within the coronary sinus to increase coronary artery pressure and relieve chronic angina symptoms [123]. The reducer stent decreases the diameter of the vein to increase pressure in the coronary arteries, which then increases blood fow to ischemic areas of the heart. Finally, several groups have described the deployment of mitral valve annuloplasty devices within the coronary sinus and/or great cardiac vein which surrounds the mitral valve annulus, in attempts to reduce mitral regurgitation [124, 125].

## **10.9 Notable Engineering Parameters and Design Criteria Associated with the Coronary System**

When designing and testing the devices used in the interventional procedures that utilize the coronary system (see Sect. 10.8), a thorough understanding of the geometric parameters of this vasculature is critical. The main purpose of the following text is to summarize, at a basic level, the important anatomical parameters that one needs to consider when designing interventional devices and/or associated delivery procedures related to the coronary system.

From an engineering perspective, the development of any medical device requires knowledge and application of several important parameters; this is especially true for coronary system devices due to the complexity and variations found in these vessels. As with any device placed in the human body, a solid understanding of the fundamental anatomical properties of the tissue with which the device interacts is vital to obtain acceptable results regarding: (1) delivery effcacy; (2) long-term device stability; and/or (3) overall performance. These parameters are important not only from a chronic perspective but also for initial device delivery. Although biological reactions to materials placed inside the human body must be understood to guarantee long-term stability and performance of the devices, the following discussion focuses on the macroscopic physical properties of the coronary vessels.

To simplify the coronary system to its basic structure, each vessel branch can be defned in terms of a fexible cylinder or tube. A tube is a hollow cylindrical structure of a known but variable length, radius, and wall thickness. This means the coronary arterial and venous networks can be represented by a large number of interrelated tubes that supply blood to and receive blood from one another. The parameters described here are those that must be defned to fully understand the geometric and dynamic properties of this tube network so devices may be optimized to interact with them.

## **10.9.1 Diameter**

The most basic parameter that must be known about arteries and veins is their diameter. However, neither artery nor vein diameters are constant along their lengths [7, 126–128]. Typically, coronary arteries decrease in diameter along their length [126–128]. Thus, the left main and right coronary arteries generally have the largest diameters of the entire coronary arterial network; these diameters range from 1.5–5.5 mm for both vessels, with means of 4.0 mm and 3.2 mm, respectively [9, 73]. Note that disease state can greatly affect these diameters. The more bifurcations an artery undergoes, the smaller its diameter will generally become. In the case of the coronary arteries, the vessels located at the very end of the network are the capillaries, which are typically about 5–7 μm in diameter or 600 times smaller than the left main or right coronary artery diameters [129].

Conversely, veins increase in diameter as they move from their source to their termination. Thus, the largest diameter venous vessel in the heart is the coronary sinus, which serves as the primary collector of cardiac venous blood and feeds it to the right atrium. The coronary sinus has a diameter that ranges from 4.5 to 19 mm at its ostium, and the average diameters are 6 to 10 mm [44, 46, 130, 131]. The difference in diameter from one end of the venous system to the other is roughly a factor of 1200. However, in diseased states such as heart failure, it has been reported that the ostium tends to increase in diameter [130]. Although a diameter is commonly reported, the coronary sinus is typically elliptical (or ovular) in cross section and therefore has major and minor axis dimensions rather than a true diameter or circular cross section [43, 50]. In one study, the average major axis diameter of the coronary sinus was reported to be approximately 14.6 mm, while the minor axis diameter was reported to be 9.1 mm [132]. It is also important to recall that, because arteries and veins are made up of compliant tissue, their diameters change throughout the cardiac cycle because of pressure changes that occur during systole and diastole [133].

Many devices must be designed to be effective within various vessel diameters. For example, the design of coronary stents and balloon angioplasty catheters relies heavily on the diameter of the vessels in which they are meant to reside. If a stent or balloon is designed with too large a diameter, when it is deployed within the artery it may cause a wall strain so great that it could dissect or perforate the artery. On the other hand, if the design has a diameter that is too small, the device will be ineffective, as the lumen will not be opened enough to cause any signifcant increase in lumen size or fow. Additionally, diameter must be considered when designing a left ventricular pacing lead. Because this lead is designed for placement in an inferolateral tributary of the coronary sinus, it must have a small enough diameter to ft inside the vein, yet a large enough diameter to stay reliably in its intended location. If such criteria are not met, the leads may not be useful or safely placed.

## **10.9.2 Cross-Sectional Profle**

Cross-sectional shape profle is another parameter that is closely related to vessel diameter. Cross-sectional shape profle is determined by the shape of the vessel that results after slicing it perpendicular to its centerline. In a hypothetical cylinder, this profle would be a perfect circle. When arteries are diseased, they may contain signifcant amounts of atherosclerotic plaque, which causes their cross-sectional profles to change to a variety of different and often complex profles, depending on the amount and relative orientation of the plaque (Fig. 10.16). To date, coronary venous shape profles have not been well documented, but they can be considered in general as noncircular because of the lower pressures within the vessel as well as the more easily deformable vessel walls in relation to the arteries. In Fig. 10.13, an example of the differences between arterial and venous cross-sectional profles can be observed.

Cross-sectional shape should be considered for the design of coronary stents and angioplasty balloons. Because coronary arteries are typically circular in cross section, stents and balloons are designed also to be circular in their cross section. Calcifed arteries are typically less circular; therefore, when infated, the balloons and stents generate a shape that has a uniform circular cross section, which is more consistent with a healthy coronary artery. Thus, the therapy aims to return this shape and optimize blood fow through the artery. It is important to note that if similar devices were ever needed within the relatively healthy coronary venous network, particularly the coronary sinus, a different design would be considered as the cross-sectional profle is generally noncircular.

### **10.9.3 Ostial Anatomy**

Understanding the anatomy of the ostium of each of the three most prominent vessels in the coronary system (right coronary artery, left main coronary artery, and coronary sinus) is especially important when interventional procedures require cannulation of one of these ostia to access the associated vessel lumen to perform a specifc procedure. This is the case in most coronary vessel procedures; however, occasionally, one may want to temporarily block off or place a fow-through catheter in the ostium.

The ostia of the coronary arteries must be cannulated for all percutaneous coronary intervention procedures. The artery ostia are generally open with no obstructions except when, in extreme disease cases, coronary plaques form. In some reported cases, ostia can become partially or even fully occluded (with no ostia access). On the other hand, when ostial calcifcations are not present, there are generally no naturally occurring anatomical structures that impede entrance to these vessels.

## **10.9.4 Vessel Length**

Each tube that makes up a section of the coronary arterial or venous network is also a branch that arises from a parent vessel. Each of these vessel branches has starting and ending points. Typically, in early anatomical studies, vessel lengths were measured directly on a specimen after hearts were extracted. With the advent of 3D medical imaging technologies such as MR imaging and CT angiography, coronary vessel lengths can be measured in vivo by reconstructing the vasculature, from 2D slices, in 3D space [141–143]. The length of the coronary sinus is often defned from its ostium at the right atrium to the location of the valve of Vieussens or the point of the vein of Marshall intersection. Interestingly, the relative length of the coronary sinus in humans can vary from 15 to 65 mm [43]. Although there is published data on incidence and qualitative morphology of the tributaries of the coronary sinus, length is generally not addressed [35, 40]. Vessel length should be considered when designing a variety of devices. For example, length is an important consideration in implantation of left ventricular leads into the inferolateral branches of the coronary sinus. Optimal lead designs (or selection for use) should consider the average length between the coronary sinus ostium and the lead implantation branch for normal and/or diseased human hearts. Prior knowledge of this parameter, either in a specifc patient or across a population, might improve ease of implant and long-term effcacy of therapy. This information could also be useful in understanding the likelihood of a lead dislodging after initial implantation. Typical arc lengths for the major coronary veins are presented in Table 10.1 [139].

Understanding the potential length of coronary arteries is important when designing percutaneous coronary intervention devices including guidewires, stents, balloons, imaging catheters, etc. These tools must be long enough to access any region, including distal locations, of the coronary vasculature in a wide variety of patient anatomies for delivery. When percutaneous coronary angioplasty or stenting procedures are performed, it is critical that the physician knows the exact location along the length of an artery where the occlusion occurs, and the relative distance needed from catheter entry to that site. These parameters are often measured using contrast angiography. External markers can also be built into the distal end of the catheters to inform interventionalists how far into the vasculature the device has been advanced. Thus, reducing the fuoroscopy time, as the imaging is not needed until the device is close to the region of interest.

The length of the coronary artery occlusion or dissection within the vessel is also important to understand, as the stent or balloon needs to be sized to treat the entirety of the diseased region. This length can be estimated on fuoroscopy; however, intravascular imaging such as IVUS or OCT allow for more comprehensive understanding of the length needed to treat the occlusion or dissection.

#### **10.9.7 Wall Thickness**

Coronary vessel walls have various thicknesses related to their function as discussed earlier in this chapter. When a device is placed into the coronary system, there is always a danger of the device inadvertently breaking through the vessel wall. These perforations may occur when a device is advanced with a level of force and angle of incidence that the vessel wall cannot tolerate. This situation, although not very common, is very dangerous and may be lethal if not dealt with appropriately. Artery perforations are more often fatal than those in veins for two reasons: (1) high arterial blood pressure generates more blood lose; and (2) oxygenated blood is not reaching the heart distal to the perforation, which may cause ischemia or myocardial infarction.

Although no device is meant to perforate the coronary vessels, each should be developed with the worst-case scenario of perforation in mind, such that the deployed devices will not be problematic for either patients or physicians. The wall thickness of the larger coronary arteries is roughly 1 mm [147, 148]. Additionally, the anterior and middle cardiac vein wall thickness (0.11–0.17 mm) are signifcantly larger than that of the lateral veins and inferior vein of the left ventricle (0.09–0.13 mm) [126]. Vein wall thickness in apical regions of all four major left ventricular veins was signifcantly smaller than those in basal regions; average vein wall thickness in apical regions ranged from 0.09 to 0.13 mm, in comparison to 0.11–0.17 mm in basal regions. Examples of relative wall thickness for arteries and veins can be observed in Fig. 10.13.

#### **10.9.8 Motion Characteristics**

Since the vessels of the coronary system are located relative to the epicardium, it follows that they are not stationary due to the motion of the heart. Along with the simple 3D displacement that occurs over time because of motion, there are other mechanical parameters that are dynamic, such as curvature, stress, and torsion. Each of these fundamental mechanical parameters can have a signifcant effect on devices placed in the lumen of a deforming vessel. Thus, devices such as stents and leads must be designed to withstand all types of intravascular stress, curvature, and torsion changes that patients are expected to experience over their lifetimes. Additional necessary considerations include: (1) the relative changes in the 3D path of each vessel; (2) changes in lumen diameter during a given cardiac cycle; and (3) the relative fuctuations associated with alterations in contractility (e.g., the effect of exercise and increasing cardiac output).

## **10.9.9 Relationship to Myocardium and Epicardial Fat**

The relationship between the coronary vessel and the myocardium is an important parameter to take into consideration when designing various cardiac devices. As noted above, both coronary arteries and veins exhibit signifcant tortuosity on the surface of the heart and sometimes course into the myocardium (see Sect. 10.2). Additionally, most adult human hearts contain varying amounts of fat, particularly in the subepicardial region [149]. Epicardial fat is signifcant, as it may comprise up to half of the heart's weight, particularly in obese patients that have hypertension and atherosclerotic coronary artery disease [150–154]. In general, large epicardial fatty deposits are in the atrioventricular sulci and surround the anterior and inferior descending coronary arteries; note that coronary vessels are typically displaced off the epicardial surface of the heart and are surrounded by epicardial fat [153–155]. As can be seen in Figs. 10.12 and 10.13, adipose cells can surround both epicardial arteries and veins.

The distance between a vessel and the myocardium may be increased due to the epicardial fat, and this larger separation may signifcantly affect cardiac device effcacy, particularly for transvenous pacing leads. Pacing thresholds for leads implanted in coronary veins have previously been shown to be smaller at distal positions than at more proximal positions [3, 39, 156]. While larger vein circumferences may contribute to the need for higher pacing voltages near the base of the heart, a larger amount of epicardial fat in that region could also play a role. With increasing rates of obesity, particularly in the United States, epicardial fat must be considered when designing devices that depend on close proximity to myocardial tissue.

This separation between the coronaries and myocardium should also be considered during endocardial ablation procedures as both radio-frequency and pulse-feld ablations have been shown to cause coronary artery spasms in some cases [157, 158]. Endocardial ablation is used to treat cardiac arrythmias by killing the cells that are triggering the irregular rhythm or by killing cells to isolate or obstruct the arrhythmic circuit (see Chaps. 31 and 32). The coronary arteries are anatomically close to multiple common ablation locations (see Sect. 10.9.10), and if there is not a thick layer of myocardium or epicardial fat between the ablation site and the coronary artery, spasm may occur. Coronary spasms can be dangerous, as ischemia or myocardial infarctions may occur do to the reduced blood fow. Therefore, physicians must be aware of a patient's risk or the likelihood of collateral damage to the coronaries during ablation, so that they are prepared to quickly address the complication with vasodilators or coronary catheterization.

Similarly, as the coronaries are located on the epicardial surface of the heart, it is important to understand the locations of the vessels when performing ventricular epicardial ablation. Ventricular epicardial ablation is performed on the external surface of the heart and may be used to treat ventricular tachycardia arrythmias when endocardial ablation is insuffcient. As the electrophysiologists are aiming to ablate the myocardium, they must be aware of the positioning of the coronary vasculature to avoid damaging these vessels and causing spasms. The presence of epicardial fat may increase the diffculty of visualizing the coronaries during the procedure, so pre-procedural angiography or CT may help avoid inadvertently ablating over a coronary artery.

## **10.9.10 Nearby Clinically Relevant Anatomy**

Understanding the location of the coronary vasculature relative to other cardiac anatomies is important to minimize or prevent collateral damage to these vessels during unrelated interventions such as ablation and valvular repairs or replacements. Additionally, it is important to protect adjacent anatomies from potential damage or unwanted stimulation during coronary interventions, such as intravenous pacing.

## **10.9.10.1 Associations Between the Veins and Arteries**

It is important to understand the anatomical relationships between the coronary arteries and veins so that one does not damage one system when a device is implanted in the other system [159, 160]. For example, active-fx left-sided leads can be deployed in the coronary venous system for patients with lead dislodgement challenges [161, 162]. In these lead placement procedures, it is important to consider a patient's vein-to-artery distance because there is risk for arterial perforation if the lead is actively fxed in a vein and inadvertently damages a coronary artery due to its close proximity. Another example is retrograde myocardial drug delivery via the coronary venous system or retrograde coronary venous arterialization, which may be used to gain access for the percutaneous treatment of chronic total occlusions (CTOs) [99, 163–165]. A coronary vein located near the diseased artery may be an ideal target for this therapeutic approach.

The proximity of the circumfex artery relative to the coronary sinus also has clinical implications. For example, circumfex artery compression is a concern for indirect mitral valve annuloplasty devices that are deployed in the coronary sinus [124, 132, 166–168]. The risk of compression is high if the circumfex courses between the valve and the coronary sinus.

#### **10.9.10.2 Mitral Isthmus**

The mitral isthmus is a relatively common ablation location for treating atrial fbrillation, so its location relative to both the coronary sinus and circumfex artery is relevant. The mitral isthmus is the region of the left atrial lateral wall located between the mitral annulus and the inferior left pulmonary vein ostium. Ablating this region is typically performed from within the coronary sinus so that the energy can be applied near the left atrium of the heart without the need for a trans-septal puncture. Thus, the anatomical relationship between the coronary sinus and the left atrial mitral isthmus is important [169–171]. Ineffective ablation applications within this region have been reported to be pro-arrhythmic [172]. Therefore, it is important to understand and ensure the anatomical proximity of the mitral isthmus to the coronary sinus to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. Also, as previously described, ablation in close proximity to a coronary artery can cause vessel spasms, which may lead to occlusion and ischemia. Damage to the circumfex can occur during these procedures depending on the circumfex artery's course and proximity to the ablation site (Fig. 10.19) [173].

## **10.9.10.3 Cavotricuspid Isthmus**

The cavotricuspid isthmus (CTI) is another region commonly ablated to treat atrial futter or atrial fbrillation. The CTI is the region of the right atrium that is between the inferior vena cava ostium and the tricuspid valve. Like the mitral isthmus and the circumfex artery, the CTI and right coronary artery are close in proximity (Fig. 10.19). It has been reported that RCA spasm commonly occurs during CTI ablation [157]. Thus, it is important for physicians to understand this proximity and be prepared to check for RCA occlusion and to utilize vasodilators or catheterize the coronary for intervention.

### **10.9.10.4 Phrenic Nerve**

Another clinically relevant nearby structure one needs to consider when performing any cardiac procedure is the phrenic nerve, which stimulates the diaphragm. A challenge associated with left ventricular lead implantation procedures is avoiding undesired stimulation of the left phrenic nerve. The left phrenic nerve is located posterior to the heart near the left lateral wall (Fig. 10.20). Phrenic nerve stimulation at implantation or follow-up has been reported to occur in approximately 30% of patients, although clinically relevant complications only occur in a minority of these cases [174]. In these cases, the patients may experience a lower quality of life and CRT failure [174].

## **10.10 Summary**

Several hundred years ago, the general function of the coronary system was unknown. Now, the coronary system is understood to be a highly variable network of arteries and veins supplying and draining the myocardium of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, respectively. Due to advances in biomedical technologies, the coronary system has been utilized in a variety of therapeutic applications, including CRT and mitral valve repair. Additionally, debilitating cardiac disorders such as coronary artery disease can be alleviated by surgical or interventional approaches, such as coronary artery bypass grafts or less invasive stenting. Nevertheless, a critical understanding of the structural and geometric anatomical characteristics of the coronary system is required to further optimize the development and delivery of future medical devices. These devices need to be engineered to successfully deliver therapies to a variety of patients with unique coronary vasculature anatomies.
