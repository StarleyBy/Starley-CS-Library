## **17 Basic ECG Theory, 12-Lead Recordings and Their Interpretation**

Enrique Vergara-Escudero, Sarah Vieau, and Paul A. Iaizzo

#### **Abstract**

The recorded electrocardiogram (ECG) remains one of the most vital monitors of a given patient's cardiovascular status and is used today in nearly every clinical setting. This chapter discusses the ECG as a measure of how the heart's electrical activities may change over time, as action potentials within each myocyte propagate throughout the heart during each cardiac cycle. By utilizing the resultant electrical felds present in the body, electrodes can be placed around the heart to measure potential differences as the heart depolarizes and repolarizes. Furthermore, we present the techniques for obtaining ECG data.

Electrocardiography has progressed rapidly since it was frst employed in the early 1900s. New, smaller, and more sophisticated instruments and innovative analysis techniques are continually being developed. The trend has been toward developing smaller, easier-to-use devices to gather and remotely send information to aid patient diagnosis and treatment.

#### **17.1 The Basics of the Electrocardiogram**

The electrocardiogram (ECG or EKG) is a noninvasive graphical representation of the heart's electrical activity over time. It leverages electrodes placed around the subject to record changes in the heart's electrical activity, which propagates throughout the body due to the underlying myocardial conductivity. In other words, the ECG indirectly records the dipole vectors (magnitudes and directions) of depolarization and repolarization of the patient's cardiomyocytes over time. For an ECG, the human body can be considered a largevolume conductor. As the heart is attached and surrounded by conductive tissue, the instantaneous electrical activity of the heart can be picked up from virtually any point in the skin.

During the cardiac cycle, the heart contracts in response to action potentials moving through the heart's chambers in a coordinated fashion (see also Chap. 11). As this occurs typically, one part of the cardiac tissue is depolarized, and another is at its resting potential (approx. −90 mV). This results in a charge separation, or dipole, illustrated in Fig. 17.1. The moving dipole causes current fow in the surrounding body fuids between the ends of the heart, resulting in fuctuating electric felds throughout the body. This is much like the electric feld that would result, for example, if a standard battery was suspended in a saltwater solution (an electrically conductive medium). The opposite poles of the battery would cause current fow in the surrounding fuid, creating an electric feld that could be detected by electrodes placed in the solution. A similar electrical feld around the heart can be detected using electrodes attached to the skin. The intensity of the voltages detected depends on the orientation of the electrodes with respect to that of the dipole ends. The amplitudes of the signals are proportional to the masses of tissue involved in creating that dipole at any given time. Typically, one employs electrodes on the skin's surface to detect the voltages of these electrical felds, which gives rise to the electrocardiogram.

It is important to note that because the surface ECG is measured from the skin, any potential differences within the

#### **17.2 ECG Recording Devices**

The invention of electrocardiography had an immeasurable impact on the feld of cardiology. It has provided insights into the structure and function of healthy and diseased hearts. Thus, the ECG has evolved into a powerful diagnostic tool for heart disease, including the detection of arrhythmias, myocardial infarction, and/or hypertrophy, among others. The ECG is the standard of care in cardiology, and technological advances are continually being made.

#### **17.3 History of the ECG**

The discovery of intrinsic electrical activities within the heart can be traced back to the 1840s. In 1842, Dr. Carlo Matteucci experimented with frog hearts and reported that an electrical current accompanies each heartbeat. Soon after, the German physiologist Emil DuBois-Reymond described the frst action potential accompanying muscle contraction. Rudolph von Koelliker and Heinrich Miller recorded the frst cardiac action potential using a galvanometer in 1856. Subsequently, the invention of the capillary electrometer in the early 1870s by Gabriel Lippmann led to the frst recording of a human electrocardiogram, by Augustus D. Waller.

The capillary electrometer is a thin glass tube containing a column of mercury that sits above sulfuric acid. With varying electrical potentials, the mercury meniscus moves, which can be observed through a microscope. Using this capillary electrometer, Waller was the frst to show that the underlying cardiac electrical activity precedes the mechanical contractions. He was also the frst to show that the heart's electrical activity can be seen by applying electrodes to a subject's hands, feet, and mouth. Waller's work was the frst description of this so-called "limb leads." In 1889, he published his research, which showed tracings of both large mammalian and human hearts, using his fve-electrode—10 lead system. Interestingly, Waller would often publicly demonstrate his experiments with his dog, Jimmy, who would stand in jars of saline during the recording of the ECG. In 1891, Bayliss and Starling showed evidence of triphasic cardiac electrical activities associated with each beat, using the capillary electrometer.

A breakthrough in cardiac electrocardiography came with the invention of the string galvanometer by Willem Einthoven in 1901. He reported the frst electrocardiogram using his string galvanometer the following year. Einthoven's string galvanometer consisted of a massive, 600-pound electromagnet with a thin silver-coated string stretched across it; electric currents that passed through the string would cause it to move from side to side in the magnetic feld generated by the electromagnet. The oscillations in the string would provide information regarding the strengths and directions of the underlying electrical currents. The defections of the string were then magnifed using a projecting microscope and were recorded on a moving photographic plate (Fig. 17.2). Years earlier, utilizing recordings from a capillary electrometer, Einthoven was also the frst to label the defections of the heart's electrical activity, initially named fve defections from the isoelectric line as ABCDE. After a mathematical correction and following the tradition of labeling successive points of the curve, he defned the P, Q, R, S, and T waveforms. According to Mr. Snellen, Einthoven's biographer, he chose the PQRST sequence to "[…] eliminate confusion with the uncorrected tracings and to allow space for possible later additions […]."

Einthoven reduced the number of electrodes Waller proposed to three by eliminating the mouth and right leg electrodes. The resulting electrodes were used to construct *Einthoven's Triangle,* a concept used today. The standard

Following Einthoven's invention of the string galvanometer, electrocardiography quickly became a research tool for physiologists and cardiologists. Much of the current knowledge involving arrhythmias was obtained through recording ECGs. In 1924, Einthoven was awarded the Nobel Prize for his work and clinical innovations.

During this same era, Thomas Lewis was one of the pioneering cardiologists who utilized the capabilities of the ECG to further scientifc knowledge of arrhythmias. His fndings were summarized in his books *The Mechanism of the Heart Beat* and *Clinical Disorders of the Heart Beat*, published in 1911 and 1912, respectively. He also published over 100 research papers describing his work. Importantly, Lewis was also the frst to use the terms sinoatrial node, pacemaker, premature contractions, paroxysmal tachycardia, and atrial fbrillation. He also suggested a modifed position of the recording electrodes to obtain a confguration that can help detect atrial activities and their relations to ventricular activities, known today as the *Lewis lead confguration*.

Myocardial infarction and angina pectoris have also been extensively studied with ECG: even using the early string galvanometer device. Numerous clinical investigators further studied the changes within ECG signals associated with the onset of myocardial infarction in animals and humans. Their use of exercise during ECG analyses stemmed from the observations that many of their patients experienced angina only during physical exertion. This technique was not routinely used during this period, since it was also considered clinically very dangerous. Nevertheless, with advances in protocols and technologies, the ECG emerged as an essential and commonly employed diagnostic tool for all types of physicians.

The electrical impulse from the sinoatrial node is then conducted rapidly throughout the right and left atria by the inter-nodal bundles in a down-left-anterior direction, depolarizing both atria and giving rise to the P-wave (Fig. 17.6). The P-wave in a healthy heart is usually around 80–100 milliseconds (ms) in duration. As the P-wave ends, the atria are depolarized, and the electromechanical translation that leads to contraction can occur. The return to the isoelectric line after the P-wave locates the impulse at the *atrioventricular (AV) node*, also known as the Aschoff & Tawara node (see also Chap. 11 for additional history on the conduction system). The AV node functions as an electrical gatekeeper per se, due to the body of fbrous tissue that encases it. Importantly, it delays the impulse from the SA node to the His system, mechanically allowing sequential rather than simultaneous contractions of atria and ventricles. The electrical delay is represented in the ECG as the PR segment that lasts around 50 to 120 ms. The total atrial activation time takes roughly 120–200 ms from the beginning of the P-wave; this time is referred to as the PR interval (see Sect. 17.7.5). The repolarization of the atria produces a waveform known as the Ta wave, which, under normal circumstances, is obscured by the much larger voltage signals of the QRS complex.

As the electrical impulse continues through the *Bundle of His*, it triggers the depolarization of cardiomyocytes along its anatomical pathway. It is widely accepted that four major depolarization dipole vectors compose the overall ventricular depolarization: the left middle-septal vector (vector I), the low-septal/apical vector (Vector II), the free biventricular free walls vector (Vector III), and the fnal/basal/late vector (vector IV) (Fig. 17.6). These four depolarization vectors result in the recordable QRS complex, which in the healthy heart is approximately 100 ms in duration.

The reading of a QRS complex will vary according to the derivate being analyzed, as the defections from the isoelectric line will depend on the approximation or divergence of the vector from the electrode. By convention, an upward defection from the isoelectric line (wave) is said to be "positive," and a downward defection is said to be "negative." Per standard clinical nomenclature, the frst negative defection from the isoelectric line in a QRS complex is named the "Q-wave," and it typically refects the vector I of depolarization. If no positive defection follows, the complex receives the name of "QS wave," which includes all depolarization vectors. The frst positive defection from the isoelectric line in a QRS complex is called the "R-wave,"which typically refects vectors II and III of ventricular depolarization. The negative defection from the isoelectric line following an R-wave is called the "S-wave." This nomenclature also employs capital letters (QRS) to designate the "large" amplitude waves and lowercase letters (qrs) to name the "smaller" waves. However, the threshold to defne a "large" or "small" amplitude is still debatable. QRS complexes are typically composed of two or three waves, but in some conditions, such as a bundle branch block, more than three waves can be detected. In patients in which more than three waves can be detected, all subsequent positive defections from the isoelectric line are *R'* (R prime), and all subsequent negative defections are *S′* (S prime). The complete ventricular activity is enclosed within the QT interval, measured from the frst wave of the QRS complex until the end of the T-wave. The normal QT interval duration is continuously a matter of discussion, and variations are reported between patient's sex, age, and ethnic groups. Overall, the normal duration oscillates between 360 and 450 ms. In 1920, Henry C. Bazzet introduced the concept of correcting (c) the QT interval to the heart rate (R-R interval). He suggested a formula where:

$$QTc = \frac{Measured QT}{\sqrt{RR interval}}$$

Further investigation revealed that Bazzet's formula tended to overcorrect the QT at high heart rates and undercorrect it at low heart rates. These limitations led to the development and use of different formulas for such conditions and populations, namely Fridericia's, Framingham's, Hodges', and Rautaharju's, among others. Clinical disturbances that prolong the QTc interval include hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, and congenital long QT syndrome (LQTS). At the same time, a short QTc is related to hypercalcemia, hyperkalemia, hyperthermia, acidosis, and congenital short QT syndrome. Both extremes pose risks for inducing cardiac arrhythmias, particularly a long QTc increases the risk of an "R-on-T" phenomenon, where ventricular depolarization begins before complete repolarization occurs and may trigger a polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (Torsade's de Pointes).

Lastly, the recorded ECG waveforms in some individuals may elicit a potential referred to as the U-wave. Its presence is not fully understood but is considered by some to be caused by late repolarizations within the vast Purkinje system, and it has been found prominent in hypokalemic states and hyperthyroidism. If detected, the U-wave will be toward the end of the T wave and have the same polarity (positive defection). However, it typically has a lower amplitude and ascends more rapidly than it descends (the opposite of the T wave).

#### **17.5 Details of the 12-Lead EKG**

The ECG is typically measured from electrodes placed on the skin's surface; this can be done by placing a pair of electrodes directly on the skin and then monitoring the potential difference between them as these electrical signals are transmitted throughout the body. The detected waveform features depend not only on the amount of cardiac tissue involved but also on the relative orientation of the electrodes, i.e., with respect to the major dipoles in the heart. Recall that ECG waveforms will look different when measured from different electrode positions, and typically, an ECG investigation is obtained using several different electrode locations (e.g., limb leads, precordial, or *Lewis* confguration) or confgurations (unipolar, bipolar, modifed bipolar) that have been standardized. We will discuss the relative placements of the electrodes to create the "frontal" or "limb" and "transverse" or "precordial" plane leads.

#### **17.5.1 Frontal Plane Leads**

The standard for a clinical ECG today starts by placing three electrodes: one in the right arm (RA), one in the left arm (LA), and one in the left leg (LL). When joining the three points, a virtual equilateral triangle (*Einthoven's Triangle*) is formed in the torso of the body, as illustrated in Fig. 17.8. The position of the electrodes may vary as long as the shape and orientation of the triangle remain similar in shape and the heart is contained inside the created triangle. A fourth electrode, which serves as an electrical ground, is often placed in the right leg (RL). A given electrode will serve as either a negative ([−]) or positive ([+]) electrode, depending on the recording being acquired. With this position of the electrodes, three bipolar frontal plane ECG leads are created: lead I (between RA[−] and LA[+]), lead II (between RA [−] and LL[+]), and lead II (between LA[−] and LL[+]). The positive and negative signs shown in Fig. 17.8 indicate the polarity of each lead; this is a universal convention.

As an example, if the Lead II ECG trace shows an upward defection, it would mean that the voltage measured at the left leg (or bottom apex of the triangle) is more positive than the voltage measured at the right arm (or upper right apex of the triangle). One time point where this happens is during the P-wave. #### **17.6 The ECG Recording Equipment and Paper Traces**

The modern ECG module consists of a user interphase, a pre-visualization screen, buttons to adjust speed and voltage, a digital galvanometer, and typically a microdot thermal printer. The ECG paper employed today is usually thermally reactive, with the heated tip of the microdot thermal printer allowing for the generation of the waveform trace. The paper often contains pre-printed millimeter markings on the X and Y axes and a thicker marker every 5 mm. The standard printing speed of 25 mm/s allows for the universal clinical interpretation and measurement of the recorded waves, intervals, and segments. With this printing speed, every millimeter in the X-axis represents 0.04 seconds (40 ms), and the thicker markings every 5 mm represent 0.2 seconds (200 ms). On the Y-axis, the voltage is set so that every 10 mm represents a 1 mV defection. This means that every millimeter in the Y-axis represents 0.1 mV. These settings can be manipulated to better detail a waveform or tracing, considering this will alter the interpretation of the 12 lead ECG. Usually, every employed 12-Lead ECG machines capture 10-second recordings once the button is pressed unless the confguration is manipulated to do otherwise.

### **17.7 The Basic Interpretation of a Non-Pathologic 12-Lead ECG**

Once printed, modern ECG machines offer the clinician a computer reading of the waves, intervals, and segments' duration and a suggested diagnostic suspicion. However, no clinical decisions should be made based only on the information offered by these incorporated algorithms, given that they do not consider the clinical context or specifc patient variables. The careful interpretation of a 12-Lead ECG should always be done by a trained professional, considering the specifc patient variables. The most important factor to remember when interpreting an ECG in a non-emergent scenario, is to perform a systematic and methodic interpretation. We will briefy review the steps to interpret and identify a non-pathologic ECG tracing, starting with how to identify an ECG that has been correctly acquired.

# 17.7.1 An Adequately Acquired Standard 12-Lead ECG

The first three elements that should be checked those performing the assessment, once the ECG has been printed, are that the leads reflect a correct positioning of the electrodes, that the voltage is set to 10 mm/mV, and that the speed is set to 25 mm/s. To check whether the leads reflect the correct positioning of the electrodes, look for (1) positive P-waves in frontal leads II, III, and aVF, and a negative P-wave in aVR. Then, (2) look for a progression of the R-wave in the precordial leads, where the R-wave should be smaller in V1 than in V2 and progressively get larger toward V5-V6. Lastly, (3) check that lead tracings are complete and no excessive interferences are present (e.g., movement artifacts).

To ensure the ECG trace was printed using a standard speed and voltage, look in the borders of the paper for a marking that states "25 mm/s" and "10 mm/mV." Alternatively, at the leftmost part of the tracing, an indicator "tower" provides the voltage and speed. This symbol should be 5 mm on the X-axis and 10 mm on the Y-axis when the settings are 25 mm/s and 10 mm/mV, respectively.

Interpretation should follow once it is established that the ECG tracing was correctly acquired and using the recommended standardized settings. If any of these criteria are absent, adjust the electrodes/settings and acquire a new tracing.

# 17.7.2 Rhythm: How to Know If It Is a Sinus Rhythm?

A sinus rhythm means that the SA node determines the ongoing atrioventricular sfrequency and that the electrical impulse are properly following the heart's conduction system. To identify a sinus rhythm, several criteria should be checked:

- 1. The P-wave is present in all leads.
- 2. The P-wave precedes every QRS complex, and a QRS complex follows every P-wave.
- 3. The P-wave is positive in leads II, III and aVF, and negative in aVR, in the absence of dextrocardia (which is extremely rare).
- 4. The morphology of the P-wave is the same in all the waveforms of the same lead.
- 5. The R-R interval is constant across waveforms.
- 6. Cardiac electrical rhythm between 60–100 bpm.
- 7. QRS complex duration is between 80–100 ms (in the absence of a bundle branch block).

It should be noted that the absence of one of these criteria does not mean the predominant rhythm is not a sinus rhythm, given that several rhythms may be present in the same ECG trace, but it should warrant further investigation. A common example of this is the presence of a physiologic respiratory

sinus arrhythmia, where during inspiration, the R-R interval gets narrower, and during exhalation, the R-R interval gets wider. This artifact can be easily eliminated by asking the patient to hold their breath for 10 seconds while the ECG trace is being acquired.

#### 17.7.3 The Functional Electrical Heart Rate

The functional electric heart rate can be estimated using the R-R interval measurements and a few different formulas in the setting of a constant R-R interval and a known ECG printing speed. The first step is to find an R-wave that falls precisely in a marker of the millimeter. Then, find the R-R interval by counting the small squares (millimeter markers) between the selected R-wave and the R-wave of the next waveform. Once this number is obtained, we can input it into the formula listed below. As the heart rate is measured in beats per minute (bpm), but the usual ECG tracing is only 10 seconds, we must adjust the time scale to find the heart rate. Given that one small square is 0.04 seconds, this means that 25 small squares are 1 second, and 1500 small squares are 60 seconds (1 minute). This means that if we divide 1500 by the number of small squares (mm) in the R-R interval, we will obtain the heart rate:

Heart Rate = 
$$\frac{1,500}{\text{# of small squares in R} - \text{R interval}}$$

Another method, popularized for the rapidness to know if the heart rate is within the normal range, this method goes as follows: first, find an R-wave that falls precisely in a marker of 5 millimeters (big square marker), then count the number of big squares between the present R-wave and the next. Each big square between the R-wave represents a cardiac frequency (300, 150, 100, 75, 60, 50 ...). This method provides the clinician with a fast approximation that the heart rate is within normocardia (60–100 bpm): i.e., if the R-R interval is within 3–5 big squares. Importantly, the estimation of the heart rate described here only applies to the electrical heart rate. Discrepancies between the electrical heart rate and the pulse must be further investigated.

# 17.7.4 The Electrical Frontal QRS Axis of the Heart

The direction and magnitude of the overall dipoles of the ventricular depolarization at any instant are also known as the heart's *frontal QRS electrical axis*, which can be plotted as a vector originating in the center of Einthoven's triangle such that the direction of the dipole is typically assessed in degrees. Considering that the bipolar lead I (DI) has a

horizontal disposition from RA to LA, this horizontal "line" is considered the baseline, or 0° line. Geometrically, all internal angles of Einthoven's triangle add up to 180°. Considering this, the line that composes the left margin of the Einthoven triangle (from RA to LL, composing lead DII) has a 60° angle with respect to the baseline. Similarly, the line that composes the right margin of the Einthoven triangle (from LA to LL, composing lead DIII) also has a 60° angle with respect to the baseline. The remaining 60° corresponds to the inferior angle of Einthoven's triangle. The lines composed by leads DII and DIII are rearranged to meet at the center of the baseline (Fig. 17.10).

Similar lines are created for each lead, to add the unipolar derivates for a more precise estimation of the QRS electrical axis. Given that the negative electrode from any unipolar leads is the aggregate of the two remaining ones (i.e., the negative electrode for a VF is the aggregate of aVR and aVL signals), a vertical line perpendicular to the 0 line is created for aVF. Two lines with a 30-degree angle are created from aVR in the direction of the intersection between aVL and aVF, and between aVL and the intersection between aVF and aVR.

The overlapping of these lines creates a fgure similar to an asterisk or star (Fig. 17.10b) that serves as a guide to manually obtain the frontal QRS electrical axis. Each point from this star represents an additional 30°. By convention, the degrees on the bottom of the 0° line receive a positive (+) sign. In contrast, the degrees in the superior part of the 0° line receive a negative (−) sign.

Several methods can be employed to estimate where the frontal QRS electrical axis is oriented. A simple one is to search for the frontal lead where the QRS waveform is most iso-biphasic (indicating that the vector dipole is initially approaching and then distancing from the electrode). Then, using the "star" (Fig. 17.10b), we fnd which one is the perpendicular lead (i.e., if the most iso-biphasic QRS waveform is found in DII, the perpendicular lead is aVL). After this, we observe the waveform in the perpendicular lead QRS waveform: if it is predominantly positive—meaning the dipole vector is approaching the positive electrode, then the frontal QRS electrical axis is approaching the degrees corresponding to the positive pole; on the contrary, if the QRS waveform is negative, meaning the dipole vector is distancing from the positive pole and approaching the negative, then the frontal QRS electrical axis is approaching the degrees corresponding to the negative pole. For example:

- Most iso-biphasic QRS of the frontal leads are found on DI. The perpendicular lead to DI is aVF. If the QRS is predominantly positive in aVF, the frontal QRS electrical axis is +90°.
- *or*

- Most iso-biphasic QRS of the frontal leads are found on DIII. The perpendicular lead to DIII is aVR. If the QRS is predominantly negative in aVR, the frontal QRS electrical axis is +30°.
- *or*
- Most iso-biphasic QRS of the frontal leads are found on aVR. The perpendicular lead to aVR is DIII. If the QRS is predominantly positive in DIII, the frontal QRS electrical axis is +120°.

The frontal QRS electrical axis can also be estimated using the defection (positive or negative) and heights of the R-waves. #### **17.7.5 The ECG Waveform, Segments and Intervals**

Of clinical importance in the ECG waveform are several parameters (regions), which include the P-R interval, QRS duration, S-T segment, and the Q-T interval (Fig. 17.5)**.** The P-R interval is measured from the beginning of the P-wave to the beginning of the QRS complex. It is normally 120 to 200 ms long. This measures the time it takes for an impulse to travel from sinoatrial excitation through the atria and atrioventricular node and induces the general onset of ventricular depolarization.

Determination of the P-R interval provides useful information as to whether a patient may be eliciting some degree of heart block. Elongated P-R intervals (longer than ~200 ms) in the absence of other disturbances indicate that conduction through the atrioventricular node is delayed (frst-degree AV block). Conduction in the atrioventricular node may even prolong with each beat and fail (2nd Degree AV Block, Mobitz I) or fail spontaneously, which would elicit a P-wave without a subsequent QRS complex before the next P-wave (2nd Degree AV block, Mobitz II). An ECG trace showing P-waves and QRS complexes beating independently indicates the atrioventricular node has ceased transmitting impulses (3rddegree AV block). The PR interval can also be found to have a duration of <120ms in pre-excitation syndromes, such as Lown-Ganong-Levine and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndromes. For additional information on atrioventricular blocks and pre-excitation syndromes, see Chaps. 11 and 18.

The QRS wave indicates how long the overall ventricular depolarization process takes, typically around 80 to 100 ms. A prolonged QRS complex (> 100 ms) may indicate a conduction block or delays in electrical propagation through the bundle branches or Purkinje fbers that run through the ventricle. Delays or alterations in the conduction pathway through the ventricles can impact ventricular contraction and possibly cause desynchrony between the right and left ventricles. This, in turn, will negatively impact overall cardiac function and, ultimately, hemodynamics.

The S-T segment is the period when the ventricles are completely depolarized and is measured from the S-wave to the beginning of the T-wave. Depression of the S-T segment (below the baseline) can indicate regional ventricular ischemia. S-T segment elevation or depression can also be used as an indication of many other abnormalities, including myocardial infarction, coronary artery disease, and/or pericarditis. For patients with established coronary artery disease, it is important to compare current ECG recordings to historical ones to establish if any new regions of ischemia or infarction exist.

The Q-T interval by convention is measured from the beginning of the QRS complex to the end of the T-wave; this is the time segment from when the ventricles begin their depolarization to the time when they have repolarized to their resting potential and is normally close to 400 ms in duration. Prolonged Q-T intervals (which are usually no more than 40% of the cardiac cycle length) are normally an indication of delayed repolarization of the cardiomyocytes, possibly caused by irregular opening or closing of sodium or potassium channels. Importantly, a long Q-T interval may indicate that a patient is at risk of developing ventricular tachyarrhythmia, as discussed above. (see Chaps. 18, 22 and 32 for more detailed descriptions of such).

## **17.8 Computer ECG Analyses**

The use of computers for the analyses of ECG began in the 1960s. In 1961, Hubert Pipberger described the frst computer analysis of ECG signals, an analysis that could recognized basic abnormal activities. Computer-assisted ECG analyses were introduced into the general clinical setting in the 1970s. Subsequently, the use of computers, microcomputers, and microelectronic circuits has had a huge impact on the feld of electrocardiography. Further the sizes of such equipment has been drastically reduced: i.e., to pocket size or even smaller for some applications. This has allowed broader deployment of continuous monitoring of patients over much longer time periods, which has greatly helped in the diagnoses of patients with infrequent cardiac symptoms (e.g., paroxysmal arrhythmias).

Computer programs can also provide summaries of information recorded from the ECG, including heart rate, multiple types of arrhythmias, and specifc variations in QRS, ST, QT, or T patterns. Additionally, there are several computer techniques that have been developed to further identify patients with cardiac dysfunction and previous myocardial infarctions that may be at risk for sudden death, e.g., signalaveraged ECGs, microvolt T-wave alternans, and/or heart rate variability. Signal-averaged ECGI is a technique that uses computer analysis to identify late potentials appearing at the end of the QRS complex; these late potentials have been associated with an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias. Microvolt T wave alternans is another method that may be used to identify patients at risk for ventricular arrhythmias. This technique measures the presence of small changes in T-wave amplitudes that occur on an alternating beat-tobeat basis. Similarly, heart rate variability analysis measures subtle variations in ventricular rate to assess a patient's autonomic status (modulation of heart rate due to the infuence of the autonomic nervous system). It is noteworthy that patients with low heart rate variability following a myocardial infarction may be at higher risk for sudden death. Studies have shown these techniques have high negative predictive values. They have been proposed as screening techniques but are still not widely used in the clinical setting, but with applications utilizing artifcial intelligence this will likely change.

#### **17.9 Long-Term ECG Recording Devices**

Currently, there are four general types of devices that are utilized in the collection of long-term electrocardiographic recordings: continuous recorders, event recorders, real-time monitoring systems, and implantable recorders.

Continuous recorders, like the Holter monitor described above, are attached to the surface of the body and continuously record signals for a predetermined duration (usually 24 to 48 hours). Such systems record from at least three different ECG leads. When using this method, patients must also record their daily activities and the time of any onset of symptoms (e.g., were they beginning to exercise?).

Event recorders are another type of instrument used for ECG collection. There are two basic types of event recorders—a symptom event recorder and a loop memory recorder. A symptom event recorder is activated by the patient to store a recording when their symptoms appear and, therefore, contains a post-event ECG recording. This type of recorder can be worn continuously or can be a hand-held device that records the rhythm when the unit is activated and placed on the precordium. Pre-event, or loop recorders, are similar to post-event recorders, but a memory loop is used to enable the recording of information several minutes before and after the onset of symptoms. Today, there are many different event recorder devices, including those connected to traditional ECG wires or devices consisting of a wireless patch electrode that adheres to the skin and can store and transmit data. A patient activator to the prompt recording of the ECG during symptoms can be included on the event recorder itself or as a separate device, worn like a wristwatch, for example. Data from event recorders can also be transmitted wirelessly so that physicians can view the data electronically. The trend has been toward decreasing size, maximizing information, and improving data review by physicians and perhaps patients. Examples of loop recorders are shown in Figs. 17.14 and 17.15. For additional discussion of such technologies, see Chap. 21.

The third type of ECG instrument in clinical use today is a real-time monitoring system. Data are not recorded within the device, but rather are transmitted trans-telephonically to a distal recording station. Such instruments are commonly used for monitoring of patients that have a potentially dangerous condition, so the technician can quickly identify the rhythm abnormality and make arrangements for proper management of the condition.

#### **17.10 Summary**

As cardiomyocytes depolarize and propagate action potentials throughout the heart, an electrical dipole is created. By utilizing the resultant electrical feld present in the body, electrodes can be placed around the heart to measure potential differences as the heart depolarizes and repolarizes. This measurement gives rise to the ECG, normally consisting of the P-wave via atrial depolarization, the QRS complex for ventricular depolarization, and the T-wave associated with ventricular repolarization.
