**21 Insertable Cardiac Monitoring**

Paul Krause

#### **Abstract**

Insertable Cardiac Monitors (ICMs) have become an important tool for caring for patients with cardiac disease. ICMs can be used to identify cardiac arrhythmias in patients that otherwise such symptoms would be challenging to identify. While other tools exist in the market for monitoring cardiac arrhythmias, the ICM is unmatched in its accuracy and reliability to convey information to clinicians and is especially useful for moderate and highrisk cardiac patients. Over their 25+ years of clinical use, the technologies incorporated by ICM manufacturers have led to more accurate arrhythmia assessments, minimally invasive form factors, longer-lived batteries, and seamless data collection mechanisms. Today's clinical community has an ever-increasing focus on the use of patient data to guide and enhance care, which makes the ICM a unique and powerful tool for the future of medicine.

ICMs can produce and wirelessly send insightful recordings of the given patient's ECG when an arrhythmia occurs or when the patient feels a symptom (i.e., self-trigger recording).

Many ICMs are used for patients who are experiencing syncope (i.e., episodes of fainting) that may be brought about by an underlying arrhythmia [1]. Having a retrospective recording of the patient's ECG at the time of the syncopal event allows the clinician to determine whether an underlying arrhythmia may be the culprit. Knowing that an infrequently or frequently occurring arrhythmia is present helps the clinician to assess whether a cardiac ablation or implantation of a pacemaker or implantable defbrillator may be helping in eliminating their syncope episodes. # **21.2 Clinical Problems Addressed by ICMs**

ICMs are medical devices that have the potential to guide the clinical management of any patient who suffers from an arrhythmia or is suspected of having an arrhythmia. The patient population that could be helped by an ICM is large and broad [6].

In practice, however, the ICM is just one of a growing list of technologies capable of monitoring arrhythmias, and not all patients will opt for the required procedure for getting an ICM. Signifcantly, current FDA restrictions and insurance considerations limit ICM uses to specifc clinical scenarios for which there is established clinical evidence supporting their uses. As a result, ICM use is most prevalent in patients who have a high-risk arrhythmia, or one that happens infrequently and is tough to uncover. However, emerging research and clinical case studies show cases where ICMs help to diagnose patients beyond their traditional uses.

## **21.2.1 Tools for Monitoring Arrhythmias**

Figure 21.2 shows the wide array of tools that are commonly used for monitoring arrhythmias. All have common characteristics, such as the ability to continuously record ECG signals, and the ability to share stored data with a clinician for later review. These tools differ, however, in the degree of accuracy in their associated clinical assessments that they provide, as well as the seamlessness with which the data review is integrated with clinical practice.

At one end of the spectrum lie consumer devices that can assess one's ongoing heart rate. Some wrist-worn devices (e.g., the Apple Watch or Fitbit monitors) measure a pulse using an integrated optical photoplethysmography sensor that assesses a pulse rate from the patient's wrist [7, 8]. These devices can assess periodic pulse rates on a near-continuous basis using the optical plethysmography sensor, but cannot collect a full ECG recording on a continuous basis. Other devices (e.g., the AliveCor Kardia® device) can record ECGs but generally require the person using it to touch an electrically active portion of the device to complete the electrical circuit needed to collect an ECG [9]. # **21.2.2 Indicated Uses of ICMs**

In the United States, Medtronic ICMs are indicated in the following patient populations [14]:

- Patients with clinical syndromes or situations at increased risk of cardiac arrhythmias.
- Patients who experience transient symptoms such as dizziness, palpitation, syncope, and chest pain that may suggest a cardiac arrhythmia.

Indication statements for other countries and for manufacturers' ICMs are very similar. This indication statement is relatively broad and encompasses a much larger patient group than is typically chosen to receive ICMs. Today, Medicare and private insurance providers will generally only reimburse ICM procedures in patients with conditions with well-established clinical evidence supporting its effcacy and cost-effectiveness (i.e., those conditions described in Sects. 21.2.3 and 21.2.4).

In practice, the choice of which ICM manufacturer's model to use in a patient is usually driven by features and functionality, and not by differences in the indication statements. The sole exception to this is use in pediatric patients. Only one manufacturer (Medtronic) currently has an indication statement that is inclusive of pediatrics (in this case, patients at least two years old).

# **21.2.3 ICM Use in Patients with Syncope**

The original use of ICMs is to identify a potential arrhythmia in patients with syncope, more commonly referred to as "fainting." Over a third of patients may experience a syncope event within their lifetimes [15]. Fainting spells can often lead to injury and may come with signifcant lifestyle limitations on the patient (e.g., restrictions in driving a motor vehicle). Understanding the underlying cause of syncope so that an appropriate treatment can be initiated is a signifcant and important problem for the clinician, the patient, and the patient's caregivers.

Syncopal events are transient loss of consciousness caused by a lack of perfusion in the brain [16]. Often, the interruption in cranial perfusion is precipitated by a cardiac arrhythmia such as a heart block, a bradyarrhythmia, or a ventricular fbrillation that prevents effective ventricular pumping. However, syncope can be initiated by other nonarrhythmic means, including orthostatic hypotension, seizures, and mechanisms mediated by neurologic refex mechanisms. Figure 21.4 shows examples of arrhythmias that can precede a syncopal event.

If a syncopal event is preceded by a cardiac arrhythmia it can be treated effectively with a therapeutic implantable device. A heart block or bradyarrhythmia can be treated by a pacemaker that initiates electrical stimulation in the absence of normal sinus rhythm [17]. Patients at risk of ventricular fbrillation can be implanted with an ICD that can detect and shock a tachyarrhythmia back to normal sinus rhythm [17]. Ideally, the clinician obtains an ECG recording from the time of syncope, a gold standard known as "symptom-rhythm correlation." However, because syncopal events can, in some patients, be relatively rare and unpredictable, this correlation can be diffcult to obtain without the use of extended cardiac monitoring. Several studies [18] have shown that the prognostic value of extended monitoring provided by ICMs is superior to that of shorter-term solutions like Holter Monitoring. Nevertheless, as noted above, many clinicians will start with these less invasive and cheaper modalities before progressing to insertion of an ICM, often compelled by insurance requirements.

## **21.2.4 ICM Monitoring of AF**

Atrial fbrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia, with an estimated global prevalence of 50 million in 2020 [3]. It is characterized by rapid, uncoordinated electrical activation of the upper chambers of the heart. The unorganized, chaotic atrial rhythm leads to a lack of coordinated pumping in the upper chambers of the heart and can conduct through the AV node to the ventricles, leading to a rapid and erratic ventricular rate.

While AF is generally not immediately life-threatening, it can signifcantly impact the patient's health and well-being. The fast and irregular ventricular rate can cause symptoms, often described as "palpitations" or as a "pounding" sensation. In patients who experience AF regularly, these symptoms can have a signifcant impact on their quality of life.

The rapid and irregular ventricular rates can also impact the patient's long-term cardiac health. It has been shown that patients experiencing a high degree of AF are at a higher likelihood of Heart Failure [19], and may have an increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias [20].

Finally, the presence of AF increases stroke risk [21]. The ineffcient pumping of the atria during AF leads to pooling of the blood, which facilitates clotting that can lead to a stroke.

Unfortunately, AF is often under-recognized, especially in patients that are asymptomatic to its presence. Traditional tools for monitoring heart rhythm like Holter recorders and MCOT often miss episodes of AF in patients, because they can only be worn for days or weeks at a time [22]. Figure 21.5 demonstrates this in a patient; only ICMs have the ability to continuously monitor patients over years, making them much better suited to assessing patients with intermittent AF. Because they are so well suited to identifying AF, ICMs can be ideal tools for assessing the cause of a patient's palpitations. Again, in patients who have undergone a cardiac ablation, an ICM can be useful in assessing the effcacy of the procedure (see also Chaps. 31 and 32).

Finally, patients who have experienced a stroke are often assessed with an ICM because un-diagnosed AF raises the risk of a second stroke. If the presence of an underlying AF is known, that patient may be treated with antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications or may undergo a cardiac ablation to eliminate the AF.

The CRYSTAL-AF study evaluated the impact of ICM use in patients who had a prior cryptogenic stroke; that is, a stroke whose cause and origin is unknown [23]. In this study, AF was detected in 8.9% of the patients monitored by ICMs, vs. 1.4% of patients in the control group. Importantly, this increased rate of AF discovery suggests that monitoring with an ICM was superior to conventional approaches.

The STROKE-AF study also evaluated ICM uses in patients with a prior stroke, this time in patients with strokes of known origin [24]. Similar to the CRYSTAL-AF study, ICM-monitored patients also elicited a much higher rate of detected AF (21.7% vs. 2.4%).

While it may not be surprising that a medical device capable of continuous patient monitoring did a better job in uncovering arrhythmias, these studies were important milestones that spurred adoption of ICM use in post-stroke patients. Interestingly, neither study showed that ICM use signifcantly reduced the likelihood of a secondary stroke. Nonetheless, these studies have established the ICM as an important tool in the management of stroke patients.

### **21.2.5 Advanced Uses of ICMs**

While ICMs are most commonly used in patients with Syncope and AF, there is growing evidence demonstrating their utility in other cardiac and non-cardiac-related disease states. While the initial research shows promise, there has not yet been enough evidence demonstrating clinical effectiveness and safety to make these commonly used.

A patient diagnosed with epilepsy may actually be experiencing syncope caused by an underlying arrhythmia, and the use of extended cardiac monitoring can be very useful in the differential diagnosis [25, 26]. Another emerging area of interest is the use of ICMs in assessing a patient's risk of serious ventricular tachycardias. While ICMs cannot deliver therapy to treat ventricular tachycardia, they have been shown to be a useful tool in assessing risk in patients who are being considered for an ICD [27]. There are many patients in the "gray zone" of VT risk; that is, they have some VT risk factors but nothing that defnitively indicates them for an ICD. The presence of premature ventricular contractions and non-sustained ventricular tachycardias recorded by an ICM can be powerful information to help the clinician as he or she weighs whether to implant an ICD.

### **21.3 A Brief History of ICMs**

While implantable cardiac devices (such as pacemakers and implantable defbrillators) have long been capable of continuous monitoring of rhythm, prior to the development of ICMs all rhythm-focused devices required the placement of a lead wire into the myocardium. The development of the ICM represented an easier, more cost-effective, and less invasive approach to cardiac monitoring when no cardiac stimulation was required for the patient. Furthermore, the track record of successful monitoring of cardiac arrhythmias without the need for an invasive myocardial lead has led to alternative approaches for other types of cardiac devices, including the subcutaneous ICD [28].

# **21.3.1 The Invention of ICMs**

The inspiration for the original ICM came during a conversation over breakfast after a hockey game in the 1980s. Dr. George Klein was a prominent cardiac electrophysiologist in London, ON, and, in true Canadian fashion, got to know a Medtronic engineer, Randal Schulhauser, through their weekly hockey game. Over a post-game breakfast, Dr. Klein and Randal discussed a recent plane crash in nearby Lake St. Claire. The news that week was dominated by the search for the plane's fight recorder, commonly known as the "black box." Their discussion led Dr. Klein to ask Randal why Medtronic couldn't produce a black box for his patient's hearts. He had several patients who were fainting unexpectedly and was having trouble understanding what led to his patient's fainting spells. Much like a government investigator after a plane crash, he wanted data to help him reconstruct the cause of the event.

Dr. Klein knew that such clinical observations in his practice were not unique, and estimated that every cardiac electrophysiologist cared for a large number of patients with syncope of unknown cause. With this information, he was able to gain support from Medtronic leadership that there existed a market for this type of cardiac device. Medtronic initiated a project to produce a small lot of prototype cardiac recorders that could be used in a pilot clinical study. Randal and the Medtronic team modifed one of Medtronic's existing pacemakers, as shown in Fig. 21.7.

The device provided a single lead ECG, using two electrodes highlighted by the arrows in Fig. 21.7, separated by roughly 45 mm. One ECG electrode was formed in the pacemaker's polyurethane header, and a second was created by the titanium case. To maximize the separation of the two ECG electrodes, all but a small circle of the case was insulated using parylene, an insulative polymer. Many of Dr. Klein's contemporaries believed a single ECG recording from a vector of such short spacing would not be suffcient to diagnose their patients. However, the preliminary clinical study demonstrated the effectiveness of such monitoring [29], and it's interesting to note that, even today, many modern ICMs continue to follow the 45 mm electrode spacing convention set in 1987!

Even after a successful clinical study with a prototype monitor, it was not clear that any medical device manufacturers would choose to develop a commercial version of the product. These new models had relative strengths and weaknesses compared to those on the market and provided clinicians with new choices for their patients. Also during this time period, ICM usage started to become a more common part of cardiology practices, with nursing staff trained and dedicated to following the ICM data that was routinely sent through remote monitors.

In 2014, the release of a new device model from Medtronic, the Reveal LINQ™, represented an infection point in the utilization of ICM technology in patients. The minimally invasive form factor and enhanced data communication made the implant procedure very appealing to both clinicians and patients. # **21.4 Technical Operation of ICMs**

ICMs are, in essence, miniaturized computers that monitor every patient's heartbeat over the course of 4+ years. They are complex, highly-engineered machines, but their main functions can be summarized quite simply as: they collect an ECG, analyze the patient's heart rhythm, and communicate it to a clinician, alerting them when it may be abnormal.

### **21.4.1 ECG Collection**

The foundation of successful ICM monitoring is clear ECG signals. A clinician cannot diagnose an arrhythmia in a patient if the given ECG is uninterpretable. The frst step in collecting an ECG is amplifying the signal measured by the ECG electrodes, as shown in Fig. 21.11. An ECG is a biopotential signal sensed between an opposing pair of electrodes. In the case of a traditional surface ECG signal recordings, the electrodes are adhesives placed on the skin in traditional lead locations (i.e., the left and right arm, left and right leg, and V1-V6). In the case of an ICM, the ECG electrodes are embedded into the case of the device. In Fig. 21.1, the black, toenail-shaped components at each end of the device are the ECG electrodes.

Even though the electrodes used in an ICM are located below the surface of the skin, the signal collected very closely approximates an ECG collected on the cutaneous surface [30]. However, because the ECG electrodes on an ICM are more closely spaced than the standard ECG electrodes, they pick up a signal that is approximately 5x lower in amplitude than would be typical of a cutaneous system. As a result, the frst step in collecting ECG is amplifying it so that it appears larger and more readable for the clinician.

The next step in the process of collecting ECG is removing the noise. A high-quality ECG is one in which the signifcant markers (including the P- and T-waves and QRS

"Noise" refers to electrical signals that detract from the desired ECG signal, and can be caused by myopotentials from the skeletal muscle surrounding the ICM, or electromagnetic interference from the patient's environment. The best, most clear ECGs are ones in which the amplitudes of the QRS complexes greatly outweigh the amplitude of any noise that is present. ECGs with large QRS complexes and small amounts of noise are most interpretable for clinicians, and also present the best input signal for automated heart rhythm analysis (as described in the following section). Examples of ECGs with clear and noisy signals are shown in Fig. 21.12.

Noise removal is accomplished in modern ICMs by a variety of techniques, including bandpass fltering [31]. Bandpass fltering is a process in which parts of the signal occurring at frequencies inside a zone of interest are accentuated, and those outside the zone of interest are removed. Applying a bandpass flter to an ECG has the effect of signifcantly "cleaning up" the signal, by accentuating frequencies that contribute to the P-waves and QRS complexes and removing noise. Surface ECG recorders found in cardiology clinics use bandpass flters in the range of 0.05–150 Hz [32]. However, ECGs collected subcutaneously have unique sources of noise, so ICM manufacturers have tuned their bandpass range slightly from that standard.

Some forms of noise (in particular, noise from skeletal muscle potentials) have content that overlaps the frequency zone of interest and thus cannot be easily eliminated using standard bandpass fltering techniques. Accordingly, no ECG recording collected is consistently noise-free, and ICM recordings are often plagued by some level of noise.

Since the level of noise cannot be controlled, clinicians focus on maximizing the amplitude of the signals they are interested in seeing, to ensure they are not obscured when noise occurs. The amplitudes of a patient's ECG signals can also vary based on where the implanting clinician has chosen to insert the ICM in the upper chest. Section 21.5.1 will describe approaches used when inserting an ICM as means to maximize the amplitudes of the collected ECG signals.

### **21.4.2 ECG Rhythm Analysis**

For the vast majority of the monitoring period, an ECG signal tells us very little about the patient. Note, when the heart rhythm is normal, the ECG signal is of little interest to the clinician. As a result, the ICM performs continuous analysis of the heart rhythm, so that periods of atypical heart rhythm can be captured and stored for the clinician to review.

The basis of automated heart rhythm analysis is identifying QRS complexes. As shown in Fig. 21.13, the bandpass fltered ECG signal is used as an input to the QRS detection algorithm. The frst step is an additional round of bandpass fltering, to further reduce unwanted noise from the ECG signal. After fltering, the signal is rectifed so that its entirety is detected as a positive value. The positive, rectifed signal is then compared against a threshold. Because QRS complexes are typically the largest component of an ECG signal, when the signal is large enough to exceed the predetermined threshold, it is taken as an indication that a QRS complex is present. If determined to be present, the patient's heart rate is estimated by measuring the time interval from which the prior QRS complex had occurred.

Manufacturers have developed sophisticated algorithms to avoid falsely detecting a QRS complex because its threshold was tripped by noise or another component of the ECG. One manufacturer applies what is referred to as a "blanking period" for a fraction of a second after a detected QRS complex, so that the QRS complex does not cause multiple threshold crossings, as shown in Fig. 21.14. After blanking, the threshold is slowly adjusted downward, to avoid being tripped by T- and P-waves as shown in Fig. 21.14. Other manufacturers use approaches that are distinct in their detailed operations, but similar in intent.

Once the heart rate is assessed, algorithms are applied to determine when a sustained arrhythmia is present. ICM algorithms detect the presence of bradyarrhythmia, tachyarrhythmia, and asystolic pauses. Bradyarrhythmia-focused algorithms will determine that an arrhythmia is present when a specifc number of beats have occurred slower than a preset rate cutoff rate. The number of beats and rate cutoffs are typically selected by the clinician, based on what he or she feels may be signifcant for that given patient, using a programmer (as described later, in Sect. 21.5.1 below). For example, a patient's ICM may be programmed to declare a bradyarrhythmia whenever eight consecutive beats occur slower than at a rate of 40 bpm.

Tachyarrhythmia-focused algorithms determine that an arrhythmia is present when a specifc number of beats have occurred faster than a programmed rate cutoff, chosen by the clinician. For example, a patient's ICM may be programmed to declare a tachyarrhythmia whenever 16 consecutive beats occur faster than 200 bpm. Because tachyarrhythmias can be more challenging to detect, some manufacturers do not require the fast beats to be consecutive. In these cases, a tachyarrhythmia may be declared whenever 30 out of 40 beats occur faster than 200 bpm, to allow for some possibility of "missed" QRS complexes.

Asystolic, pause-focused algorithms, determine that an arrhythmia is present when no QRS complex occurs for a longer-than-expected duration. This is usually caused by some sort of block in the AV conduction within the given patient's heart, or a block within the sinus node (see Chap. 11 for more details on the conduction system of the heart). For example, an ICM may declare an asystolic pause when no QRS complex is seen detected for over 5 s.

However, it is very diffcult to discern the atrial rate from an ECG, because the P-waves present during AF are typically very small, and oftentimes not visible at all. Two performance measures characterize the quality of an arrhythmia detection algorithm. The "sensitivity" of an algorithm characterizes how good an algorithm is at picking up real events, and is defned as the percentage of time a real clinical arrhythmia is automatically detected and stored by the ICM. For example, a bradycardia detection algorithm with sensitivity of 99% will, on average, detect 99 of every 100 true clinical bradycardias. The "positive predictive value" (or PPV) of an algorithm is derived to characterize how reliable a given algorithm is at ignoring false events, and is defned as the percentage of time an algorithm detects an event that it really is a true, clinical event. # **21.5 Clinical Management of ICM's**

While ICMs are much simpler in operation than many medical devices, there are still well-established medical procedures to effectively manage their use, including: (1) inserting a device in that patient, (2) following his or her data remotely, and (3) ultimately explanting or removing the device at "end of life" (battery) or before.

# **21.5.1 Inserting an ICM**

Today, one of the appealing aspects of a currently employed ICM is the ease of which it can be placed in a patient. Most

medical devices require a signifcant amount of cutting, suturing, and pocket formation to facilitate the implantation of the device. Because ICMs are so small, they are easily inserted using a simple procedure that can be typically performed within minutes, with only utilizing local anesthesia. In fact, the product category is referred to as Insertable Cardiac Monitors to reinforce that their placement procedure involves only a small insertion that is less invasive than the implantation procedures required for other types of medical devices. Also noteworthy, the simple insertion procedure enabled by the smaller size led to a change in insurer's reimbursement coding for ICMs. The procedure was reclassifed from a major to a minor surgical procedure, which allowed for a wider range of clinicians to implant ICMs.

The most challenging aspect of inserting an ICM is in determining its optimal placement. As described in Sect. 21.4.1, the ICM's subcutaneous electrodes are more closely spaced than surface electrodes used in traditional ECG monitoring. These more closely spaced electrodes will produce an ECG signal smaller in amplitude than a standard confguration, but in most patients an insertion location can be found that produces a signal of adequate amplitude. Through continued clinical practice, it has been found that an ICM placed in the zone shown in Fig. 21.21, at a 45 degree angle to the sternum, will typically produce ECG signals with maximal R-wave amplitudes [34].

The depth at which the ICM is inserted will also impact the success of the procedure for the patient. Too deep of an implant may result in ECG electrodes that are too closely adjacent to muscle or adipose tissue, which can negatively impact the quality of the ECG signals that are sensed. Too shallow of an implant can yield a good ECG signal but be more visible under the skin, which may also be uncomfortable for the patient, and increase the likelihood of the device eroding through the skin. Manufacturers provide recommendations for depth of implant that attempt to strike an appropriate balance, generally around 8 mm below the patient's skin surface [14].

Once a location and angle of insertion is chosen for a given patient, the implant location is cleaned and a local anesthetic applied. After the insertion target has been anesthetized, a skin opening is made, using a supplied tool (e.g., shown in Fig. 21.22), often facilitated by a gentle pinch of the skin. The tool makes a controlled incision at a width and depth that is also suitable for insertion of the ICM. Next, the insertion tool is inserted through the incision and advanced to make a subcutaneous pocket for the ICM. Once the tool has been inserted as far as possible, the plunger is inserted and the tool is fipped 180 degrees. The fipping of the tool causes the incision to "tent" in a way that facilitates an easy entry of the ICM through the incision site. The ICM comes pre-loaded in the insertion tool, and is slowly advanced using the plunger, while holding the cutaneous surface to facilitate its entry. When the ICM is completely advanced, it will be completely enclosed under the skin and the insertion tool can be removed. (Figure 21.23 shows the process in its entirety.) A wound closure adhesive is usually suffcient to close and protect the pocket, though sutures can be used if desired.

# **21.5.2 Data Review**

The ICM's main clinical purpose is to record arrhythmias that may be of interest to the clinician. Accordingly, clinics utilizing ICMs have defned procedures for periodically reviewing data sent by the ICM for each patient within their clinic. While the ICM insertion procedure is very simple, often taking only minutes, consistent reviewing of ICM data can require an ongoing commitment of time, as well as resources from the cardiac care group.

It should be noted that early generations of ICMs required the patient to return to their care clinic, to have their data interrogated using a clinician programmer. The programmer (previously shown in Fig. 21.20) was the only means to interrogate the ECG data collected by the ICM, as wireless, remote monitoring protocols had not yet been developed. Because the patient usually only visited their clinic a few times each year, the ICM memory was often flled and potentially interesting ECG recordings were sometimes overwritten. Additionally, visiting the clinic for the sole purpose of having his or her data read was considered as an inconvenience for the patient, and consumed clinic time and resources.

The incorporation of wireless communication protocols into ICMs have greatly increased the ease of managing a patient's data review. The latest generation of ICMs send data (such as the example report shown in Fig. 21.24) on a daily or weekly basis, or when something of clinical signifcance has occurred. Today, some utilized ICM models also allow the clinician to make parameter adjustments (e.g., adjusting a bradycardia detection cutoff from 40 bpm to 35 bpm) to the patient's ICM over its wireless communication channel. This enables the clinician to make adjustments to the patient's settings without the patient having to come into the clinic, which can be a huge convenience for both the patient and healthcare providers. By making appropriate parameter adjustments, clinics care teams can avoid the transmissions of large numbers of false positives that can waste the clinic's time and potentially obscure clinicallyrelevant data.

# **21.5.4 ICM Removal**

At some point in the patient's life, the ICM's data is no longer of interest to the clinician (e.g., if a defnitive diagnosis has been made) or the device's battery has expired. When this occurs, a decision must be made by the clinician and patient whether to remove the ICM. Because the ICM is very small and often unnoticeable to the patient, the decision is often made to leave the device as-is.

If the patient elects to have it removed, it can be explanted using a procedure performed under local anesthetic. Because ICMs are so easy to insert, in many cases the device removal may be a more invasive procedure. Manufacturers have developed recommended procedures for performing the removal [14].

If the patient elects not to have the ICM removed after its battery end of life, there will be some ongoing considerations. For example, ICMs can be detected by metal screening detectors at airports and arenas, so the patient may need to continue to carry a device identifcation card. At the end of the patient's life, if he or she is cremated, the device will likely need to be removed to prevent its battery from rupturing in the cremator. ICMs are generally compatible with MRI (with some restrictions), so its presence should not signifcantly limit the availability of imaging options for the patient.

# **21.6 Future Directions**

"Digital health," or the use of data to guide patient care, is an exciting area of research within the medical community today. While many of the current technologies driving digital health today are wearable or consumer devices like the Apple Watch, ICM's have unique capabilities that make them especially powerful. Data from ICMs is provided continuously for years, without the need for the patient to recharge or otherwise interact with it. Data from ICMs comes from within the body, and can provide unique insights that wearable devices cannot. Finally, data from ICMs is "medical grade"; that is, regulated by FDA (or other global regulatory bodies, see Chap. 47) to ensure it is accurate and reliable. ICM data represents a unique opportunity for the digital health community to explore new frontiers in patient care.

#### **21.6.2 New ICMs for Other Disease States**

ICMs have traditionally utilized the ECG signal exclusively as its main biosensor data that is collected and analyzed. As a result and as noted above, ICMs have most often been used to diagnose and monitor patient populations that are at risk of arrhythmia, including patients with unexplained syncope and stroke. However, new ICM models have begun to incorporate additional biosensors, such as impedance sensors and accelerometers, which have the potential to broaden the types of clinical conditions that an ICM is capable of detecting and/or monitoring.

Recent clinical studies have utilized ICMs in patients with known Heart Failure, using special software that enabled and utilized an impedance sensor within the Medtronic Reveal LINQ™ ICM [39]. These incorporated sensors measured the body's impedance (that is, the opposition the body provides against an electrical current) to, be used, for example, to assess the given patient's thoracic congestion. Thoracic congestion is often a predecessor of a decompensation cardiac event that leads to the patient being hospitalized. The LINQ-HF study showed that a decrease in impedances, as measured by an implanted ICM, could predict a decompensation event. Changes in other data collected from the ICM, (including Heart Rate Variability and activity levels assessed by the ICM's accelerometer,) were also shown to correlate with presenting decompensation events. While this new monitored health status information is not available in ICMs today outside of clinical studies, future clinical workfows could proactively utilize ICMs in Heart Failure patients to identify those at risk of future or ongoing heart failure decompensations. For example and importantly, the "early warning signs" provided by ICMs could be used to adjust diuretic medications in patients before they end up in an emergency room with severe decompensation.

# **21.6.3 ICM Uses in Pre-clinical Animal Research or in Wildlife Management**

As ICM use has increased in the clinical care of humans, the interest in its use in animals by the veterinarian and animal research community has also grown. Veterinarians have the same need for monitoring cardiac risk in animals that clinicians have for their human patients: e.g., some zoo animals will present with cardiac disease. Additionally, scientists who study wildlife have a strong desire for insights into the animal's cardiac condition and activity levels that can be uniquely derived by employed ICMs. ICMs are especially useful tools for wildlife research and animal care, because alternatives available to humans (such as Holter monitors or wearable ECG recorders) are diffcult or impossible to use in such animals.

The frst use of ICMs in non-human species centered on the monitoring of wild brown black bears in northern Minnesota [40]. Scientists interested in monitoring the bears' heart rhythm and activity during hibernation used ICMs to gain new insights. Because the ICM was not designed specifcally for animals, some procedural adaptations are needed. Most animal ECGs differ in meaningful ways from those of the human ECG. The QRS complex, for example, may have a signifcantly different amplitude than is typical in humans and requires a different detection threshold programmed into the ICM. Additionally, the normal sinus rates of the animal may be signifcantly different from the human, so the typical rates used to trigger a bradycardia or tachycardia will need adjustments. Usually, R-wave and arrhythmia adjustments can be made using the ICM's clinician programmer, though in some cases custom changes to the ICM designs by the manufacturer are required.

Today, wireless communication in the wild can also present some challenges. Home monitors rely on cellular communication, which is often unreliable in remote, unpopulated areas. Similarly, animals monitored in captivity are often enclosed in buildings made of steel and concrete, which can present challenges for cellular communication as well. Additionally, the home monitors required to transmit data from the animal being monitored rely on AC power, which may not be available in the wild. One solution for addressing this is to use a combination of battery packs and solar cells to power the home monitor for a limited duration during periods that may be of greatest interest (e.g., during transitions in and out of hibernation in bears). As technology progresses, new capabilities will address many of the current challenges in these animal uses, making this an even more attractive tool for veterinarians and wildlife scientists.
