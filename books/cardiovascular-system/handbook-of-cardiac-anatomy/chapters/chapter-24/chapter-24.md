# **24 Monitoring and Managing the Critically Ill Patient in the Intensive Care Unit**

Kevin Kirkland, Fahd O. Arafat, and Gregory J. Beilman

*All exact science is dominated by the idea of approximation.*

Bertrand Russell (1870–1972)

#### **Abstract**

The need for better acquisition and monitoring of patient physiological information within and outside of healthcare settings is increasingly important as our healthcare system serves an ever-aging population of more critically ill patients. Monitors serve several purposes, including the identifcation of shock and abnormal cardiac physiology, evaluation of cardiovascular function, and/or to allow for optimizing titration of therapy. An important function of an effective monitoring device is the reliable detection of abnormal physiology. Despite much research on the uses of monitoring techniques in critical care, there is little evidence to support improved outcomes related to the routine uses of monitors. Mainstays of invasive monitoring in the intensive care unit (ICU), include central venous pressure monitoring and arterial pressure monitoring; with pulmonary arterial monitoring used less frequently. Recent trends in monitoring have included developments of less invasive monitoring techniques that yield a number of cardiovascular parameters potentially useful to clinicians. New noninvasive measures of tissue perfusion (e.g., sublingual capnometry and tissue hemoglobin saturation (StO2)) have signifcant potential for the identifcation and treatment of pathophysiologic states resulting in inadequate tissue perfusion. Developers of new monitors, despite facing regulatory requirements that are less stringent than those of drug manufacturers, will be expected to demonstrate the clinical effcacies of new devices. In the fnal analysis, the most important "monitor" is a critically thinking healthcare provider at the bedside carefully evaluating the patient's response to intervention and therapy.

# **24.1 Brief History of Advances in Critical Care**

The treatment of shock is closely related to experiences during times of war. Ambroise Pare, a French military surgeon practicing during the sixteenth century, frst described the ligature to control bleeding in 1545. During World War I, the need for operative interventions for bowel perforation became more prominent, with surgery available due to the accessibility of general anesthetics. The earlier discovery of blood types by Karl Landsteiner in 1901, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930, enabled the safe and practical use of blood transfusions for the resuscitation of combatinjured wounded warriors during World War II. Care advances utilized in the Korean War included new surgical techniques such as vascular anastomosis and new medical therapies including renal dialysis. Use of positive pressure ventilation and renal dialysis broadened during the Vietnam War, in response to an increase in the development of complications in patients with previously non-survivable injuries, who were now able to be resuscitated. Examples of this included acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and renal failure.

The frst described use of an intensive care unit (ICU) as a separate area to care for patients was in the early 1950s; during a poliomyelitis epidemic in Denmark. In the early 1960s, the frst coronary care unit was established in Kansas City, KS; with the observations that new techniques of cardiopulmonary resuscitation could reduce mortality in patients suffering myocardial infarction. Their use expanded to postsurgical patients during the mid-1960s, as more complicated surgical operations mandated closer observations of patients and more aggressive interventions in the ICU. More specifcally, physiological monitoring displays were introduced in the ICU in the 1970s. Unfortunately, monitoring displays have not changed substantially since then. In contrast, the last four decades have seen signifcant efforts and resources expended toward improving data display designs in other high-risk felds such as aviation and power plant control. These efforts have yielded marked improvements in the safety and effciency of air travel and nuclear power plant operations; the time has come to bring such innovations to the ICU.

Our ability to care for critically ill patients has greatly improved. The frst "monitors''were the fve senses of the physician. René Lanneac described the frst stethoscope, as an extension of one's sense of hearing, in the late eighteenth century. The development of neurosurgery as a specialty in the early twentieth century (and the need to monitor blood pressure during these operations) led to the invention of the sphygmomanometer. ECG was one of the very frst technical approaches to monitor patient physiology and was clinically developed in 1908 by the British cardiologist, Sir Thomas Lewis. The need to monitor the physiology of astronauts in space led to further innovations with the use of continuous electrocardiography (EKG) and other types of monitors. Some of today's most innovative monitors still employ elements of Lewis's original EKG strip chart. ICU monitoring techniques developed over the last four decades have resulted in signifcant improvements in our overall understanding of cardiac physiology and pathophysiology. The last three decades have seen a plethora of invasive and noninvasive monitoring tools developed for critical care use. Despite these tools, the most important machine available to the clinician remains one's fve senses, mandating a careful examination of patients on a frequent basis.

Therefore, the ideal monitor for the diagnosis of shock would be able to identify the rate of ATP production and turnover. ### **24.2.2 Evaluations of Cardiac Function**

Another reason for consideration of advanced monitoring is for frequent evaluation of cardiac function. In particular, serious illness in patients with underlying cardiac disease (e.g., cardiomyopathy, congestive heart failure, congenital heart disease, etc.) may require a more careful titration of therapies to prevent decompensation. Situations that commonly require invasive monitoring, include (1) traumatic injuries, (2) infections, (3) emergent and elective operations, and/or (4) decompensation of the underlying disease.

### **24.2.3 Titration of Vasoactive Therapy**

Many patients receive advanced monitoring due to hemodynamic instability or for the need to titrate vasoactive medications with other therapeutic purposes (e.g., optimization of cerebral perfusion in patients presenting with neurologic insult). Most vasoactive agents have short half-lives, requiring frequent dose adjustments to achieve monitoring targets. Therefore, though invasive, continuous hemodynamic monitoring is often unavoidable in order to achieve an appropriate clinical response to the therapy utilized.

Another important, but often overlooked, reason for invasive monitoring in the critically ill patient, is to allow for a more defnitive diagnosis and/or the decision for end point of treatment. Continuous data collection, even while the clinician is away from the bedside, can lend itself to making swift adjustments in care as needed, based on each individual's responses to therapies being used. This can allow the clinician to perform other critical duties (Fig. 24.1).

# **24.3 Monitors: Do They Help?**

Despite our modern reliance on monitors in the ICU, a number of monitoring end points commonly used, may show conficting data regarding benefts with respect to patient outcomes, including (1) continuous ECG monitoring, (2) pulse oximetry, (3) pulmonary artery catheters, and/or (4) intracranial pressure monitoring. For example, with regards to pulse oximetry, Pedersen et al. in the Cochrane database systems review of 2003, [1] noted that "the conficting subjective and objective results of the studies, despite an intense methodological collection of data from a relatively large population (>20,000 patients) indicates that the value of perioperative monitoring with pulse oximetry is questionable in relation to improved quality outcomes effectiveness and effcacy."

Another randomized, non-blinded study was performed in 2006, [2] which was designed to compare the effects of continuous and standard monitoring of pulse oximetry on patient outcomes in 1219 subjects. Ochroch and colleagues observed that there was no difference in the rates of ICU readmissions from the post-cardiothoracic surgery care foor, mortality rates, or overall estimation of costs of hospitalization between the continuous pulse oximetry (CPOX) and standard monitor groups. The same can be said regarding pulmonary artery catheters. Shah et al. [3] noted that "Despite almost 20 years of randomized controlled trials, a clear strategy leading to improved survival with a pulmonary artery catheter has not been devised." This has led to the near abandonment of the use of pulmonary artery catheters in many institutions.

One nuance with regard to such monitors is that the use of such devices will not change the outcome for a fatal disease, if there is no treatment available for that diagnosis [4]. For instance, while you can monitor the progression of end-stage organ function in a patient with metastatic cancer, it is unlikely that utilizing a monitor to guide therapy in such a patient will affect the ultimate survival outcome of the patient.

On the other hand, positive evidence demonstrating the benefcial effects of physiologic monitoring has been associated with signifcant decreases in anesthesia-related deaths, since the early 2000s. Anesthesia care is highly dependent on multiple physiologic monitors. As the use of continuous monitoring has increased, the anesthesia-related mortality rate of 1 per 20,000 reported in the late 1970s has decreased to a rate of 1 in 300,000, at the turn of the century. Most clinicians with expertise in this area would agree that much of this decrease in mortality is related to both a better understanding of pathophysiology and more widespread uses of continuous monitoring.

Telemedicine applied to the ICU is an innovative approach to providing critical care services and to treating critically ill patients, especially those residing in broad or remote geographic areas (Fig. 24.2). Their study looked at a single open model surgical ICU, that for a 16-week period, was provided with 24-hour off-site monitoring. Compared with the baseline periods prior to the intervention, the mortality rates, lengths of stay, and costs were all reduced. In another study, from a large university medical center, administrators retrospectively reviewed their data from 2011 and 2012, which showed a reduction in mortality rates from 6.5% before to 4.9% after the implantation of enhanced monitoring systems [6]. # **24.4 Invasive Monitoring Techniques in the ICU**

# **24.4.1 Central Venous Pressure (CVP) Monitoring**

Pressure monitoring in a central venous location, using a large bore catheter, is likely the most frequently utilized invasive monitoring technique in current ICUs. Central venous catheters allow an estimate of "cardiac preload," and are typically placed via the internal jugular or subclavian venous route (Fig. 24.3). These monitors are very accurate for the identifcation of situations in which cardiac outputs are affected by low preloads. However, the assumption is made when monitoring central venous pressures that this value is proportional to pulmonary artery pressures, which is proportional to left atrial pressures, which is proportional to left ventricular end-diastolic volumes. Unfortunately, there are many common conditions in ICU patients that may elevate CVPs, that do not relate to increased flling of the left ventricle. Such conditions include pneumonia, positive pressure ventilation, acute respiratory distress syndrome, pulmonary emboli, and others. Thus, one should have a strong concern for the clinical situation in which a high CVP does not correlate with the prior diagnosed clinical condition of the patient.

# **24.4.2 Arterial Blood Pressure Monitoring**

Arterial blood pressure monitoring can be performed using both noninvasive and invasive techniques. Noninvasive monitors have progressed signifcantly over the years and currently allow a hands-off approach to intermittent measurement of blood pressure. Unfortunately, it is diffcult to noninvasively measure blood pressure more frequently than every 5 min, due to patient comfort and the potential for inducing pressure sores. In situations where more frequent measures are necessary, invasive catheters placed percutaneously into a peripheral artery are utilized (Fig. 24.4). Typically, continuous beat-to-beat blood pressure is measured using an arterial catheter placed in one of several positions (most commonly radial, femoral, and/or axillary arteries). However, it should be noted that such monitoring can be associated with injury, greater expense, and the need for specialized skills to both place the catheter and interpret the data [10].

Recently, a continuous noninvasive arterial pressure (CNAP) measurement was made possible by using a fnger cuff (Fig. 24.5). It has been shown to be superior to intermittent oscillometric measurements in detecting rapid changes in arterial pressure [11]. Such an approach employs a doublefnger cuff that is easy to place on the patient's hand, a pressure transducer mounted on the forearm, and an upper arm oscillometric cuff for calibration. ### **24.4.3 Pulmonary Artery Catheters**

Pulmonary artery catheterization was frst described in 1970, by Drs. Swan and Ganz [13], and since has been widely used as diagnostic tools and to understand the physiology of the cardiovascular system during critical illness. The pulmonary artery catheter is typically inserted via central venous access via the femoral, subclavian, or internal jugular route. A balloon at the tip of the catheter, allows the catheter to be "foated" to the right side of the heart and into the pulmonary artery, where it wedges into a smaller branch of the pulmonary artery (Fig. 24.6). The associated monitoring system then allows the measurement of stroke volumes, cardiac outputs, mixed venous saturations, and pressures on the right side of the heart (Table 24.2). This catheter produces a more accurate measure of intravascular volumes than CVP, because it bypasses the situations that can falsely elevate CVP; additionally, hence the information it provides can be utilized to distinguish among the various types of shock (Table 24.3). Since the pulmonary artery pressure monitoring system is a high fow, low resistance system, those conditions which falsely elevate pulmonary artery pressure do not affect pulmonary artery wedge pressure. As noted previously, use of this catheter has signifcantly declined in the last decade due to a paucity of data supporting improved mortality, general ICU or hospital length of stay, or value added, costs for adult patients in an intensive care unit [14, 15]. Despite this decreased use, the pulmonary artery catheter can still be indispensable to assist in the evaluation and titration of therapy of the patient with multisystem disease.

### **24.4.4 Complications of Invasive Monitors**

A major issue with all invasive monitors involves complications most often are related to insertion, infection, and/or problems associated with an indwelling catheter. Insertion complications can result when the catheter: (1) is placed in the wrong vessel; (2) creates a pneumothorax; or (3) causes bleeding related to coagulopathy or inappropriate dilation of a vessel (Fig. 24.7). Furthermore, centrally-placed catheters may allow air within the central circulation, occasionally resulting in stroke [16]. Unfortunately, catheter-associated central line infections are common and thus can represent a major and expensive source of hospital morbidity. Typical infection rates for central venous catheters are in the range of 3–5 infections per 1000 patient catheter days. Thus, it is important to remove central venous catheters as soon as they are no longer necessary. As indwelling foreign bodies, catheters raise the risk of thrombosis and embolism [17]. Both of these risks are well described in the literature, and attempts to decrease these risks with heparin bonding or using other coatings, are the current standard of care.

# **24.5 Less Invasive Monitoring Techniques**

There are many new monitors making their way into daily clinical use. The utility of these monitors varies widely depending on the given clinician experience and the understandings of monitoring end points, patient populations chosen for study of these monitors, and the clinical relevance of these monitors. The two questions clinicians should ask themselves when evaluating any new monitoring strategy are: (1) does this monitoring system provide clinically relevant information needed to make a decision about the patient's status?; and (2) how should the clinician intervene related to the results of the monitor? If results from the monitoring system are not clinically relevant or do not allow decisions to be made with respect to needed intervention, then the monitor is likely not useful for patient care.

### **24.5.1 Cardiac Hemodynamics**

There are several new measurements available allowing for less invasive measurements of cardiac hemodynamics.

### **24.5.1.1 Pulse Contour Wave Processing**

Recently, several investigations have validated the benefts of employing less invasive measures of hemodynamics, including pulse contour wave processing and the ability to predict whether a patient is going to respond to fuid boluses, as the initial step of the hemodynamic resuscitation. Some of these commonly used methods (e.g., Pulse index Continuous Cardiac Output-PiCCO, Lithium Dilution Cardiac Output-LiDCO, FloTrac) are based on assessing changes in left ventricular outputs during positive pressure ventilation, e.g., pulse pressure variations (PPVs) and stroke volume variations (SVVs). Underlying these measures, intrathoracic pressure increases during respiration, causing right atrial pressures to increase as a consequence; cardiac preloads will decrease, leading to decrease in right ventricular outputs and thus left ventricular outputs after a few beats. The PiCCO monitor utilizes intermittent arterial thermodilution with beat-to-beat analyses of the arterial pulse waveforms to provide several hemodynamic parameters. Ultrasonography and echocardiography have been used to measure fow volumes and diameters of the aorta and left ventricular function for noninvasive hemodynamic evaluations (Fig. 24.8). The described methods include transthoracic or transesophageal echocardiograms (TEE): see also Chaps. 18 and 23. Transthoracic methods are less invasive but signifcantly less accurate, while transesophageal techniques require a sedated and/or mechanically ventilated patient. Subramanian and co-authors suggest a method of rapid echocardiographic assessment using a standardized algorithm [23]. This approach uses transesophageal echocardiography for assessment, but requires an experienced operator to prevent misinterpretation of fndings. Over the last few years, several studies within different ICU and critical care settings have demonstrated the feasibility of utilizing TEE in the management of the patient presenting hemodynamic instability. It is now clear that this semi-invasive tool can provide critical real-time information about the cardiac performance. Further, even when invasive continuous monitoring by a pulmonary artery catheter is used, TEE can further help defne a patient's diagnosis, by acquiring morphological and functional information that can be inte-

# **24.5.1.3 CO2 Partial Rebreathing Technique**

The CO2 partial rebreathing technique can be used via a modifed Fick technique in mechanically ventilated patients. For example, the Noninvasive Continuous Cardiac Output-NiCCO device (Respironics, Murrysville, PA, USA) allows measurements of cardiac output using this method. The technique involves the measurements of end-tidal CO2 obtained before and during rebreathing periods. The ratios of the changes in end-tidal CO2 allows for a noninvasive estimate of cardiac output [27]. To achieve optimal results with the NiCCO monitor, the patient should be maintained under fully controlled mechanical ventilation. This technique is limited by the fact that the calculation includes both blood fow perfusing ventilated portions of the lung and dead-space blood fows. Therefore, these results can be signifcant underestimations of cardiac outputs; corrected by making an estimate of shunt fraction (the amount of blood fow bypassing ventilated alveoli). Compared with conventional cardiac output methods, the partial CO2 rebreathing technique is noninvasive, can easily be automated, and can provide realtime and continuous cardiac output monitoring [28, 29].

#### **24.5.2 Perfusion Monitors**

There are a number of recently developed, noninvasive measures of regional tissue perfusion, including: (1) near-infrared spectroscopic measurements of StO2, (2) sublingual and other capnometry methods, and (3) the uses of the central venous oxygen saturation-SCVO2 catheters.

#### **24.5.2.1 Refectance Near-Infrared Spectroscopy**

Tissue hemoglobin saturation (StO2) is derived from nearinfrared spectroscopy and is a potentially useful noninvasive adjunct for monitoring critically ill patients. StO2 refects changes in microcirculatory tissue perfusion. Unlike pulse oximetry signals (SpO2), which measures oxygen contents in large, pulsatile vessels, StO2 measures the oxygen contents in vessels less than 1 mm in diameter (i.e., arterioles, capillaries, and venules) and thus describes the oxygen contents at the tissue level. Thus, StO2 alerts the clinician that peripheral blood fow is being redistributed to vital organs, as the normal balance between the proportion of oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin in the peripheral tissues undergoes adverse changes. There are several studies demonstrating correlations between StO2 and multiple organ failure, ICU admissions, ICU outcomes, and mortalities [30, 31]. In a patient presenting with septic shock, StO2 has been shown to correlate with higher mortality [32]. Additionally, one study conducted on 158 emergent cancer patients, who presented to the emergency department with hypotension and/or systemic infammatory response syndrome, it was reported that a SpO2 less than 70% signifcantly increased the risks of ICU admissions [31]. In a study of 620 ICU patients, Iyegha at al [33]. concluded that low StO2 (<70%) was common and associated with poor outcomes in SICU patients. However no studies to date, have utilized randomized sampling or established a cause-and-effect relationship between a specifc intervention that aimed toward normalization of StO2 and related patient outcomes.

### **24.5.2.2 Capnometry**

In this the patient in a state of shock, it is common to see redistributions of blood fows away from the gastrointestinal tract; resulting in increased gastrointestinal mucosal pCO2. Many investigators have observed this effect in both septic and hemorrhagic shock [34, 35]. Unfortunately, techniques to measure pCO2 in the gastrointestinal tract have been limited by the efforts involved in appropriate placements of catheters within that area. This has led to the development of sublingual capnometry, capitalizing on the fact that the oral tissues are embryologically continuous with the gastrointestinal tract. This technique has the potential to allow rapid data collection regarding adequacy of tissue perfusions in critically ill patients, however needed clinical studies utilizing this technology are limited at this time.

# **24.5.2.3 Central Venous O2 Saturation Monitors**

The measurements of mixed venous oxygen saturations in either the pulmonary artery (SvO2) or in the right atrium (ScvO2) have been studied as refections of degrees of resuscitation of patients in a variety of settings [36–38]. It has also been used to guide the treatment of shock [39]. This technique has the potential advantage of yielding an end point for both resuscitative efforts and intravenous access. Since a patient's decreased cardiac output and resultant decreases in oxygen delivery to tissues will result in increased peripheral tissue extraction of oxygen from circulating blood, a low ScvO2 in a critically ill patient indicates that the given patient is likely suffering from inadequate cardiac output. One potential shortcoming of this technique, is the lack of sensitivity related to mixing of venous blood returning from organs with high and low metabolic needs, resulting in falsely high readings. Despite these theoretical concerns, a recent study by Rivers and colleagues [35] used ScvO2 as an end point of resuscitation, in a group of septic patients presenting to an emergency department, and demonstrated improved survivals in patients who received interventions designed to improve ScvO2 levels to greater than 70% (defned as early goal-directed therapy). These encouraging fndings have resulted in incorporation of early goal-directed therapies into protocols for treatment of patients with severe sepsis or septic shock.

# **24.5.3 Subcutaneous Continuous Glucose Monitoring**

Hyperglycemia is common in acutely ill patients, especially within the ICU population [40]. Tight glucose control has been proposed to be crucial in these critically ill patients, especially for reductions in both morbidity and mortality in SICU populations. However, recent studies reported no evidence that intensive insulin therapy with the goal of tight glycemic control, is benefcial for ICU patients. According to the literature, tight glycemic control can be associated with higher incidences of hypoglycemia and death [41, 42].

### **24.5.4 Artifcial Intelligence**

Electronic health records provide a plethora of potential clinical applications for artifcial intelligence (AI). Interest in AI-related critical care research is growing rapidly with evidence demonstrated in a bibliometric analysis by Tang et al., which found 329 publications on the subject from 1986 to 2017 in comparison to 869 publications between 2018 and 2022 [43]. AI encompasses broad applications, including the use of machine learning to detect symptoms, predict pathology, and propose therapy options. Machine learning models can support clinical decision making by helping ICU providers visualize and interpret enormous amounts of dynamic information [44]. AI has been used in the ICU for monitoring ventilatory support and to predict length of stay, readmission, mortality, and risks of developing complications (e.g., sepsis, ARDS) [45]. ChatGPT/GPT-4 (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) is an AI-based chatbot that was launched in 2022, and since has been shown to perform well on academic standardized testing with the abilities to score above the median on the Medical College Admission Test (MCAT) and pass the United States Medical Licensing Exam (USMLE). An article written by Lu et al. in 2023 discussed the potential applications of ChatGPT/GPT-4 in the ICU, including: (1) data analyses, (2) risk stratifcations, (3) alert generation, (4) decision support, (5) continuous care improvements, and (6) enhanced education/training [46]. Since 2017, hundreds of hospitals have used the Epic Systems Corporation Deterioration Index (DTI), a proprietary machine learning model, which has demonstrated modest abilities to predict clinical deterioration [47]. Early electronic warning monitoring systems could eventually allow for more timely interventions and reductions in potentially avoidable deaths, from failure to recognize or act [47]. With the continuous evolution of AI technologies, it will be essential for intensivists to understand and utilize AI-based tools when appropriate, along with their clinical expertise, to more effectively monitor patients in the ICU [46].

# **24.5.5 Conclusions**

The need for monitoring of the critically ill patient has grown as the healthcare system has developed the enhanced abilities to provide higher acuity care for elderly patients with more comorbidities. Monitors serve several purposes, including: (1) the identifcation of shock and abnormal cardiac physiology, (2) the evaluation of cardiovascular function, and (3) to allows on the better abilities for the titration of therapy. An important function of an effective monitoring device is reliable detection of abnormal physiology. Despite much research on the use of monitoring techniques in the critical care setting, there is little evidence of improved outcomes related to the routine uses of many such monitors. Developers of new monitors (despite facing regulatory requirements that are considered less stringent than those of drug manufacturers) will increasingly be expected to demonstrate clinical effcacies of new devices. This will be often diffcult to accomplish in a patient population with highly varied clinical statuses. However, it is essential to remember the monitoring devices are not therapies. For example, if a monitoring device alerts to a problem or condition that is not treatable or has room for improvement in therapy, then the problem is not with the monitoring device, but perhaps rather the current lack of an effective therapy. Clinical outcomes that are used to evaluate monitoring devices should be interpreted with caution, as monitoring devices alone only provide measurements which can then be used to guide more effective therapeutic interventions. Clinical outcomes are best used to evaluate therapies or monitoring and interventions together, with the perspective that the goals of monitoring devices are to provide supportive clinical information [48]. Currently, the mainstays of invasive monitoring in the ICU include, central venous pressure monitoring and arterial pressure monitoring, with pulmonary arterial monitoring used less frequently. Recent trends in monitoring have included, the development of less invasive monitoring techniques that yield a number of cardiovascular parameters potentially useful to clinicians. New noninvasive measures of tissue perfusion (e.g., StO2, sublingual capnometry) and semi-invasive techniques (e.g., TEE) have signifcant potential in the enhanced identifcations and assessments of treatments for pathophysiologic states associated with inadequate tissue perfusion. Telemedicine in the ICU is a new trend that is so far associated with reduced patient mortalities and morbidities. Artifcial intelligence models will continue to evolve; with the need for further evaluation and validation before expanding clinical applications to improve monitoring and critical care setting. In the fnal analysis, the most important "monitor" is the *healthcare provider* at the bedside directly evaluating the patient's response to intervention and therapy.
