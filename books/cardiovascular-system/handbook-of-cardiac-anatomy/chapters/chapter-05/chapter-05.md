#### **Abstract**

The development of the vertebrate heart relies on the intricate coordination of four major embryonic regions: the frst heart feld (FHF), the second heart feld (SHF), the cardiac neural crest (CNC), and the proepicardial organ (PEO). Each of these regions plays a crucial role in cardiac development, governed by complex regulatory mechanisms and precise timing. This chapter elucidates how these regions interact to form the fnal structure of the heart in relationship with the developmental timeline of human embryology.

#### **5.2 First Heart Field Contribution to the Linear Heart Tube, Left Ventricle, and Atria**

During vertebrate embryo development, the cells destined to form the heart represent one of the earliest lineages to emerge [26, 27]. By approximately day 15 of human development, the primitive streak is established [1] and the initial mesodermal cells migrating through this structure are committed to becoming myocytes or heart cells [28, 29] (Fig. 5.2). These dedicated mesodermal cells migrate to an anterior and lateral position, giving rise to both the bilateral frst heart feld and the more medially located second heart feld [13, 14, 18, 19, 30] (Fig. 5.1a). Specifcally, the posterior boundary of the bilateral frst heart feld extends to the frst somite in the lateral mesoderm on both sides of the midline [11, 31] (Fig. 5.1a).

### **5.3 Second Heart Field Contribution to the Outfow Tract, Right Ventricle, and Atria**

The initiation of primary linear heart tube looping is believed to be orchestrated by a cascade of signals that establish the left and right sides of the embryo [35]. The primary heart tube undergoes looping to the right of the embryo, bending to facilitate the convergence of the infow (venous) and outfow (arterial) ends between days 22 and 28 of human development (Fig. 5.4). This crucial process precedes the division of the heart tube into four chambers and is indispensable for the correct alignment and septation of the mature cardiac chambers. Notably, during looping, the primary heart tube experiences a signifcant increase in length, expanding four- to fvefold at both the outfow and infow poles through the incorporation of progenitor cells originating from the second heart feld (SHF), located in the pharyngeal mesoderm [10–21]. These multipotent progenitor cells give rise to the myocardium, smooth muscles, and endothelial cells within the developing heart [15].

Early experiments in the 1970s had already demonstrated that the distal right ventricle and outfow tract (OFT) are added later to the looping heart through the addition of cells situated outside the early heart [15, 36]. However, researchers at that time still presumed that the primary linear heart tube contained all the cell lineages necessary to construct the adult heart. It was not until the rediscovery of these progenitor cells in 2001 (initially termed the "anterior heart feld" or "secondary heart feld") that the clinical signifcance of congenital heart defects was linked to cells in this heart feld, marking a signifcant advancement in understanding heart development [10–12, 15]. The designations "anterior heart feld" and "secondary heart feld" are now regarded as subpopulations of the SHF, which constitutes a larger feld of progenitor cells in the pharyngeal mesoderm [15, 37]. The SHF, in turn, is situated within a broader feld of the multipotent cranial mesoderm (cardio-craniofacial morphogenetic feld) that plays pivotal roles in the development of both the arterial pole of the heart and craniofacial morphogenesis [15]. Specifcally, the SHF (Figs. 5.1b and 5.2c) is located along the splanchnopleuric mesoderm (beneath the foor of the foregut) at the attachment site of the dorsal mesocardium [10–21].

During looping, the anterior SHF (previously termed the "anterior heart feld" or "secondary heart feld") cells undergo epithelial-to-myocardial transformation at the outfow (arterial) pole and contribute additional myocardial cells to the developing outfow tract, forming the great vessels (aorta and pulmonary trunk) and the right ventricle. This lengthening of the primary heart tube appears to be a critical process for the proper alignment of the infow and outfow tracts before septation. Failure of this process may lead to ventricular septal defects and malpositioning of the aorta [33]. Recent evidence has also suggested that the posterior SHF contributes to the infow tract, forming parts of the left and right atria. Thus, the SHF comprises two regions: (1) an anterior region or compartment that contributes to the outfow tract and (2) a posterior region or compartment that contributes to the infow tract as well as possibly the proepicardial organ [13, 18, 20, 38–40]. Defects in the development of the posterior SHF can lead to conotruncal, atrial, and atrioventricular (AV) septal defects, which are major forms of congenital heart defects in humans [15].

By day 28 of human development, the chambers of the heart are positioned and delineated by visible constrictions and expansions that mark the sinus venosus, common atrial chamber, atrioventricular sulcus, ventricular chamber, and conotruncus (proximal and distal outfow tracts) [1, 33] (Fig. 5.4).

During weeks 3–4 of human development, CNC cells emigrate from the neural tube, traversing through aortic arches 3–6 (Fig. 5.1b), eventually reaching the developing outfow tract of the heart during weeks 5–6. All these septa eventually merge with the atrioventricular (AV) cushions, which also divide the left and right AV canals and serve as a source of cells for the AV valves. While arterial and atrioventricular valves share similarities, primarily arising from endocardial cushions, contemporary insights have revealed the distinct reliance of arterial valves and their adjacent structures on progenitors originating from both the second heart feld (SHF) and cardiac neural crest (CNC) cells [57, 58].

Before septation, the right atrioventricular canal and right ventricle shift rightward, causing realignment of the atria and ventricles directly above each other. This facilitates venous blood fow from the sinus venosus directly into the presumptive right ventricle, bypassing the presumptive left atrium and ventricle [1, 33]. This realignment also allows the left ventricle a direct outfow path to the truncus arteriosus and subsequently to the aorta.

Functional septation of the heart in the atrial region occurs only after birth. At birth, signifcant changes in the circulatory system transpire due to the transition from fetal reliance on the placenta for oxygenated blood to self-oxygenation via the lungs. During fetal life, only a small proportion of blood (about 5% of the cardiac output) fows through the pulmonary system because fuid-flled lungs create high fow resistance, resulting in low-volume fow into the left atrium from the pulmonary veins. This setup permits the high-volume blood fow from the placenta to pass through the inferior vena cava into the right atrium, where it is directed across the foramen ovale into the left atrium. Oxygenated blood then fows into the left ventricle and out to the body via the aorta. At birth, umbilical blood fow ceases, discontinuing the high-volume fow from the placenta. Additionally, alveoli and pulmonary vessels open when the infant takes its frst breath, reducing resistance in the lungs and allowing more fow into the left atrium from the lungs. This reversal in pressure difference between the atria pushes the fexible septum primum against the ridged septum secundum, closing off the foramen ovale and ostium secundum, leading to complete septation of the heart chambers [1] (Fig. 5.6).

## **5.5 Proepicardial Organ and Coronary Artery Development**

The fnal signifcant contributor to vertebrate heart development discussed in this chapter is the proepicardial organ (PEO) [18, 24, 25, 59]. Before heart looping, the primary heart tube comprises the endocardium, cardiac jelly, and myocardium. It is only at the onset of heart looping that epicardial cells from the PEO envelop the myocardium, constituting the fourth layer of the primary heart tube, known as the epicardium [18, 60] (Fig. 5.3). These cells ultimately give rise to the coronary vasculature. Initially, a neural crest origin of the coronary vessels was postulated, but recent lineage tracing studies have revealed that neural crest cells contribute to the tunica media of the aortic and pulmonary trunks, not the coronary arteries [32, 59, 61]. Subsequent experiments conclusively demonstrated that the coronary vasculature arises from the proepicardial organ (PEO), a cluster of cells in the dorsal mesocardium of the sinus venosus or septum transversum. These cells, derived from an independent population of splanchnopleuric mesoderm cells, migrate onto the primary heart tube (Fig. 5.3) between days 22 and 28 of human development, coinciding with the initiation of heart looping [1, 33].

Before migration, these cells collectively constitute the proepicardial organ (or proepicardium). Intriguingly, prior to migration into the heart tube, three lineages of coronary vessel cells (smooth muscle, endothelial, and connective tissue cells) are segregated within the proepicardial organ [32, 62]. These cells subsequently converge to form coronary vessels de novo through the process of vasculogenesis [63]. Recent studies have also revealed that the epicardium provides a factor essential for normal myocardial development and serves as a source of cells forming the interstitial myocardium and cushion mesenchyme [33, 39]. Understanding the embryological origin of the vascular system and its molecular regulation may shed light on the varying susceptibility of different vascular components to atherosclerosis [32, 64]. Moreover, it has been proposed that epicardium-derived cells could potentially serve as a source of cells for myocardial regeneration following myocardial infarction [25]. Lastly, among the various stem cell populations identifed in the mature heart, Isl1-positive cells may represent a population of resident cardiovascular stem cells derived from residual SHF cells [15, 65, 66]. Consequently, strategies aimed at cardiac repair through manipulation of cardiac progenitor cells hinge on a thorough understanding of how lineage choices are regulated in the SHF and PEO [15, 59, 67].

#### **5.6 Cardiac Maturation**

Despite the embryonic heart being fully formed and functional by the eleventh week of pregnancy, rapid growth and maturation continue throughout fetal and neonatal stages, with several clinically signifcant changes unfolding after birth. During fetal development, spanning from the completion of embryogenesis in the frst trimester until birth, cardiac growth primarily occurs through cell division [68–71]. However, within weeks after birth, cardiac growth shifts predominantly to cell hypertrophy, where existing cardiac cells increase in size rather than proliferating signifcantly [68–70]. The precise timing and regulatory mechanisms governing this transition remain incompletely understood. Traditionally, mature cardiac cells were believed to lose their ability to divide. Nevertheless, recent investigations have suggested that limited cell division does occur in adult hearts damaged by ischemia [72–74].

This discovery has sparked renewed interest in understanding the regulation of cell division during cardiac maturation. Additional maturational changes in the fetal and neonatal heart include alterations in the composition of cardiac muscles, differences in energy production, and maturation of contractile function. These changes, alongside physiological alterations in the transitional circulation as discussed earlier, signifcantly infuence the treatment of newborns with congenital heart disease, particularly those requiring interventional procedures or cardiac surgery.

Hemodynamic changes accompanying birth entail a substantial increase in left ventricular cardiac output to meet the heightened metabolic demands of the newborn. This enhancement in cardiac output occurs despite the neonatal myocardium possessing less muscle mass and cellular organization than the mature myocardium. Notably, the newborn myocardium comprises 30% contractile proteins (mass) and 70% noncontractile mass (membranes, connective tissues, and organelles), whereas the adult myocardium is 60% contractile mass [70]. Fetal myocardial cells are spherical, with both myocardial cells and myofbrils within them randomly oriented. As the fetal heart matures, these myofbrils increase in size and number and align themselves along the long axis of cell rows, likely contributing to improved myocardial function [68]. A fetal myocardial cell exhibits higher glycogen levels than the mature myocardium, indicating a heightened reliance on glucose for energy production. Experiments conducted in non-primate model systems demonstrate the fetal myocardium's ability to meet metabolic needs primarily through lactate and glucose utilization [75]. Conversely, the adult heart predominantly metabolizes long-chain fatty acids for energy, although it retains the capacity to utilize carbohydrates [75, 76]. The timing and clinical impact of this metabolic shift in ill or nonfeeding neonates with cardiovascular disease remain uncertain, although it is presumed to occur shortly after birth due to an increase in serum longchain fatty acids with feeding.

Moreover, maturing myocardial cells undergo changes in the expression of numerous intrinsic contractile proteins, potentially accounting for some maturational differences in cardiovascular function. For instance, the gradual upregulation of myosin light chain 2 (MLC 2) expression in the ventricle from the neonatal period through adolescence is deemed crucial in humans. In the fetal ventricle, two myosin light chain forms, MLC 1 and MLC 2, are expressed in equal proportions [70, 77]. MLC 1 is associated with enhanced contractility and has been documented to increase contractility in isolated muscle from patients with tetralogy of Fallot [78]. Following birth, there is a progressive increase in MLC 2, or the "regulatory" myosin light chain, which exhibits slower force development but can be phosphorylated to enhance calcium-dependent force development in mature cardiac muscle [70, 79]. Additionally, there is variability in actin isoform expression during cardiac development. The human fetal heart predominantly expresses cardiac alphaactin, whereas the mature human heart expresses skeletal alpha-actin [68, 80]. Actin plays a vital role in interacting with myosin crossbridges and regulating ATPase activity, with studies in mouse models suggesting that the transition to skeletal actin may contribute to enhanced contractility in the mature heart [68, 81, 82]. Furthermore, there are developmental alterations in the regulatory proteins of the sarcomere with potential functional implications. Initially, the fetal heart expresses alpha- and beta-tropomyosin, regulatory flaments, in nearly equal proportions. However, after birth, there is a decrease in beta-tropomyosin and an increase in alpha-tropomyosin, possibly optimizing diastolic relaxation [68, 83, 84]. Conversely, elevated levels of betatropomyosin in the neonatal heart are associated with early mortality due to myocardial dysfunction [85]. Additionally, the isoform of the inhibitory troponin, troponin I, changes postnatally, with the fetal myocardium predominantly containing the skeletal isoform [68, 86]. Following birth, cardiac troponin I expression begins, becoming the sole isoform by approximately 9 months of age [68, 87, 88]. Cardiac troponin I can be phosphorylated to enhance calcium responsivity and contractility, correlating with improved function in the mature heart. The skeletal form of troponin I may serve to protect the fetal and neonatal myocardium from acidosis [70, 81, 89]. However, the full impact of these developmental changes on contractile proteins and their effect on cardiac function or perioperative treatment of newborns with heart disease remain unclear.

Two of the most clinically relevant features of the immature myocardium are its heightened requirement for extracellular calcium and its decreased sensitivity to beta-adrenergic inotropic agents. The neonatal heart exhibits reduced volume and levels of functionally mature sarcoplasmic reticulum, the intracellular calcium storage site [68]. Consequently, the fetal myocardium relies heavily on extracellular calcium, allowing exogenous calcium administration to augment cardiac contractility when necessary. Furthermore, neonates and infants display increased sensitivity to calcium channelblocking drugs compared to older children or adults, putting them at risk of severe depression of myocardial contractility with these agents [68, 70, 90]. Additionally, although data in humans are limited, there appears to be a signifcantly decreased responsiveness to beta-agonist agents in the immature myocardium as well as in older children with congenital heart disease [70, 91–93]. This reduced responsiveness may stem from a scarcity of receptors, sensitization to endogenous catecholamines at birth or in heart failure, or a combination thereof. Consequently, newborns and infants often require higher doses of beta-agonist inotropic agents. Alternative medications, including phosphodiesterase inhibitors, are frequently employed adjunctively to enhance contractility in newborns with myocardial dysfunction [70].

In summary, while the heart's structure is largely formed in the frst trimester of pregnancy, cardiac growth and maturation persist in the fetus, newborn, and child. Many of these developmental changes, particularly the decreased intracellular calcium stores in the immature sarcoplasmic reticulum and reduced responsiveness to beta-agonist inotropic agents, signifcantly impact the care of newborns, infants, and children with congenital heart disease, particularly those needing early surgical intervention.

#### **5.7 Summary of Embryonic Contributions to Heart Development**

The intricate interplay of the four major embryonic regions—the frst heart feld, the second heart feld, the cardiac neural crest, and the proepicardial organ—underscores the complexity of human heart development. Each region makes a distinct contribution to heart formation, yet they rely on one another for the creation of a fully functional organ. Moreover, unraveling the mechanisms underlying human heart development holds promise for understanding the origins of congenital heart disease. While the genetic regulatory pathways governing these developmental processes are still being elucidated, several recent molecular articles have delved into these topics extensively [4, 9, 19, 33, 54, 94, 95]. A comprehensive molecular exploration of heart development lies beyond the scope of this chapter. However, enhancing our comprehension of the embryological foundations of cardiac development, coupled with the delineation of the genes orchestrating heart development, holds signifcant promise for unveiling a multitude of novel clinical applications aimed at addressing congenital and adult heart diseases.
