# Tricuspid Atresia

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Epidemiology
*   **Tricuspid atresia:** Congenital absence or abnormality of the tricuspid valve resulting in no direct communication between the right atrium and right ventricle. Most patients have a dominant left ventricle and a rudimentary right ventricle.
*   **Prevalence:** 0.04–0.05 per 1000 live births; represents **1–3% of all congenital heart disease (CHD)**.

### Embryology and Classification
*   **Muscular type (75%):** Embryological abnormality arises early in gestation; a dimple or localised fibrous thickening in the floor of the right atrium.
*   **Membranous type (25%):** Abnormality occurs later in gestation; imperforated tricuspid valve.
*   **Classification by great artery relationship:**
    *   **Normally related great arteries (70%):** PA-IVS or VSD ± pulmonary stenosis.
    *   **Transposed great arteries (30%):** PA-VSD or VSD ± pulmonary stenosis.

### Associated Anomalies
*   VSD in 90% (40% close spontaneously or decrease in size → restrictive VSD causes subpulmonary or subaortic obstruction)
*   Coarctation of aorta or interrupted aortic arch (8%)
*   Persistent left-sided SVC, juxtaposition of atrial appendages, right aortic arch, transposition of great arteries

### Pathophysiology
*   **Normally related great arteries:** Obligatory right-to-left shunt across interatrial communication → systemic desaturation. Blood reaches PA via VSD and hypoplastic RV. Subpulmonary stenosis develops in ~50% (restrictive VSD or infundibular level).
*   **Transposed great arteries:** LV connected to PA → abundant pulmonary flow, minimal cyanosis but marked LV volume overload. Pulmonary valve normal in 2/3, stenotic/atretic in 1/3. Restrictive VSD causes subaortic stenosis.

### Natural History
*   Overall survival: **70% at 1 year, 55% at 5 years, 50% at 10 years**.
*   Restrictive VSD → intense hypoxia and death unless PDA or collaterals present. 90% die by 1 year without palliation.
*   Tricuspid atresia with transposed great arteries and subaortic stenosis (restrictive VSD) is an ominous combination.

### Diagnosis
*   **Symptoms:** Cyanosis (progressive), hypoxic spells, congestive heart failure (if transposed arteries).
*   **Signs:** Cyanosis ± clubbing, single S2, systolic thrill (if pulmonary stenosis), 3/6 holosystolic VSD murmur at lower LSB, hepatomegaly.
*   **ECG:** Left axis deviation, LVH, tall right atrial P wave, biatrial P wave.
*   **CXR:** Normal or slightly increased heart size, enlarged RA, flat inferior border (absence of RV), decreased pulmonary markings (most), plethoric fields with cardiomegaly (if volume overload).
*   **Echocardiography:** Absence of right AV connection with hyperechogenic shelf at TV location (diagnostic). RA enlarged; LA may be enlarged if pulmonary flow increased. Interatrial communication always present. RV hypoplastic, LV large and hypertrophied.
*   **Catheterisation:** Limited role. RA pressure slightly > LA pressure with prominent 'A' wave. PVR assessment required before BCPS or Fontan. Balloon atrial septostomy for restrictive interatrial communication.

### Management Principles
*   **Initial treatment depends on pulmonary blood flow:**
    *   **Low pulmonary flow (cyanotic):** Prostaglandin E₁ (maintain PDA)
    *   **High pulmonary flow:** Pulmonary artery banding
    *   **Restrictive interatrial communication:** Balloon atrial septostomy
    *   **Balanced circulation:** Monitoring only

### Surgical Strategy — Fontan Circulation
*   No surgical cure; palliation with **Fontan circulation** (diverts systemic venous return directly to pulmonary arteries).
*   **Staged approach:**
    *   **Neonatal/early infancy (if inadequate flow):** Modified Blalock-Taussig shunt
    *   **Neonatal/early infancy (if excessive flow):** Pulmonary artery banding
    *   **3–6 months:** Bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (BCPS) — SVC anastomosed to PA
    *   **2–6 years:** Completion — total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC)

### TCPC Techniques
*   **Extracardiac TCPC with fenestration:** Gore-Tex® tube (16–24mm) anastomosed end-to-end to IVC and end-to-side to RPA.
*   **Lateral tunnel TCPC:** Gore-Tex® baffle connecting IVC and SVC inside RA; SVC anastomosed to PA.

### Traditional 'Ten Commandments' (Fontan)
1.  Age >4 years
2.  No pulmonary artery distortion from prior shunt
3.  No systemic or left AV valve leak
4.  Normal venous drainage
5.  Normal ventricular function (EF ≥60%)
6.  Normal heart rhythm
7.  Normal right atrial size
8.  Low PA pressure (≤15 mmHg)
9.  Low PVR (<4 units/m²)
10. Adequate PA size (PA:aorta ratio ≥0.75)

### Current Modifications to Criteria
*   Age progressively lower (but child should be crawling for musculoskeletal contribution to venous return)
*   Pre-operative abnormalities can be addressed before Fontan
*   Pre-operative sinus rhythm and normal RA size not prerequisites (but post-op arrhythmias impair prognosis)
*   Mean PAP >15 mmHg not an absolute threshold — consider in context
*   **PVR (<4 units/m²) remains the most important criterion**
*   **PA size assessed by McGoon ratio or Nakata index** (lower values increase risk)

### Pulmonary Artery Size Assessment
*   **McGoon ratio** = (pre-branching RPA diameter + LPA diameter) / descending aorta diameter at diaphragm
    *   Risk of Fontan failure higher if ratio <1.8
*   **Nakata index** = (pre-branching RPA CSA + LPA CSA) / BSA (m²)
    *   Lower index associated with worse outcomes
*   *Limitation:* Indices do not account for vascular compliance or PA distortion.

### Advantages of Venous-to-Pulmonary Shunt
*   Desaturated (systemic venous) blood shunted to lungs → greater efficiency in increasing arterial oxygen saturation
*   Reduces volume load on the heart (arterial shunt adds ventricular volume and workload)

### Fenestration
*   Allows right-to-left shunt through defect
*   Maintains cardiac output at expense of mild desaturation
*   Prevents undue elevation of systemic venous pressure → reduces capillary leakage
*   Improves long-term outcomes: functional capacity, liver function, reduction of portal hypertension complications (varices, malabsorption, protein-losing enteropathy)

### Complications
*   **Short-term:** Low cardiac output, persistent pleural effusion, chylous leak, thrombus formation, acute liver dysfunction.
*   **Long-term:** Hepatomegaly/ascites, supraventricular arrhythmias (freedom 90% at 5 years, 85% at 10 years), progressive desaturation (venous pathway obstruction, baffle leak, pulmonary AV fistula), protein-losing enteropathy, failing systemic ventricle (requiring transplant).

### Outcomes
*   **Short-term mortality:** ~1–2% (improved due to TCPC design, shorter bypass time, staged procedures, fenestration). Higher mortality in heterotaxy syndrome and hypoplastic left heart syndrome.
*   **Long-term survival:** 85–93% at 5 years, 83–91% at 10 years.
*   Heart transplant for failing Fontan may have adverse outcomes (difficult haemodynamic assessment, complex anatomy, multiple prior procedures, unique pathology).

### Fontan Conversion
*   For failing atriopulmonary connection (progressive RA dilatation → turbulent slow flow, thrombi, supraventricular arrhythmias)
*   Converts to TCPC (usually extracardiac Gore-Tex® conduit)
*   **Critical adjuncts:** Fenestration, atrial wall reduction plasty, removal of intracavitary thrombi, arrhythmia surgery (modified Maze), possible permanent pacemaker
*   Symptomatic improvement reported; impact on survival/transplantation unclear.

## 1 What is tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

Tricuspid atresia is a **congenital absence or abnormality of the tricuspid valve** resulting in no direct communication between the right atrium and right ventricle.

Most patients in this group have a **dominant left ventricle** and a **rudimentary right ventricle** (Figure 1).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 2 What is the embryology of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Normal Development</summary><div class="details-content">

The tricuspid valve develops shortly after division of the atrioventricular canal. The tricuspid valve leaflets originate from the **inferior endocardial cushion** (septal leaflet) and **ventricular muscle** (anterior and posterior leaflets), following resorption of the muscle tissue.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Muscular Type (75%)</summary><div class="details-content">

Tricuspid atresia occurs if the leaflets **fail to develop** (muscular type, Figure 2A). This occurs if the embryological abnormality arises **early in gestation**.

The atrioventricular junction is formed by a **dimple or a localised fibrous thickening** in the floor of the right atrium.

![](_page_1_Picture_6.jpeg)

**Figure 2. A) Muscular type; and B) membranous type of tricuspid atresia.**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Membranous Type (25%)</summary><div class="details-content">

Tricuspid atresia occurs following **complete fusion of developing valve leaflet components** (membranous type, Figure 2B). This develops if the abnormality occurs **later in gestation**.

The atrioventricular junction is formed by an **imperforated tricuspid valve**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Tricuspid Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Partial fusion of the valve leaflet components results in **tricuspid valve stenosis**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

*   **Membranous type:** Imperforated tricuspid valve.

## 5 What cardiac anomalies are associated with tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Ventricular septal defect (VSD)** — seen in **90%** of patients with tricuspid atresia. At birth, the VSD usually permits adequate (but not excessive) pulmonary blood flow. Progressively, **40% of these defects close spontaneously or decrease in size**. The resultant restrictive VSD causes **subpulmonary obstruction** in patients with normally related great arteries or **subaortic obstruction** in patients with transposed great arteries.
*   **Coarctation of the aorta or interrupted aortic arch** — seen in **8%** of cases.
*   **Persistent left-sided superior vena cava**
*   **Juxtaposition of the atrial appendages**
*   **Right aortic arch**
*   **Transposition of the great arteries**

</div></details>

## 7 What is the pathophysiology of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

The pathophysiology varies depending on ventriculo-arterial concordance or discordance.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Normally Related Great Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

Systemic return crosses an interatrial communication by an **obligatory right-to-left shunt** and mixes with pulmonary return in the left atrium. **Systemic desaturation is always present**, as the mixed blood flows into the left ventricle.

Blood flow reaches the pulmonary artery via a **VSD and hypoplastic right ventricle** (or subpulmonary pouch), as commonly seen (Figure 3). If the VSD size becomes restrictive, subpulmonary flow is obstructed. In about **50% of all patients**, subpulmonary stenosis develops at the level of the restrictive VSD or at the level of the infundibulum.

![](_page_3_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 3. Tricuspid atresia with normally related great arteries.** Obligatory flow across the interatrial communication, with flow to the pulmonary circulation passing through a VSD and subpulmonary chamber. Ao = aorta; PA = pulmonary artery; LV = left ventricle; RV = right ventricle; RA = right atrium; LA = left atrium.

Occasionally, the VSD is large with normal-sized pulmonary arteries, or the ventricular septum is intact with pulmonary atresia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transposed Great Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

The left ventricle is connected to the pulmonary artery, resulting in **abundant pulmonary arterial flow**, producing minimal cyanosis but marked **left ventricular volume overload**.

*   Pulmonary valve is normal with increased pulmonary blood flow in **two thirds** of cases.
*   Pulmonary valve is either stenotic or atretic with decreased pulmonary blood flow in **one third** of cases.

In this subgroup, the aorta receives blood via a VSD and the hypoplastic right ventricle. **Restrictive VSD or infundibular stenosis** will result in subaortic stenosis (Figure 4). In severe cases, decreased systemic cardiac output and metabolic acidosis ensue.

![](_page_4_Figure_3.jpeg)

**Figure 4. Mechanisms of subarterial obstruction to blood flow:** A) tricuspid atresia with normally related arteries and pulmonary or subpulmonary stenosis; B) tricuspid atresia and transposition of the great arteries, with subaortic stenosis as a result of a restrictive VSD.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 8 What is the natural history of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Overall Survival</summary><div class="details-content">

The overall survival rates of patients with tricuspid atresia are:
*   **70% at 1 year**
*   **55% at 5 years**
*   **50% at 10 years**

Infants with normally related great arteries accompanied by **increased pulmonary blood flow** tend to live longer than the group with decreased pulmonary blood flow.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Restrictive VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

In patients with tricuspid atresia and a restrictive VSD, **intense hypoxia and death ensue** unless a patent ductus arteriosus or adequate systemic-to-pulmonary collaterals are present.

The VSD often closes spontaneously or is excessively obstructive, so that **90% of patients die by 1 year**. Only few survive beyond 6 months of age without surgical palliation.

In exceptional cases, longer survival with a balanced circulation has been reported up to **32–45 years of age**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transposed Great Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

Tricuspid atresia with transposed great arteries and subaortic stenosis (restrictive VSD) is an **ominous combination**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 9 What are the symptoms of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The clinical features are determined by the **degree of pulmonary blood flow**.
*   In the majority of patients, **cyanosis is progressive** due to increasing infundibular stenosis or restriction of the VSD.
*   A history of **hypoxic spells** may be present, including cyanosis on the first day of life.
*   In some patients with transposed great arteries, **congestive heart failure** is the presenting symptom.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 10 What are the clinical findings of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Cyanosis**, with or without clubbing
*   **Single 2nd heart sound**
*   A **systolic thrill** may be palpable when tricuspid atresia is associated with pulmonary stenosis
*   A **3/6 holosystolic or early systolic murmur of VSD** is usually present at the lower left sternal border
*   **Hepatomegaly** may indicate an inadequate interatrial communication or congestive heart failure

</div></details>

## 11 What are the ECG features of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Left axis deviation**
*   **Left ventricular hypertrophy** is usually present
*   **Tall right atrial P wave**
*   **Biatrial P wave**, if left atrial volume is increased

</div></details>

## 12 What are the radiological features of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The heart size is **normal or slightly increased**
*   **Enlarged right atrium**
*   The **inferior border is relatively flat** due to the absence of the right ventricle
*   **Decreased pulmonary marking** in most patients with normally related great arteries and subpulmonary stenosis
*   A **plethoric pulmonary field and cardiomegaly** can be seen in infants with pulmonary volume overload (e.g., tricuspid atresia with transposed great arteries and an unrestrictive VSD)

</div></details>

## 13 What are the echocardiographic features of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Absence of the right atrioventricular connection** with presence of a **hyperechogenic shelf** in the location of the normal tricuspid valve is **diagnostic** (Figure 5).

![](_page_6_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 5. Echocardiographic image demonstrating tricuspid atresia and a ventricular septal defect.**

*   The right atrium is **enlarged** and the left atrium may be enlarged if pulmonary blood flow is increased.
*   An **interatrial communication is always present** but there might be bulging of the atrial septum towards the left.
*   The **right ventricle is usually hypoplastic**, whereas the **left ventricle is large and hypertrophied**.
*   The size of the interatrial communication and VSD, the presence and severity of pulmonary stenosis, ventriculo-arterial connection and ventricular function should also be investigated.
*   Patients with transposed great arteries should be examined for possible **subaortic stenosis and aortic arch anomalies**.

</div></details>

## 14 What are the cardiac catheterisation findings of tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac catheterisation has a **limited role** at present in the management of tricuspid atresia.

*   A diagnostic cardiac catheter will show the **right atrial pressure slightly higher than the left atrial pressure**, with a **prominent 'A' wave** in the right atrium.
*   An **assessment of pulmonary vascular resistance** is required prior to a bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt or Fontan operation.
*   Interventions are performed mainly for **balloon atrial septostomy** in cases of restrictive interatrial communication.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 15 What are the principles of management of a patient with tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

The initial treatment of tricuspid atresia depends on the **amount of pulmonary blood flow**.

*   **Prostaglandin E₁** should be started in cyanotic babies to maintain patency of the ductus arteriosus.
*   **Pulmonary artery banding** should be carried out in babies with high pulmonary flow.
*   **Balloon atrial septostomy** may be carried out as a part of the initial catheterisation to improve the right-to-left atrial shunt.
*   If the circulation is **balanced**, monitoring only is required.

</div></details>

## 16 What are the principles of surgery for a patient with tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Fontan Circulation Concept</summary><div class="details-content">

There is **no surgical cure** for tricuspid atresia; therefore, palliation of the functional single ventricle with a **Fontan circulation** is employed.

The Fontan circulation was initially described in 1971 by Dr. Fontan and Dr. Kreutzer separately as a treatment for tricuspid atresia. The circulation is designed to **divert systemic venous return directly to the pulmonary arteries** in patients without a subpulmonary pumping chamber (Figure 6).

![](_page_8_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Evolution of right ventricular bypass surgery for tricuspid atresia:** A) tricuspid atresia; B) right atrial appendage to main pulmonary artery (Warden, 1954); C) superior vena cava to right pulmonary artery side-to-side anastomosis (Haller 1966); D) classic Glenn shunt with a valved conduit to the left pulmonary artery (Fontan and Baudet, 1971); E) pulmonary trunk implanted onto the right atrial appendage (Kreutzer, Ross, Miller, 1974); F) right atrium to right ventricle valved conduit (Bowman, Björk, 1979); G) pulmonary trunk to superior vena cava-right atrial junction (Doty, 1981).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Staged Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

Currently, the Fontan circulation is achieved with:
1.  **Bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (BCPS):** Superior vena cava anastomosed to the pulmonary artery.
2.  **Completion of the Fontan circulation by total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC):** Inferior vena cava connected to the pulmonary artery via a conduit placed alongside the atrium (**extracardiac TCPC**) or inside the atrial mass (**intracardiac TCPC**).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 20 What are the differences in current management from the traditional 'ten commandments' for a Fontan circulation?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Age:** The age for the Fontan operation has progressively come down, although the operation should be performed after a child is at least **crawling** to benefit from the contribution of musculoskeletal contraction on venous return.
*   **Pulmonary artery distortion:** Any distortion or other anatomical obstruction can be **addressed before** the Fontan operation.
*   **AV valve regurgitation and venous drainage:** Significant atrioventricular valve regurgitation and abnormal venous drainage can be **corrected before** the Fontan operation. Severe and longstanding volume overloading prior to Fontan circulation should be avoided.
*   **Sinus rhythm and RA size:** Pre-operative sinus rhythm and normal right atrial size do not seem to be a prerequisite for a successful outcome, but the **development of arrhythmias after the procedure is associated with impaired prognosis**.
*   **Mean PAP:** A raised pre-operative mean pulmonary artery pressure (>15 mmHg) should **not be taken as a threshold in isolation** but should be considered in the context of the evaluation of pulmonary blood flow. Significantly higher mean PAP or the need for pulmonary vasodilators, however, represent a peri-operative risk factor and subsequent failure.
*   **PVR:** Pulmonary vascular resistance is the **most important pre-operative criterion** and should be **<4 units/m²**. Assessment of the pulmonary vascular bed can be difficult in the presence of aortopulmonary collaterals and systemic-to-pulmonary artery shunts.
*   **PA size:** The dimensions of the right and left pulmonary arteries, expressed as a McGoon ratio, is considered an important risk factor for mortality or take-down of the Fontan circulation.

</div></details>

The risk of failure of the Fontan circulation is higher in a patient with a **McGoon ratio <1.8**.

## 22 What are the advantages of a venous to pulmonary shunt over a systemic to pulmonary shunt?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Desaturated blood** (systemic venous rather than arterial blood) is shunted to the lungs with a **much greater efficiency** on the increments of arterial oxygen saturation.
*   Diversion of the systemic venous return to the lungs **reduces the volume load on the heart**, whereas an arterial shunt constitutes an additional ventricular volume and workload to the systemic ventricle.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 23 How does fenestration assist the Fontan circulation?</summary><div class="details-content">

Fenestration of the Fontan circulation can be created with the atrial baffle conduit, allowing a **right-to-left shunt** through this defect.

*   **Cardiac output is maintained** at the expense of some arterial desaturation
*   **Prevents undue elevation of the systemic venous pressure** to prevent capillary leakage in the interstitial compartments of the body
*   **Improved long-term outcomes**, including:
    *   Better functional capacity
    *   Preservation of liver function
    *   Possible reduction of longstanding portal hypertension (varices, malabsorption, protein-losing enteropathy)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 24 What are the short-term complications of surgery following a Fontan operation?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Low cardiac output** with or without heart failure, often due to postoperative increased pulmonary vascular resistance, low pulmonary flow and reduced systemic preload. A functional fenestration can mitigate this problem at the expense of varying degrees of desaturation.
*   **Persistent pleural effusion**
*   **Chylous leak**
*   **Thrombus formation** in the systemic venous pathways
*   **Acute liver dysfunction**, generally due to venous hypertension

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 25 What are the long-term complications of surgery following a Fontan operation?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Hepatomegaly and ascites**
*   **Supraventricular arrhythmia.** Freedom from clinically significant arrhythmias after 5 and 10 years is **90% and 85%**, respectively.
*   **Progressive decrease in oxygen saturation** due to obstruction of the venous pathways, leakage in an intra-atrial baffle or development of a pulmonary arteriovenous fistula.
*   **Protein-losing enteropathy**
*   **Failing systemic ventricle** requiring heart transplant

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 26 What are the short-term outcomes after a Fontan operation for tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Mortality is approximately **1–2%**.
*   The progressive improvement in peri-operative outcomes of recent years is attributable to several factors:
    *   More energy-efficient design (TCPC)
    *   Shorter bypass time
    *   Use of staged procedures (BCPS initially followed by TCPC)
    *   Use of fenestration
*   Higher mortality remains in patients with complex cases, such as **heterotaxy syndrome** and **hypoplastic left heart syndrome**.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 27 What are the long-term outcomes after a Fontan operation for tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The survival rate at **5 and 10 years** is **85–93%** and **83–91%**, respectively.
*   Chronic elevation of the systemic venous pressures is likely to play an important role and is reflected in late complications (atrial arrhythmias, hepatic dysfunction).
*   **Heart transplant for a failing Fontan circulation** may be associated with adverse outcomes due to:
    *   Difficulty in assessing haemodynamics
    *   Complex anatomy
    *   Multiple prior procedures
    *   Unique pathologic changes

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 28 What is a Fontan conversion?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Problem with Traditional Atriopulmonary Connection</summary><div class="details-content">

Traditional procedures for right ventricular bypass, where the right atrium was used as a conduit between the IVC and the pulmonary tree, were inefficient due to **progressive dilatation of the atrial chamber**, leading to turbulent, slow flow. Right atrial dilatation often led to:
*   Deposit of large thrombi within its cavity
*   Frequent occurrence of supraventricular arrhythmias

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rationale for Conversion</summary><div class="details-content">

Several 4D-MRI studies have indicated that forcing low-velocity venous flow through a **tube-like conduit of constant diameter** increases significantly the overall efficiency of the circulation, reducing overall pressure and greatly reducing thromboembolism and arrhythmias.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Procedure</summary><div class="details-content">

Several centres have adopted the **Fontan conversion operation**, where a new TCPC is constructed, usually using an extracardiac Gore-Tex® conduit.

**Critical adjuncts to the operation include:**
*   **a)** Fenestration
*   **b)** Atrial wall reduction plasty
*   **c)** Removal of all intracavitary thrombi
*   **d)** Arrhythmia surgery (usually modified Maze procedure)

A **permanent pacemaker** may also be required to ensure adequate atrioventricular resynchronisation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Outcomes</summary><div class="details-content">

Marked improvement has been reported in symptoms following Fontan conversion, but whether this leads to an increase in survival or the need for transplantation is still unclear.

</div></details>
