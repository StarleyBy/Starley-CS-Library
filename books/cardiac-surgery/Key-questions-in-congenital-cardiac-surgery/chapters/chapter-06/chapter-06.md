# Congenital Angiography and Catheter Interventions

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Radiation Safety

*   **Factors increasing exposure:** Age, body size, distance, study length, frame rate
*   **ALARP tactics:** Iso-centring, lowest frame rate, collimation, minimise beam-on time, remove anti-scatter grids for <20 kg
*   **Hazards:**
    *   Skin: erythema at 2 Gy, hair loss at >6 Gy, telangiectasis at 10–15 Gy
    *   Eye: cataracts (operators wear protective goggles)
    *   Malignancy: stochastic effect, brain/skin/thyroid highest risk (wear thyroid shield)

| Pulmonary stenosis | 0° + 30° cranial | 90° |
| RVOT/MPA | 10° LAO + 40° cranial | 90° |

### Three-Dimensional Rotational Angiography (3DRA)

*   C-arm rotation over 3–5 seconds with timed contrast injection
*   Reconstruction <30 seconds, can be rotated in 3D
*   Fusion imaging: pre-registered CT/MRI overlaid on live fluoroscopy → reduced contrast/radiation

### Haemodynamic Waveforms

| Waveform | Normal Values | Abnormalities |
|----------|---------------|---------------|
| **RA** | Mean 2–8 mmHg | Elevated a wave (tricuspid stenosis/atresia, ↓RV compliance); elevated v wave (RV failure, severe TR); prominent y descent (constriction, restriction); blunted y descent (tamponade) |
| **RV** | Systolic 15–30 mmHg, EDP 2–8 mmHg | — |
| **PCWP** | 4–12 mmHg | Reflects left atrial pressure; a wave (atrial contraction), v wave (atrial filling) |
| **Aortic** | — | Widened pulse pressure (AR, PDA, ruptured sinus, AV malformations); reduced pulse pressure (<50% of diastolic: tamponade, heart failure, AS) |

### Pressure Gradients

*   Peak-to-peak systolic gradient (catheter) vs maximum instantaneous gradient (Doppler) — different timing; mean gradients are better for comparison
*   Doppler gradients are not "overestimates" — measured at different point in cardiac cycle

### Valvuloplasty

| Procedure | Indications | Technique | Success Criteria |
|-----------|-------------|----------|------------------|
| **Aortic** | Neonatal critical AS; AS with depressed LV function; peak gradient ≥50 mmHg (or ≥40 mmHg with symptoms) | Femoral/carotid access; balloon:annulus ratio 0.8–0.9; Tyshak II balloons | >50% ↓gradient; >25% ↑valve area; ≤mild AR |
| **Pulmonary** | Critical PS; peak gradient >40 mmHg (catheter) or >60 mmHg (echo); RV dysfunction | Femoral vein; balloon:annulus ratio 1.2–1.4; longer balloons for stability | — |

### Stenting and Angioplasty

| Vessel | Indications | Considerations |
|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Pulmonary artery** | Gradient 20–30 mmHg; RV pressure >⅔ systemic; flow discrepancy 35/65% | Balloon angioplasty first in small/complex anatomy; stents should have adult-size potential; MDT discussion |
| **Pulmonary vein** | Congenital, post-operative, post-ablation | Stents better than balloons; rarely curative in congenital disease; pulmonary haemorrhage risk |
| **Ductus arteriosus** | Cyanotic heart disease (TOF, PA), HLHS hybrid, non-HLHS with single-stage repair contraindicated | Straight ductus (≤2 bends); stent diameter 3.5–4 mm (term), 3 mm (<3 kg); femoral vessel injury risk |
| **Coarctation** | Recoarctation with gradient >20 mmHg; native coarctation in unstable patients (palliation) | Primary stenting preferred >20 kg; covered CP Stent™ reduces rupture risk; open cell stents for arch hypoplasia |

### Defect Closure

| Defect | Indications | Devices | Complications |
|--------|-------------|--------|---------------|
| **PDA** | Premature <2.4 kg with low PVR; large PDA with reactive PVR (after testing); contraindicated in Eisenmenger | Coils (<2 mm); Amplatzer Duct Occluder, Vascular Plug II, MVP | Embolisation, aortic/pulmonary obstruction, haemolysis |
| **Secundum ASD** | RV volume overload; paradoxical emboli; platypnoea-orthodeoxia | Self-centring (Amplatzer Septal Occluder, Figulla, Cera, GORE CARDIOFORM); non-self-centring (GORE Septal Occluder) | — |
| **VSD** | Haemodynamically significant (LV volume overload, heart failure, elevated RV pressures); contraindicated if PVR >7 WU/m², sepsis, need for antiplatelet contraindication | Muscular VSD Occluder (waist + 8 mm discs), Membranous VSD Occluder (unequal discs), Duct Occluder II | Heart block, aortic/tricuspid valve damage, embolisation |
| **Fontan fenestration** | Favourable haemodynamics: balloon occlusion → ↑SaO₂ with modest ↑Fontan pressure or <20% ↓CO | ASD occluder, PFO occluder, VSD occluder, vascular plug, covered stent | — |

### Hybrid VSD Closure

*   Median sternotomy, beating heart, no CPB
*   RV free wall puncture → wire across VSD → sheath into LV → deploy LV disc → pull back → deploy RV disc
*   For patients where CPB contraindicated or challenging anatomy

### Percutaneous Pulmonary Valve Replacement (PPVR)

**Indications:**
*   PR >25% with RVEDVi >150 mL/m², RVESVi >80 mL/m², RVEF <47%, or LVEF <55%
*   RVOT obstruction with RV systolic pressure ≥70% systemic + at least moderate TR
*   Heart failure symptoms, exercise intolerance (≤70% predicted VO₂), syncope, QRS >160 ms

**Anatomical criteria:** Previously implanted conduit (16–29 mm) or RVOT/PA of similar diameter

**Valves:**
*   **Edwards SAPIEN 3/Ultra:** Balloon-expandable, cobalt-chromium frame, bovine pericardial leaflets; 23–29 mm; 16 Fr sheath
*   **Edwards Alterra Adaptive Prestent:** Self-expanding stent for native RVOT → landing zone for SAPIEN 3
*   **Medtronic Melody:** Bovine jugular vein valve in platinum-iridium frame; 18–22 mm; 22 Fr sheath

**Pre-procedural:** CMR/CTA to define anatomy; coronary compression assessment with balloon inflation in landing zone (especially post-arterial switch/Ross)

### Atrial Septostomy

*   Rashkind procedure (1966): balloon passed across interatrial septum, inflated, pulled back to tear fossa ovalis
*   Performed at bedside with echo guidance when possible
*   Indications: TGA with restrictive/intact atrial communication; mitral atresia/HLHS requiring L→R shunt; PA-IVS, tricuspid atresia, TAPVD requiring R→L shunt; left atrial hypertension on ECMO

## 1. Radiation Safety</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Factors Contributing to Higher Radiation Exposure

Factors such as age, body size, distance between the X-ray generator and the body, length of the study, and frame rate of fluoroscopy and image acquisition contribute to the level of exposure.

**Tactics to decrease radiation dose and improve image quality:**
*   Adequate positioning, with patients iso-centred and straight on the table
*   Using the lowest acceptable frame rate during pulsed fluoroscopy and cine angiography
*   Avoiding exposure of unnecessary body parts (arms, skull)
*   Using the lowest acceptable magnification mode
*   Using collimators and filters
*   Centring the region of interest correctly in the field
*   Keeping the image intensifier as close to the patient as possible and the X-ray tube as far away as possible
*   Using angiographic projections that reduce operator exposure
*   Minimising beam-on time
*   Removing anti-scatter grids when catheterising small children (<20 kg)

#### Potential Hazard Factors of Higher Radiation Exposure

**Radiation-induced skin reaction** (may not appear until 2–3 weeks after exposure):
*   Threshold for mild transient skin erythema: 2 Gy
*   Erythema and hair loss expected with peak skin doses exceeding 6 Gy
*   Doses of 10–15 Gy may cause telangiectasis and chronic skin changes
*   Higher exposure may cause skin ulceration

**Radiation eye injury:**
*   High doses can damage conjunctiva, iris, sclera, and retinal vessels
*   Lens may sustain irreversible damage from relatively low doses → cataracts
*   Operators advised to wear protective goggles

**Malignancy:**
*   No clear exposure threshold (stochastic effect)
*   Most radiation-induced damage is rapidly repaired; occasional misrepair of DNA breaks can cause point mutations, chromosomal translocations, and gene fusions linked to cancer
*   Organs with higher risk: brain, skin, thyroid
*   Operators advised to wear full body shield and thyroid shield

## 3. Three-Dimensional Rotational Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Three-dimensional rotational angiography (3DRA) is used to guide various transcatheter therapies, including percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation, coarctation stent placement, anatomical evaluation of single-ventricle palliation, and pulmonary artery anatomy.

**Technique:**
*   Rotational angiographic images are acquired with C-arm rotation in the axial plane over 3–5 seconds
*   Timed contrast injection uniformly opacifies the vascular anatomy during acquisition
*   Images are dynamic and demonstrate cardiac structures in 3D perspective, allowing measurements throughout the cardiac cycle (Figure 2)

![](_page_3_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Three-dimensional angiographic reconstructions demonstrating: A) transverse arch aneurysm (\*); and B) ascending aortic compression with balloon inflation in the RVOT during coronary artery assessment in preparation for transcatheter pulmonary valve intervention.**

*   Reconstruction occurs immediately after rotation (<30 seconds) but requires manual optimisation
*   Reconstructed images can be rotated in 3D to determine optimal views

**Fusion imaging:** 3DRA technology can use pre-registered CT or MRI datasets, allowing live guidance of interventional procedures with a 3D roadmap overlay (Figure 3). This reduces contrast and radiation exposure and shortens procedural times.

![](_page_4_Picture_4.jpeg)

RBCA = right brachiocephalic artery; LCCA = left common carotid artery; LSCA = left subclavian artery.**

## 4. Haemodynamic Waveforms</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Right Atrial Pressure Waveform (Figure 4)

Normal mean right atrial (RA) pressure: **2–8 mmHg**

![](_page_5_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 4. Right atrial pressure waveform.**

**Normal waves:**
*   **a wave:** Atrial contraction; follows P wave; usually dominant; absent in atrial fibrillation/flutter
*   **c wave:** Occasional; follows a wave by PR interval; ventricular contraction causing tricuspid valve bulging
*   **v wave:** Passive venous filling during RV systole (tricuspid closed); follows T wave
*   **x descent:** Pressure decline after atrial contraction (atrial relaxation)
*   **y descent:** Pressure fall after tricuspid opening (rapid ventricular filling)

**Abnormalities:**
*   **Cannon a wave:** AV asynchrony (complete heart block) — atrium contracts against closed tricuspid valve
*   **Elevated a wave:** Restricted atrial emptying (tricuspid atresia/stenosis, ↓RV compliance)
*   **Elevated v wave:** RV failure, severe tricuspid regurgitation, restrictive physiology
*   **Increased c wave:** AV block
*   **Prominent x descent:** Constrictive physiology, RV ischaemia
*   **Blunted x descent:** Atrial fibrillation
*   **Prominent y descent:** Constrictive pericarditis, restrictive myopathy
*   **Blunted y descent:** Tamponade, RV ischaemia

---

#### Right Ventricular Pressure Waveform (Figure 5)

Normal RV systolic pressure: **15–30 mmHg** (equal to main PA pressure)
RV end-diastolic pressure (RVEDP): **2–8 mmHg** (equal to RA pressure)

![](_page_7_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 5. Right ventricular pressure waveform. RVEDP = right ventricular end-diastolic pressure.**

**Systole:** Rapid pressure rise → tricuspid closure, pulmonary valve opening. Upstroke occurs immediately after QRS complex.

**Diastole:**
*   Early rapid-filling wave (tricuspid opening)
*   Slower-filling period
*   Atrial systole (a wave) — identical to RA a wave; end of a wave = RVEDP; atrial systole accounts for 25% of RV diastolic filling

---

#### Pulmonary Capillary Wedge Pressure (Figure 6)

PCWP is an occluded pressure reflecting downstream left atrial pressure. In the absence of pulmonary venous obstruction or mitral valve disease, it reflects left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP).

![](_page_8_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Pulmonary capillary wedge pressure. LVDP = left ventricular diastolic pressure.**

**Normal range:** 4–12 mmHg

**Waveform components:**
*   **a wave:** Atrial contraction
*   **v wave:** Left atrial filling during LV contraction
*   **c wave:** Mitral valve closure (may not be visible)
*   **x descent:** Left atrial relaxation
*   **y descent:** Left ventricular diastole

**Clinical use:** Diagnostic catheterisation; critical care (Swan-Ganz catheter)

---

#### Aortic Pressure Waveform

During ventricular contraction, ascending aortic pressure parallels LV pressure. After aortic valve closure, aortic pressure declines more slowly than LV pressure (aortic capacitance, influenced by pressure waves from thoracic aorta and branches).

**Diastolic decline** depends on:
*   Aortic valve integrity (aortic regurgitation)
*   Capacitance and resistance of peripheral circulation
*   Abnormal aortopulmonary connections (PDA, collaterals)

**Pulse pressure = systolic pressure – diastolic pressure**

**Widened pulse pressure** (diastolic pressure < pulse pressure) causes:
*   Aortic regurgitation, PDA, ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm
*   Large aortopulmonary collaterals, aortopulmonary window
*   Peripheral vasodilation, arteriovenous malformations

**Reduced pulse pressure** (<50% of diastolic value) causes:
*   Tamponade, heart failure, cardiogenic shock, aortic stenosis

## 5. Pressure Gradient Measurement</summary><div class="details-content">

The most frequently utilised cardiac pressure data are the amplitudes of the measured pressures themselves and the pressure differences (gradients) between adjacent areas (e.g., ascending and descending aorta; Figure 7).

![](_page_9_Figure_8.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Left heart cardiac catheterisation pressure measurement demonstrating coarctation of the aorta with significant gradient on pullback from ascending to descending aorta. Gradient is calculated by comparing systolic peak-to-peak pressures across the affected area.**

**Key concepts:**
*   Gradients result from fixed or dynamic obstructions; magnitude generally reflects severity
*   Pressures measured with fluid-filled catheters and manometers calibrated to mid-chest height
*   **Peak-to-peak systolic gradient:** Difference between peak systolic pressures in adjacent chambers/areas
*   **Doppler-derived gradient:** Maximum instantaneous pressure gradient (occurs before peak pressures)

**Important:** Doppler gradients are NOT "overestimates" — they are obtained at a chronologically different time in the cardiac cycle than peak-to-peak gradients. Mean pressure gradients from both modalities represent better comparison.

**Clinical consideration:** Doppler gradients are measured on unsedated patients; catheterisation gradients are measured on sedated/anaesthetised patients with likely lower cardiac output.

</div></details>

## 7. Valvuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Aortic Valvuloplasty

**Class I indications:**
*   Newborn with duct-dependent isolated valvular aortic stenosis (AS)
*   Children with isolated valvular AS with depressed LV systolic function
*   Children with isolated valvular AS with resting peak systolic valve gradient (catheter) ≥50 mmHg
*   Children with isolated valvular AS with resting peak systolic valve gradient (catheter) ≥40 mmHg AND symptoms (angina/syncope) or ischaemic ST-T changes at rest or with exercise

**Pre-procedural assessment:** Echocardiography — valve morphology, peak instantaneous and mean gradient, annulus diameter, insufficiency, LV function

**Technique:**
*   General anaesthesia
*   Vascular access: femoral artery (or carotid artery percutaneous/cutdown, rarely umbilical)
*   Haemodynamic assessment of gradient
*   Aortic angiography to delineate valve plane and assess regurgitation (LV angiography rarely adds value and may impair LV function)
*   Balloon selection: balloon:annulus ratio 0.8–0.9; increase in 0.5–1 mm increments; Tyshak II® balloons (low profile, compliant) favoured

![](_page_14_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 10. Angiographic evaluation of aortic valve annulus: A) measurements of aortic valve annulus (arrow) and sinotubular junction after retrograde aortic root injection in adult patient (pacing catheter in RV for balloon stability); B) measurements via transcarotid approach in neonatal critical aortic stenosis with thickened leaflets (arrow).**

**Success criteria (in clinical context):**
*   >50% decrease in pressure gradient
*   >25% increase in aortic valve area
*   Mild or less aortic regurgitation

---

#### Pulmonary Valvuloplasty in Isolated Pulmonary Stenosis

**Indications:**
*   Critical pulmonary valve stenosis
*   Peak-to-peak catheter gradient >40 mmHg or echocardiographic peak instantaneous gradient >60 mmHg
*   Clinically significant pulmonary valvular obstruction with RV dysfunction

**Technique:**
*   General anaesthesia (sedation in older patients)
*   Vascular access: femoral vein (arterial line optional)
*   Biplane RV angiogram to confirm obstruction site, measure pulmonary valve annulus (Figure 11), assess RV function
*   Haemodynamic assessment of gradient
*   Balloon selection: balloon:annulus ratio 1.2–1.4; longer balloons for stability (20 mm neonates/infants, 30 mm children, 40 mm adolescents/adults)

![](_page_15_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 11. Angiographic evaluation of pulmonary valve annulus in lateral projection after RV angiogram demonstrating thickened pulmonary valve leaflets with doming (asterisks). RV = right ventricle; RVOT = right ventricular outflow tract; MPA = main pulmonary artery; A = pulmonary valve annulus.**

**Complications:** More common in neonates/infants with severe stenosis. Rare: RBBB, femoral venous obstruction, tricuspid valve injury, pulmonary regurgitation, balloon rupture.

**Technical note:** Use flow-directed catheter (balloon wedge) to cross tricuspid valve, then soft-tipped wire to cross pulmonary valve before balloon angioplasty catheter positioning — prevents catheter/wire passing through chordae and causing damage.

## 8. Pulmonary Artery and Pulmonary Vein Interventions</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Pulmonary Artery Angioplasty and Stent Placement

**Indications for balloon angioplasty alone:**
*   Severe main or branch pulmonary artery stenosis, particularly in very small patients or complex anatomy where primary stent implantation not viable

**Significant stenosis defined by:**
*   Measurable gradient 20–30 mmHg across stenotic area
*   RV or proximal MPA pressure >⅔ systemic pressure (from distal obstruction)
*   Relative flow discrepancy between lungs of 35%/65% or worse

**Caveats:** In low pulmonary flow situations (Glenn, Fontan), gradient is unreliable. With decompression run-off to compliant contralateral artery, pressure gradient alone is not a good indicator of severity.

**Indications for stents:**
*   Main or branch PA stenosis with inadequate or no persistent response to balloon dilation (Figure 12)

![](_page_17_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Lateral projection angiogram demonstrating: A) stenosis of left pulmonary artery (LPA)(arrow); B) improvement of LPA diameter (arrow) following balloon angioplasty and stent placement.**

**Stent considerations:**
*   Ideal: stents with adult-size potential; smaller stents are palliative (commitment to surgical removal or future enlargement)
*   Risks: vessel perforation, arrhythmia, bleeding
*   Stent-specific risks: misplacement, embolisation (may require emergent surgical removal), jailing of adjacent branch vessels
*   Stents beyond bifurcation can cause complex branch stenosis requiring extensive reconstructions
*   **MDT discussion recommended** with both interventional cardiology and surgical teams

---

#### Pulmonary Vein Angioplasty and Stent Placement

**Indications:**
*   Isolated congenital lesion or associated with other cardiac defects
*   Acquired after corrective surgery for anomalous pulmonary venous connections
*   Complication of extreme prematurity
*   Complication of pulmonary vein isolation ablation (adult AF)

**Technique:**
*   Stents achieve better results and longer patency than balloons; final stent diameter important
*   Paediatric pulmonary vein interventions in congenital disease are rarely curative
*   Pulmonary artery wedge angiograms with levophase useful if transseptal access not available
*   Cutting and drug-eluting balloons may be used

![](_page_19_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 13. Angiographic images demonstrating: A) left pulmonary vein stenosis at veno-atrial junction (arrow), and venous collateralisation (asterisk); B) unobstructed drainage in atrial cavity (arrow) following stent placement.**

**Complications:** Vessel dissection, stent malposition/embolisation, pulmonary haemorrhage (not uncommon — cross-match blood, monitor in critical care)

## 9. Ductus Arteriosus Interventions</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Ductus Arteriosus Stenting

**Indications:**
*   Cyanotic heart disease (severe tetralogy of Fallot, all forms of pulmonary atresia) — alternative to aortopulmonary surgical shunts
*   Neonates with hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) — hybrid procedure (alternative to Norwood)
*   Neonates with non-HLHS lesions where single-stage repair contraindicated

**Patient selection:** Most favourable with morphologically straight ductus (≤2 bends), requiring reliable palliation for 3–6 months

**Stent size:**
*   Full-term neonates: 3.5–4 mm diameter (adequate palliation without excessive flow)
*   <3 kg: 3 mm diameter

**Complications:** Femoral vessel injury/occlusion, duct injury/spasm requiring ECMO or bypass, stent malposition/embolisation (may require surgery)

**Hybrid procedure (HLHS and non-HLHS):** Surgical placement of bilateral branch pulmonary artery bands + stent delivery through median sternotomy (no CPB)

---

#### Patent Ductus Arteriosus Closure

**Indications:**
*   Small/premature neonates (<2.4 kg) with low PVR and persistent L→R shunt — control overcirculation, prevent endarteritis
*   Infants with large PDA and elevated PVR — full haemodynamic study to assess vasoreactivity; closure if reactive
*   **Contraindicated** in Eisenmenger syndrome (persistent R→L shunt)

**Devices:**
*   **Coils:** Small PDA (<2 mm). Flipper® Cook coil (3 mm × 3 loops to 8 mm × 5 loops); Nit-Occlud® coil (larger ducts, controlled release)
*   **Devices (Figure 14):** Amplatzer™ Duct Occluder, Amplatzer™ Duct Occluder II, Amplatzer™ Vascular Plug II, MVP™ microvascular plug

![](_page_21_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 14. Patent ductus arteriosus closure devices: A) Amplatzer™ Duct Occluder; B) Amplatzer™ Duct Occluder II; C) Amplatzer™ Vascular Plug II.** *Reproduced with permission from Abbott.*

Device selected based on morphology (Krichenko classification: tubular, conical, saccular, elongated; Figure 15) and minimum duct diameter.

![](_page_22_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 15. Angiographical images demonstrating PDA morphologies: A) tubular; B) conical; C) saccular; D) elongated.**

**Complications (rare):** Device embolisation, aortic/pulmonary obstruction (small infants), haemolysis (more common with coils)

## 10. Atrial Septostomy and ASD Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Atrial Septostomy/Stenting

Balloon atrial septostomy (Rashkind procedure, 1966) is the longest established interventional cardiac catheterisation procedure.

**Technique:**
*   Performed at bedside with echo guidance when possible (minimises radiation)
*   Access: femoral or umbilical vein
*   Balloon passed across interatrial septum (PFO if present), inflated, quickly pulled back to tear fossa ovalis membrane
*   Stent may be placed under fluoroscopy for muscular septum/restrictive muscular communication

**Indications:**
*   TGA with restrictive/intact atrial communication — enhance atrial mixing
*   Restriction to obligatory L→R atrial shunt (mitral atresia, HLHS) — unrestrictive L→R shunting
*   PA-IVS, tricuspid atresia, TAPVD — unrestrictive R→L shunting
*   Left atrial hypertension on ECMO support — decompression

---

#### Transcatheter Closure of Secundum Atrial Septal Defect

**Indications:**
*   Right heart volume overload
*   Sequelae of paradoxical emboli (stroke, recurrent TIA)
*   Symptomatic transient R→L shunting causing cyanosis where communication not needed for cardiac output (platypnoea-orthodeoxia)

**Contraindications:**
*   Advanced pulmonary vascular disease
*   Restrictive left heart physiology (elderly with impaired LV function)

**Procedure:**
*   Echocardiographic guidance (intracardiac or TOE)
*   Defect sizing with compliant balloon inflated until flow stops (echo imaging)
*   Measure balloon waist (echo/fluoroscopy)
*   Select device based on measurement

**Available devices:**
*   **Self-centring:** Abbott Amplatzer™ Septal Occluder (Figure 16A,B), Occlutech Figulla Flex II®, CeraFlex™, Cardia Ultrasept™, GORE® CARDIOFORM ASD Occluder
*   **Non-self-centring:** GORE® Septal Occluder (Figure 16C,D); variants of self-centring devices

![](_page_24_Figure_13.jpeg)

**Figure 16. Commonly used devices for ASD closure: A) Amplatzer™ Septal Occluder; B) same device after placement under fluoroscopy and TOE guidance (anteroposterior facing position); C) GORE® Septal Occluder; D) same device after placement.** *Reproduced with permission from Abbott.*

In the United States, only Abbott Amplatzer™ Septal Occluder and GORE® Septal Occluder are approved for secundum ASD closure.

## 11. Fontan Fenestration Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

**Background:** Creating a R→L shunt decompression valve during Fontan completion reduces venous pressures and may prevent hepatic ischaemia (portal hypertension, cirrhosis, protein-losing enteropathy). Fenestration is deemed necessary in borderline physiology (diminished ventricular function, pulmonary artery lesions, elevated PVR).

**Benefit-cost:** Fenestration reduces venous pressures at the expense of lower systemic oxygen saturations. Chronic desaturation can lead to significant complications.

**Pre-closure evaluation:** Thorough haemodynamic assessment with temporary balloon occlusion — closure reasonable if:
*   Systemic oxygen saturation improves
*   Modest rise in Fontan pressure

## 12. Ventricular Septal Defect Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Transcatheter VSD Closure

VSD is the commonest congenital heart defect (25% of all defects) but is one of the rarer interventions.

**Indications:** Haemodynamically significant VSD (LV volume overload, heart failure, elevated RV pressures from L→R shunting). In some countries, small defects closed for cultural/social reasons.

**Contraindications:**
*   Pulmonary vascular disease (>7 Wood units/m²)
*   Sepsis
*   Contraindication to antiplatelet therapy
*   Defects with other cardiac lesions requiring surgery

**Risks:** Small infants <5 kg or abnormal septal wall planes carry additional risk. Perimembranous septum close to aortic valve, conduction tissue, tricuspid valve — heart block and valve damage remain serious complications.

**Devices (Figure 19):**
*   **Amplatzer™ Muscular VSD Occluder:** Self-expandable nitinol, two flat discs 8 mm larger than central connecting waist (7 mm long); waist size determines device size; delivery sheath 6–9 Fr (Figure 20)
*   **Amplatzer™ Membranous VSD Occluder:** Two discs of unequal size; sizes 4–18 mm; delivery sheath 7–9 Fr
*   **Amplatzer™ Duct Occluder II**

## 13. Percutaneous Pulmonary Valve Replacement</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Indications

Consider PPVR in patients with severe pulmonary regurgitation or stenosis who meet anatomical criteria for available valves.

**Haemodynamic criteria (any of the following):**
*   Pulmonary insufficiency >25% with:
    *   RVEDVi >150 mL/m²
    *   RVESVi >80 mL/m²
    *   RVEF <47%
    *   LVEF <55%
*   RVOT obstruction with RV systolic pressure ≥70% systemic + at least moderate TR
*   Symptoms/signs of heart failure, exercise intolerance (≤70% predicted peak VO₂ for age/sex), syncope (arrhythmia)
*   QRS duration >160 ms

**Anatomical criteria:** Previously implanted conduit 16–29 mm, or RVOT/pulmonary artery with similar diameters

**Additional considerations:** Valve-in-valve placement for prior surgical biological or transcatheter pulmonary valves. Assess and treat associated anomalies (PA stenosis, bifurcation stenosis, residual intracardiac defects) before PPVR.

**Pre-procedural imaging:** CMR or CTA to define anatomy

**Coronary compression risk:** Increases with prior coronary interventions (arterial switch, Ross). Perform coronary challenge: angiographic assessment with simultaneous balloon dilation in landing zone.

## 14. Coarctation of the Aorta Intervention</summary><div class="details-content">

Modern practice has evolved to predominantly primary stent angioplasty in patients >20 kg.

#### Balloon Angioplasty Indications

*   Recoarctation with transcatheter systolic gradient >20 mmHg or significant collateral vessels (suitable angiographic anatomy, irrespective of age)
*   Patients with univentricular heart or significant ventricular dysfunction (may not exhibit high gradients but are candidates)

**Native coarctation balloon angioplasty** is reasonable as a palliative measure to stabilise patients regardless of age when extenuating circumstances present (severely depressed ventricular function, severe MR, low cardiac output, end-organ dysfunction, systemic disease) (Figure 24).

![](_page_33_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 24. Lateral view angiographic images demonstrating: A) tight discrete hour-glass coarctation (arrows); B) improvement (red arrows) following balloon angioplasty.**

**Recurrence:** Higher for younger patients (<6 months); small but important aneurysm risk at any age.

#### Stent Placement Indications

*   Recurrent coarctation in patients of sufficient size for safe stent placement, where stent can be expanded to adult size, with transcatheter systolic gradient >20 mmHg (Figure 25)

![](_page_34_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 25. Lateral view angiographic images demonstrating: A) tight hourglass coarctation with mild pre-stenosis dilatation; B) improvement following balloon angioplasty and stent insertion (asterisk).**

**Stent selection:**
*   Where possible, use stent expandable to adult size (limiting factor: sheath size)
*   Covered CP Stent™ (Figure 26) offers protection from vessel disruption/bleeding; sheaths limit use to teenage/adult patients

![](_page_34_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 26. Covered CP Stent™ used for coarctation of the aorta.**

**Transverse arch hypoplasia with discrete coarctation:** May be treated with stent angioplasty (open cell design stents ideally suited) — challenges with crossing major head/neck vessels.
