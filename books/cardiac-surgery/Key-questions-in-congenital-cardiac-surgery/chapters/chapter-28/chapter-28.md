# Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Lesions

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Anatomy of the LVOT and Aortic Root
- **LVOT components:** Subaortic (anterior MV leaflet, membranous/muscular septum), aortic valve (three semilunar cusps, sinuses of Valsalva, coronary ostia), supravalvular (sinotubular junction to ascending aorta)
- **Aortic annulus:** No true anatomic structure; corresponds to lowest points of leaflet attachment (three-pointed crown) — "virtual basal ring"
- **Sinotubular junction (STJ):** Where commissure tips meet ascending aorta; normally narrower than mid-sinus level
- **Embryology:** Semilunar valves form 4th week; neural crest cells form endocardial cushions; spiral separation into aorta and PA

### Classification of LVOT Lesions by Sector
| Sector | Stenosis | Regurgitation | Dilatation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subvalvular | Membrane, HOCM, tunnel, accessory tissue | — | — |
| Valvular | BAV, UAV, quadricuspid | BAV, connective tissue diseases | Root dilatation (Marfan, BAV aortopathy) |
| Supravalvular | Williams syndrome (hourglass/diffuse) | — | Post-stenotic |

### Subaortic LVOTO
- **Discrete subaortic membrane:** Not present at birth, develops later; fibrous ring 3–5 mm below aortic valve
- **Other causes:** HOCM, accessory MV tissue/chordae, SAM, posterior septal deviation (with VSD), tunnel LVOTO, AVSD morphology

### Valvular Aortic Stenosis (Most Common)
- **Bicuspid aortic valve (BAV):** 1–2% of population; male predominance 2:1; may function normally for decades
- **Unicuspid (UAV):** All three commissures fused → "windsock" or "keyhole" orifice
- **Quadricuspid:** Extremely rare (usually truncus arteriosus)

#### Anatomical Classification of Aortic Valve Cusp Lesions
| Feature | Quadricuspid | Tricuspid | Bicuspid | Unicuspid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of cusps | 4 | 3 | 2 (1 full, 1–2 hypoplastic) | 1 |
| Number of commissures | 4 | 3 | 2 (1–2 full, 1 hypoplastic) | 1 |
| Number of raphes | 0 | 0 | 1–2 | 1 |

### Congenital Aortic Regurgitation
- Isolated congenital AR is rare
- **Causes:** BAV leaflet stretch, connective tissue diseases (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos, Turner), infective endocarditis, aortoventricular tunnel, post-intervention complication

### Supravalvular Aortic Stenosis
- Rare at birth; appears in early childhood
- **Williams syndrome:** Gene defect; associated with PA branch stenosis, coronary ostial stenosis
- **Morphology:** Hourglass (discrete) or diffuse (extending to arch)
- **Coronary risk:** Ostial stenosis, kinking of LMCA, membrane extending into sinuses

### Associated Abnormalities
- **Shone complex:** Parachute MV, supravalvular mitral ring, subaortic stenosis, coarctation
- **Endocardial fibroelastosis (EFE):** Fibrous replacement of endocardium
- **HLHS:** Extreme end of LVOTO spectrum
- Post-repair: Subaortic membrane after coarctation/arch repair; LVOT obstruction after AVSD repair

### Aetiology and Pathophysiology
- **Flow theory:** Abnormal foetal blood flow inhibits left heart development
- **Progressive nature:** Foetal AS at 18–20 weeks may progress to aortic atresia/HLHS by term
- **Foetal circulation:** LVOTO well-tolerated (systemic circulation via ductus)
- **Postnatal:** Ductal closure → LV must support systemic circulation; severe obstruction → low cardiac output, acidosis, death

### Clinical Presentation
- **Severe neonatal AS (gradient >30 mmHg):** Low cardiac output, acidosis, poor perfusion, death unless ductus maintained
- **Moderate AS:** Poor feeding, sweating on feeds, FTT, tachycardia, tachypnoea, diminished femoral pulses, systolic murmur
- **Mild AS:** Asymptomatic, incidental murmur
- **AR:** Dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND, wide pulse pressure, diastolic murmur, hyper-pulsatile arteries

### Natural History
- **Critical AS/HLHS with restrictive IAS:** Death within hours without treatment
- **Severe AS/HLHS:** High first-year mortality; survivors develop PVD
- **Mild AS/BAV:** Progressive stenosis over decades; LV hypertrophy → risk of lethal arrhythmias; late symptoms: dyspnoea, fatigue, angina, syncope (dangerous)

### Diagnostic Imaging
| Modality | Key Findings |
|---|---|
| **ECG** | LVH, strain pattern (late), ventricular ectopics |
| **CXR** | Often unremarkable; may show cardiomegaly, plethoric lungs (severe failure) |
| **Echocardiography** | Mainstay. Assess: level of obstruction, mechanism (membrane, valve morphology, septal deviation), severity (Doppler), AR, associated anomalies, LV function/hypertrophy/EFE |
| **MRI** | Anatomy, obstruction level, valve morphology, root size, regurgitant fraction, LV volumes/function |
| **CT** | Superior for infants (fast acquisition); 3D reconstruction, coronary anatomy, root sizing, arch anomalies |
| **Catheterisation** | Rarely diagnostic; used for balloon valvuloplasty (neonates), coronary assessment (Williams) |

### Indications for Intervention
- **Subvalvular (isolated):** Symptoms, peak gradient >50 mmHg, LVH on echo; CPET if symptoms vague
- **Neonatal valvular AS:** Gradient >30 mmHg (poor perfusion after ductal closure); intervention almost always indicated
- **Child/adult valvular AS:** Symptoms, peak gradient >50 mmHg, Doppler velocity >3.5 m/s
- **Aortic regurgitation:** Symptoms not controlled medically, progressive LVEDD increase, CPET limitation

### Treatment — Neonates and Infants
**"Resuscitate, Review, Repair, Recover, Rehabilitate"**
- **Resuscitate:** PGE₁ (reopen ductus), inotropes, ventilation, renal support
- **Review:** Correct acidosis, improve LV function
- **Repair options:** Balloon valvuloplasty (favoured) vs open valvotomy
- **Outcomes:** Comparable survival; balloon → greater gradient reduction but more AR; open → less AR but higher residual gradient
- **Recover/Rehabilitate:** All interventions non-definitive; reintervention inevitable

### Treatment — Children and Adults
- **Balloon valvuloplasty:** Minimal leaflet calcification, no more than mild AR
- **Open valvotomy:** First intervention option
- **Repair techniques:** Leaflet resuspension (Trusler, plication, free edge reinforcement), leaflet reconstruction (patch repair, augmentation), bicuspidisation/tricuspidisation (raphe resection, commissurotomy)
- **Replacement:** Ross/Ross-Konno (preferred in growing children — growth potential, no anticoagulation), homograft/bioprosthetic (no growth, limited durability), mechanical (durable but lifelong anticoagulation)
- **Avoid prosthetic replacement** until valve suitable for adult life can be implanted

### Supravalvular AS Repair
- **Anaesthesia risk:** Restricted coronary filling, possible proximal coronary stenoses
- **Techniques:** Transverse aortotomy, inspect coronaries, shave thickened media
- **Patch repair:** McGoon (one sinus), Doty inverted Y (two sinuses), Brom (three sinuses)
- **Diffuse disease:** Augment whole ascending aorta ± arch

### Severe Multi-Level LVOTO / HLHS
- Single-ventricle palliation (Norwood, hybrid, transplantation) or comfort care/termination
- If LV adequate and MV normal → attempt biventricular circulation
- **Tunnel LVOTO:** Ross-Konno procedure (resection + pulmonary autograft root replacement)

### Surgical Techniques
- **Approach:** Median sternotomy, CPB, cardioplegic arrest
- **Subvalvular:** Through aortic valve (or tricuspid/AVSD defect). Peel membrane (extends to MV and aortic leaflets). Myectomy confined to muscle between left and right commissures (avoid bundle of His). Remove accessory chordae (preserve MV function). EFE resection (indications unclear)
- **Prosthetic AVR:** Implant largest possible valve; annular enlargement (Nicks, Manougian, Konno) may be needed; protect coronaries and bundle of His

### Ross and Ross-Konno Operations
- Replace aortic valve with pulmonary autograft → growth potential, no anticoagulation
- Harvest pulmonary autograft with RVOT muscle tongue; incise between LVOT and RVOT across infundibular septum; implant autograft; reimplant coronary buttons
- Replace pulmonary valve with homograft (most common) or other valved conduit
- **Not suitable for:** BAV aortopathy, Marfan, other connective tissue diseases

### Bentall Operation (Root Replacement)
- Mainly ACHD population (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos, Turner, BAV aortopathy)
- **Special challenges:** Post-arterial switch (10% develop root dilatation/AR), post-truncus/DORV/Rastelli (calcified VSD patch), post-Fontan (coronaries difficult to locate), situs inversus/isomerism (coronary anomalies)

### Valve-Sparing Root Replacement
| Feature | Yacoub Remodelling | David V Reimplantation |
|---|---|---|
| Annulus distensibility | + | – |
| Cusp motion | ++ | (+) |
| Valve stability | + | + |
| STJ stability | + | + |
| LV-aortic junction stability | – | + |
- **David:** Resect sinuses, harvest coronaries, sleeve Dacron® tube over commissures, anchor to annulus, reimplant coronaries
- **Yacoub:** Tailor graft to replace sinuses, suture to aortic wall, reimplant coronaries; annulus remains outside graft → risk of continued dilatation

### Personalised External Aortic Root Support (PEARS)
- For root dilatation with minimal/mild AR (Marfan, similar aortopathies)
- Polymer mesh jacket made from 3D CT model; placed around root and ascending aorta (beating heart, no CPB)
- Jacket can be made 20% smaller than aorta → abolishes/reduces mild AR
- **Outcomes:** >200 cases, 16 years, no rupture/dissection; operative mortality 1%

### Complications of LVOT Surgery
- Bleeding, heart block (bundle of His damage), residual/recurrent stenosis (membrane, tunnel)
- Postoperative low output (poor LV diastolic compliance, especially with EFE)
- AR (leaflet prolapse after commissural incision)
- Aneurysm (patch dilatation after supravalvular repair)
- Myocardial ischaemia (coronary distortion in supravalvular AS repair)

### Long-Term Results
| Lesion | Key Outcomes |
|---|---|
| **Neonatal AS — Balloon** | 70% survival at 10 years; 26% freedom from reintervention; 61% freedom from moderate AR |
| **Neonatal AS — Open valvotomy** | 70–90% survival at 10 years; 66–78% freedom from reintervention; up to 90% freedom from moderate AR (case-dependent) |
| **Ross in infancy** | Operative mortality <10% (older children 1–3%); 95% survival at 5 years; freedom from AR 90% (2y) → 70% (5y); freedom from reintervention 37% at 5 years (mostly RV-PA conduit) |
| **Supravalvular AS** | Operative mortality 2–3%; residual gradient 15–20% (diffuse disease); 80% survival at 10 years; freedom from late reoperation 90% (10y) → 80% (20y) |
| **Discrete subaortic stenosis** | Operative mortality <5%; reoperation 10–15%; late AR up to 20%; 10-year survival >90% |
| **Tunnel LVOTO** | Higher operative risk, worse survival and freedom from reoperation than discrete |

## 1. Anatomy of the Left Ventricular Outflow Tract and Aortic Root</summary><div class="details-content">

#### What is the Left Ventricular Outflow Tract?

The **left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT)** represents the passage from the left ventricular (LV) cavity to the ascending aorta. It has three components:

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Subaortic** | Formed by anterior mitral valve leaflet, membranous septum, and muscular septum to which the aorta is attached (Figure 1) |
| **Aortic valve** | Three semilunar cusps suspended from aortic wall; create three sinuses (right, left, non-coronary); sinuses bulge outward creating flow vortices important for valve closure and coronary perfusion (recognised by Leonardo da Vinci) |
| **Supravalvular** | From sinotubular junction into proximal ascending aorta |

![](_page_0_Picture_7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1.** Anatomical structures of the subaortic outflow (viewed through the aortic valve). *Reproduced with permission from Professor Robert H. Anderson.*

#### What is the Aortic Annulus?

There is **no actual anatomic structure** that can be classed as an "annulus". It corresponds to the **lowest points of attachment of the aortic valve leaflets**, which form a three-pointed crown (Figure 2). An alternative name is **"virtual basal ring"** .

![](_page_1_Picture_5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.** Aortic root complex from "annulus" (green — lowest leaflet attachment) to sinotubular junction (blue). Leaflet attachment outline (red) forms a three-pointed crown. *Reproduced with permission from Professor Robert H. Anderson.*

#### Commissures and Subcommissural Area (Figure 3)

- **Commissure:** Meeting point of leaflets
- **Subcommissural area:** Tissue beneath commissure; composed of:
  - Muscular septum (beneath left/right commissure)
  - Membranous septum (beneath right/non-coronary commissure)
  - Anterior mitral valve leaflet (beneath left/non-coronary commissure)

![](_page_2_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 3.** Subcommissural area of the aortic valve and annulus. LVOT = left ventricular outflow tract. *Reproduced with permission from Professor Robert H. Anderson.*

#### What is the Sinotubular Junction?

The **sinotubular junction (STJ)** is the point where the tips of the commissures meet the ascending aorta. The aortic diameter at the STJ is normally **less than** that at the mid-sinus level.

#### Aortic Root Complex (Figure 4)

The whole complex of the aortic valve, sinuses of Valsalva, and coronary ostia represents the **aortic root**.

![](_page_3_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4.** Aortic root complex from aortic valve to sinotubular junction. *Reproduced with permission from Professor Robert H. Anderson.*

#### Embryological Development of the Aortic Valve

- Semilunar valves (aortic and pulmonary) begin to form in the **4th week** of gestation
- **Neural crest cells** migrate into the primitive arterial trunk, forming endocardial cushions
- Aortic and pulmonary valves differentiate as the primitive trunk undergoes spiral separation into aorta and pulmonary arteries

## 3. Subaortic Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Causes of Subaortic LVOTO (Figure 6)

**Intraventricular obstruction:**
- Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM)
- Accessory mitral valve tissue (accessory chordae, fibrous nodules/cysts)
- Systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve (SAM)

**Subvalvular stenosis:**
- Discrete membrane or ring (Figure 7)
- Posterior deviation of outlet IVS (with perimembranous or subaortic VSD)
- LVOT shape (atrioventricular septal defect)

In some cases, obstruction is **"tunnel"-like**, involving the whole outflow tract.

![](_page_5_Picture_13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 6.** Mechanisms of subaortic LVOTO: A) Subaortic membrane; B) Subaortic fibrous ridge; C) Septal hypertrophy; D) Mitral valve cysts; E) Accessory chordae; F) Abnormal aortoventricular angle.

#### Discrete Subaortic Membrane (Figure 7)

- **Never present at birth** — develops later
- Forms as a ring of fibrous tissue attached circumferentially to:
  - Interventricular septum
  - Anterior mitral valve leaflet
  - Fibrous trigones
- Located approximately **3–5 mm below** the aortic valve

![](_page_6_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7.** Operative image of resected subaortic membrane. *Reproduced with permission from Professor Robert H. Anderson.*

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 4. Valvular Aortic Stenosis (Congenital)</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic valvular lesions are the **most common form of LVOTO**. Stenosis results from:
- Reduced size of an otherwise normally structured valve
- Reduction of orifice area due to leaflet fusion or thickening
- Combination of both

#### Anatomical Variability

| Feature | Quadricuspid | Tricuspid | Bicuspid | Unicuspid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Number of cusps | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 |
| Cusp size | Non-equal | Equal | 1 full + 1–2 hypoplastic | — |
| Number of commissures | 4 | 3 | 2 (1–2 full + 1 hypoplastic) | 1 |
| Number of raphes | 0 | 0 | 1–2 | 1 |

#### Bicuspid Aortic Valve (BAV)

- **By far the most common anomaly**
- Presents with high variability
- May function normally for several decades
- Affects **1–2% of the population**
- Male:female ratio **2:1**

#### Unicuspid Aortic Valve (UAV)

- Fusion of all three commissures
- Creates a **"windsock orifice"** or **"keyhole opening"**
- Usually found between left and non-coronary leaflets
- Valve leaflets often thickened with myxomatous nodules

#### Quadricuspid Aortic Valve

- **Extremely rare**
- Usually found in the context of **truncus arteriosus**
- Typically regurgitant (may be stenotic)

#### Aortic Atresia

- Extreme end of the spectrum
- Valve is imperforate

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 5. Congenital Aortic Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

Isolated congenital aortic regurgitation is **rare**.

#### Causes

- **BAV:** Leaflet stretch → failure of coaptation (aggravated by sinus and STJ dilatation)
- **Connective tissue diseases:** Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, Turner syndrome (regurgitation with aortic root dilatation)
- **Infective endocarditis**
- **Aortoventricular tunnel:** One leaflet not attached to ventriculo-arterial junction → gap at annulus extending as tunnel into LV
- **Complication of surgery or balloon dilatation** for stenosis

</div></details>

## 6. Supravalvular Aortic Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

#### General Features

- Rarely evident at birth; may appear in early childhood
- Often associated with a gene defect as part of **Williams syndrome**
- Commonly affects pulmonary arteries as well (Figure 8)

#### Morphology (Figure 9)

- Narrowing at and just above the sinotubular junction
- **Hourglass (discrete diaphragm-like)** or **diffuse** (extending along ascending aorta and arch to isthmus)
- Externally: waist at STJ (may appear mild)
- Degree of obstruction increased by ridge of thickened media projecting into lumen

## 7. Associated Abnormalities and Syndromes</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Complex LVOTO / Complex Aortic Stenosis

When multiple associations occur, this is referred to as **complex LVOTO**.

#### Post-Repair Development

- **Subaortic membrane** may develop after successful repair of coarctation or aortic arch interruption (± VSD closure)
- After **AVSD repair**: ring or fibrous patch may develop in LVOT; also obstruction from secondary chordae from superior bridging leaflet

#### BAV and Coarctation

- BAV occurs in at least **10% of patients with coarctation**
- Both lesions strongly associated with **Turner syndrome**

#### Shone Complex (Figure 11)

Combination of:
- Mitral valve abnormalities (inflow ridge/supravalvular mitral membrane, parachute MV)
- LVOTO
- Aortic arch abnormalities (coarctation)

![](_page_11_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11.** CT in Shone complex: A) Small LV cavity (red asterisk), prominent IVS protruding into LVOT, small aortic annulus; B) Coarctation (white asterisk); C) Aortic valve stenosis (red arrow).

#### Endocardial Fibroelastosis (EFE)

- Left ventricular cavity may be undersized and/or endocardium replaced with fibrous tissue
- Associated with aortic valve stenosis and LVOTO

#### Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome (HLHS)

- Extreme end of the spectrum
- Left side of heart incapable of supporting systemic circulation

## 8. Aetiology and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Aetiology

- Wide spectrum of conditions; no single common causative factor
- **Flow theory:** Abnormal blood flows within embryonic heart may inhibit development
- **Genetic causes** also proposed
- **Progressive nature:** Foetus with AS at 18–20 weeks (adequate LV) may progress to aortic atresia with small LV and HLHS by term
- HLHS: ~1 in 3,500 births; BAV: 1–2% of population
- Both have **2:1 male:female ratio**
- Apart from Williams syndrome, no frequent genetic associations with recognised loci

#### Pathophysiology

**Foetal circulation (Figure 12):**
- Oxygenated blood from placenta passes through foramen ovale → left heart → LV → aortic valve
- LVOTO has **little impact** in foetus — systemic circulation maintained by RV through patent ductus arteriosus (Figure 13)

![](_page_12_Picture_7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 12.** Normal foetal circulation with bypass of pulmonary circulation via foramen ovale.

![](_page_13_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13.** Foetal circulation in severe LVOTO — systemic circulation maintained via ductus arteriosus.

**Postnatal circulation:**
- After birth, ductus closes → LV must maintain systemic circulation
- Severe LVOTO → low cardiac output, acidosis, rising lactate, diminished urine output → death
- If ductus remains open: systemic flow maintained, but as PVR falls → increased PBF → high output demand on RV → cardiac failure

## 9. Clinical Presentation and Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Symptoms and Signs

**Severe LVOTO (neonatal):**
- Low cardiac output state
- Acidosis, rising lactate, diminishing urine output → death
- If ductus open: systemic flow maintained but cardiac failure develops

**Moderate LVOTO:**
- Poor feeding, sweating on feeds, failure to thrive
- Tachycardia, tachypnoea, dynamic cardiac impulse
- Hepatomegaly, subcostal recession, grunting
- Cool pallid skin with mottling, diminished femoral pulses
- Systolic murmur

**Mild LVOTO:**
- Asymptomatic
- Detected by systolic murmur on routine examination

**Aortic Regurgitation:**
- May be asymptomatic initially
- Symptoms: dyspnoea, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea
- Severe/sudden onset: tachycardia, fatigue, chest pain
- Signs: wide pulse pressure, diastolic murmur, hyper-pulsatile peripheral arteries (especially prominent in infants/teenagers); visible pulsation of carotids, abdominal wall, or femoral fossa

#### Natural History

**Severe/critical AS and HLHS:**
- Neonates with restrictive IAS may not survive first few hours without treatment
- High risk of death throughout first year
- Survivors develop pulmonary vascular disease (persistent high PAP)

**Mild AS / BAV:**
- May not develop symptoms until well into adult life
- Mildly stenotic valve at birth tends to become more stenotic with time
- LV hypertrophy develops → increasing risk of potentially lethal ventricular arrhythmias
- Late symptoms: increasing dyspnoea on exercise, fatigue, angina pectoris on exercise
- **Syncope on exercise or at rest** → late and dangerous symptom, associated with increased mortality

## 11. Indications for Intervention</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Subvalvular LVOTO (Isolated)

- **Symptoms**
- **Peak-to-peak gradient >50 mmHg** on echocardiography
- Signs of LV hypertrophy
- Cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) if symptoms vague or not admitted

#### Subvalvular LVOTO with VSD (Posterior Malalignment)

- Intervention indicated by criteria for VSD closure
- Outlet obstruction increases L→R shunting through VSD → lowers threshold for surgical closure

#### Subvalvular LVOTO with AVSD

- Normally improves or disappears when AVSD is addressed
- Rarely requires surgery in neonatal period

#### Valvular Aortic Stenosis

- **Neonates:** Gradient >30 mmHg → likely poor systemic perfusion after ductal closure; intervention almost always indicated
- **Children:** Symptoms, peak gradient >50 mmHg, Doppler velocity >3.5 m/s
- No standardised indexed valve orifice area values for children
- Adult congenital population: follows general indications for acquired AS

#### Aortic Insufficiency

- Primary AI rare in neonates; not uncommon as complication of valvotomy/balloon valvuloplasty
- **Indications:** Insufficiency not controlled medically with diuretics and ACE inhibitors; progressive increase in LVEDD indexed to BSA; CPET limitation (older patients)
- Where AI associated with AS: indications follow dominant component (stenosis appears worse with significant regurgitation due to larger stroke volume)
- Adult congenital population: same indications as acquired disease

</div></details>

## 12. Treatment Principles — Neonates and Infants with Severe Aortic Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

**Principles:** Resuscitate, Review, Repair, Recover, Rehabilitate

#### Resuscitate

- **Prostaglandin infusion** to encourage ductus reopening
- Inotropic support, mechanical ventilation, renal support as needed

#### Review

- Correct metabolic acidosis
- Improve ventricular systolic function

#### Repair Options

| Procedure | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Balloon aortic valvuloplasty** (generally favoured) | Less invasive; greater gradient reduction | More postoperative AR (if balloon too large) |
| **Open surgical valvotomy** (preferred in some centres) | More precise relief; less AR | Requires sternotomy and CPB |

#### Evidence

- Large meta-analyses: **comparable risks and survival** between balloon and open valvotomy
- Transcatheter: greater gradient reduction
- Open valvotomy: lower rates of postoperative AR
- Reintervention rates: tend to be higher with balloon (not widely demonstrated)

#### Recover / Rehabilitate

- All interventions are **not definitive**
- Future interventions are **inevitable**

</div></details>

## 13. Treatment Principles — Children and Adults with Congenital Heart Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

#### General Principles

- Late-presenting clinically significant AS results from progressive nature (minor/moderate disease at birth)
- PGE₁ ineffective beyond 2–3 weeks of age
- Decision to treat based on: peak gradient >50 mmHg, symptoms (even if gradient <50 mmHg), LVH on echo

#### Balloon Dilatation

Consider if:
- Minimal calcification in valve leaflets
- No more than mild regurgitation

#### Open Aortic Valvotomy

Consider especially for first intervention

#### If Gradient Not Adequately Relieved

Valve repair or replacement indicated. Less-than-perfect results often accepted when replacement not possible (small size, other contraindications). **Avoid prosthetic replacement until valve suitable for adult life can be implanted.**

#### Repair Techniques

| Category | Techniques |
|---|---|
| **Resuspension of prolapsing leaflets** | Trusler technique, leaflet central plication, free edge reinforcement |
| **Leaflet reconstruction** | Patch repair of post-balloon leaflet tear, patch augmentation to improve coaptation, patch repair of primary leaflet perforation |
| **Bicuspidisation/tricuspidisation of BAV/UAV** | Raphe resection + leaflet reconstruction (BAV), commissurotomy + reconstruction (UAV) |

#### Replacement Options

| Type | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Autograft (Ross/Ross-Konno)** | Grows, no anticoagulation | Recurrent RVOT interventions (homograft at initial operation) |
| **Homograft/bioprosthetic** | No anticoagulation | No growth, limited durability in children; smallest tissue valve 19 mm → root enlargement (Nicks, Konno) |
| **Mechanical** | Durable | Lifelong anticoagulation (complex in children); smallest 16 mm; pannus formation possible even with adequate anticoagulation |

## 14. Supravalvular Aortic Stenosis Repair</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anaesthesia Risk

- Restricted coronary filling in diastole
- Possible proximal coronary stenoses must be considered
- **No percutaneous interventional options**

#### Surgical Principles

- Restore unobstructed aortic root and ascending aorta
- Address coronary anomalies

#### Surgical Sequence

1. Transect aorta or open with S-shaped incision into non-coronary sinus
2. Inspect coronary ostia; remove any obstruction
3. Inspect aortic valve
4. If enlarging multiple sinuses: incise coronary sinuses lateral to ostium (left of right ostium, right of left ostium) — little space in left sinus, may confine to two sinuses to avoid coronary damage
5. Incise non-coronary sinus centrally
6. Shave off thickened media (especially above left coronary orifice)
7. Reconstruct incised sinuses with patch (Gore-Tex®, bovine/porcine pericardium)
8. Diffuse disease: augment whole ascending aorta ± arch

#### Surgical Techniques (Figures 28–29)

| Technique | Sinuses Enlarged | Description |
|---|---|---|
| **McGoon** (Figure 28B) | 1 | Single patch |
| **Doty inverted Y** (Figure 28C) | 2 | Inverted Y-shaped patch |
| **Brom** (Figure 29) | 3 | Three-patch repair using heterologous material |
| **Myers' modification** | 3 | Using native ascending aorta |

## 15. Severe Multi-Level LVOTO and Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Management Options

- **Single-ventricle palliation:** Norwood strategy, hybrid procedures, neonatal cardiac transplantation
- **Comfort care** (no active intervention) — viable choice for some parents
- **Termination of pregnancy** (where legal, if diagnosed in foetal life)

#### Attempting Biventricular Circulation

If LV cavity adequate and MV structurally/functionally normal → initial objective is biventricular circulation

#### Tunnel LVOTO

- Very difficult to relieve by surgical resection alone
- Aortic valve annulus often equally small; cannot be enlarged without disrupting valve
- **Treatment:** Resection of tunnel obstruction + replacement of aortic root with pulmonary root (**Ross-Konno procedure**)

## 16. Surgical Techniques for LVOT Lesions</summary><div class="details-content">

#### General Principles

- Relieve obstruction, achieve long-lasting result, preserve native aortic valve
- Median sternotomy, CPB, single right atrial cannula, arterial return to aorta, cardioplegic arrest
- LA venting or intermittent circulatory arrest (especially neonates)
- Hockey-stick incision into non-coronary sinus or transverse incision at STJ
- TOE before chest closure (if patient large enough)

#### Subaortic Lesions (Figures 30–31)

- **Approach:** Nearly all through aortic valve (some via tricuspid valve for VSD, or primum defect for AVSD)
- **Discrete membrane:** Usually peels off myocardium easily with blunt + sharp dissection. Ridge extends onto mitral and aortic leaflets and subcommissural triangles — must remove to prevent future aortic valve dysfunction (Figure 30)

![](_page_33_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 30.** TTE: A) Discrete subaortic membrane extending onto MV; B) Complete resection → significant LVOT diameter augmentation; C) Laminar flow on colour Doppler.

- **Myectomy:** If significant LVH. Avoid bundle of His (beneath right and non-coronary commissures) — confine resection to muscle leading to commissure between left and right cusps
- **Accessory chordae:** Remove, preserving MV function. In AVSD patients, accessory chordae may be present from superior bridging leaflet to septum (Figure 31)

![](_page_34_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 31.** TOE: Long LVOT segment obstructed by accessory MV tissue fibrosis: A) Before; B) After resection.

- **Mitral valve cysts/accessory tissue tags:** Usually easy to remove; avoid injury to anterior leaflet
- **Endocardial fibroelastosis (EFE) resection:** Indicated when LV filling restricted (concept under review, not always beneficial). Via mitral valve and retrograde through LVOT if with AVR. Resection needs to extend to LV apex. Indications and benefits not clearly defined

## 17. Prosthetic Aortic Valve Replacement and Ross/Ross-Konno Operations</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Prosthetic AVR in Children

- Same principles as adult congenital population
- **Implant as large a valve as possible** (no growth)
- May require annular enlargement (Nicks, Manougian, Konno, modifications)
- Special care: ensure coronary ostia not interfered with (risk of kinking proximal coronaries with annular enlargement)
- Large prosthesis may press on bundle of His → complete heart block
- Choice based on patient and family preferences

#### Ross and Ross-Konno Operations (Figure 32)

**Concept:** Replace aortic valve with patient's own pulmonary valve (autograft) → growth potential, no anticoagulation

**Suitability:**
- Suitable for subvalvular/supravalvular congenital stenosis, isolated valvular disease (especially stenosis)
- **Not suitable for:** BAV aortopathy, Marfan, other connective tissue diseases

**Operative steps:**
1. CPB with cardioplegic arrest
2. Harvest pulmonary autograft (avoid damage to LMCA and first septal perforator of LAD)
3. Harvest with tongue of RVOT anterior wall
4. Incise between LVOT and RVOT across aortopulmonary fold and infundibular septum
5. Suture autograft directly on muscle ridge using anterior RV wall to enlarge LVOT
6. Reimplant coronary buttons
7. Complete aortic root
8. Reconstruct RVOT continuity with valved conduit (homograft most common; also bicuspidised homografts in neonates/infants, xenograft stentless bioprosthetic, bovine jugular vein conduit, stented valved conduit in older patients)

![](_page_35_Picture_5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 32.** Ross-Konno operation: A) Harvest aortic root, pulmonary infundibulum, coronary ostia (red dotted lines); B) Harvest pulmonary infundibulum with RVOT anterior wall tongue (red circle), mobilise coronaries, incise between LVOT and RVOT across aortopulmonary fold and infundibular septum (red asterisk); C) Suture pulmonary autograft on muscle ridge using anterior RV wall to enlarge LVOT, reimplant coronary buttons; D) RV→PA valved conduit (blue asterisks) reconstructs RVOT continuity.

**Implant techniques:**
- Inclusion implant within dilated aortic annulus
- Free implant (graft sutured to annulus itself)

**Konno modification:** Incision into ventricular septum between LVOT and RVOT, patched to enlarge annulus. Often not necessary — after aortic valve removal, generous resection of narrow LVOT tunnel can abolish obstruction. Pulmonary autograft harvested with RVOT muscle tongue used to patch incised septum.

</div></details>

## 18. Root Replacement Techniques: Bentall, Valve-Sparing, PEARS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Bentall Operation

**Indications:** Adolescent/adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) — aortic regurgitation with connective tissue diseases (Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan, Turner, BAV aortopathy)

**Special challenges (Figure 33):**

| Scenario | Challenge |
|---|---|
| **Post-arterial switch** (10% develop root dilatation/AR in 30 years) | Overriding pulmonary trunk/branches; reimplanted coronaries on anterior root, often above neo-STJ |
| **Post-truncus/DORV/Rastelli** | Aortic annulus partially constituted by VSD patch; patches calcify → difficult suturing |
| **Post-Fontan (single ventricle)** | Coronaries difficult to locate (especially after Norwood) |
| **Situs inversus/isomerism/arterial malposition** | Pay particular attention to coronary origin and course |

All operations on CPB; may require deep hypothermic circulatory arrest.

![](_page_37_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 33.** Aortic root dilatation post-arterial switch. Note anteroposterior great vessels, MPA overriding aorta. Both coronary ostia superiorly displaced between pulmonary trunk and neo-aorta (red insert). RPA = right pulmonary artery; LPA = left pulmonary artery; Ao = aorta; MPA = main pulmonary artery; RCA = right coronary artery; LMCA = left main coronary artery.

#### Valve-Sparing Root Replacement

**Indication:** Aortic regurgitation due to root/annular dilatation with structurally intact valve (tricuspid or bicuspid) — leaflets pulled apart by dilatation

**Goal:** Preserve valve in young population by bringing leaflets back into coaptation through root reduction

| Feature | Yacoub Remodelling | David V Reimplantation |
|---|---|---|
| Annulus distensibility | + | – |
| Cusp motion | ++ | (+) |
| Valve stability | + | + |
| STJ stability | + | + |
| LV-aortic junction stability | – | + |

**David V reimplantation:**
1. Resect sinuses of Valsalva
2. Harvest coronary ostia as buttons
3. Sleeve Dacron® tube over three commissures, suspend at appropriate height above annular plane
4. Interrupted sutures anchor tube to aortic annulus
5. Continuous sutures attach resected sinus edges to tube
6. Reimplant coronary buttons
7. Anastomose tube distally to ascending aorta or arch

**Yacoub remodelling:**
1. Resect sinuses
2. Harvest coronary buttons
3. Tailor proximal end of Dacron® tube graft to replace sinuses
4. Continuous sutures attach resected sinus edges to tube
5. Reimplant coronary buttons
6. Anastomose tube distally to normal-diameter aorta

**Note:** In Yacoub technique, aortic valve and annulus remain outside graft → annulus remains at risk of continued dilatation

**Indicators of early failure:** Inappropriate patient selection, need for cusp repair, coronary reimplantation

#### Personalised External Aortic Root Support (PEARS, Figure 34)

**Indication:** Root dilatation with minimal/mild AR (Marfan syndrome, similar aortopathies)

**Procedure:**
1. Create 3D model from CT of patient's aortic root, ascending aorta, arch
2. Make polymer mesh jacket from model
3. Place jacket around root and ascending aorta (attaches where aorta connects to LV outflow, below coronary ostia, extends to just beyond innominate artery)
4. Sutured loosely to aortic adventitia
5. Close axial seam
6. Jacket can be made up to 20% smaller than patient's aorta (fitted under controlled hypotension)

**Outcomes:**
- Mild AR abolished or significantly reduced in all cases
- Operation usually on beating heart, no CPB
- First operation 16 years ago; >200 cases, no subsequent rupture/dissection
- Operative mortality 1% in experienced hands

**FIGURE 34.** PEARS procedure: A) 3D model from CT; B) Polymer mesh jacket made from model; C) Jacket placed around aortic root and ascending aorta, seam sutured closed; D) MRI of first patient (Marfan) 10 years apart — no further dilatation. BCA = brachiocephalic artery; LCA = left coronary artery; RCA = right coronary artery.

## 19. Complications and Long-Term Results</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Complications of LVOT Surgery

- **Bleeding**
- **Heart block** (bundle of His damage)
- **Residual or recurrent stenosis** (especially subaortic membrane, tunnel LVOTO)
- **Postoperative low cardiac output** (poor LV diastolic compliance, especially if EFE present even after resection)
- **Aortic regurgitation** (leaflet prolapse after commissural incision)
- **Aneurysm formation** (patch dilatation after supravalvular repair)
- **Myocardial ischaemia** (coronary distortion in supravalvular AS repair)

#### Long-Term Results

| Lesion | Outcomes |
|---|---|
| **Neonatal AS — Balloon valvuloplasty** | 70% survival at 10 years; 26% freedom from reintervention; 61% freedom from moderate AR at 10 years |
| **Neonatal AS — Open surgical valvotomy** | 70–90% survival at 10 years; 66–78% freedom from reintervention; up to 90% freedom from moderate AR (case-dependent) |
| **Ross operation in infancy** | Operative mortality <10% (older children/young adults 1–3%); 95% survival at 5 years; freedom from AR 90% at 2 years → 70% at 5 years; freedom from reintervention 37% at 5 years (mostly RV-PA conduit replacement) |
| **Supravalvular AS** | Operative mortality 2–3%; residual gradient 15–20% (mainly diffuse stenosis); 80% survival at 10 years; freedom from late reoperation 90% at 10 years → 80% at 20 years |
| **Discrete subaortic stenosis** | Operative mortality <5%; reoperation 10–15%; late AR up to 20%; 10-year survival >90% |
| **Tunnel LVOTO** | Heterogeneous morphology and age → statistics unreliable. Higher operative risk, decreased freedom from late death and reoperation compared to discrete subaortic stenosis |

</div></details>
