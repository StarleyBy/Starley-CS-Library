# Right Ventricular Outflow Tract

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Embryology
*   **Primitive heart tube:** Contains sinus venosus, primitive atrium, primitive ventricle, **bulbus cordis** and **truncus arteriosus**.
*   **Bulbus cordis and truncus arteriosus** give rise to left and right ventricular outflow tracts, pulmonary trunk, and aorta.
*   **5th week:** Bulbar ridges develop from mesenchymal cell proliferation (including neural crest migration).
*   **6th week:** Ridges fuse → spiral-shaped **aorticopulmonary septum** divides bulbus cordis and truncus arteriosus into aorta and pulmonary trunk. Spiralling causes pulmonary trunk to twist around aorta.
*   **Semilunar valves:** Develop from swellings of subendocardial tissue around aortic and pulmonary orifices; swellings hollow out to form thin-walled cusps.

### Pulmonary Stenosis with Intact Ventricular Septum (PS-IVS)
*   Rare congenital spectrum resulting in isolated narrowing of pulmonary valve and/or RVOT.
*   **Extreme form:** Pulmonary atresia with IVS (PA-IVS) — associated with hypoplastic right heart (HRH).
*   **Anatomical features:**
    *   **Pulmonary valve:** Raphe between leaflets, leaflet thickening/dysplasia, fused leaflets (sleeve-like or single orifice). Reduced annulus size typical in Noonan syndrome.
    *   **Pulmonary artery:** Post-stenotic dilatation beyond infancy.
    *   **RV:** Progressive hypertrophy; hypoplastic (spectrum: tripartite → absent trabecular part → inlet only).
    *   **Interatrial septum:** ASD always present; restrictive in 5–10%.
    *   **Coronary arteries:** Sinusoids (fistulae) between RV cavity and coronary arteries in PA-IVS. RV hypertension → fistulae perfused in systole, under-perfused in diastole → steal phenomenon.
    *   **Coronary circulation dependency:** RV pressure-dependent or entirely dependent if proximal discontinuity or peripheral stenoses. Decompression of RV → ischaemia/infarction.
    *   **Left ventricle:** Cavity size may be reduced.

### Classification of Pulmonary Stenosis (Table 1)
| Severity | RVOT gradient (mmHg) | RV:systemic pressure ratio | Indexed TV diameter (mm/cm²) | Indexed area (cm²/cm²) |
|----------|---------------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|------------------------|
| Mild | <40 | <0.5 | 8–10 | 0.75–1.0 |
| Moderate | 40–80 | 0.5–1 | 3–10 | 0.3–1.0 |
| Severe | >80 | >1 | <3 | <0.3 |

### Aetiology and Associations
*   **Genetic:** Noonan syndrome, Williams syndrome.
*   **Non-genetic:** Congenital rubella syndrome.
*   **Associated lesions:** PFO, VSD, TGA, DORV, Ebstein's anomaly.
*   **Epidemiology:** Isolated PS = 5.8% of all congenital defects; ~50% of malformed hearts include PS as a component.

### Pathophysiology
*   **Critical PS/PA:** Pulmonary circulation entirely ductus-dependent. Complete mixing at atrial level → desaturation, cyanosis.
*   **Sinusoids present:** Coronary circulation RV-dependent with variable myocardial ischaemia.
*   **TV Z-score ≤ –3:** Diminutive RV cavity → circulation comparable to tricuspid atresia.
*   **TV Z-score ≥0:** TR and large RV cavity.
*   **Atrial/ventricular shunting with right-to-left:** Cyanosis present; Qp:Qs <1.
*   **No shunting:** Normal arterial saturation; Qp:Qs =1.
*   **Moderate-severe PS (forward flow allowed):** Less critical; RV hypertensive with reduced compliance → increased RA pressure, right-to-left atrial shunting, variable cyanosis. Sinusoids rare.

### Natural History
*   Survival into adulthood: 40–50%. Survival beyond 7th decade reported.
*   Most common cause of death: Heart failure (~30%).
*   Untreated PA-IVS: Potentially lethal (myocardial ischaemia or severe cyanosis). Sudden death common.

### Symptoms and Signs
*   **Neonates with severe PS/PA:** Life-threatening cyanosis → requires PGE₁.
*   **Mild-moderate stenosis:** Poor feeding, slow weight gain, dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance.
*   **Murmur:** 2nd left intercostal space with thrill (does not radiate to carotids). Cyanosis on exertion.

### Diagnostic Features
*   **Physical examination (Table 2):** RV hypertrophy signs, systolic ejection click (unless severe), ejection systolic murmur + thrill.
*   **ECG (Table 3):** RV hypertrophy (right axis deviation, tall R in V1, right atrial enlargement in severe PS).
*   **CXR:** Depends on severity and age. Large cardiac shadow, prominent RA, RV enlargement (if not hypoplastic), mild post-stenotic dilatation, oligaemia when ductus constricts.
*   **Echocardiography (gold standard):** Level of stenosis, valve anatomy, RVOT anatomy, Doppler flow velocity (peak/mean gradient), pulmonary trunk/branch anatomy, Z-scores (PV, TV, RV), sinusoids presence/direction.
*   **Catheterisation:** Not routine unless discrepancy. Peak-to-peak gradient from RV to MPA on pull-back. Assess pulmonary vascular bed, RV sinusoids (RV injection), Qp:Qs and cardiac output (oxygen saturations).

### Therapeutic Options by Lesion Type
*   **PS-IVS:** Relieve RVOT obstruction (promote RV cavity growth); decompress RV (even if small).
*   **PA-IVS:** Avoid RV decompression (especially if RV-dependent coronary circulation); univentricular or 1½-ventricle repair.

### Decision-Making: Univentricular vs Biventricular Repair
*   **Mild RV hypoplasia:** TV and RV cavity ≥2/3 normal size; well-developed RVOT. TV Z-score: –1.5 to 0.
*   **Moderate RV hypoplasia:** TV and RV cavity ~1/2 normal size (1/3 to 2/3); outflow tract allows effective valvotomy. TV Z-score: –3 to –1.5.
*   **Severe RV hypoplasia:** TV and RV cavity ≤1/3 normal size; outflow tract severely hypoplastic/obliterated. TV Z-score: –4 to –6.

### Medical Treatment for Severe Cyanosis
*   Neutral thermal environment (minimise O₂ consumption)
*   Oxygen (possibly intubation)
*   **PGE₁** if duct-dependent circulation suspected
*   Catecholamines (dopamine, dobutamine) if needed

### Therapeutic Options — Severe PS with Well-Developed RV, Mild/No TR
*   Surgical pulmonary valvotomy (via pulmonary artery)
*   Balloon angioplasty (severe PV stenosis)
*   Laser/radiofrequency (membranous atresia) → create opening → balloon dilation to 6–8mm. If hypoxaemia persists, stent ductus arteriosus.
*   Modified Blalock-Taussig (BT) shunt if obstruction not adequately relieved.

### Therapeutic Options — PA with Hypoplastic RV and Tricuspid Stenosis
*   RV volume <35–40% unlikely to sustain cardiac output (but estimates unreliable).
*   **Inadequate criteria:** TV diameter <8mm (neonate), TV:MV diameter ratio <0.7, TV Z-score ≤ –3.
*   **Ventricular morphology:**
    *   Tripartite + PA connected to RV → open RVOT.
    *   Monopartite (inlet only), wide separation between cavity and PA → systemic-to-PA shunt + atrial septostomy (before BT shunt if fossa bulging into LA).
    *   Borderline + PA connected to ventricular mass → attempt RVOT opening with limited interatrial communication.

### Therapeutic Options — PA with Hypoplastic RV and Coronary Sinusoids
*   Very high mortality.
*   **Options:** Staged palliation to single-ventricle, heart transplant. Rare reports of sinusoid ligation/occlusion (if antegrade coronary perfusion demonstrated).
*   **Neonatal:** Systemic-to-PA shunt or prolonged PGE₁ (anaesthesia induction can be fatal due to uncontrolled ischaemia).
*   **3–4 months:** Cavopulmonary anastomosis.
*   **If TR and TV cannot be repaired:** RV exclusion (patch closure of TV + tube graft from aorta to RV cavity to maintain coronary perfusion via sinusoids).
*   LV failure risk increases with age; few survive beyond 3–4 years.

### Therapeutic Options — Older Infants and Children with PS
*   **Indications for balloon valvuloplasty:** RV-PA resting gradient >80 mmHg (infants) or >50 mmHg (children >1 year) with cyanosis due to right-to-left atrial shunting.
*   Rationale: Prevent RV infundibular hypertrophy and myocardial fibrosis.

### Indications for Transcatheter Balloon Angioplasty
*   Transpulmonary peak velocity >4 m/s (peak instantaneous gradient >60 mmHg)
*   Peak instantaneous gradient >50 mmHg (normal cardiac output)
*   Neonates with moderate-severe stenosis (especially duct-dependent circulation) — urgent
*   Adults with moderate-severe stenosis, exercise intolerance, pressure overload, risk of RV fibrosis

### Principles of Transcatheter Balloon Angioplasty
*   GA or IV sedation + local anaesthesia
*   Femoral venous access → curved end-hole or balloon-tipped catheter into RV
*   Balloon size: exceed PV diameter by 20–25%. Length: 20 mm (neonates/infants), 30 mm (children), 40 mm (adolescents/adults)
*   Inflate until annular indentation eliminated (further pressure not beneficial)
*   Post-procedure: record RV pressure and gradient

### Complications of Balloon Angioplasty
*   Arrhythmia 1%, bleeding 0.9%, femoral vein thrombosis 0.6%, hypoxia 0.4%, death 0.2%, TR 0.2%, femoral vein tears 0.2%, arterial thrombosis 0.2%, cardiac perforation 0.1%, respiratory arrest 0.1%

### Surgical Principles — Pulmonary Valvotomy
*   CPB, mild hypothermia, cardioplegic arrest
*   Vertical incision in proximal MPA into anterior sinus of Valsalva
*   Incise fused commissures, detach cusps from lateral attachments
*   Infundibular resection (if needed) through PV or RA
*   Excise hypertrophied muscular bands (parietal and septal extensions of infundibular septum)
*   **Risks:** Septal branches of LAD, medial papillary muscle of TV
*   Close PA directly or with enlargement patch
*   Close ASD
*   **Target:** RV/LV pressure ratio <0.75, RV-PA gradient <50 mmHg

### Surgical Principles — Transannular Patch
*   **Indications:** PV Z-score < –3, highly dysplastic/bulky valve requiring valvectomy
*   CPB, mild hypothermia, cardioplegic arrest
*   Open valve annulus, debulk RVOT, augment anterior surface of RVOT-PV-MPA complex with patch
*   Avoid LAD (emerges lateral to incision)

### Surgical Principles — Monocusp Valve
*   Creates competent, non-stenotic pathway → physiological RV mechanics
*   Incision over anterior RVOT, PV, MPA complex
*   Tailor monocusp from autologous pericardium, decellularised porcine pericardium, or other matrix (width ≈2× hypoplastic PV diameter)
*   Attach cusp with continuous 6-0 polypropylene suture
*   RVOT patch/hood from Gore-Tex® or bovine pericardium (continuous 5-0 polypropylene)

### Initial Surgical Management (Table 4)
*   **Mild PS (TV Z-score –1.5 to 0):** Valvotomy ± BT shunt (if cyanosis), RVOT patch (if subvalvular PS), PFO left open.
*   **Moderate-severe PS (TV Z-score –4 to –1.5):** RVOT patch, BT shunt, PFO left open.
*   **Critical PS/PA (TV Z-score –6 to –4 ± sinusoids):** BT shunt, atrial septostomy.

### Definitive Surgical Treatment (Table 5)
*   **Mild PS:** Close ASD, enlarge RV and RVOT ± transannular patch or pulmonary homograft; take down BT shunt.
*   **Moderate PS:** Close ASD, modified bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (Glenn), enlarge RV and RVOT ± transannular patch or pulmonary homograft; take down BT shunt.
*   **Severe PS:** Fontan circulation; RA-to-RV or RA-to-PA communication; take down BT shunt.

### Pulmonary Valved Conduits for RVOT Reconstruction

**Homografts (pulmonary or aortic)**
*   Advantages: Wide size range, excellent handling, can anastomose bifurcating grafts directly to branch PAs
*   Disadvantages: Limited small sizes (neonates), limited shelf life (~2 years), high cost, immunological response (calcification, especially second homograft)

**Xenografts**
*   **Medtronic Freestyle™ (porcine aortic root, 19–29mm):** Excellent short-term durability; not suitable for neonates; handling difficult.
*   **Medtronic Contegra™ (bovine jugular vein, 12–22mm):** Freedom from reintervention 90% at 3 years, 66% at 7 years. Young age at implantation = risk factor for reintervention and distal conduit stenosis. Graft dilatation and decreased durability with elevated RV pressures or PH.

**Valved Conduits (bioprosthetic valves + synthetic conduits)**
*   Hancock® (porcine aortic valve in woven fabric conduit)
*   Carpentier-Edwards™ (porcine aortic bioprosthesis in woven polyester conduit)
*   BioIntegral No-React® BioPulmonic™ (porcine valve in porcine pericardium tube, No-React® treatment reduces calcific degeneration)
*   Advantages: Long shelf life, wide size range, rigid/semi-rigid annulus
*   Disadvantages: Size limitations (not for neonates/small children); not amenable to catheter-based dilatation

### Pulmonary Valve Replacement (PVR) Prostheses

**Stented Bioprostheses** (Carpentier-Edwards Perimount™, Magna Ease™, St Jude Trifecta™, Medtronic Mosaic™, Hancock™ II)
*   Advantages: Easy to implant, no lifelong anticoagulation, catheter interventions/reimplantation possible
*   Disadvantage: Degeneration by calcification (freedom from reintervention ~94% at 5 years, reduced in younger patients)

**Stentless Bioprostheses** (Edwards Prima Plus™, Medtronic Freestyle™, St Jude Toronto SVP®, LivaNova Pericarbon Freedom™)
*   Advantages: Excellent flow dynamics, no lifelong anticoagulation, catheter interventions possible
*   Disadvantages: Difficult handling, risk of external compression, reduced durability in younger patients

**Mechanical Valves** (St Jude Regent™, LivaNova Carbomedics®, On-X®, Medtronic Open Pivot™)
*   Rarely used in pulmonary position
*   Indications: Need for anticoagulation (e.g., left-sided mechanical valve), reduce reinterventions
*   Recent data: Freedom from reintervention 85% at 10 years (vs 80% reoperation rate for bioprosthesis at 10 years)
*   Advantages: Long-term durability, wide size range (smallest 16mm)
*   Disadvantages: Thromboembolism risk (low pressure, slow flow), lifelong anticoagulation, inefficient leaflet closure (low pulmonary diastolic pressure), no catheter interventions

### Newer Techniques

**Percutaneous Pulmonary Valve Implantation (PPVI)**
*   Non-surgical option for RVOT and pulmonary trunk dysfunction
*   Feasible, safe, effective for conduit stenosis and regurgitation; reduces number of lifetime operations
*   **Devices:** Medtronic Melody® (bovine jugular vein valve in CP stent), Edwards SAPIEN XT™ (bovine pericardial leaflets in cobalt stent)
*   **Technique:** Femoral venous access → delivery system to RVOT → identify landing zone (proximal to bifurcation, distal to muscular RVOT) → uncover valve → inflate inner then outer balloon → implant valve stent assembly
*   **Advantages:** Treats both stenosis and regurgitation, good availability (small valves for children), fully percutaneous, good stented valve performance
*   **Disadvantages:** Size limitation (dilated/hyperdynamic RVOT relative contraindication — SAPIEN XT™ available in larger sizes than Melody®), stent fractures, coronary artery compression, conduit/valve calcification (relative contraindication), migration/displacement

**Injectable Pulmonary Valve Implantation**
*   BioIntegral No-React® Injectable BioPulmonic Valve™ (porcine pulmonary valve in self-expandable stent covered by No-React®-treated pericardium; sizes 15–31mm)
*   Insertion in RVOT on beating heart without CPB (reduces damage to dilated, malfunctioning RV); requires full sternotomy (minimally invasive techniques used)
*   **Technique:** Median sternotomy → expose MPA, bifurcation, RVOT → purse-string sutures on distal RVOT → delivery trocar into MPA → inject valve into RVOT under digital control → secure with transmural sutures → assess with intra-op TOE
*   **Advantages:** Not limited by small catheter/vascular access, off-the-shelf availability, wide size range, less invasive than traditional PVR, good stented valve performance, fast recovery, easy reproducible technique, consistent RV reverse remodelling, can perform additional RVOT/PA procedures (reduction-plasty, branch PA enlargement) simultaneously
*   **Disadvantages:** Calcified RVOT may preclude implantation; no long-term follow-up data

## 1 Describe the embryology of the right ventricular outflow tract</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Primitive Heart Tube</summary><div class="details-content">

The primitive heart tube contains small constrictions, including the sinus venosus, primitive atrium, primitive ventricle, **bulbus cordis** and **truncus arteriosus**. The bulbus cordis and truncus arteriosus give rise to the left and the right ventricular outflow tracts, pulmonary trunk and aorta (Figure 1).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Bulbar Ridges (5th Week)</summary><div class="details-content">

During the 5th week of embryological development, **bulbar ridges** develop from proliferation of mesenchymal cells on the wall of the bulbus cordis (Figure 2). They are also derived from neural crest mesenchymal cells by passing through the primitive pharynx (Figure 3).

![](_page_1_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Development of bulbar ridges from proliferation of mesenchymal cells on the wall of the bulbus cordis.**

![](_page_1_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 3. Mesenchymal cells also migrate from the neural crest by passing through the primitive pharynx.**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Aorticopulmonary Septum (6th Week)</summary><div class="details-content">

During the 6th week of embryological development, the ridges fuse together, forming the **spiral-shaped aorticopulmonary septum**, which divides the bulbus cordis and truncus arteriosus into the aorta and pulmonary trunk. The spiralling of the aorticopulmonary septum results in the pulmonary trunk twisting around the aorta. Proximally, the pulmonary trunk lies anterior and to the right of the aorta near the ventricles, and then distally, it lies posterior and to the left of the aorta (Figure 4).

## 2 Describe the development of the semilunar valves</summary><div class="details-content">

The semilunar valves develop from **separate swellings of subendocardial tissue** around the aortic and pulmonary orifices. These swellings are hollowed out to form the thin-walled cusps of the semilunar valves (Figure 5).

## 3 What is pulmonary stenosis with an intact ventricular septum?</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary stenosis with an intact ventricular septum (PS-IVS) is a rare congenital spectrum of defects that results in the isolated narrowing of the pulmonary valve and/or the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). The extreme presentation of this anomaly is **pulmonary atresia with IVS (PA-IVS)**, which is associated with hypoplastic right heart (HRH).

Several anatomical features can be found in both PS-IVS and PA-IVS, involving the:

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### a) Pulmonary Valve</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Raphe between two or more leaflets
*   Leaflet thickening (mucoid thickening or dysplasia)
*   Fusion of two or more leaflets leading to a sleeve-like or single orifice valve
*   Reduced pulmonary annulus size is not frequent but is **typical in Noonan syndrome**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### b) Pulmonary Artery</summary><div class="details-content">

May present with a **post-stenotic dilatation** beyond infancy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### c) Right Ventricle (RV)</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Progressive hypertrophy
*   Right ventricular enlargement and tricuspid regurgitation may also be seen (rare)
*   Generally, according to the degree of PS, the RV will be **hypoplastic** (spectrum including a tripartite RV, an absent trabecular part, or RV with only the inlet portion)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### d) Interatrial Septum</summary><div class="details-content">

An atrial septal defect is **always present**, and it is restrictive in **5–10%** of cases.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### e) Coronary Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

Coronary fistulae (sinusoids) can develop in patients with PA-IVS between the RV cavity and both coronary arteries. Due to severe intracavitary RV hypertension, the fistulae are perfused during systole and under-perfused in diastole, which can cause a **steal phenomenon** and relative ischaemia of the RV and LV.

Furthermore, the coronary circulation can be stenosed on the arterial side, often proximally. In such cases, myocardial perfusion can be **codependent on RV pressure**, or **entirely dependent** in cases of proximal discontinuity between one or both coronary arteries and the aorta, or in the case of peripheral stenoses caused by intimal hyperplasia.

Decompression of such ventricles may result in myocardial ischaemia or infarction in the distribution of the involved coronary arteries (Figure 6).

![](_page_5_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 6. The presence of fistulae, coronary stenoses and coronary occlusion are thought to be related to the degree of hypoplasia of the RV and can be correlated to the **Z-score of the TV annulus**. The **lower the Z-score of the TV, the higher the incidence of severe coronary flow dependency to RV pressure**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### f) Left Ventricle</summary><div class="details-content">

The cavity size may be reduced.

</div></details>

## 4 How is pulmonary stenosis classified?</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary stenosis can be graded as mild, moderate or severe according to the RVOT gradient, the RV:systemic pressure ratio, the indexed diameter and indexed area of the pulmonary valve (Table 1).

For treatment purposes, it is useful to classify pulmonary stenosis according to the right ventricular cavity and its predicted capability to sustain the pulmonary circulation. **Tricuspid valve diameter expressed as a Z-score** relates directly with the RV cavity and can be used as a more objective parameter in decision-making.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Table 1. Quantification of pulmonary stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

| Severity | RVOT gradient (mmHg) | RV:systemic pressure ratio | Indexed TV diameter (mm/cm²) | Indexed area (cm²/cm²) |
|----------|---------------------|----------------------------|------------------------------|------------------------|
| Mild | <40 | <0.5 | 8–10 | 0.75–1.0 |
| Moderate | 40–80 | 0.5–1 | 3–10 | 0.3–1.0 |
| Severe | >80 | >1 | <3 | <0.3 |

## 8 What is the pathophysiology of pulmonary stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

The pathophysiology depends on the degree of RVOT obstruction, dimension of the TV and RV, the degree of shunting through the ASD and/or ductus arteriosus, and the presence or absence of fistulae.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Critical Pulmonary Stenosis or Pulmonary Atresia</summary><div class="details-content">

The pulmonary circulation is **entirely dependent on the patency and diameter of the ductus arteriosus**. There is complete mixing at the atrial level with subsequent desaturation and cyanosis (Figure 7).

![](_page_8_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Oxygen saturations and pressures in a patient with pulmonary atresia with an intact ventricular septum.** Note that the ductus arteriosus is patent and there is an atrial septal defect.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Presence of Sinusoids</summary><div class="details-content">

The coronary circulation is **RV-dependent** with varying degrees of myocardial ischaemia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Tricuspid Valve Z-Score Categories</summary><div class="details-content">

*   If the tricuspid annulus is small (**Z-score ≤ –3**), the right ventricular cavity is diminutive and the circulation is comparable to that associated with tricuspid atresia.
*   If the tricuspid annulus is large (**Z-score ≥0**), there is TR and a large RV cavity.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Shunting Patterns</summary><div class="details-content">

*   In patients with an atrial or ventricular level defect and right-to-left shunting, cyanosis will be present and the **Qp:Qs ratio will be <1**.
*   If there is no atrial or ventricular shunting, pulmonary stenosis can be associated with normal arterial saturation and **Qp:Qs =1**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Neonates</summary><div class="details-content">

Most neonates are born with cyanosis caused by ductal closure, high RV pressure and TR.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Moderate to Severe Pulmonary Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

In cases of moderate to severe pulmonary stenosis (Figure 8), as some forward flow is allowed through the RVOT, the pulmonary circulation is not exclusively dependent on patency and diameter of the ductus arteriosus. This results in a less critical clinical picture in cases of ductal closure or pulmonary hypertension.

Sinusoids are rare in pulmonary stenosis, and myocardial ischaemia is not prominent. The RV remains hypertensive with reduced compliance, thereby increasing RA pressure, right-to-left shunting at an atrial level, and varying degrees of cyanosis.

![](_page_8_Figure_4.jpeg)

**Figure 8. Oxygen saturations and pressures in a patient with severe pulmonary stenosis and post-stenotic dilatation of the main pulmonary artery.** The right ventricle remains hypertensive.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 9 What is the natural history of isolated pulmonary stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Survival into adulthood varies between **40–50%**, according to the method of reporting. Survival beyond the 7th decade has been reported by a few authors.
*   The commonest cause of death is **heart failure**, accounting for approximately **30%** of cases.
*   The prognosis is worse with increasing degrees of pulmonary stenosis.
*   If untreated, **PA-IVS is a potentially lethal condition**, with death usually secondary to myocardial ischaemia or severe cyanosis caused by reduced pulmonary blood flow. **Sudden death is common**.

</div></details>

## 10 What are the symptoms and signs of pulmonary stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

The presentation depends on the age and severity of the pulmonary stenosis.

*   **Neonates with severe pulmonary stenosis or pulmonary atresia** present with **life-threatening cyanosis**, which requires a **PGE₁ infusion** to maintain patency of the ductus arteriosus.
*   In cases of mild to moderate stenosis, patients may develop **poor feeding** or **slow weight gain**.
*   Other reported symptoms include **dyspnoea** and **reduced exercise tolerance**.
*   On physical examination, a **murmur** can be heard on the **2nd left intercostal space** with a thrill, which does not radiate to the carotid arteries.

## 13 What are the echocardiographic features of pulmonary stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography coupled with Doppler interrogation of flow across the pulmonary valve and pulmonary artery is the **diagnostic gold standard**.

It offers exhaustive information regarding:

*   **a)** Level of stenosis — valvular, subvalvular or supravalvular
*   **b)** Anatomy of the pulmonary valve and nature of the stenosis (restricted motion of leaflets or dysplasia)
*   **c)** Anatomy of the RVOT, including presence of muscle bands and dynamic obstruction
*   **d)** Flow across the RVOT:
    *   **i)** In systole — assess maximal velocity, estimate pressure gradient across stenosis (Figure 9)
    *   **ii)** In diastole — indication of RV diastolic dysfunction and restrictive physiology
*   **e)** Anatomy of the pulmonary trunk and its branches
*   **f)** Sizing of the pulmonary valve, tricuspid valve and right ventricle, including **Z-scores**
*   **g)** Presence of any sinusoids and the direction of flow through them

## 16 Describe the decision-making process between a univentricular or biventricular repair</summary><div class="details-content">

The decision between univentricular or biventricular repair depends on the adequacy of the RV to support the pulmonary circulation. The criteria include TV diameter expressed as a Z-score, as well as RV morphology and size (Figure 12).

![](_page_15_Figure_10.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Nomogram used to convert echocardiographic tricuspid valve diameters into Z-score values, based on body surface area (BSA).**

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Mild RV Hypoplasia</summary><div class="details-content">

The tricuspid valve and RV cavity are approximately **two thirds or greater** than the calculated normal size, and the RV outflow tract is well developed. This usually correlates with a tricuspid valve **Z-score of –1.5 to 0**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Moderate RV Hypoplasia</summary><div class="details-content">

The tricuspid valve and the RV cavity are approximately **one half** of calculated normal size (range: one third to two thirds normal), and the outflow tract is developed enough to allow an effective pulmonary valvotomy. This usually correlates with a tricuspid valve **Z-score of –3 to –1.5**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Severe RV Hypoplasia</summary><div class="details-content">

The tricuspid valve and RV cavity are **one third or less** of the calculated normal size, whilst the outflow tract is severely hypoplastic or obliterated and therefore not amenable to an effective pulmonary valvotomy. This usually correlates with a tricuspid valve **Z-score of –4 to –6**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 17 What are the principles of medical treatment for neonates and infants with severe cyanosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

The presence of severe cyanosis in a neonate with suspected pulmonary stenosis or atresia is an indication for **urgent treatment**.

*   The infant should be maintained in a **neutral thermal environment** to minimise oxygen consumption.
*   **Oxygen** should be administered, possibly by intubation.
*   **Prostaglandin E₁ (PGE₁)** may be indicated if a duct-dependent circulation is suspected.
*   If necessary, catecholamines such as **dopamine or dobutamine** can be introduced.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 18 What are the therapeutic options for neonates and infants with severe pulmonary stenosis, a well-developed right ventricle and mild or no tricuspid regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

The aim is to relieve obstruction and have a right ventricle with normal size and function.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Treatment Options</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** **Surgical pulmonary valvotomy** (via the pulmonary artery). It can be difficult to define the anatomy of the valve if dysplastic. If the valve annulus is small, stenosis may not be completely relieved.
*   **b)** **Balloon angioplasty** in patients with severe pulmonary valve stenosis.
*   **c)** **Laser or radiofrequency** in patients with membranous atresia. A fibreoptic guidewire attached to a laser or a 2Fr ring-tipped radiofrequency coil (Coe) is activated to produce an opening in the valve, which is then enlarged by balloon angioplasty to **6–8mm** (Figure 13). When moderate to severe hypoxaemia persists, the ductus arteriosus can be stented open in the same session.

![](_page_17_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 13. Radiofrequency ablation and perforation of the right ventricular outflow tract with balloon dilation.**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### If Obstruction Not Adequately Relieved</summary><div class="details-content">

It may be necessary to provide additional pulmonary blood flow. In those infants in whom it appears that adequate pulmonary blood flow cannot eventually be attained through the pulmonary valve, a **modified Blalock-Taussig (BT) shunt** can be fashioned surgically.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 19 What are the therapeutic options for neonates and infants with pulmonary atresia, hypoplastic right ventricle and tricuspid stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

A right ventricular volume of **<35–40%** has been considered as unlikely to sustain total cardiac output. Estimates of right ventricular volumes, however, are not very reliable in infants.

If the tricuspid valve is too small, one can assume that no significant benefit will be derived from opening the pulmonary outflow.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Values Considered Inadequate</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** Tricuspid valve diameter **<8mm** in a neonate
*   **b)** Tricuspid to mitral valve diameter ratio **<0.7**
*   **c)** Tricuspid valve diameter **Z-score of –3 or smaller**
*   **d)** Ventricular morphology:
    *   **i)** If the ventricle is **tripartite** and the pulmonary artery is connected to the right ventricle → the right ventricular outflow can be opened
    *   **ii)** If the ventricle is **monopartite** (small inlet portion only) with wide separation between the ventricular cavity and the pulmonary artery → systemic-to-pulmonary arterial shunt + atrial septostomy (if fossa bulging into LA, septostomy should be performed before BT shunt implant)
    *   **iii)** If the ventricle is **borderline** and the pulmonary artery is connected to the ventricular mass → RVOT opening can be attempted with a limited interatrial communication to favour flow across the ventricle, thus facilitating its development

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 20 What are the therapeutic options in neonates and infants with pulmonary atresia, hypoplastic right ventricle and coronary sinusoids?</summary><div class="details-content">

This is a **particularly difficult situation with high mortality**.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Available Options</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Staged palliation towards **single-ventricle circulation**
*   **Heart transplant**
*   Rare reports of **ligation or occlusion of the sinusoids** when antegrade coronary perfusion is demonstrated

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Neonatal Period</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Systemic-to-pulmonary arterial shunt or **prolonged PGE₁ infusion** is generally recommended
*   **Induction of anaesthesia** in these neonates can be fatal due to uncontrolled ischaemia
*   In those who survive initial palliation, a **cavopulmonary anastomosis** is usually performed at **3–4 months** of age

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Tricuspid Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

TR is a serious complication as it reduces the systolic pressure achieved by the right ventricle.

If the tricuspid valve cannot be repaired, an **RV exclusion procedure** should be considered:
*   Patch closure of the tricuspid valve
*   Insertion of a tube graft from the aorta to the RV cavity to maintain coronary perfusion via the sinusoids

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Prognosis</summary><div class="details-content">

The likelihood of left ventricular failure increases progressively with age, with **few surviving beyond 3–4 years of age**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 21 What are the therapeutic options in older infants and children with pulmonary valvular stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Relief of valvular obstruction is recommended in those infants with intermittent or persistent cyanosis.
*   The rationale for relieving obstruction is to prevent the development of right ventricular infundibular hypertrophy with secondary stenosis and to avoid myocardial fibrosis in later life.
*   Although surgical valvotomy was the previously standard treatment modality, currently **percutaneous intervention** is used in preference.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Indications for Balloon Valvuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** Infants with a RV-PA resting gradient **>80mmHg** or those with cyanosis due to right-to-left atrial shunting
*   **b)** Children older than 1 year of age, with a RV-PA resting gradient **>50mmHg**

Not infrequently, a second procedure is required at an older age.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 22 What are the indications for transcatheter balloon angioplasty in a patient with pulmonary stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Transpulmonary peak velocity >4 m/s** with an echocardiographically derived peak instantaneous gradient of **>60mmHg**
*   **Peak instantaneous pressure gradient >50mmHg** when cardiac output is normal
*   **Infants presenting in the neonatal period** with moderate or severe stenosis, especially those with a duct-dependent pulmonary circulation — should be treated **urgently**
*   **Adults** with moderate to severe stenosis, persistent exercise intolerance, pressure overload and the risk of right ventricular fibrosis

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 23 What are the principles of a transcatheter balloon angioplasty?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   General anaesthesia or IV sedation with local anaesthesia
*   A curved end-hole catheter or a balloon-tipped angiographic catheter is introduced into the RV via femoral venous access
*   An angioplasty balloon of appropriate size is selected and tracked over the guidewire to the pulmonary valve
*   RVOT angiography allows measurement of the ventriculo-arterial junction diameter
*   Balloon size should **exceed the pulmonary valve diameter by 20–25%**
*   Balloon length: **20mm** (neonates/infants), **30mm** (children), **40mm** (adolescents/adults)
*   Balloon is de-aired carefully and connected to a syringe containing a weak solution of about **20–25% contrast medium in saline**, inflated by hand or with an inflation device
*   During inflation, an **hourglass impression** of the pulmonary valve on the balloon is usually noticed
*   Balloon is inflated until **elimination of the annular indentation** — any further pressure does not add beneficial effect
*   After inflation and withdrawal, record the pressure in the RV and the gradient across the pulmonary valve

</div></details>

*   Hypoxia — **0.4%**

*   Cardiac perforation — **0.1%**

## 25 Describe the surgical principles of a pulmonary valvotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

1.  Cardiopulmonary bypass is generally used with **mild hypothermia and cardioplegic arrest**.
2.  A vertical incision is made in the proximal main pulmonary artery and carried down into the anterior sinus of Valsalva.
3.  Any fused commissures are incised and the cusps are detached from their lateral attachment to the arterial wall.
4.  If required, infundibular resection is carried out through the pulmonary valve or right atrium.
5.  Any hypertrophied muscular bands on the parietal and septal extensions of the infundibular septum are widely excised. The resection starts on the septal limb, passing a clamp behind each hypertrophied mass before transection.
6.  **It is important to pay attention to:**
    *   Septal branches of the left anterior descending coronary artery
    *   Attachment of the tricuspid valve medial papillary muscle (both potentially at risk)
7.  The pulmonary artery is closed directly or with an enlargement patch.
8.  The atrial septal defect is then closed.
9.  At the end of the procedure, check the RV/LV pressure ratio and RV-PA pressure gradient.
    *   **Target:** RV/LV pressure ratio **<0.75** and RV-PA pressure gradient **<50mmHg**

</div></details>

## 26 Describe the surgical principles of a transannular patch</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Indications</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** Pulmonary valve diameter Z-score **< –3**
*   **b)** Highly dysplastic and bulky valve requiring valvectomy

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Procedure</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Cardiopulmonary bypass is generally used with mild hypothermia and cardioplegic arrest.
*   Once the valve annulus has been opened, the RVOT is debulked and an **unobstructed patch** is used to augment the anterior surface of the right ventricular outflow tract, pulmonary valve and main pulmonary artery complex.
*   The patch is sutured directly on the RVOT muscle, and attention is paid to avoid the **left anterior descending coronary artery**, which emerges lateral to the incision.

</div></details>

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 27 Describe the surgical principles of a monocusp valve</summary><div class="details-content">

This technique can create a competent and non-stenotic pathway, leading to physiological conditions and right ventricular mechanics that more closely resemble those following pulmonary valve-sparing techniques.

*   Following an incision over the anterior surface of the right ventricular outflow tract, pulmonary valve and main pulmonary artery complex, a patch is tailored from autologous pericardium, thin decellularised porcine pericardium or other matrix scaffolds.
*   The width of the monocusp is approximately **twice the diameter of the hypoplastic pulmonary valve**.
*   The cusp is attached with a continuous **6-0 polypropylene suture** along the remaining leaflets of the pulmonary valve or the main pulmonary artery wall and muscle rim of the RVOT incision.
*   A RVOT patch or hood is tailored from a Gore-Tex® or bovine pericardial patch and sutured along the RVOT with a continuous **5-0 polypropylene suture**.

</div></details>

## 31 Which valve prostheses are available for pulmonary valve replacement (PVR)?</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary valve replacements (PVR) can be performed using a **stented bioprosthesis, stentless bioprosthesis or mechanical prosthesis**.

The operation is performed using CPB, normothermia or mild hypothermia, on a beating heart or with cardioplegic arrest.

The implant technique is similar for all prosthesis types, with a single continuous suture in most cases. RVOT and MPA remodelling can be carried out at the same time.

In patients with congenital heart disease, particular attention should be paid to excluding any residual intracardiac shunt if PVR is performed on a beating heart.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 32 What are the advantages and disadvantages of stented bioprosthetic valves?</summary><div class="details-content">

Stented bioprosthetic valves include Carpentier-Edwards Perimount™ and Magna Ease™, St Jude Medical Trifecta™, Medtronic Mosaic™ and Hancock™ II. These are porcine and bovine pericardial valves designed for the aortic position.

**Advantages:**
*   Easy to implant
*   No need for lifelong anticoagulation
*   Allow catheter interventions or reimplantation

**Disadvantage:**
*   Degeneration by calcification — freedom from reintervention approximately **94% at 5 years**, especially reduced in younger patients

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 33 What are the advantages and disadvantages of stentless bioprosthetic valves?</summary><div class="details-content">

Stentless bioprosthetic valves include Edwards Prima Plus™, Medtronic Freestyle™, St Jude Toronto SVP® and LivaNova Pericarbon Freedom™, designed for the aortic position.

**Advantages:**
*   Excellent flow dynamics
*   No need for lifelong anticoagulation
*   Allow catheter interventions or reimplantation

**Disadvantages:**
*   Difficulty in handling
*   Risk of external compression
*   Potential need for reoperation/reintervention, especially in younger patients (reduced durability)

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 34 What are the advantages and disadvantages of mechanical valves?</summary><div class="details-content">

Mechanical valves include St Jude Medical Regent™, LivaNova Carbomedics®, LivaNova Carbomedics Top Hat™, Cryolife On-X®, and Medtronic Open Pivot™.

*   Generally bi-leaflet, made of pyrolytic carbon, graphite, and titanium.
*   Traditionally **rarely used in the pulmonary position**.

**Indications:**
*   Need for anticoagulation for other reasons (e.g., left-sided mechanical valve)
*   Need to reduce the number of reinterventions

Recent reports have challenged the well-established use of stented bioprosthetic valves, reporting a **freedom from reintervention of 85% at 10 years** against an 80% reoperation rate at 10 years for a bioprosthesis.

**Advantages:**
*   Long-term durability
*   Wide range of sizes available (smallest size 16mm)

**Disadvantages:**
*   Potential high rate of thromboembolism (low pressure, slower flow across valve)
*   Need for **lifelong anticoagulation therapy**
*   Potential inefficient leaflet closure (low pulmonary diastolic pressure)
*   Impossibility of catheter interventions or investigations

</div></details>

## 35 Describe the principles of the newer techniques that have been introduced to treat pulmonary valve disease</summary><div class="details-content">

Most patients with pulmonary stenosis and pulmonary atresia present with longstanding pulmonary valve dysfunction, including recurrent stenosis or regurgitation resulting from commissurotomy and degeneration of implanted prostheses.

Chronic valve dysfunction leads to **RV dysfunction and exercise limitation**. Function of the RV is furthermore jeopardised by repetitive surgery.

Some surgeons try to minimise the effect of repetitive operations on cardiac function by performing most of the RV and pulmonary artery procedures on CPB with a **beating heart and normothermia**. Thus, early death and morbidity has consistently been reported to be low in recent years.

New techniques have become available to reduce the number of open interventions or the use of CPB, including:
*   **Percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation (PPVI)**
*   **Injectable pulmonary valve implantation**

</div></details>

## 36 Describe the principles of percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation</summary><div class="details-content">

Percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation (PPVI) is a **non-surgical option** for treating right ventricular outflow tract and pulmonary trunk dysfunction.

With the growing numbers of patients developing RV-to-PA conduit dysfunction after repair of congenital heart disease, the importance of a technique with lower morbidity and mortality, good patient acceptance and efficacy comparable to surgery cannot be underestimated.

Over the last two decades, PPVI has become a **feasible, safe and effective treatment** for both conduit stenosis and regurgitation. Median follow-up data show good freedom from reoperation and recatheterisation, thereby potentially reducing the number of lifetime operations.

*   **Edwards Lifesciences SAPIEN XT™ transcatheter pulmonic valve:** Patches of bovine pericardium mounted inside a cobalt stent (Figure 18).

## 37 Describe the technique for implantation of a percutaneous pulmonary valve</summary><div class="details-content">

1.  Prepare the delivery system with inner and outer balloon and catheter.
2.  Crimp the valve down in a 2mL syringe by symmetrical squeezing and elongating action.
3.  Make a small incision on either groin for femoral venous cannulation.
4.  Under fluoroscopy, advance the delivery system over the guidewire to reach the RVOT.
5.  Identify a suitable landing zone — **proximal to the bifurcation, distal to the muscular RVOT** — with the stent covering the length of the landing zone (conduit or transannular patch).
6.  Once the desired position is reached, uncover the valve by withdrawing the outer shaft of the delivery system over the balloon catheter shaft up to the black double ring marker. Perform angiography to assess position.
7.  Once the valve is uncovered, inflate the inner balloon and confirm position; repeat angiography to reassess position.
8.  With the inner balloon inflated, the second operator inflates the outer balloon to its full dimension to implant the valve stent assembly until it opposes the walls of the bare metal stents.
9.  Deflate both balloons and remove the delivery system.
10. Advance a multi-track catheter over the wire to measure a pull-back gradient and detect any residual regurgitation.
11. After the procedure, perform routine observations as indicated after interventional catheterisation.

</div></details>

## 39 Describe the principles of injectable pulmonary valve implantation</summary><div class="details-content">

A **No-React® Injectable BioPulmonic Valve™** (BioIntegral Surgical Inc, Toronto, Canada) consists of a porcine pulmonary valve mounted inside a **self-expandable stent** covered by No-React®-treated pericardium, and is available in sizes **15–31mm** (Figure 19).

The implant technique allows insertion of the prosthesis in the RVOT on a **beating heart without CPB**, thus reducing potential damage on a dilated, often malfunctioning RV. It, however, requires surgical access to the right ventricle via a **full sternotomy** (minimally invasive techniques have been used).

## 40 Describe the technique for implantation of an injectable pulmonary valve</summary><div class="details-content">

1.  Gently compress the chosen valve with the supplied loop restrictor and slide into the barrel of the delivery trocar, with the introducer tip secured on the barrel.
2.  Following median sternotomy, expose the main pulmonary artery (MPA), bifurcation and right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT).
3.  Place two purse-string sutures on the distal RVOT, just below the pulmonary valve annulus.
4.  After partial heparinisation, introduce the delivery trocar through a small incision within the purse-string sutures and advance the barrel through the introducer tip into the MPA.
5.  Under digital control, inject the valve from the barrel into the RVOT while slowly withdrawing the delivery trocar system (Figure 20).
6.  After deployment, secure the valve with a few transmural sutures placed at the proximal and distal rim of the valve.
7.  Assess valve performance with intra-operative TOE, then close the chest routinely.

![](_page_33_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 20. Implantation of an injectable pulmonary valve:** A) before insertion, check valve direction (commissure facing distally); B) gently squeeze valve inside trocar; C) insert trocar into RVOT via small incision with introducer; D) once trocar in place, push valve into position with external manual guidance to avoid misplacement. After deployment confirmed, retrieve trocar and tie purse-string.
