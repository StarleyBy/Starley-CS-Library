# Ventricular Septal Defects

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Classification
*   **Ventricular septal defect (VSD):** A congenital cardiac malformation in which there is a hole or pathway between the ventricular chambers (ISNPCHD).
*   **Five major types:** perimembranous (most common), inlet, muscular, subarterial, Gerbode defect (LV → RA).
*   **Perimembranous:** occupies the space of the membranous septum, adjacent to fibrous continuity between valves.
*   **Muscular VSD:** exclusively muscular borders, can occur in any part of the muscular septum.
*   **Subarterial (doubly committed juxta-arterial):** positioned directly beneath semilunar valves, often with fibrous continuity between aortic and pulmonary valves.
*   **Gerbode defect:** deficiency of the AV component of the membranous septum with shunting from LV to RA.

### Epidemiology and Associations
*   Isolated VSD is the **second most common congenital cardiac malformation** (after bicuspid aortic valve): ~2 per 1000 live births.
*   Accounts for ~1/5 of all forms of congenital heart disease.
*   >50% of patients with a congenitally malformed heart have a VSD (isolated or with other anomalies).
*   **Subarterial defects** are more common in patients of **Asian descent**.
*   Associated anomalies: ASD, PDA, coarctation of the aorta, interrupted aortic arch, AVSD, TOF, DORV, common arterial trunk, TGA, univentricular heart.

### Pathophysiology and Natural History
*   Left-to-right shunt; magnitude determined by defect size, PVR, outflow obstruction, ventricular compliance.
*   Large VSD → heart failure (dyspnoea, poor feeding, infections, failure to thrive).
*   **Spontaneous closure:** common in small VSDs (<5 mm), most frequent during first year of life (up to 5 years). Mechanisms: margin fibrosis, valve leaflet adherence, septal hypertrophy.
*   Aortic valve leaflet prolapse into VSD causes aortic insufficiency — indication for surgery.
*   Unrepaired large VSD → irreversible pulmonary vascular disease by 1–2 years → Eisenmenger syndrome (PVR > SVR, cyanosis, death by age 40).

### Diagnosis
*   **ECG:** may be normal; with significant shunt — LVH, RVH, or biventricular hypertrophy.
*   **Chest radiograph:** increased pulmonary vascularity, cardiomegaly (biventricular enlargement).
*   **Echocardiography:** gold standard — defect type, borders, shunt, volume overload, associated anomalies, valve involvement.
*   **Catheterisation:** for suspected irreversible PH, older children/adults with moderate-large VSD, Qp:Qs calculation (surgery if Qp:Qs >1.5–2:1).

### Medical Therapy
*   For mild/moderate heart failure (bridge to spontaneous closure or surgery).
*   For severe heart failure (bridge to surgery).
*   Agents: cardiac glycosides, diuretics, ACE inhibitors, nutritional support.

### Palliative Treatment — Pulmonary Artery Banding (PAB)
*   Rarely performed today. Indications: Swiss cheese septum, neonatal apical muscular VSD, contraindications to CPB, LV preparation for TGA/ccTGA.
*   Target distal PA pressure: 30–50% of systemic pressure (for biventricular heart).

### Indications for VSD Closure
*   **Emergent (0–3 months):** large VSD with severe, intractable heart failure.
*   **Elective (4–6 months):** large VSD with heart failure responsive to medical therapy.
*   **Older children:** Qp:Qs >2:1, ventricular dilatation (even if Qp:Qs <2:1), aortic valve prolapse (even without AI), prior endocarditis.
*   **All doubly committed and juxta-arterial VSDs** — regardless of shunt size (no spontaneous closure, risk of AI).
*   **Contraindications:** dominant right-to-left shunt, PVR >8 units/m² with no response to vasodilators.

### Transcatheter Device Closure
*   **Perimembranous VSD:** experimental — risk of heart block and valve damage; reserve for non-surgical candidates.
*   **Subarterial VSD:** experimental — risk of aortic and pulmonary valve damage.
*   **Muscular VSD:** safe, especially for difficult-to-access defects. Hybrid approach (RV anterior wall access) available.

### Surgical Closure
*   **Approaches:** transatrial (most common), transpulmonary, transaortic, RVOT, RV apical, left ventricular (rare).
*   **Perimembranous VSD:** right atriotomy, patch (autologous pericardium, xenopericardium, PTFE, Dacron). Sutures through septal tricuspid leaflet to protect conduction tissue.
*   **Subarterial VSD:** transpulmonary approach, patch sutured to pulmonary valve annulus.
*   **Muscular VSD:** approach depends on location. Swiss cheese septum — PAB or early repair with endocardialisation.
*   **Structures at risk:** AV node and His bundle, tricuspid valve leaflets, aortic valve leaflets.

### Outcomes
*   **Operative mortality:** <1% (STS data: 0.5% for patch closure).
*   **Complications:** arrhythmias (5–15% transient, <2% require pacemaker), respiratory failure (<3%), others (<2%).
*   **Long-term:** >97% excellent outcomes. Patients with aortic leaflet prolapse require lifelong follow-up.

## 1 What is a ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Definition by ISNPCHD</summary><div class="details-content">

The International Society for Nomenclature of Paediatric and Congenital Heart Disease (ISNPCHD) defines a ventricular septal defect (VSD) as **"a congenital cardiac malformation in which there is a hole or pathway between the ventricular chambers"**.

This definition has been incorporated into both the International Paediatric and Congenital Cardiac Code (IPCCC) and the Eleventh Iteration of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11).

Synonyms for ventricular septal defect include: 'interventricular communication', 'interventricular septal defect', 'interventricular septum defect', 'ventricular septum defect', and 'VSD'.

A VSD is defined both on the basis of its **geographic location within the ventricular septum** and its **margins or borders as seen from the morphological right ventricle**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Special Cases: DORV and Univentricular Heart</summary><div class="details-content">

In the setting of **double-outlet right ventricle (DORV)** , the VSD provides the outflow from the morphological left ventricle. The interventricular communication is not the same geometric locus as the ventricular septal defect, although many still describe the outlet from the left ventricle as the 'ventricular septal defect'.

In **univentricular atrioventricular connections with a functionally single left ventricle and an outflow chamber**, the communication (formerly called a bulboventricular foramen) is both a ventricular septal defect and an interventricular communication.

In **common arterial trunk (truncus arteriosus)** , the hole usually closed by the surgeon to provide septal integrity is the right ventricular margin of the channel between the ventricles, rather than the geometric interventricular communication.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 2 How are ventricular septal defects classified?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Five Major Types of VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Multiple systems of nomenclature have been used to describe and classify VSDs (Figure 1). Nevertheless, VSDs can all be grouped into one of five types:

*   **a) Perimembranous VSD** (commonest form)
*   **b) Inlet VSD**
*   **c) Muscular VSD**
*   **d) Subarterial VSD**
*   **e) Gerbode defect** — a communication between the left ventricle and the right atrium

**Definitions for the top two levels of the above hierarchy:**

*   **Ventricular septal defect (VSD):** "a congenital cardiac malformation in which there is a hole or pathway between the ventricular chambers".
*   **Perimembranous central ventricular septal defect:** "a congenital cardiovascular malformation in which there is a ventricular septal defect that: 1) occupies the space that is usually closed by the interventricular part of the membranous septum; 2) is adjacent to the area of fibrous continuity between the leaflets of an atrioventricular valve and an arterial valve; 3) is adjacent to an area of mitral-tricuspid fibrous continuity; and 4) is located at the centre of the base of the ventricular mass".
*   **Inlet ventricular septal defect without a common atrioventricular junction:** "a congenital cardiac malformation in which there is a ventricular septal defect that opens predominantly into the inlet component of the right ventricle in the absence of a common atrioventricular junction".
*   **Trabecular muscular ventricular septal defect:** "a congenital cardiac malformation in which there is a ventricular septal defect within the trabeculated component of the ventricular septum".
*   **Outlet ventricular septal defect:** "a congenital cardiac malformation in which there is a ventricular septal defect that opens to the outlet of the right ventricle between or above the limbs of the septal band".
*   **Ventricular septal defect — haemodynamically insignificant:** "a congenital cardiac malformation in which there is one or more small, clinically insignificant ventricular septal defect(s) in the absence of flow-related cardiac chamber dilatation or abnormal elevation of pulmonary arterial pressure".
*   **Multiple ventricular septal defects:** "a congenital cardiac malformation in which there are multiple ventricular septal defects, which could be of any type".

## 3 What are the morphological features of the different types of VSDs?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Inlet VSD Without Common AV Junction</summary><div class="details-content">

An inlet ventricular septal defect without a common atrioventricular junction is defined as "a congenital cardiac malformation in which there is a ventricular septal defect that opens predominantly into the inlet component of the right ventricle in the absence of a common atrioventricular junction".

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Muscular VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

A muscular VSD represents a congenital cardiovascular malformation in which there is a VSD that has **exclusively muscular borders** (Figure 4).

</div></details>

#### Subarterial VSD (Doubly Committed Juxta-Arterial)</summary><div class="details-content">

A subarterial VSD represents a congenital cardiovascular malformation in which there is a VSD positioned **directly beneath one or both semilunar valves** (Figures 5 and 6).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Gerbode Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

A **Gerbode defect** represents a deficiency of the atrioventricular component of the membranous septum permitting shunting of blood from the left ventricle to the right atrium.

## 4 Describe the anatomy of the conduction system associated with a VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Triangle of Koch and AV Node</summary><div class="details-content">

In a patient with concordant atrioventricular connections and a perimembranous VSD, the **atrioventricular node** is located at the apex of the triangle of Koch. The triangle of Koch is located in the superficial endocardium of the right atrium and has the following boundaries:

*   **a)** the coronary sinus orifice
*   **b)** the tendon of Todaro
*   **c)** the hinge point of the septal leaflet of the right atrioventricular valve

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Conduction Bundle Pathway</summary><div class="details-content">

The conduction bundle starts at the atrioventricular node, penetrates the central fibrous body, and passes to the left side of the septum along the **posteroinferior rim of the VSD**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Distinguishing Perimembranous from Muscular Inlet VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

It is important to distinguish the common perimembranous VSD from the less common muscular VSD in the inlet septum under the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve.

| Feature | Perimembranous VSD | Muscular Inlet VSD |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Border | Includes fibrous portion along tricuspid valve (where tricuspid, mitral and aortic valves meet) | Consists entirely of ventricular septal muscle |
| Relationship to tricuspid annulus | Directly adjacent | Separated by a bar of muscle |
| Conduction system position | **Posteroinferior** to the VSD | **Anterosuperior** to the VSD |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Coexisting Perimembranous and Muscular Inlet VSDs</summary><div class="details-content">

Rarely, a perimembranous VSD and a muscular inlet VSD will both exist in the same patient. In this rare situation, conduction tissue is located in the **muscular bridge between the two VSDs** (Figure 7). This muscular bridge may be quite narrow; therefore, to avoid heart block, these two VSDs should be closed with **a single patch**.

![](_page_12_Picture_9.jpeg)

![](_page_13_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Coexistence of a perimembranous VSD and muscular inlet VSD:** A) conduction tissue is located in the bridge of muscle between the two VSDs; B) opened right atrioventricular junction — the most inferior VSD is a muscular inlet defect, separated by a muscular bar from the superior perimembranous VSD. The apex of the triangle of Koch points directly to the attachment of the muscle bar; C) left ventricular view showing both defects.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Other VSD Types and Conduction Tissue</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Muscular VSD in trabecular or outlet septum:** remote from conduction tissue.
*   **Doubly committed juxta-arterial VSD with muscular posteroinferior rim:** remote from conduction tissue.
*   **Doubly committed juxta-arterial VSD with perimembranous inferior rim:** conduction system passes posteroinferior to the perimembranous border (similar to any other perimembranous VSD).

</div></details>

## 5 What is the embryological aetiology of a ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Normal Embryonic Development</summary><div class="details-content">

An interventricular communication (interventricular foramen) is part of the normal circulatory pattern during the **first 8 weeks of foetal life**. This embryonic interventricular communication is bounded by:

*   **a)** tubercles growing from the ventricular aspect of the atrioventricular cushions (once the aorta is committed to the left ventricle)
*   **b)** crest of the muscular ventricular septum (forms the floor)
*   **c)** inner heart curvature (forms the roof)
*   **d)** septal and parietal cushions within the outflow tract (will fuse to separate aortic and pulmonary outflow tracts)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Perimembranous VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

A perimembranous VSD results from **failure of the embryonic interventricular communication to close** (Figure 8A).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Muscular VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Muscular VSDs result from **failure of the trabeculations of the muscular ventricular septum to coalesce**. These defects can occur in any part of the muscular septum (Figure 8B).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Subarterial VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

A subarterial VSD results from **failure of muscularisation of the fused proximal septal and parietal outflow cushions** or hypoplasia of the septal and parietal outflow cushions (Figure 8C).

## 6 What anomalies are associated with a ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Atrial septal defect (ASD)
*   Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA; also known as patent arterial duct [PAD])
*   Coarctation of the aorta
*   Interrupted aortic arch or aortic arch hypoplasia
*   Atrioventricular septal defect
*   Malformations involving the ventricular outflow tracts:
    *   **a)** tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)
    *   **b)** double-outlet right ventricle (DORV)
    *   **c)** common arterial trunk (CAT, truncus arteriosus [TA])
    *   **d)** transposition of the great arteries (TGA)
*   Functionally univentricular heart ('single ventricle')

Many patients, however, have an **isolated VSD**, without any associated anomalies.

## 7 What is the epidemiology of a ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Isolated VSD is the **second most common congenital cardiac malformation** (after bicuspid aortic valve) and occurs at a rate of approximately **2 per 1000 live births**.
*   Isolated VSDs account for about **one-fifth of all forms of congenital heart disease**.
*   Over **50% of patients with a congenitally malformed heart** have either an isolated VSD or a VSD associated with other major defects.
*   Chromosomal, familial, and geographic factors influence normal closure of the embryonic interventricular communication.
*   **Perimembranous defects** are the commonest form of VSD.
*   **Subarterial defects** are more common in patients of **Asian descent**.
*   Children of patients with a VSD have a higher incidence of congenital heart disease (although not necessarily specifically a VSD).

</div></details>

## 8 What is the pathophysiology of a ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Determinants of Shunt Magnitude</summary><div class="details-content">

A ventricular septal defect usually results in a **left-to-right shunt**, the magnitude of which is determined by:

*   **a)** size of the VSD
*   **b)** pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)
*   **c)** degree of obstruction to pulmonary or aortic outflow
*   **d)** ventricular compliance

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Early vs Late Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Early in life:** pathophysiology is predominantly determined by the pulmonary vascular resistance.
*   **Later in life:** the degree of narrowing and eventual closure of the VSD may become more important.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Consequences of Left-to-Right Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

Excessive left-to-right shunting results in **heart failure**, which manifests as:
*   Dyspnoea
*   Poor feeding
*   Frequent upper respiratory infections
*   Failure to thrive

Children with a **restrictive VSD** will have less left-to-right shunting and may remain asymptomatic with little or no medical therapy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Right-to-Left Shunt Causes</summary><div class="details-content">

Right-to-left shunting causes **cyanosis** in:
*   Patients with obstruction to pulmonary outflow (e.g., tetralogy of Fallot)
*   Patients with elevated PVR

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 9 What is the natural history of a ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinical course can be quite variable and depends on the size of the VSD, magnitude of the left-to-right shunt, PVR, and associated cardiac malformations.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Small VSDs and Spontaneous Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

Most VSDs are **restrictive** and usually less than **5 mm** in diameter.

Small VSDs often undergo **spontaneous closure**, which most frequently occurs during the **first year of life**, but continues to a lesser degree until about **5 years of age**. Spontaneous closure becomes rare after 5 years of age.

Mechanisms of spontaneous closure:
*   **a)** fibrosis of the margins induced by haemodynamic changes and flow
*   **b)** adherence of tricuspid or aortic valve leaflets to the margins of the defect
*   **c)** septal hypertrophy (most common with muscular VSDs)

**Important exception:** Spontaneous closure (or partial closure) from prolapse of an aortic valve leaflet can cause **aortic insufficiency** and represents an **indication for surgical repair**. (It is easier to close the VSD than to repair or replace the aortic valve later in life.)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Large VSDs</summary><div class="details-content">

In patients with larger VSDs, symptoms develop soon after birth when the elevated foetal and neonatal PVR falls.

With increasing age, increased PVR may develop secondary to increased pulmonary blood flow and hypertensive pulmonary vascular disease.

Children with unrepairable non-restrictive VSDs are at risk of developing **irreversible pulmonary vascular disease** at **1 to 2 years of age** (and later in life).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Eisenmenger Syndrome</summary><div class="details-content">

**Eisenmenger syndrome** may develop if a large VSD is not closed. It most frequently appears in the **second and third decades of life**, and typically leads to death by the age of 40.

Characterised by:
*   **a)** irreversible pulmonary vascular disease
*   **b)** PVR greater than systemic vascular resistance
*   **c)** reversal of the ventricular shunt with resultant cyanosis
*   **d)** eventual right ventricular failure

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 10 What are the symptoms of a ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Symptoms of Left-to-Right Shunt (Heart Failure)</summary><div class="details-content">

A VSD with significant left-to-right shunting will result in congestive heart failure, often presenting with:

*   **a)** tachypnoea or dyspnoea
*   **b)** repeated pulmonary infections
*   **c)** hepatomegaly
*   **d)** sweating, especially while eating
*   **e)** feeding intolerance
*   **f)** failure to thrive

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Symptoms of Right-to-Left Shunt (Cyanosis)</summary><div class="details-content">

A VSD with significant right-to-left shunting will result in systemic hypoxia and lack of pulmonary blood flow, presenting with:

*   **a)** failure to thrive
*   **b)** cyanosis
*   **c)** clubbing

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 11 What are the signs of a ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Murmur Characteristics</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with a restrictive VSD have a **loud holosystolic murmur**, best heard at the left sternal border:

*   **a)** The intensity of the murmur can be quite variable, but it can be **inversely related to the size of the defect** (smaller defects often have louder murmurs).
*   **b)** As PVR rises, the murmur becomes shorter, softer, and limited to the early part of systole. The murmur may actually disappear entirely, leaving a **loud second heart sound** (caused by closure of the pulmonary valve), which may be indicative of severe pulmonary hypertension.
*   **c)** The murmur may be accompanied by a **hyperactive precordium** and a **ventricular bulge**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Other Signs</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Some patients with a large VSD may or may not have a murmur but may present with **hepatomegaly** and **jugular venous distension**.
*   Typically, cyanotic children (such as those with tetralogy of Fallot) may use **squatting** to increase pulmonary blood flow.

</div></details>

*   **a)** right ventricular hypertrophy
*   **b)** left ventricular hypertrophy
*   **c)** combined right and left ventricular hypertrophy (biventricular hypertrophy)

Abnormal findings can include:

*   **Cardiomegaly** with biventricular enlargement
*   In extreme cases, a **pleural effusion** can be seen

*   **f)** assessment of the morphology of the ventricular outflow tracts

## 15 What are cardiac catheterisation findings of a patient with a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

In the developed world, most patients with an isolated large VSD have their defect surgically closed by **4–6 months of age**, when pulmonary arterial pressures are unlikely to be irreversibly high. Consequently, cardiac catheterisation does not impact on the decision to perform surgical closure in the vast majority of cases.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Indications for Catheterisation</summary><div class="details-content">

In selected patients with a simple VSD, cardiac catheterisation is still necessary, as it can provide:

*   **a)** pulmonary arterial pressure
*   **b)** ratio of pulmonary to systemic flows (Qp:Qs ratio)
*   **c)** pulmonary vascular resistance
*   **d)** response of the pulmonary arterial vasculature to vasodilators (oxygen, nitric oxide [NO], prostaglandin — including prostacyclin [PGI₂])

Cardiac catheterisation is specifically indicated in an older child or a young adult with:

*   **a)** **moderate-large VSD** where there is a high likelihood of irreversibly elevated pulmonary arterial pressures (including those with cyanosis or bidirectional/right-to-left shunt on echocardiography)
*   **b)** **small VSD** to calculate the Qp:Qs ratio — surgery is usually indicated if **Qp:Qs >1.5:1** and almost certainly indicated if **Qp:Qs >2:1**

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 16 What are the principles of medical therapy for a patient with a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Indications for Medical Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Medical management is indicated in neonates and young infants presenting with:

*   **a)** mild congestive heart failure from a small to moderate defect (as a bridge to resolution by spontaneous closure or restriction)
*   **b)** more severe forms of congestive heart failure from a large defect (as a bridge to definitive surgery)
*   **c)** increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)
*   **d)** endocarditis (prophylactic antibiotic administration)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Medical Therapy for Congestive Heart Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** cardiac glycosides (e.g., digitalis and digoxin)
*   **b)** diuretic therapy
*   **c)** afterload reduction with angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors
*   **d)** supplemental nutritional support for those with poor feeding or failure to thrive
*   **e)** optimal antibiotic therapy for those with recurrent pulmonary infections

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 17 What are the principles of palliative treatment for a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Artery Banding (PAB) — Definition</summary><div class="details-content">

Placement of a **pulmonary artery band (PAB)** is a surgical procedure performed without cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) (Figure 14). The operation involves placing a tie, ligature, or device around the main pulmonary artery to decrease pulmonary blood flow and palliate a patient with a VSD.

PAB is **rarely performed today**.

![](_page_26_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 14. Pulmonary artery banding:** A) umbilical tape passed around the MPA; B) ends of the tape sewn together; C) band progressively tightened. Target distal PA pressure: 30–50% of systemic pressure for biventricular heart; for univentricular heart, as close to Fontan pressure as possible (mean ~15 mmHg). Ao = aorta; MPA = main pulmonary artery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Traditional Indications for PAB</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** palliation of neonate/infant with VSD and contraindications to CPB
*   **b)** Swiss cheese type of multiple VSDs
*   **c)** neonatal apical muscular VSD
*   **d)** neonatal PAB combined with repair of aortic coarctation (VSD + coarctation)
*   **e)** neonatal PAB combined with repair of aortic coarctation (AVSD + coarctation)
*   **f)** postponing choice between biventricular repair and univentricular palliation in VSD with ventricular imbalance
*   **g)** postponing choice between biventricular repair and univentricular palliation in unbalanced AVSD
*   **h)** complex anatomy where better biventricular repair may be possible later when structures are larger (e.g., complete AVSD, crisscross heart)
*   **i)** univentricular physiology with unrestricted pulmonary blood flow (to drop PAP and PVR for future palliation)
*   **j)** extremely marasmic, septic infant
*   **k)** infant with contraindication to CPB (e.g., recent intracranial bleed)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Newer Indications for PAB</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** left ventricular preparation for an involuted subpulmonary left ventricle in late-presenting TGA (in preparation for arterial switch operation)
*   **b)** left ventricular preparation and/or reduction of tricuspid regurgitation in ccTGA (often without a sizeable VSD)
*   **c)** PAB as an adjunct to another procedure:
    *   **i)** PAB as adjunct to bidirectional Glenn shunt (to maintain antegrade pulmonary blood flow)
    *   **ii)** PAB as part of a palliative arterial switch operation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Controversial Indications for PAB</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** routine initial PAB for ccTGA VSD (strategy to preserve left ventricular function after anatomical repair)
*   **b)** adjunctive PAB with physiological correction of late-presenting simple TGA (for future conversion to anatomical repair)
*   **c)** PAB for reversing advanced pulmonary vasculopathy in unoperated congenital heart disease with Eisenmenger syndrome

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Target Pressure for Adequate Banding</summary><div class="details-content">

Adequate banding has traditionally aimed to reduce the distal pulmonary arterial pressures to **50% of systemic level**, so long as pulmonary blood flow will maintain adequate oxygenation.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 18 What are the indications for therapeutic closure of a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

The definitive treatment of a VSD is **complete closure** of the defect, usually performed surgically but may be performed by transcatheter device in selected patients.

![](_page_28_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 15. Algorithm describing therapeutic strategies for VSD.** AI = aortic insufficiency; AVSD = atrioventricular septal defect; DORV = double-outlet right ventricle; PAB = pulmonary artery banding; PM = perimembranous; TGA = transposition of the great arteries; TOF = tetralogy of Fallot.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Criteria for Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

The criteria for closure of a VSD are based upon the:

*   **a)** size and type of the VSD
*   **b)** natural history and intervening symptoms
*   **c)** associated cardiac malformations
*   **d)** complicating factors

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Infants (0–3 months)</summary><div class="details-content">

Infants with a **large VSD and severe, intractable congestive heart failure** (not responsive to medical therapy) should undergo surgical closure at any time during the **first 3 months of life**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Infants (4–6 months)</summary><div class="details-content">

Infants with a large VSD and congestive heart failure that **responds to medical therapy** are managed with conservative follow-up, medical therapy, and surgical closure at about **4–6 months of age**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Older Children with Restrictive VSDs</summary><div class="details-content">

Older children with restrictive VSDs should undergo closure in the presence of:

*   **a)** Qp:Qs >2:1
*   **b)** ventricular dilatation (even if left-to-right shunt is <2:1)
*   **c)** prolapse of the aortic valve leaflets into the VSD (even in the absence of aortic insufficiency)
*   **d)** previous endocarditis

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Doubly Committed and Juxta-Arterial Defects</summary><div class="details-content">

**All doubly committed and juxta-arterial defects (conal VSD)** should be closed regardless of the magnitude of the left-to-right shunt, in order to protect the aortic valve from prolapse and possible insufficiency, as there is **no likelihood of spontaneous closure**. (It is easier to close the VSD than to repair or replace the aortic valve later in life.)

</div></details>

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 19 What are the contraindications for therapeutic closure of a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Patients with a **dominant right-to-left shunt** and calculated PVR **>8 units/m²**, who show **no response to pulmonary arterial vasodilatation**, are not candidates for surgical closure. (These patients often have a history of some previous clinical improvement due to the decreased left-to-right shunt in response to increased PVR.)
*   Asymptomatic patients with a **small VSD and Qp:Qs <1.5:1**, and without a history of prolapse of the aortic valvar leaflet(s), endocarditis, or ventricular dilatation, do not require closure of their VSDs — **with the exception of doubly committed and juxta-arterial defects**.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 20 What are the principles of device closure for a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Perimembranous VSD — Experimental</summary><div class="details-content">

Transcatheter device closure of a perimembranous VSD is currently **experimental** and is associated with the risk of **heart block** and damage to the leaflets of the tricuspid and aortic valves. Hence, it should be reserved for patients who are **not surgical candidates** and who require VSD closure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Subarterial (Doubly Committed Juxta-Arterial) VSD — Experimental</summary><div class="details-content">

Transcatheter device closure of a doubly committed juxta-arterial VSD is also currently **experimental** and is associated with risk of damage to the leaflets of the aortic and pulmonary valve. Hence, it should be reserved for patients who are **not surgical candidates** and who require VSD closure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Muscular VSD — Safe and Effective</summary><div class="details-content">

Transcatheter device closure of **some muscular VSDs** can be safely accomplished (Figure 16). This approach may facilitate closure of a muscular VSD that is difficult to approach with conventional surgery.

![](_page_30_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 16. Transcatheter device closure of a mid-muscular VSD.**

The procedure is performed in the catheterisation laboratory under general anaesthesia, using fluoroscopy and transoesophageal echocardiography for guidance.

Access is obtained through **both the left ventricle** (via femoral or carotid artery) and **the right ventricle** (via femoral or internal jugular vein) to create a loop between the guidewires, ensuring complete control over device positioning.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hybrid Approach for Muscular VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

A **hybrid approach** uses access to the anterior wall of the right ventricle via a sternotomy, with the device inserted through a sheath surgically implanted through the right ventricular anterior wall.

**Complications of both percutaneous and hybrid procedures:** malposition of the occluding device with residual shunt, device dislodgement, or device entrapment in the subvalvar apparatus of the atrioventricular valves.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 21 What are the principles of surgery for repair of a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

Almost all VSDs are closed using **median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass, and cardioplegia-induced diastolic arrest** of the heart.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Factors Influencing Surgical Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** location of the VSD
*   **b)** location of the conduction pathways
*   **c)** relationship of the atrioventricular or arterial valvar leaflets to the VSD
*   **d)** associated cardiac malformations that require repair

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Six Surgical Approaches (Figure 17)</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** right atrial approach
*   **b)** transpulmonary approach
*   **c)** transaortic approach
*   **d)** right ventricular outflow tract approach
*   **e)** right ventricular apical approach
*   **f)** left ventricular approach

A **combined approach** may be required with multiple defects or defects that involve more than one region of the septum.

![](_page_32_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 17. Surgical approaches to VSD repair:** transatrial, transaortic, transpulmonary, RVOT, and right ventricular apical approaches. Ao = aorta; LV = left ventricle; MPA = main pulmonary artery; RA = right atrium; RV = right ventricle; RVOT = right ventricular outflow tract.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 22 What structures are at risk during surgical repair of a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Atrioventricular node and conduction pathways**
*   **Atrioventricular valve leaflets**, especially the tricuspid valve
*   **Arterial valve leaflets**, especially the aortic valve

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 23 What are the principles of surgery to repair a perimembranous VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Access and Exposure</summary><div class="details-content">

Almost all perimembranous VSDs are closed via median sternotomy, with CPB, bicaval venous cannulation, and antegrade cardioplegia.

Although most perimembranous VSDs are accessed through a **right atriotomy** (Figures 17 and 18A), some are approached via a **right ventriculotomy**, which can also be used to repair associated malformations:
*   Common arterial trunk (truncus arteriosus)
*   Tetralogy of Fallot
*   Double-outlet right ventricle
*   Subarterial doubly committed VSD

![](_page_34_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 18. Surgical repair of perimembranous VSD:** A) opened right atrium — VSD visualised through the tricuspid valve; B) closure with Dacron patch using continuous running suture; C) closure with interrupted suturing technique; D) after patch closure — right ventricle filled with saline to 'float' the tricuspid valve. Strip of autologous pericardial pledget (black arrow) seen where patch is sutured to the septal leaflet. ASL = anterosuperior leaflet; PL = posterior leaflet; SL = septal leaflet.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Patch Closure Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

Most perimembranous VSDs are closed with a **patch** (Figures 18B and 18C), secured using a continuous or interrupted suturing technique.

**Patch materials:**
*   Autologous tissue (unfixed or fixed pericardium)
*   Heterologous tissue (fixed bovine or porcine pericardium)
*   Synthetic materials (PTFE [Gore-Tex® or Impra®] or PET [Dacron®])

Some smaller perimembranous VSDs may be closed **primarily**, often with pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Protecting the Conduction System</summary><div class="details-content">

The patch is usually sutured to the rim of the VSD, placing sutures on the **right ventricular side** of the interventricular septum.

Once the annulus of the septal tricuspid valve leaflet is reached, the sutures are passed **through the septal leaflet** to avoid damage to the atrioventricular node and conduction pathways (Figure 18D). This portion of the suture line is often reinforced with a strip of autologous pericardium (or interrupted pledgets).

**Structures at risk:**
*   AV node and conduction tissue
*   Leaflets of the tricuspid valve
*   Leaflets of the aortic valve

</div></details>

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 24 What are the principles of surgery to repair a subarterial VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Most subarterial VSDs are closed using a **transpulmonary approach**, although some may be closed with a right ventriculotomy at the level of the outflow tract.
*   A patch may be secured using a continuous or interrupted suturing technique.
*   Often the superior portion of the patch is sutured to the **annulus of the pulmonary valve**, because in hearts with a doubly committed juxta-arterial VSD, the pulmonary and aortic valves may only be separated by a thin fibrous raphe. (To avoid damage to the aortic leaflets, the sutures are passed through the base of the pulmonary leaflets.)

**Structures at risk:**
*   **a)** leaflets of the pulmonary valve
*   **b)** leaflets of the aortic valve
*   **c)** AV node and conduction tissues (if the subarterial VSD has perimembranous extension)

</div></details>

## 25 What are the principles of surgery to repair a muscular VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Access and Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

Depending on its location, a muscular VSD may be accessed using:
*   Right atrial approach
*   Right ventricular approach
*   Transpulmonary approach
*   Transaortic approach
*   Left ventricular approach

Most muscular VSDs are closed with a patch (continuous or interrupted suturing technique) (Figure 19). Some smaller muscular VSDs may be closed primarily (pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures or continuous suture).

![](_page_36_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 19. Surgical patch repair of a mid-muscular VSD using a continuous suturing technique.**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Swiss Cheese Septum</summary><div class="details-content">

A complex form of muscular VSD is known as a **'Swiss cheese septum'** , where multiple defects are spread across a large portion of the interventricular septum.

**Treatment options:**

*   **a)** **Palliation with pulmonary artery banding** — allows the child to grow, reducing the risk of early complete repair. While the band is in place, some defects may spontaneously close.
*   **b)** **Early repair** with a combination of direct suture closure, patch closure, and **endocardialisation** — suturing together bands of right ventricular muscle to obtain a continuous surface of muscle on the right side of the interventricular septum (Figure 20).

![](_page_37_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 20. Surgical repair of a 'Swiss cheese septum' via right atrial approach:** A) several defects in inlet, perimembranous, and low RVOT regions (conduction system identified by green line); B) one muscle band resected (red dotted line) to expose underlying defect; C) smaller defects repaired with primary suture and endocardialisation; D) larger defects repaired with porcine pericardial patches. ASL = anterosuperior leaflet; PL = posterior leaflet; SL = septal leaflet; SMT = septomarginal trabeculation.

</div></details>

## 26 What are the short-term outcomes of surgery following repair of a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

An analysis from the **Congenital Heart Surgery Database of the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS)** of 7474 patch closures of a VSD from 2015 to 2018 (117 hospitals in North America) demonstrated:

*   **a)** Aggregate rate of **operative mortality: 0.5%**
    *   (Operative mortality defined as: 1) all deaths during hospitalisation in which the operation was performed, even if after 30 days; and 2) all deaths after discharge but before the end of the 30th postoperative day)
*   **b)** Aggregate average postoperative length of stay: **9.9 days**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 27 What are the complications of surgery following repair of a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Arrhythmias (Most Common)</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **5–15%** of patients experience a transient postoperative arrhythmia (managed medically, with temporary pacing, cardioversion or defibrillation)
*   **<2%** of patients experience a postoperative arrhythmia that requires a **permanent pacemaker**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Respiratory Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **<3%** experience postoperative respiratory insufficiency requiring reintubation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Other Complications (<2%)</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Postoperative low cardiac output
*   Pleural effusion requiring drainage
*   Unplanned readmission within 30 days
*   Respiratory insufficiency requiring mechanical ventilation >7 days
*   Chylothorax
*   Pneumothorax
*   Unplanned cardiac reoperation (exclusive of bleeding)
*   Unplanned non-cardiac reoperation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rare Complications (<1%)</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Pulmonary hypertension
*   Pericardial effusion requiring drainage
*   Pneumonia
*   Postoperative septicaemia
*   Postoperative cardiac arrest
*   Bleeding requiring reoperation

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 28 What are the long-term outcomes of surgery following repair of a VSD?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Operative mortality associated with surgical repair of an isolated VSD is **<1%**.
*   Risk of any major morbidity (including AV block requiring a pacemaker, emergent reoperation, and significant residual shunting) is **<2%**.
*   Thus, **over 97% of patients have excellent outcomes**.
*   Most patients survive repair with **no long-term negative impact** from their surgical repair.
*   Patients with aortic insufficiency related to prolapse of an aortic valve leaflet require **lifelong follow-up**, but usually will not require surgical intervention on the aortic valve.

</div></details>
