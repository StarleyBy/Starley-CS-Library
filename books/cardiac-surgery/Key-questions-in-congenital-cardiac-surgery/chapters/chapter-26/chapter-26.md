# Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Anatomy
- **Hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS)** is a spectrum of cardiac defects in which the **left ventricle cannot support the systemic circulation**.
- True hypoplasia (proportionally small but structurally normal left heart) is rare. HLHS typically presents with:
  - Mitral valve: stenotic, dysplastic, or atretic
  - Left ventricle: slit-like (mitral atresia) or small/globular with endocardial fibroelastosis (mitral stenosis)
  - Aortic valve: atretic, dysplastic, or stenotic
  - Ascending aorta: thread-like (atresia) to small/near-normal (stenosis)
  - Isthmus: small/tapering, often with pre-ductal coarctation shelf
- **Coronary perfusion:** Retrograde from main PA through ductus and backward along aortic arch.
- **Borderline LV:** Smaller than expected but with patent valves; may sustain circulation only at rest.

### Embryology and Epidemiology
- Intact ventricular septum suggests trigger **after 8 weeks gestation** (closure of embryonic interventricular communication) → acquired foetal disease.
- Theory: Diversion of blood away from left heart via oval foramen reduces flow → eliminates growth stimulus.
- **Incidence:** ~1 per 5,000 live births; **4th most common cardiac defect**; most common univentricular lesion.
- **Male predominance:** 2:1.

### Subclassification (3 types)
| Type | Mitral valve | Aortic valve |
|---|---|---|
| Combined atresia | Atretic | Atretic |
| Mitral stenosis + aortic atresia | Stenotic | Atretic |
| Combined stenosis | Stenotic | Stenotic |

### Associated Anomalies
- Intact/highly restrictive atrial septum (~10%) → poor prognosis unless alternative decompression
- Tricuspid valve dysplasia
- Aberrant right subclavian artery
- Family history of left-sided defects (coarctation, bicuspid aortic valve)

### Pathophysiology
- **Parallel circulations:** RV stroke volume distributed by relative PVR and SVR.
- **At birth:** Ductal closure → sudden drop in systemic perfusion → lethal without treatment.
- **Coronary steal:** Falling PVR → increased PBF at expense of systemic flow → diastolic pressure drop → compromised coronary perfusion.
- **Restrictive IAS:** Pulmonary venous obstruction → lymphatic changes, elevated PVR → profound postnatal hypoxia.

### Natural History
- Intrauterine death: up to 10%
- Untreated: **70% die within 1 week** of birth; very few survive >1 month
- Survival depends on patent ductus and elevated PVR (inevitable pulmonary vascular disease)

### Clinical Presentation
- Born normal, saturations often >90%, no murmur
- Progressive tachycardia, tachypnoea, grunting
- Weak/absent femoral pulses, cool mottled peripheries, hepatomegaly
- Metabolic acidosis, rising lactate, widening AV oxygen gap

### Diagnosis
- **Foetal echocardiography:** 4-chamber view shows only 3 chambers (hypoplastic LV)
- **Postnatal echocardiography:** Assess ductal patency, RV function, TR, PV function, IAS restriction, MV anatomy, LV size, AoV/ascending aorta, arch/coarctation, retrograde flow
- **CT:** For arch anatomy when echo incomplete (no anaesthesia needed)
- **CXR:** Plethoric lungs, enlarged cardiac silhouette; intact septum → mimics obstructed TAPVC
- **Catheterisation:** Little role, significant risk

### Treatment Options
1. **Termination of pregnancy** (gestational age-dependent)
2. **Comfort care** (no active intervention)
3. **Primary heart transplantation** (limited by donor availability)
4. **Staged surgical palliation** (Norwood sequence → Fontan circulation)

### Stage I Palliation (Norwood Procedure)
- **Damus-Kaye-Stansel procedure:** Disconnect PA trunk, connect pulmonary valve/trunk to aorta → neo-aortic trunk
- **Arch repair:** Relieve coarctation/obstruction
- **Pulmonary blood flow source:**
  - Classic Norwood: Modified BT shunt (brachiocephalic artery)
  - Sano modification: RV→PA conduit (left of neo-aorta)
  - Birmingham modification: RV→PA conduit (right of neo-aorta)
- **Atrial septectomy:** Ensure unrestricted pulmonary venous return
- **Target saturations:** 75–80% (higher → pulmonary over-circulation)

### Stage II Palliation (BCPS) — age 3–6 months
- Disconnect previous shunt/conduit
- **Bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (BCPS):** SVC → RPA
- Alternative: **Hemi-Fontan** (SVC not disconnected from RA)
- Additional procedures: PA stenosis relief, TV repair, relief of subpulmonary stenosis/coarctation/pulmonary vein stenosis

### Stage III Palliation (Fontan/TCPC) — age 3–5 years
- Connect IVC to pulmonary arteries → total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC)
- **Extracardiac TCPC:** Gore-Tex® tube from IVC to PA outside RA (no arrest, some without CPB)
- **Lateral tunnel Fontan:** Intra-atrial tunnel (requires arrest)
- **Intracardiac TCPC:** Tube through atrial cavity (for heterotaxy, dextrocardia, anomalous PV return)

### Hybrid Procedure
- **Alternative to Norwood** for low birth weight (<2.5 kg) or postnatal collapse
- Maintain ductal patency with **stent or PGE₁**
- Control PBF with **bilateral PA bands** (median sternotomy) or percutaneous flow limiters
- **Rashkind septostomy** for wide interatrial communication
- No cardiopulmonary bypass in neonatal period
- Stage I Norwood delayed 4–6 weeks, or stages I+II combined at 4–6 months

### Comparison of Stage I Techniques
| Feature | Classic Norwood | Sano Modification |
|---|---|---|
| PBF source | BT shunt (systemic artery) | RV→PA conduit |
| Diastolic steal | Yes (coronary steal) | No |
| Ventricular incision | No (only RA) | Yes (RV incision) |
| PA distortion | Less | More (left-sided; Birmingham modification improved) |
| Postoperative stability | Less stable | More stable |
| 1-year survival (trial 2005–2008) | Inferior | Superior (benefit lost by 3–5 years) |

### Indications and Contraindications
**Absolute indication:** Diagnosis alone (near-certain death without intervention)

**Relative contraindications (increased risk):**
- Extreme prematurity: <32 weeks (very high risk); 32–37 weeks (moderate risk)
- Low birth weight: 2.5–3 kg (caution); <2.5 kg (increasing risk)
- Poor RV function: Severe IAS restriction, moderate/severe TR (structural > functional), PV dysfunction, obstructed PV drainage
- Rhythm disturbances: Congenital AV block, absence of sinus rhythm
- Non-cardiac: Major neurological injury, syndromic abnormalities, NEC, diaphragmatic hernia, pulmonary lymphangiectasia, renal agenesis, tracheal stenosis

### Peri-operative Mortality
- **Stage I:** 10–15% (high-volume, high-quality programmes)
- **Inter-stage attrition:** Reduced by home monitoring
- **Stage II (BCPS):** 2–3%
- **Stage III (Fontan):** 2–4%
- **Survival at 1 year:** 70–75%
- **Longest survivors:** 35–40 years (very few)

### Morbidity
**Stage I:** All complications of neonatal cardiac surgery with CPB

**Stage II:**
- Prolonged pleural drainage / chylothorax
- Cyanosis (elevated PVR)
- Diaphragmatic palsy (requires plication)
- Pulmonary artery stenosis (requires intervention)
- Pulmonary artery thrombosis (especially with bilateral SVC)

**Stage III (Fontan):**
- Thrombosis of Fontan circuit (elevated PVR, slow flow)
- Paradoxical embolism (via patent fenestration)

### Long-term Prognosis
**Fontan complications (emerging from teenage years onward):**
- Arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation/flutter) — less common with modern tunnels
- Pulmonary arterial stenosis
- Tricuspid valve dysfunction
- Residual/emergent aortic arch obstruction
- Deteriorating cardiac function (diastolic dysfunction nearly universal)
- Protein-losing enteropathy, ascites, hepatic congestion/cirrhosis, plastic bronchitis

**Interventions:** TV replacement, dilatation/stenting of PA or arch stenosis

**Definitive therapy:** Heart transplantation (limited by donor shortage)

**Destination therapy:** Wearable VADs in selected single-ventricle patients

## 1. Definition and Anatomical Spectrum</summary><div class="details-content">

#### What is Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome?

**Hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS)** is a term used to describe a combination of cardiac defects in which the **left ventricle is unable to support the systemic circulation**.

True hypoplasia of the left heart (fully formed but proportionally small) is rare (Figure 1).

![](_page_0_Picture_7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1.** HLHS with patent but too small mitral valve (MV) and aortic valve (AoV). The LV is proportionally small ("borderline LV"). RV forms the cardiac apex. ASD = atrial septal defect. *Image reproduced with permission from Diane E. Spicer.*

#### Anatomical Spectrum

HLHS represents a spectrum (Figures 1–5) with:
- Intact ventricular septum
- Concordant AV and VA connections
- Usually normal-sized atria

**Components:**

| Structure | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Mitral valve** | Stenotic, dysplastic, or atretic |
| **Left ventricle** | Slit-like (mitral atresia, Figure 2) or reduced cavity with concentric hypertrophy ± endocardial fibroelastosis (mitral stenosis, Figure 3) |
| **Aortic valve** | Atretic, severely dysplastic, or stenotic (Figure 4) |
| **Coronary arteries** | Normal location; perfused retrograde from main PA through ductus and backward along arch |
| **Ascending aorta/arch** | Thread-like (aortic atresia) or small to near-normal (aortic stenosis, Figure 5) |
| **Isthmus** | Small and tapering, often with pre-ductal coarctation shelf |
| **Systemic circulation** | Supported by interatrial communication, RV, pulmonary trunk, and patent ductus (Figures 5, 6) |

#### Borderline Left Ventricle

Conditions with LV smaller than expected but with patent mitral and aortic valves are termed **"borderline" LV** or **hypoplastic left heart complex**.

**Challenge:** Determining whether these LVs can sustain systemic circulation. In some patients, this may be achieved only at rest, with minimal or no capacity to increase cardiac output in response to exercise (no ability to increase stroke volume).

Several anatomical and functional algorithms have been developed to predict behaviour of borderline ventricles, but none are definitive.

**Note:** Varying degrees of LV hypoplasia can occur with other conditions (AVSD with RV dominance, DORV with mitral atresia). These are functionally univentricular hearts but are **not** considered HLHS variants, even though they undergo similar surgical treatment.

## 2. Developmental Origin and Embryology</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Timing

Since the ventricular septum is intact, the trigger disturbing LV growth likely occurs **after 8 weeks gestation** (the usual time of embryonic interventricular communication closure). HLHS may be considered an **acquired disease of foetal life**.

#### Proposed Theory

At some point in foetal life, blood is diverted from passing right-to-left through the oval foramen, reducing flow through the left heart and eliminating the stimulus for normal development.

#### Genetic Factors

Multiple genetic factors may produce the trigger for flow diversion, but **no single causative factor** has been identified.

#### Progressive Nature

Foetuses shown in mid-pregnancy to have stenotic aortic valves with reasonable LV size and function may progress to aortic atresia with severe LV dysfunction by term.

## 3. Subclassification and Associated Anomalies</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Subclassification (3 types)

Since HLHS involves acquired foetal disease of the mitral and aortic valves:

| Type | Mitral valve | Aortic valve |
|---|---|---|
| Combined mitral and aortic atresia | Atretic | Atretic |
| Mitral stenosis with aortic atresia | Stenotic | Atretic |
| Combined mitral and aortic stenosis | Stenotic | Stenotic |

#### Associated Cardiac Anomalies

**Intact or near-intact (highly restrictive) atrial septum** (~10% of patients, Figures 2, 7) — carries a poor prognosis unless there is an alternative connection decompressing pulmonary venous return.

- **Tricuspid valve dysplasia**
- **Aberrant right subclavian artery** — may originate from distal aortic arch beyond left subclavian artery, passing behind trachea and oesophagus to reach right arm

#### Extracardiac Abnormalities

Infrequent. There may be a **family history** of less severe left-sided cardiac defects (coarctation, bicuspid aortic valve, or combination) on both parents' sides.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 5. Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Foetal Life

With an unrestrictive atrial communication, HLHS has little impact on systemic circulation and perfusion, as it is supported by the RV through a patent arterial duct.

#### Postnatal Circulation

The same setup continues, but now the pulmonary and systemic circulations behave as **parallel systems**:
- Blood passes through arterial duct → descending aorta
- Flow to upper body and coronary arteries **retrogradely** through aortic arch

#### Ductal Closure

Shortly after birth, with progressive reduction in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), the arterial duct naturally closes → **sudden, severe drop in systemic perfusion** → lethal unless treatment rapidly administered to reopen the duct.

#### Parallel Circulation Physiology

RV stroke volume is distributed according to **relative resistance** in pulmonary and systemic circulations. As PVR falls over the first few days:
- Pulmonary blood flow increases at expense of systemic flow
- Lower PVR causes drop in diastolic pressure → **compromised coronary perfusion**

**Vicious cycle** → cardiac arrest without intervention. This is **pulmonary over-circulation with "coronary steal"** .

Progressive heart failure from pulmonary overflow with systemic hypoperfusion and lactic acidosis develops → lethal if untreated.

#### Restrictive Interatrial Septum

If severely restrictive, obstruction to flow is linked to development of lymphatic and pulmonary vascular bed changes → **irreversible elevated PVR** precluding surgical palliation. After birth: profound hypoxia.

Attempts at in utero balloon dilatation of severely restrictive IAS have had limited success.

#### Stabilisation

If HLHS recognised before birth and appropriate treatment instituted after delivery (maintain ductal patency, ensure adequate interatrial mixing), reasonable stability can be maintained for a few days.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 6. Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Intrauterine death:** Occurs in up to 10% of cases
- **Untreated:** 70% of affected neonates die within **1 week** of birth following ductal closure; very few alive after 1 month
- Survival depends on:
  - Arterial duct remaining patent (occasionally spontaneous)
  - PVR remaining elevated (preventing excessive pulmonary flow and cardiac work)
- Survivors inevitably develop **pulmonary vascular disease**
- If PVR drops significantly, **heart failure rapidly develops**
- With restrictive/intact atrial septum: **life-threatening hypoxia** usually occurs immediately after birth

</div></details>

## 7. Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Ideal Scenario

Diagnosis during foetal life.

#### At Birth

- Baby looks normal
- Arterial saturations often >90%
- Usually **no murmur**

#### Progressive Findings

- Tachycardia and tachypnoea
- Increased work of breathing with grunting
- **Weak or absent femoral pulses**
- **Cool, mottled peripheries**
- Often **hepatomegaly**
- Reduced or absent urinary output

#### While Ductus Remains Open

- May be no cardiac murmur
- Oxygen saturations near normal (unrestrictive IAS)
- No significant difference between pre- and post-ductal saturations or blood pressures

#### Arterial Blood Gas

- Initially normal
- As over-circulation develops or ductus closes:
  - **Metabolic acidosis**
  - **Rising serum lactate**
  - Low venous oxygen saturation with **widening AV oxygen gap**

## 8. Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Foetal Echocardiography

Ideal: Diagnosis in mid-pregnancy or before delivery (Figure 8).

![](_page_8_Picture_9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8.** Echocardiographic A) four-chamber and B) colour flow Doppler foetal images: normal heart with ventricles and atria of equal size, normal flow.

Important to recognise integrity of atrial septum and any restriction of left-to-right flow (Figure 9).

![](_page_9_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9.** Foetal echocardiographic four-chamber view: HLHS with no LV cavity seen. RV = right ventricle; RA = right atrium; LA = left atrium. Diagnostic feature: only three chambers visible due to LV hypoplasia.

#### Postnatal Diagnosis

**First line: Clinical examination.** Symptoms/signs of heart failure + weak femoral pulses in first hours after birth → suspect HLHS or other left heart obstructive lesions.

**Chest X-ray:**
- May be unremarkable prior to ductal closure
- Later: plethoric lung fields, enlarged cardiac silhouette
- Intact/highly restrictive IAS: mimics obstructed TAPVC (severe pulmonary congestion ± lymphangiectasia)

**ECG:** Right ventricular dominance (typical of most neonates)

**Echocardiography — Mainstay of diagnosis:**
- Slit-like LV cavity (atretic MV) or globular LV with mural hypertrophy (mitral + aortic stenosis)
- RV is apex-forming
- Any IAS restriction identified

**Postnatal echocardiogram should assess:**

| Structure | Specifics |
|---|---|
| Ductal patency | — |
| RV function, TR, PV dysfunction | All carry poor prognosis |
| Pulmonary veins | Anatomy and patency |
| Interatrial communication | Patency and size |
| Mitral valve | Anatomy (Figure 10) |
| LV | Size, effect on synchrony/function |
| Aortic valve and ascending aorta | Anatomy and size (Figure 11) |
| Aorta (coronaries to isthmus) | Coarctation presence/severity, retrograde flow quantification |

![](_page_10_Figure_13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 10.** Postnatal apical four-chamber view: A) Hypoplastic LV with severely stenotic (not atretic) MV and large interatrial communication; B) Reduced diastolic inflow on colour Doppler. *Images courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

**CT:** High-resolution scanning useful when aortic arch anatomy not fully delineated by echo. Modern fast scanners avoid need for anaesthesia → preferable to MRI (requires general anaesthesia).

**Cardiac catheterisation:** Little to offer, carries significant risk → **should be avoided**.

## 9. Treatment Options</summary><div class="details-content">

In developed countries, most cases diagnosed during foetal life. Good practice dictates that all available options (Figure 12) are presented to parents during consultations (paediatric cardiologist and/or cardiac surgeon, often with support services, nursing, and obstetric staff) to:
- Offer evidence-based information on therapeutic options
- Illustrate options of non-intervention, termination, and end-of-life care
- Support parents through decision-making

![](_page_12_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 12.** Management algorithm following diagnosis of HLHS. Tx = transplant; DKS = Damus-Kaye-Stansel; BTS = Blalock-Taussig shunt; RV = right ventricle; PA = pulmonary artery; bPAB = bilateral PA banding; PDA = patent ductus arteriosus; PGE₂ = prostaglandin E₂; BCPS = bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt; TCPC = total cavopulmonary connection. *Image courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

#### Main Options

1. **Termination of pregnancy** — legality varies by country and gestational age
2. **Continuation with no active intervention + end-of-life care** — baby may be born at home or non-specialist centre
3. **Surgical interventions** — staged palliation or heart transplantation (when available)

#### Pre-operative Stabilisation

- Baby should be delivered at or near specialised paediatric cardiac centre
- **Prostaglandin infusion** instituted soon after birth to maintain ductal patency
- Baby can usually breastfeed (caution for necrotising enterocolitis); some need nasogastric feeding
- With increasing work of breathing and signs of over-circulation: **elective paralysis and ventilation** to prevent cardiorespiratory failure
- Highly restrictive/intact atrial septum: attempt balloon dilatation of foramen ovale, reassess lungs for lymphangiectasia before proceeding to palliative surgery

#### Primary Heart Transplantation

Used only in selected centres; limited by donor organ availability before patient deterioration.

#### Staged Surgical Palliation (Fontan circulation)

| Stage | Age | Procedure |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage 1** | Neonatal | RV connected to systemic circulation (Damus-Kaye-Stansel), arch repair, PBF source (Norwood, Sano, or hybrid) |
| **Stage 2** | 3–6 months | Bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (BCPS) — SVC to PA |
| **Stage 3** | 3–5 years | Total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) — IVC to PA (Fontan) |

## 10. Stage I Palliation: Norwood Procedure</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Principles (Figure 13)

1. **Disconnect pulmonary trunk** from lungs (proximal to bifurcation)
2. **Damus-Kaye-Stansel procedure:** Connect pulmonary valve and trunk to aorta → create **neo-aortic trunk**
3. **Relieve systemic outflow obstruction** (coarctation, arch enlargement)
4. **Create pulmonary blood flow source**
5. **Excise atrial septum** → unrestricted pulmonary venous return to RA

![](_page_14_Picture_8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13.** Stage I palliation: Construction of neo-aorta (Damus-Kaye-Stansel), RV as systemic ventricle, arch repair. A) Classic Norwood (modified BT shunt); B) Left-sided RV-PA shunt (Sano modification); C) Right-sided RV-PA shunt (Birmingham modification). Rt BTS = right Blalock-Taussig shunt; RA = right atrium; LA = left atrium; SVC = superior vena cava; IVC = inferior vena cava; RV = right ventricle; RPA = right pulmonary artery; LPA = left pulmonary artery; LV = left ventricle; Neo-Ao = neo-aorta; PA = pulmonary artery.

#### Pulmonary Blood Flow Sources

| Technique | Shunt type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Classic Norwood** | Modified BT shunt (brachiocephalic artery → PA) | Oldest technique; no ventricular incision (only RA) |
| **Sano modification** | RV→PA conduit (left of neo-aorta) | RV incision required |
| **Birmingham modification** | RV→PA conduit (right of neo-aorta) | Less PA distortion |

#### Postoperative Assessment (Figure 14)

Echocardiography confirms:
- Adequate systemic and pulmonary blood flow
- Target saturations: **75–80%** (higher → pulmonary over-circulation at expense of systemic flow)

![](_page_15_Figure_5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 14.** Echocardiography after stage I (Norwood + Sano): A) Normal neo-aortic valve function; B) Unobstructed arch with laminar flow; C) Trace TR (now systemic AV valve); D) Adequate flow through RV→PA conduit and both pulmonary branches. *Images courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

## 11. Stage II Palliation: Bidirectional Cavopulmonary Shunt (BCPS)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Timing

Usually performed at age **3–6 months**.

#### Pre-operative Imaging (Figure 15)

Angiography (cardiac catheter), MRI, or CT to delineate systemic and pulmonary blood flow/anatomy. Important to:
- Define any pulmonary artery stenoses
- Confirm normal PVR before surgery

![](_page_16_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15.** Pre-stage II angiography: A) Aortic arch injection: unobstructed arch, adequate filling of native aortic root and coronaries; B) RV injection: adequate filling and anatomy of PA branches via RV→PA conduit. *Images courtesy of Dr. Trevor Richens.*

#### Operative Steps (Figure 16)

1. On cardiopulmonary bypass, disconnect previously implanted shunt/conduit
2. Disconnect SVC from RA
3. Connect SVC to RPA → **bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (BCPS)**

**Alternative: Hemi-Fontan operation** — achieves same objective without disconnecting SVC from RA.

![](_page_17_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 16.** Stage II palliation: A) Disconnection of modified BT shunt; B) Disconnection of SVC from RA; C) Connection of SVC to RPA; D) Completed BCPS. IVC = inferior vena cava; Neo-Ao = neo-aorta; LPA = left pulmonary artery.

#### Additional Procedures During Stage II

- Surgical enlargement (or stenting) of PA stenoses (more frequent on LPA)
- Tricuspid valve repair (now systemic AV valve)
- Relief of subpulmonary stenosis, coarctation, or other stenoses
- Relief of pulmonary vein stenosis

## 12. Stage III Palliation: Total Cavopulmonary Connection (Fontan)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Timing

Performed between **3–5 years of age** to complete palliation strategy.

#### Pre-operative Imaging (Figure 17)

Angiography, MRI, or CT to delineate anatomy. **Vital to confirm normal PVR** by measuring PA pressure.

![](_page_18_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 17.** Pre-stage III angiography: Unobstructed cavopulmonary anastomosis and PA branch anatomy. Small arteriovenous collateral coiled. *Image courtesy of Dr. Trevor Richens.*

#### Operative Goal

Connect IVC to pulmonary arteries → redirect remaining systemic venous return to lungs → **total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC)** (Figure 18).

#### Techniques

| Technique | Description | CPB | Arrest |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Extracardiac TCPC** | IVC excised from RA, connected via Gore-Tex® tube (diameter indexed to BSA) around outside of RA and in front of RPVs to PA opposite BCPS | Some centres perform without CPB | No |
| **Lateral tunnel Fontan** | Open RA, create intra-atrial tunnel between IVC orifice and PA using RA free wall + Gore-Tex®/pericardial patch. Used mostly after hemi-Fontan | Yes | Yes |
| **Intracardiac TCPC** | Gore-Tex® tube through atrial cavity (not pericardial fossa). For anatomically unsuitable cases (malaligned PA/PV, heterotaxy, dextrocardia, anomalous PV return) | Yes | Yes |

![](_page_19_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 18.** Stage III palliation: A) Extracardiac TCPC — Gore-Tex® tube between IVC and RPA in right pericardial fossa; B) Intracardiac TCPC — tube through atrial cavity separating it from PV return. BCPS = bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt; RA = right atrium.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 13. Hybrid Procedure</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Rationale

Alternative to Norwood for:
- Low birth weight (<2.5 kg, definitely <2 kg)
- Diagnosis made after postnatal collapse
- Avoiding prolonged neonatal CPB

#### Principles

- Survival depends on ductal patency (size partly determines systemic flow)
- Instead of arterial trunk anastomosis: **maintain ductal patency with stent or continued PGE₁ infusion**
- Control pulmonary over-circulation with **bands on RPA and LPA** via median sternotomy
- More recently: **percutaneous flow limiters** into branch PAs (avoiding neonatal surgery)

#### Stent Placement (Figure 19)

Stent introduced through pulmonary trunk proximal to ductus, avoiding damage to small peripheral vessels.

![](_page_20_Picture_8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19.** Hybrid procedure in small neonate (<2.5 kg): A) AP view: banding of both PA branches and surgical cannulation of MPA for ductal stenting; B) Lateral view: catheter and stent introduced into ductus under direct vision and fluoroscopy. *Images courtesy of Dr. Trevor Richens.*

#### Atrial Septum

Ensure wide interatrial communication — **Rashkind balloon septostomy**.

#### No Cardiopulmonary Bypass

Procedure performed without CPB.

#### Prostaglandin vs. Stent

Some centres prefer PGE₁ infusion over stents after PA bands. Avoiding PDA stenting in HLHS with aortic atresia (coronaries perfused retrograde via arch) may reduce risk of distal arch occlusion and myocardial ischaemia.

#### Subsequent Surgery

- **Option 1:** Stage I Norwood delayed 4–6 weeks (creating 4-stage pathway)
- **Option 2:** Stages I + II combined at 4–6 months (BCPS or hemi-Fontan)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 14. Comparison of Stage I Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

Institutional preference and experience determine choice between classic Norwood, Sano modification, or hybrid procedure.

#### Key Principles

- Postoperative saturations **75–80%** ideal
- Higher saturations → higher PBF at expense of systemic flow → poor tissue perfusion, renal failure, death from low systemic cardiac output syndrome

#### Classic Norwood vs. Sano Modification

| Feature | Classic Norwood | Sano Modification |
|---|---|---|
| PBF source | BT shunt (brachiocephalic artery) | RV→PA conduit |
| Ventricular incision | No (only RA for cannula/septectomy) | Yes (RV incision → damage + scar) |
| Diastolic steal | Yes (systemic run-off to lungs → lower diastolic pressure → coronary steal) | No (PBF from RV ejection) |
| Postoperative stability | Less stable | More stable |
| Inter-stage mortality | Higher | Lower |
| PA distortion | Less | More (especially left-sided; Birmingham modification — right-sided — improved) |

#### Hybrid Procedure

**Advantages:**
- Avoids prolonged neonatal CPB
- Useful for low birth weight or postnatal collapse

**Disadvantages:**
- Complex, steep learning curve
- Aortic atresia with pre-ductal coarctation may be relative contraindication
- Combined stage I+II after hybrid is more complex, requires reconstruction at PA band sites → increased CPB time

#### Evidence

Randomised trial (2005–2008) showed **survival benefit for Sano at 1 year**, but this **disappeared by 3–5 years** postoperatively. No conclusive evidence of superiority of either technique.

## 15. Indications and Contraindications for Surgical Palliation</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Indication

Given near-certain death without intervention, **diagnosis alone is an indication** provided parents are fully informed of:
- Risks
- Series of operations with frequent hospitalisations
- Subsequent limitations on effort capacity
- Continuing uncertainty of long-term outcome

#### Relative Contraindications (Increased Risk Factors)

| Category | Specific factors |
|---|---|
| **Extreme prematurity** | <32 weeks → very high risk; 32–37 weeks → moderate increased risk |
| **Low birth weight** | 2.5–3 kg → high caution needed; <2.5 kg → increasingly high risk |
| **Poor RV systolic function** | Severe IAS restriction, moderate/severe TR (structural > functional), PV dysfunction, obstructed PV drainage |
| **Rhythm disturbances** | Congenital AV block, absence of sinus rhythm |
| **Non-cardiac conditions** | Major neurological injury, syndromic abnormalities affecting development, NEC, diaphragmatic hernia, motor defects of legs, pulmonary lymphangiectasia, renal agenesis, tracheal stenosis |

## 16. Peri-operative Mortality</summary><div class="details-content">

| Stage | Mortality | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Stage I** | 10–15% | Highest risk. In high-volume, high-quality programmes. ECMO sometimes required for postoperative deterioration |
| **Inter-stage** | Ongoing risk | Reduced by close surveillance + home monitoring protocol |
| **Stage II (BCPS)** | 2–3% | Relatively low risk; little attrition thereafter |
| **Stage III (Fontan)** | 2–4% | Low risk |

#### Survival

- **At 1 year:** 70–75%
- Survival into adolescence quite good
- Longest survivors: **35–40 years** (very few)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 17. Morbidity Associated with Surgical Palliation</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Stage I

All known complications of neonatal cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass.

#### Stage II

- Prolonged pleural drainage or **chylothorax**
- **Cyanosis** (especially with elevated PVR)
- **Diaphragmatic palsy** (requires plication)
- **Pulmonary artery stenosis** (requires intervention)
- **Pulmonary artery thrombosis** (especially with bilateral SVC)

#### Stage III (Fontan)

- **Thrombosis of Fontan circuit** (especially with elevated PVR and "slow" Fontan flow)
- **Paradoxical embolism** (through patent fenestration)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 18. Long-term Prognosis</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Fontan Complications

Emerging from early or mid-teenage years onward:

- **Arrhythmias** (atrial fibrillation/flutter) — less common with modern tunnels (fewer classic Fontan operations)
- **Pulmonary arterial stenosis**
- **Tricuspid valve dysfunction**
- **Residual or emergent aortic arch obstruction**
- **Deteriorating cardiac function** — diastolic dysfunction nearly universal
- **Protein-losing enteropathy**, abdominal ascites, hepatic congestion/cirrhosis, plastic bronchitis

#### Interventions

Some complications may respond to:
- Tricuspid valve replacement
- Dilatation and stenting of PA or aortic arch stenosis

#### Definitive Therapy

**Heart transplantation** — the only therapy likely to affect prognosis, but limited by general shortage of donor organs.

#### Destination Therapy

**Wearable ventricular assist devices (VADs)** have been used in selected single-ventricle patients.
