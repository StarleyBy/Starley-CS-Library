# Informed Consent in Congenital Cardiac Surgery

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### The Role of Consent

*   **Autonomy:** The right of every citizen to influence their own destiny; consent is the expression of this right regarding physical contact.
*   **In healthcare:** Patients (or parents) must agree in advance before any physical intervention. Lack of consent can lead to professional disciplinary action, civil litigation, or criminal prosecution.

### Legal Definitions (English Law)

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Child** | Someone who has not yet reached 18 years |
| **Young person** | Citizens of 16 and 17 years — still children legally, but with emerging autonomy (Mental Capacity Act 2005 applies) |
| **Looked after child** | Subject to a care order (LA shares parental responsibility) or accommodated by local authority without a care order |

### Parental Responsibility

*   **Mother:** Automatically has parental responsibility
*   **Father:** Automatically if married at birth registration; unmarried fathers get it automatically when registering birth (since 2003)
*   **Acquisition:** Can be acquired later by agreement, court application, or marriage to mother
*   **Adoption:** Passed to adoptive parents on legal adoption

### Who Can Provide Consent?

*   **Parent with parental responsibility** — must act in child's best interests
*   **Children under 16** — may provide independent consent if they pass the **Gillick test** (sufficient understanding and intelligence)
*   **Young people (16–17)** — presumed to have capacity (Family Law Reform Act 1969 + Mental Capacity Act 2005)
*   **Adults (≥18)** — presumed to have capacity; if lacking capacity, proceed under Mental Capacity Act 2005 (best interests)

### Gillick Test for Competence (Under 16)

Child must understand:
*   That a choice exists
*   The nature and purpose of the procedure
*   The risks and side effects
*   The alternatives to the procedure
*   And be able to: retain information, weigh it, arrive at a decision, be free from undue pressure

**Competence rests on intelligence, maturity, and experience — not age.**

### Gillick Competent Child — Refusal of Treatment

*   Courts have **resolutely denied** otherwise competent minors the right to choose death
*   Life-saving treatment can be authorised against the child's refusal (e.g., heart transplant, blood transfusion)

### Young People (16–17) — Consent and Refusal

*   **Consent:** Can provide independent consent; parental right provides a "safety net"
*   **Refusal (elective):** Abandon procedure until dispute resolved
*   **Refusal (emergency/life-saving):** Courts invariably override child's autonomy; treatment can be given against wishes
*   **Disagreement between young person and parents:** If young person consents and parents disagree, can rely on young person's consent (but understand basis for disagreement)

### Incapacitated Adults (≥18)

*   Presumption of capacity can be rebutted under Mental Capacity Act 2005
*   If lacking capacity: proceed in patient's **best interests** (consent not required but procedure lawful)
*   **Court of Protection consultation required for:** Organ donation, non-therapeutic sterilisation, foreseeable forceful restraint, innovative/experimental treatments with ethical dilemmas
*   **Unbefriended patients:** Independent Mental Capacity Advocate (IMCA) must be appointed
*   **Advanced decisions** and **Lasting Power of Attorney** must be respected

### Disclosure of Information (Montgomery v Lanarkshire, 2015)

**Duty:** Take reasonable care to ensure patient is aware of **any material risks** involved in recommended treatment, and of any reasonable alternatives.

**Test of materiality:** Would a reasonable person in the patient's position attach significance to the risk? Or is the doctor (or should be) aware that this particular patient would attach significance to it?

**Key principles:**
*   **Dialogue is mandatory** — consent requires discussion, not a soliloquy
*   **Therapeutic exception** (rare, highly restrictive): disclosure may be withheld if it would seriously harm the patient's health (requires second opinion)
*   **No numeric threshold** for risk disclosure — materiality depends on the patient, the risk, the benefits sought, and available alternatives

### Court's Approach to Disclosure Claims

*   English courts **do not rely on expert witnesses** to set the standard for disclosure
*   Judges put themselves in the shoes of the **'reasonable' patient** and ask what such a person would want to know

### When Doubts Remain About Elective Operation

*   Abandon the procedure, reconsider, pursue different course
*   Seek colleague's opinion, MDT discussion, formal second opinion

### Role of MDT

*   No legal provision for shared responsibility — identified consultant bears responsibility for patient's outcome
*   Surgeon should **never** perform an operation believed to be counter to patient's interests
*   The "consenting" surgeon carries all responsibility for choices made when selecting what information to disclose

### Disclosure to Pregnant Mother

*   Foetus has no legal personality until born — child protection laws do not apply
*   Mother bears responsibility for risks to her own health and unborn child
*   Reasonable steps must be taken to inform mother of risks she runs; if she requests information, doctor must provide it

## 1. Foundations of Consent</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Role of Consent in Modern Society</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Choice has achieved a high priority in our society — in education, transport, healthcare provision
*   The necessity for choice reflects the fundamental role of **autonomy**: the right of every citizen to influence their own destiny
*   The need to choose to accept (consent) otherwise unwanted physical contact (touch) is self-evident
*   In less frenetic circumstances, we are entitled to choose who touches us, and when

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Why Consent is Necessary in Health Care</summary><div class="details-content">

*   A patient (or parent) must agree in advance of physical touching before any intervention can ensue
*   Performing an examination without consent is simple rudeness, irrespective of legal context
*   Lack of consent for intimate or irrelevant examinations moves into professional disciplinary regulation, civil litigation, and potentially criminal prosecution
*   Interventions in congenital cardiac surgery are more complex and threatening than simple touch, but the need for consent is built upon this elementary foundation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Is Consent Needed for Anything Other Than Treatment Interventions?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   In paediatric cardiac surgery, patient or parent must agree before confidential information can be further disclosed
*   Rules of consent (competence/capacity, correct disclosure, appropriate recording) apply equally to clinical interventions and confidentiality
*   **Consent is the legal key** that makes both physical intervention and sharing of information lawful

</div></details>

## 2. Legal Definitions and Parental Responsibility</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### What is a Child Under English Law?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   A child is someone who has not yet reached **18 years of age**
*   Legal synonyms: 'minor' and 'infant' (from Latin *infans* — unable to speak)
*   Reflects the legal rule preventing children from speaking for themselves in court (partly addressed over last two decades)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### What is a 'Young Person' Under English Law?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Citizens of **16 and 17 years** described as 'young people'
*   Still children legally, but as they approach majority, their autonomy is emerging
*   **Mental Capacity Act 2005** applies to those 16 years and over
*   Those with parental responsibility retain parallel rights to consent on their behalf, but these rights diminish as the child achieves competence or capacity

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### What is a 'Looked After Child' Under English Law?</summary><div class="details-content">

Children are 'looked after' by the local authority (LA) when:
*   **Care order:** Made subject to a care order by a family court (LA obliged to look after the child)
*   **Accommodation:** LA provides accommodation in the absence of a care order

**Key points:**
*   Care order directs LA to share parental responsibility with parents
*   Only those with parental responsibility can provide consent for treatment
*   If no care order: consent from parents
*   If order in place: local authority passport indicates whether birth parents' parental responsibility is restricted

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Who Can Provide Consent for Surgery?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Automatic parental responsibility:**
*   **Mother:** The woman who gave birth (not the egg donor, if different)
*   **Father:** If married at birth registration; unmarried fathers get it automatically when registering birth (since 2003)

**Acquired parental responsibility:**
*   Unmarried father can acquire later with mother's agreement, by court application, or by marrying the mother

**Other:**
*   Adoptive parents on legal adoption
*   May be shared with guardians appointed by parents or with local authorities

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### How Does the Parent Decide Whether to Provide Consent?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Person with parental responsibility must act in the **child's best interests**
*   These interests are usually self-evident; agreement reached after full disclosure

**Key case (biliary atresia):**
*   Clinicians wished to perform liver transplant (good prospects)
*   Parents refused consent (surgery not in child's best interests)
*   Court of Appeal held: assessment of child's best interests goes wider than narrower medical best interests; child's connection with family held great weight
*   Court refused to enforce hospital's request

This case demonstrates the balancing act performed by courts.

</div></details>

## 3. Children Under 16 — Competence and Consent</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Can Children Under 16 Provide Consent Independent of Their Parents?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Depending on maturity and proposed intervention, children from a young age may provide independent consent
*   Examples: 4-year-old for BP measurement, 6-year-old for venepuncture, 10-year-old for chest drain removal
*   Parents should not be excluded from this process; involvement of children strengthens therapeutic relationship

**Importance of previous experience:**
*   A 15-year-old newly diagnosed with asymptomatic cardiac disease may be horrified and incapable of consenting
*   Another 15-year-old with years of cardiac interventions, facing transplant, may be competent to provide independent consent

Competence depends on intelligence, maturity, and experience — not age.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### How is the Competence of a Child Younger Than 16 Established? (Gillick Test)</summary><div class="details-content">

The **Gillick test** derives from a landmark case establishing that a competent child should be allowed to provide consent independently of parents.

**The child must understand:**
*   That a choice exists
*   The nature and purpose of the procedure
*   The risks and side effects
*   The alternatives to the procedure

**And be able to:**
*   Retain the information long enough
*   Weigh the information
*   Arrive at a decision
*   Be free from undue pressure

**Important:** Competence rests on intelligence, maturity, and experience — not age. Gillick provides a high threshold for consent (public policy — undesirable to allow incompetent children to consent to interventions they cannot fully understand).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Is 'Fraser' Competence Synonymous with 'Gillick' Competence?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   During the Gillick case, Lord Fraser suggested additional guidelines specifically for doctors assisting with reproductive decision-making by children under 16
*   **These do not replace the Gillick test, nor are they synonymous with it**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Can a Gillick Competent Child Refuse Treatment?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Most Gillick competent children refusing treatment are refusing relatively trivial procedures
*   May be entitled to rely on parent's consent; clinical judgement on whether procedure could be deferred

**Life-saving treatment — courts have resolutely denied competent minors the right to choose death:**

*   **Case 1 (15-year-old heart transplant):** Girl refused consent ("would rather die than have transplant... feel different with someone else's heart"). Court overrode refusal, authorised operation — balancing resentment against certainty of death.

*   **Case 2 (14-year-old Jehovah's Witness, blood transfusion):** Court found that even if she had been Gillick competent, grave condition would have led court to authorise transfusion. In fact, she was judged incompetent (unaware of manner of death, basing views on congregation, not own experiences).

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 4. Young People (16–17) and Adults</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Can Young People Provide Independent Consent?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   People of 16 and 17 years are **presumed to have capacity** to provide consent for surgical, medical, and dental treatment
*   **Family Law Reform Act 1969:** Reduced age of majority to 18, introduced notion that 16- and 17-year-olds had *legal capacity*
*   **Mental Capacity Act 2005:** Provided presumption of *mental capacity* for this age group

**Key points:**
*   Young people can provide consent in absence of parents
*   Parental right to provide consent lasts until end of childhood — provides a 'safety net'
*   On 18th birthday, all parents' rights disappear

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### How to Deal with a Young Person Who Wishes Treatment with Which Parents Disagree?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   If parents and child disagree, exercise caution
*   If 16- or 17-year-old wishes to exercise right to consent while parents oppose, one would be entitled to rely on this consent
*   **Important:** Understand basis for disagreement; challenge presumption of capacity if suspected (assess understanding, retention, weighing, communication)
*   Wise to resolve issue before surgery if feasible

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### How to Deal with a Young Person Who Refuses Treatment Parents Wish?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Valid parental consent makes procedure 'legal' — but clinical judgement on whether proceeding against young person's wishes is both practicable and in best interests
*   English law views parental responsibility as a **dwindling right**, eroded by maturing child's increasing autonomy

**By urgency:**
*   **Elective procedures:** Abandon until dispute resolved
*   **Emergency/life-saving:** Explore alternative ways; if life or limbs under threat with no valid alternative, reluctantly restrain and proceed (e.g., postoperative embolism with ischaemic hand)
*   **Urgent (life-preserving):** Courts invariably override child's autonomy and provide order allowing lawful treatment against child's wishes (e.g., blood transfusion)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Does the Young Person's Right to Consent Extend to Research Activities?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The 1969 law did not extend this right to consent for research or interventions that do not potentially provide direct health benefit
*   However, if competent along 'Gillick' lines, a young person may be able to provide consent for these activities

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### How Can Consent be Obtained from 'Incapacitated Adult Patients'?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   All patients ≥18 years are **presumed to have capacity**
*   Presumption may be rebutted if, under Mental Capacity Act 2005, they lack capacity to make the relevant decision

**If adult has capacity:**
*   No consent can be provided from any source
*   If universal agreement that procedure is in best interests, proceed lawfully on that basis

**Court of Protection should be consulted (even with universal agreement) for:**
*   Organ donation
*   Non-therapeutic sterilisation of incapacitated adults
*   Foreseeable necessity for forceful restraint
*   Innovative/experimental treatments or ethical dilemmas

**Other considerations:**
*   **Unbefriended patients:** Independent Mental Capacity Advocate (IMCA) must be nominated
*   **Dissent from any quarter:** Resolve dispute before proceeding; if cannot, declaration from Court of Protection required
*   **Advanced decisions:** Must be respected
*   **Lasting Power of Attorney:** Donee authorised to give/refuse consent on behalf of patient
*   **Court-appointed deputy:** Unlikely to have powers for medical decisions (courts reserve these for themselves)
*   **Social services:** No role in consent for this group

</div></details>

## 5. Disclosure of Information and Legal Standards</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Why is it Necessary to Disclose Information When Seeking Consent?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Clinicians must supply enough information for an **informed decision**
*   This is separate from ensuring patients are not subjected to unwanted touch
*   Doctors often fail to appreciate how little patients understand about consequences of intervention

**Examples:**
*   Back surgery may lead to urinary/faecal incontinence or leg paralysis
*   Subclavian needle misdirection may lead to thoracotomy for haemorrhage
*   Difficult inguinal hernia repair could lead to loss of ipsilateral testicle

Disclosing alternatives may be very important:
*   Man facing excision of pulmonary metastases from osteosarcoma may be unaware cure is unlikely — might choose alternative route
*   Woman with acute appendicitis might choose antibiotics for 24 hours to attend daughter's wedding

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### What Should be Disclosed When Seeking Consent? (Figures 1–4)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Foundational case (Pearce v United Bristol Healthcare NHS Trust, 1999):**
> "If there is a significant risk which would affect the judgement of a reasonable patient, then in the normal course it is the responsibility of a doctor to inform the patient of that significant risk."

**Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board (2015, UKSC 11):**
*   Supreme Court endorsed and confirmed previous decisions
*   Reiterated GMC advice (2008) emphasising discussion that leads to consent, not a soliloquy

**Legal duty:** The doctor must take reasonable care to ensure the patient is aware of:
*   Any **material risks** involved in recommended treatment
*   Any reasonable alternatives or variant treatment

**Test of materiality:** Would a reasonable person in the patient's position be likely to attach significance to the risk? Or is the doctor (or should be) aware that the particular patient would be likely to attach significance to it?

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### To What Extent is Dialogue with the Patient or Parents Required?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Dialogue is mandatory**
*   Both courts and regulators consider open discussion between clinician and patients to be of the greatest importance

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Is There an Exception to the Imperative for Disclosure Prior to Consent?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   There is an exception, but it is **highly restrictive**
*   The court in Montgomery acknowledged the rare situation where disclosure would be **seriously detrimental to the patient's health**
*   This exception primarily refers to the health of a person making a decision about their own treatment (unlikely to apply to a parent)
*   **Therapeutic exception** should be used only with great caution and would require a second opinion as a matter of urgency

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### How Do Courts Deal with Patients' Claims of Insufficient Disclosure?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Very unusual for such cases to reach court
*   When they do, English courts **do not rely on expert witnesses** to set the standard for disclosure (unlike most clinical negligence cases)
*   Judges put themselves in the shoes of the **'reasonable' patient** and enquire what such a person would want to know before giving consent

</div></details>

## 6. Practical Considerations and Special Situations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### What Should be the Appropriate Course of Action if Doubts Remain About a Proposed Elective Operation?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Seeking consent from a fully informed person should concentrate the mind of both surgeon and patient
*   If the process fails to dispel doubts that intervention is the right thing to do:
    *   **Abandon the procedure**
    *   Reconsider the situation
    *   Pursue a different course
*   Valid strategies: seek colleague's opinion, promote MDT discussion, obtain formal second opinion

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### What is the Role of Multidisciplinary Team Deliberations in Congenital Cardiac Surgery?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   In the NHS, there is **no legal provision for shared responsibility**
*   Each patient is treated under an identified consultant who bears responsibility for outcome
*   If MDT conclusions are later challenged, a record of individual contributions must be available

**Key principle:** A surgeon should **never** perform an operation believed to be counter to the patient's interests.

*   If surgeon disagrees with MDT majority conclusion: make this known to MDT, giving reasons
*   If agreement cannot be reached: consider opinion or intervention of a surgeon colleague

**Responsibility:** The 'consenting' surgeon carries all responsibility for choices made when selecting what information to disclose (though information may reflect advice garnered at MDT).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### How Should Information be Disclosed to a Pregnant Mother?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Anything not proscribed by English law is lawful — no laws prevent pregnant women from activities that may be prejudicial to mother and baby
*   A woman with congenital heart disease is entitled to run the risk pregnancy poses to her own health — a matter only for her to decide
*   **Responsibility for carrying the risk remains with the mother**
*   Until the baby is born and has separate existence from mother, it has no legal personality and is considered part of the mother — child protection laws do not apply to a foetus

**Requirements:**
*   Reasonable steps must be taken to inform the putative mother of the risks she runs
*   If she requests information, the consulting doctor must provide it
*   Any risks to the foetus deriving from her cardiac diagnosis (heritability) or her cardiac health (ability to carry to term, provide circulation) must be disclosed

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### What is the Role of a Numeric Threshold in Choosing What to Disclose?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The assessment of whether a risk is 'material' **cannot be reduced to percentages**
*   Significance of a given risk reflects:
    *   Its magnitude
    *   The nature of the risk
    *   The effect its occurrence would have on the patient's life
    *   The importance to the patient of the benefits sought
    *   Alternative therapies available and their risks
*   Basing a clinical decision on whether a risk crosses a numerical threshold (e.g., 0.1% or 0.001%) is **arbitrary and unreasonable**

</div></details>
