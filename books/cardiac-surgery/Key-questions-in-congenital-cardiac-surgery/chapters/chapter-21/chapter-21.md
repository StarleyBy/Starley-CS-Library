# Tetralogy of Fallot

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Anatomy
*   **Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF):** The most common cyanotic congenital heart disease. A conotruncal lesion with four recurrent anatomical features:
    *   Aortic override
    *   Ventricular septal defect (VSD)
    *   Right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) obstruction
    *   Right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH)
*   All features attributable to **anterocephalad deviation of the infundibular septum** coupled with hypertrophy of the septoparietal trabeculations.

### Embryology
*   Develops during conotruncal development (5th–6th week).
*   Anterior deviation of infundibular septum → prevents alignment between primitive ventricular septum and infundibular septum → interventricular communication + aortic override.
*   Deviated infundibular septum + hypertrophied septoparietal trabeculations → RVOT obstruction.
*   RVH is a haemodynamic consequence of RVOT obstruction.

### Epidemiology and Aetiology
*   Prevalence: **0.21–0.36 per 1000 live births**.
*   Accounts for **7–10% of all congenital heart diseases**.
*   Mostly sporadic; familial cases reported (missense mutation in JAG1).
*   **22q11 deletion** seen in **12–20%** of patients.

### Phenotypic Spectrum
*   Wide spectrum depending on degree of infundibular septal deviation and RVOT obstruction.
*   Most patients have moderate to severe RVOT stenosis.
*   **Mild/no stenosis:** Eisenmenger defect.
*   **Extreme:** TOF with pulmonary atresia (TOF-PA).
*   Vast majority have **elongated RVOT** (funnel-like structure compounding stenosis).
*   Anatomical variations: VSD type, aortic-tricuspid relation, aortic-pulmonary relation, coronary artery anatomy.

### Associated Anomalies
*   **Pentalogy of Fallot:** PFO or ASD (most common).
*   Additional muscular VSDs, straddling tricuspid valve, AVSD-TOF, DORV-TOF, TOF-PA, right aortic arch (up to 15%).
*   **Coronary anomalies** (not uncommon): anomalous LAD from RCA (2–3%), accessory LAD from RCA (5–6%), single coronary artery.
*   Pulmonary artery branch anomalies (intrapericardial course variations).

### Pathophysiology Determinants
*   Degree of RVOT obstruction (fixed vs dynamic)
*   Size and position of VSD
*   Degree of RV hypertrophy
*   Associated lesions

**Fixed obstruction:** Independent of physiological/metabolic status (anatomical factors: pulmonary/suprapulmonary stenosis).
**Dynamic obstruction:** Varies with hypoxia, hydration, sympathetic activation (RVOT muscle contraction).

### Pathophysiology Scenarios by RVOT Obstruction Degree

| Degree | Shunt | Physiology | Presentation |
|--------|-------|------------|--------------|
| Minimal/mild | Large left-to-right | Equal RV and LV pressure; volume/pressure overload of lungs ("over-circulation") | "Pink tetralogy", CHF, failure to thrive |
| Moderate | Bidirectional | Well-balanced; right-to-left due to increased RVOT resistance | Mild cyanosis, mild symptoms, infrequent spells |
| Severe | Predominantly right-to-left | Critically reduced pulmonary flow; systemic flow normal until hypoxia causes bradycardia | Cyanosis, cyanotic spells, cardiorespiratory collapse |
| Extreme/PA | Duct-dependent | No antegrade pulmonary flow; saturations depend on ductus | Prostaglandin-dependent; critical at day 5–7 |

### Natural History (Untreated)
*   Survival: **~75% at 1 year, ~30% at 10 years**.
*   Major causes of death: hypoxic spells (62%), cerebrovascular events (30%).

### Symptoms
*   **Neonates (severe RVOT obstruction):** Significant cyanosis at birth (duct-dependent).
*   **Infants (mild/minimal obstruction):** CHF symptoms (poor feeding, poor weight gain, tachypnoea) — no cyanosis.
*   **Older patients:** Finger clubbing (longstanding cyanosis).
*   **Cyanotic spells:** Common in unrepaired patients >6 months; triggered by dehydration, fever, tachycardia, decreased SVR.

### Physical Signs
*   Cyanosis + systolic ejection murmur along left upper sternal border (moderate-severe TOF).
*   Second heart sound: typically **single**, normal intensity.
*   Continuous murmur if PDA patent.
*   Increased parasternal RV impulse ± thrill.
*   Growth usually normal (delayed if syndromic).
*   Minimal/mild obstruction: CHF signs (tachypnoea, hepatomegaly, poor weight gain).

### ECG
*   Rightward QRS axis
*   Various degrees of RV hypertrophy

### Chest Radiograph
*   Moderate-severe TOF: decreased pulmonary vascular markings, upturned apex, pulmonary concavity → **"boot-shaped heart"** (Figure 8).
*   Right aortic arch in up to 15%.
*   Acyanotic infants: normal or increased pulmonary blood flow (cardiomegaly, increased markings, congestion).

### Echocardiography (Gold Standard)
*   Complete segmental analysis required.
*   **Infundibular septal deviation:** Subcostal right anterior oblique and short-axis views (Figure 9).
*   **RVOT obstruction:** Nature and degree (subvalvular/infundibular, valvular, supravalvular); colour flow and pulse Doppler (Figure 10).
*   **Pulmonary artery anatomy:** Parasternal short-axis view (origin of LPA — acute angle) (Figure 11).
*   **VSD:** Type, position, shunt direction/degree (Figures 12, 13).
*   **Ductus arteriosus:** Patency (Figure 14).
*   **Coronary anomalies:** Parasternal short-axis view — anomalous LAD from RCA crossing RVOT (crucial for surgical planning) (Figure 15).
*   **MAPCAs:** May be visualised if large; require angiography/CT for delineation.

### Cardiac Catheterisation
*   Limited role for diagnosis (echocardiography sufficient).
*   RV injection confirms: aortic override degree, RVOT obstruction (Figure 16), VSD type/size (Figure 17), PA size/stenosis, RVH.
*   **Useful for:** MAPCA delineation, pre-operative and post-operative catheter-based interventions.

### Management Principles
*   **Surgical repair is the only definitive therapy.**
*   Medical treatment stabilises until surgery.
*   **PGE₁:** Neonates with duct-dependent pulmonary circulation (overdosing → apnoea, hypoxia).
*   **Beta-blockers (propranolol):** Older infants with progressive RVOT obstruction and cyanotic spells (oral or IV).
*   **Diuretics (furosemide, spironolactone):** Minimal RVOT obstruction with pulmonary over-circulation.

### Indications for Surgical Repair
*   Presence of TOF is an **absolute indication**.
*   Aim: definitive repair — close ASD/VSD, correct RVOT obstruction at all levels, re-establish biventricular circulation.

### Contraindications to Primary Repair
*   No absolute contraindications, but precluding conditions include:
    *   Extreme prematurity, low body weight (<2.5–2.8 kg)
    *   Intracranial haemorrhage
    *   Active systemic infection
    *   Extremely small branch PAs or MAPCAs (may need staged operations)

### Surgical Principles

**Historical evolution:**
1.  Initial: BTS (Blalock-Taussig shunt) for pulmonary blood flow.
2.  Then: Staged repair (BTS → repair at 1 year) with transannular patch (creating severe PR).
3.  Current: **Transatrial/transpulmonary approach** (minimises muscle injury, preserves pulmonary valve when possible — mild residual PS better than free PR long-term) (Figure 18).

**Current paradigm shift:**
*   From staged repair to **primary definitive repair** at any age without contraindication.
*   Initial palliation (BTS) now infrequent.

**Valve management:**
*   **Valve preservation:** Attempted even with moderate stenosis (Figure 19D).
*   **Transannular patch (TAP):** When valve cannot be preserved (severe PS/near atresia) (Figure 19C).
*   **Monocusp valve:** Mitigates free PR when TAP required (Figure 20).
*   Patients with TAP ± monocusp will require later pulmonary valve implantation.

**Ongoing controversies:**
*   Primary vs staged repair
*   Catheter-based palliation (RVOT/ductal stent) vs BTS
*   Transatrial transpulmonary vs transventricular approach
*   Indications, timing, modality of pulmonary valve implantation

### Treatment Algorithm (Figure 21)
*   **Asymptomatic:** Elective repair at **5–6 months**.
*   **Earlier repair:** O₂ saturations <80% or cyanotic spell.
*   **Neonates:** Primary repair unless contraindications (weight <2.5–2.8 kg, prematurity <36 weeks, pathological biventricular hypertrophy, intracranial haemorrhage, sepsis, significant end-organ dysfunction).
*   **If primary repair contraindicated:** Balloon dilatation or RVOT stent (if feasible).
*   **Post-stent patients:** Elective repair at **3–6 months** (or earlier if saturations <80%).
*   **Policy:** Avoid BTS in neonatal/early infantile period.

### Interventional Options

**RVOT stent placement (Figure 22):**
*   Excellent palliative option for critical cyanotic neonates/small infants not suitable for primary repair.
*   Alternative to BTS (Figure 23).

**Balloon dilatation:**
*   For isolated pulmonary valvular stenosis (temporary relief).

**PDA stent:**
*   Option to increase pulmonary blood flow if RVOT stenting insufficient.

**Percutaneous pulmonary valve implantation (PPVI):**
*   For patients with TAP requiring later valve implantation.
*   Transvenous approach via femoral vein.
*   **Contraindications:** Annulus/RVOT too wide, highly pulsatile RVOT, calcified narrow conduit, associated lesions requiring surgery (significant PA stenosis, TR, residual VSD).

### Complications of Surgical Repair
*   **Peri-operative mortality:** 0–1% (acyanotic), 1–3% (symptomatic neonates/infants).
*   **Complete heart block:** ~1% (requires permanent pacemaker).
*   **Low cardiac output state:** Up to 1/3 of patients (due to restrictive RV physiology).
*   **Residual lesions:** RVOT obstruction (Figure 24), pulmonary artery stenosis (Figure 25), tricuspid regurgitation — may require reintervention.

### Long-Term Outcomes
*   Survival: **~85% at 30 years**.
*   Risk of death: **~0.3% per year** (first 30 years). 40-year survival up to 90% (current era).
*   **Risk factors for late mortality:** Older age at operation, high post-repair RV/LV systolic pressure ratio.
*   **Transannular patch:** Mixed results on late survival; vast majority require later PVR.
*   **Common causes of death:** Cardiac-related sudden death, CHF, reoperation-related death.
*   **Functional status:** ~80% NYHA Class I, <10% Class III at 30-year follow-up (but exercise capacity markedly impaired).
*   **PVR risk:** Constant ~0.8% per year for 30 years.
*   **Reoperation (non-PVR):** 15–20% (residual ASD/VSD, TR, residual RVOT obstruction/aneurysm, branch PA stenosis) (Figure 26).

## 1 What is Tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) is the **most common cyanotic congenital heart disease (CHD)**, and it constitutes a wide spectrum of presentations all considered conotruncal lesions, or lesions of the right ventricle outflow tract and the ventriculo-arterial junction.

TOF has four recurrent anatomical features (Figure 1):
*   Aortic override
*   Presence of a ventricular septal defect (VSD)
*   Right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) obstruction
*   Right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH)

These are all attributable to the **anterocephalad deviation of the infundibular septum** coupled with hypertrophy of the septoparietal trabeculations.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 2 What is the embryology of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

The anatomical features of TOF develop during **conotruncal development** between the **5th and 6th week** of human embryological development.

*   When **anterior deviation of the developing infundibular septum** occurs, it prevents complete alignment between the primitive ventricular septum and infundibular septum, allowing for an **interventricular communication (IVC)** and **overriding of the aorta** over it.
*   The combination of the deviated infundibular septum and hypertrophied septoparietal trabeculations causes **RVOT obstruction**.
*   Right ventricular hypertrophy is considered as a **haemodynamic consequence** of RVOT obstruction.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 3 What are the epidemiology and aetiology of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The prevalence of TOF is **0.21–0.36 per 1000 live births**.
*   TOF accounts for approximately **7–10% of all congenital heart diseases**.
*   The exact aetiology is largely unknown, with most cases **sporadic**, although some familial cases have been reported (missense mutation in **JAG1**).
*   **22q11 deletion** is seen in **12–20%** of patients with TOF.

</div></details>

## 4 Describe the phenotypical spectrum of tetralogy of Fallot</summary><div class="details-content">

TOF has a wide spectrum of anatomical and physiological presentations, which depend on the degree of anterior deviation of the infundibular septum and RVOT obstruction (Figure 2).

*   The vast majority of patients with TOF have an **elongated RVOT** when compared to normal hearts, which contributes to the creation of the funnel-like structure of the RVOT, thus compounding the degree of stenosis (Figure 3).
*   **Anatomical variations** include those attributable to:
    *   Type of VSD
    *   Relation of the aortic valve to the tricuspid valve
    *   Relation of the aortic valve to the pulmonary valve
    *   Coronary artery anatomy (Figure 4)

## 6 What are the determinants of the pathophysiology in tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

The pathophysiology of TOF is determined by:
*   The degree of RVOT obstruction
*   The size and position of the VSD
*   The degree of right ventricular hypertrophy
*   The presence of associated lesions

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Fixed vs Dynamic Obstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Fixed obstruction:** Independent of the physiological or metabolic status of the patient; caused by anatomical factors (pulmonary and suprapulmonary valve stenosis).
*   **Dynamic obstruction:** Varies in relation to the degree of existing hypoxia, hydration, or sympathetic activation; caused by RVOT muscle contraction. In moderate to severe forms of TOF, both mechanisms usually concur to the overall degree of flow obstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### VSD Anatomy</summary><div class="details-content">

VSD anatomy and position can alter the amount of flow shunting across the septum:
*   **Restrictive VSD** can limit flow in either direction.
*   **Larger and subarterial VSD** is more prone to causing significant volume and pressure overload to the pulmonary circulation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Right Ventricular Hypertrophy</summary><div class="details-content">

The degree of RV hypertrophy determines the degree of stiffness and the resulting **diastolic dysfunction** of the RV. Severe forms of hypertrophy can result in **restrictive physiology** of the right ventricle, defined by persistent flow across the RVOT during diastole. Diagnosis is usually obtained by echocardiography (Figure 6).

![](_page_5_Figure_7.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Echocardiographic parasternal short-axis biventricular view of tetralogy of Fallot demonstrating severe diffuse thickening of the right ventricle.** RV = right ventricle; LV = left ventricle. *Image courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha, University Hospital Southampton, UK.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Complex Lesions</summary><div class="details-content">

When TOF is associated with other complex lesions (PA, AVSD, DORV), the resulting physiology is often that of the most dominant lesion, although the dominant physiological character could be enhanced.

*   **TOF-PA:** Extreme degrees of cyanosis due to complete obstruction of pulmonary blood flow. Patients present with a **duct-dependent pulmonary circulation** and are totally prostaglandin-dependent if no collateral circulation has developed.
*   **Mild obstruction + AVSD:** Severe pulmonary overload, pulmonary oedema, early cardiac failure.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 7 What are the common pathophysiology scenarios in tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_6_Figure_7.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Physiological behaviour, clinical symptoms and oxygen saturation levels in a patient with tetralogy of Fallot, according to the degree of RVOT stenosis.**

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Minimal or Mild RVOT Obstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients have a **large left-to-right shunt** across the VSD due to lower pulmonary vascular resistance. Intracavitary RV pressure equals LV pressure (systemic right ventricle), and the lungs receive both volume and pressure overload via the VSD ("**over-circulation**"). This is similar to isolated VSD physiology, resulting in various degrees of congestive heart failure ("**pink tetralogy**") and failure to thrive, often evident at an early age.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Moderate RVOT Obstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

Physiology can be **well balanced**, with shunting across the interventricular communication generally in both directions. Right-to-left shunting can still occur due to increased resistance to flow across the RVOT compared to systemic vascular resistance, accounting for relative cyanosis.

Appropriate pulmonary blood flow is maintained (but not pressure overloading), and systemic flow is adequate. Symptoms can be very mild, growth is generally maintained, and cyanotic spells are infrequent.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Severe RVOT Obstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

Shunting across the interventricular communication is **predominantly right-to-left** due to increased resistance to flow across the RVOT compared to systemic vascular resistance. Flow velocity can be difficult to measure as little flow occurs.

Although pulmonary blood flow can be critically reduced, systemic flow can be normal until bradycardia and biventricular failure occur due to hypoxia. Symptoms can be very severe: cyanosis, cyanotic spells, and even true cardiorespiratory collapse due to protracted, profound hypoxia. Growth can be maintained, but these neonates are generally critically ill.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Extreme RVOT Obstruction (including TOF-PA)</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients have **no appreciable antegrade flow** across the pulmonary valve and a **duct-dependent pulmonary circulation**. Arterial saturation depends on the size and degree of restriction of the ductus arteriosus.

As the duct naturally begins to close on **day 5–7 of life**, oxygen saturations can drop rapidly due to reduced pulmonary blood flow and right-to-left shunting. The systemic circulation is maintained mainly by shunting across the VSD as pulmonary venous return is also reduced. These patients require **resuscitation and medical intervention for survival**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 8 What is the natural history of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Survival in untreated patients is approximately **75% at 1 year of age** and **30% at 10 years of age** in most reported series.
*   The major cause of death in patients with unrepaired TOF includes **hypoxic spells (62%)** and **cerebrovascular events (30%)**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 9 What are the symptoms of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Neonates with severe RVOT obstruction** may present with **significant cyanosis at birth**. The degree of cyanosis depends on the status of the ductus arteriosus (duct-dependent pulmonary circulation).
*   **Infants with mild or minimal RVOT obstruction** may or may not present with non-specific symptoms of congestive heart failure (poor feeding, poor weight gain, tachypnoea) because of a large left-to-right shunt. These patients generally do not have cyanosis.
*   **Older patients with longstanding cyanosis** may have **finger clubbing**.
*   **Patients with significant RVOT obstruction** may have **cyanotic spells**, which is common amongst unrepaired tetralogy patients older than 6 months. Physiological changes (dehydration, fever, resultant tachycardia, decrease in systemic vascular resistance) may decrease pulmonary blood flow, thus triggering a cyanotic spell.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 10 What are the physical signs of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The presence of **cyanosis** and a **systolic ejection murmur** along the left upper sternal border are the typical physical signs of a moderate to severe form of TOF.
*   The **second heart sound** is typically **single** and of normal intensity.
*   A **continuous murmur** may be audible when the ductus arteriosus is patent.
*   The parasternal RV impulse may be increased, and a **thrill** may be palpable.
*   Patients usually have a normal growth pattern, although growth may be delayed if patients have an associated syndrome.
*   Infants with TOF who have minimal or mild RVOT obstruction may have clinical features of congestive heart failure (tachypnoea, hepatomegaly, poor weight gain).

</div></details>

## 12 What are the radiological features of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Chest radiographs in patients with moderate to severe forms of TOF show **decreased pulmonary vascular markings**, an **upturned apex of the heart**, and a **pulmonary concavity** at the upper left heart border — a **"boot-shaped heart"** (Figure 8).
*   A **right aortic arch** can be found in up to **15%** of patients with TOF.
*   The chest radiograph in acyanotic infants with TOF may be normal, or may have signs of increased pulmonary blood flow (cardiomegaly, increase in pulmonary vascular markings, pulmonary congestion).

## 13 What are the echocardiographic features of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography is the **most important and effective diagnostic modality** for delineating the anatomy of TOF.

Diagnosis and characterisation of TOF should be made in the context of a complete scan according to standard echocardiographic segmental analysis of the heart.

#### Collaterals and Associated Anomalies</summary><div class="details-content">

Systemic to pulmonary vascular collaterals (MAPCAs) may be visualised if large but require delineation by angiography or CT. Most, if not all, associated anomalies can and should be detected with echocardiography.

</div></details>

*   **b)** Degree of RVOT obstruction (Figure 16)
*   **c)** Type and size of the VSD (Figure 17)

*   **e)** Degree of right ventricular hypertrophy

## 15 What are the principles of managing a patient with tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Surgical repair is the only definitive therapeutic option** for TOF.
*   The role of medical treatment is to **stabilise the patient's physiology** until definitive surgical repair is performed.
*   Initial medical intervention depends on the clinical presentation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Prostaglandin E₁ (PGE₁)</summary><div class="details-content">

Used in neonates with a **duct-dependent pulmonary circulation** where the PDA is still patent and/or reactive to medications. Requires IV access and in-hospital surveillance. **Overdosing of PGE₁ can lead to apnoea and resulting hypoxia**, which could aggravate spelling episodes or protracted desaturations.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Beta-Blockers (e.g., Propranolol)</summary><div class="details-content">

Used in older infants with **progressive RVOT obstruction and cyanotic spells** where the ductus arteriosus has already irreversibly closed. Although in the majority of cases given orally, they can be administered intravenously in the most severe cases.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Diuretic Therapy (Furosemide and/or Spironolactone)</summary><div class="details-content">

Used in patients with TOF who have **minimal RVOT obstruction and pulmonary over-circulation**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 16 What are the indications for surgical repair of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The presence of TOF is an **absolute indication for surgical repair**.
*   The aim of surgical intervention is to provide **definitive repair** by addressing all lesions included in the tetralogy or pentalogy of Fallot and re-establish a **biventricular circulation** with closure of ASD and VSD and correction of RVOT obstruction at all levels.
*   Reactive RVH cannot be repaired and is expected to regress once obstruction has been corrected.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 17 What are the contraindications for surgical repair of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

There is **no absolute contraindication** for surgical repair in patients with TOF, although there are some conditions that preclude a definitive repair:

*   **Extreme prematurity and low body weight** (<2.5–2.8 kg) are contraindications for primary intracardiac repair. In these cases, interventional options are indicated (RVOT balloon dilatation or stent placement, or ductal stenting).
*   Conditions that preclude cardiopulmonary bypass (**intracranial haemorrhage, active systemic infection**) are contraindications for surgery.
*   **Extremely small branch pulmonary arteries** and/or the presence of **MAPCAs** may be a contraindication for primary intracardiac repair. These patients may need staged operations.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 18 What are the surgical principles of Tetralogy of Fallot repair?</summary><div class="details-content">

The surgical principles of TOF repair are to achieve biventricular physiology by closing the VSD and releasing the RVOT obstruction below and at the level of the pulmonary valve.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Historical Evolution</summary><div class="details-content">

Repair techniques have evolved over time:
1.  Initial effort to provide adequate pulmonary blood flow (original **Blalock-Taussig shunt or Blalock-Taussig-Thomas shunt — BTS**), where the right subclavian artery was anastomosed to the right pulmonary artery.
2.  Interventions aimed at releasing RVOT obstruction via the RVOT followed by VSD closure.
3.  RVOT obstruction release via the right atrium and VSD closure.
4.  Staged strategy (BTS first, followed by repair at 1 year of age) generally involving a patch across the pulmonary valve annulus (creating severe pulmonary regurgitation).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Current Trends</summary><div class="details-content">

The current trend is to complete RVOT muscle resection and VSD closure **via the right atrium** to:
*   Minimise muscle injury and resulting ventricular scarring
*   Make every effort to **preserve the pulmonary valve** even when moderately stenotic (based on the notion that mild residual pulmonary stenosis is better than free pulmonary insufficiency in the long term) (Figure 18)

![](_page_17_Figure_7.jpeg)

**Figure 18. Echocardiographic subcostal views in a patient with tetralogy of Fallot demonstrating:** A) severe RVOT obstruction, large VSD, RV hypertrophy; B) flow acceleration across RVOT and shunting into aortic root on colour flow Doppler; C) post-surgery — RVOT wide open, VSD closed with patch; D) unobstructed flow across RVOT and no shunting into aortic root. MPA = main pulmonary artery; RA = right atrium; RV = right ventricle; Ao = aorta. *Images courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha, University Hospital Southampton, UK.*

There has also been a significant **paradigm shift** from staged repair (initial palliation with BTS and subsequent definitive repair) to **primary definitive repair** in patients at any age without contraindication. In most centres, initial palliation is performed infrequently, and most patients with TOF undergo primary repair.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Valve Management</summary><div class="details-content">

*   In cases of severe PS or near atresia where the pulmonary valve cannot be maintained, a **transannular patch (TAP)** is necessary to ensure adequate forward flow (Figure 19C).
*   In some cases, free pulmonary regurgitation can be mitigated by constructing a **single cusp valve (monocusp)** (Figure 20).
*   Patients with a TAP or TAP and monocusp valve will require **pulmonary valve implantation at a later stage**.
*   Following surgery, echocardiography is used to demonstrate a functioning pulmonary valve.

![](_page_19_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 19. Principles of primary surgical repair of tetralogy of Fallot:** A) division and resection of RVOT muscle bands (red arrow) via tricuspid valve (blue arrow) accessed through right atrium; B) repair of VSD with patch via same access; C) transannular patch (TAP) to augment annulus and RVOT if required; D) if pulmonary annulus size allows (green arrow), valve can be spared and patches used to augment areas above and below the valve.

![](_page_20_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 20. Echocardiographic images following pulmonary valve reconstruction demonstrating:** A) long-axis view of monocusp flap positioned between RV and MPA at pulmonary valve annulus (red arrow); B) short-axis view of monocusp (red arrow). RA = right atrium; AoV = aortic valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Ongoing Controversies</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Primary versus staged repair
*   Catheter-based palliation (RVOT or ductal stent) versus BTS
*   Transatrial transpulmonary artery approach versus transventricular approach
*   Indications, timing, and modality of pulmonary valve implantation in repaired patients with severe pulmonary regurgitation

For this reason, some maintain that **true surgical resolution of TOF is not really achievable** in conditions with more than mild-moderate PS, as these patients will require several reinterventions on the pulmonary valve, may require subsequent interventions for arrhythmias, and may suffer from progressive heart failure due to right ventricular dilatation.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 19 Describe a treatment algorithm for tetralogy of Fallot (Figure 21)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Asymptomatic Patients</summary><div class="details-content">

Electively operated on at **5–6 months** of age.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Earlier Repair Indications</summary><div class="details-content">

Indicated when arterial oxygen saturation levels fall **below 80%** or if the patient has a **cyanotic spell**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Neonates</summary><div class="details-content">

Most neonates with TOF are offered **primary repair** unless they have a contraindication:
*   **a)** Body weight less than **<2.5–2.8 kg**
*   **b)** Prematurity **<36 weeks**
*   **c)** Pathologic biventricular hypertrophy
*   **d)** Intracranial haemorrhage
*   **e)** Sepsis or infection
*   **f)** Significant end-organ dysfunction

Balloon dilatation and/or placement of an RVOT stent may be performed if feasible.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Post-Stent Patients</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients who have RVOT dilatation or stent placement undergo elective repair at **3–6 months** of age or earlier repair if arterial oxygen saturation levels fall **below 80%**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Policy on Blalock-Taussig Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

The policy is to **avoid placement of a Blalock-Taussig shunt** in the neonatal or early infantile period.

</div></details>

## 20 What are the interventional therapeutic options for tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### RVOT Stent Placement</summary><div class="details-content">

Stent placement to a stenotic RVOT (Figure 22) is an **excellent palliative option** for critical cyanotic neonates and small infants who are not suitable for primary repair and is an alternative to a Blalock-Taussig shunt (Figure 23).

![](_page_23_Picture_2.jpeg)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Balloon Dilatation</summary><div class="details-content">

In some selective patients with isolated pulmonary valvular stenosis, balloon dilatation of the pulmonary valve without stent placement may be enough to temporarily relieve RVOT obstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### PDA Stent</summary><div class="details-content">

Stent placement to the patent ductus arteriosus could also be an option to increase pulmonary blood flow if RVOT stenting is not sufficient.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Percutaneous Pulmonary Valve Implantation (PPVI)</summary><div class="details-content">

The vast majority of patients undergoing repair with a transannular patch will undergo pulmonary valve implantation at a later stage. A transvenous approach can be used via the femoral vein to deploy a stent-mounted tissue valve.

**Contraindications to this approach include:**
*   **a)** Pulmonary valve annulus or RVOT that is too wide
*   **b)** Highly pulsatile RVOT
*   **c)** Calcified, narrow conduit
*   **d)** Associated lesion that may require surgical intervention (significant pulmonary artery stenosis, tricuspid regurgitation, residual VSD)

All patients presenting with symptomatic pulmonary regurgitation following repair of TOF should be discussed in a **multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting** to establish the best strategy (surgical or interventional) for pulmonary valve replacement based on anatomy, functional status, and associated lesions.

</div></details>

## 21 What are the complications of surgical repair for tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Peri-operative mortality** in patients who undergo primary repair for acyanotic TOF ranges from **0–1%**.
*   **Hospital mortality** in symptomatic neonates or small infants who undergo primary repair ranges from **1–3%**.
*   **Complete heart block** associated with VSD closure occurs in approximately **1%** of patients undergoing surgical TOF repair, requiring permanent pacemaker implantation.
*   Up to **one third** of patients have a significant low cardiac output state due to **restrictive physiology** of the right ventricle.
*   **Residual lesions** (Figure 24, Figure 25) including residual RVOT obstruction, pulmonary artery stenosis, and tricuspid regurgitation can be significant and may or may not require surgical or catheter reintervention.

## 22 What are the long-term outcomes following surgical repair of tetralogy of Fallot?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Reported survival rates are approximately **85% at 30 years** following repair.
*   The risk of death is approximately **0.3% per year** in the first 30 years after repair, with the 40-year survival in the current era up to **90%** based on very low early mortality.
*   **Older age at operation** and a **high post-repair RV/LV systolic pressure ratio** are risk factors for late mortality.
*   Although there are mixed results regarding impact of a transannular patch on late survival, the **vast majority of those will require pulmonary valve replacement at a later stage**.
*   Common causes of death include **cardiac-related sudden death**, **congestive heart failure**, and **death related to reoperation**.
*   Approximately **80%** of patients are in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional **Class I**, and less than **10%** are in Class III at the median follow-up of 30 years, although the exercise capacity of these patients is markedly impaired.
*   The risk of requiring pulmonary valve replacement is constant and is approximately **0.8% per year for 30 years**.
*   Approximately **15–20%** of patients subsequently required reoperation (other than pulmonary valve replacement) later after TOF repair, including for:
    *   Residual atrial or ventricular septal defect
    *   Tricuspid valve regurgitation
    *   Residual RVOT obstruction or aneurysm formation
    *   Branch pulmonary artery stenosis (Figure 26)

![](_page_26_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 26. Computed tomography 3D reconstruction of the pulmonary trunk and branches following a staged tetralogy of Fallot repair demonstrating severe stenosis of the right pulmonary artery following the initial Blalock-Taussig shunt to the same vessel.**
