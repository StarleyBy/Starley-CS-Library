# Anomalies of Coronary Arteries and Coronary Fistulae

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Normal Anatomy
- **Coronary anomalies:** Any variation in origin, course, branching pattern, or termination of coronary arteries
- **Normal anatomy:** LMCA and RCA arise from perpendicular ostia at centre of left and right aortic sinuses, ~1 cm below STJ. LMCA → LAD (anterior interventricular groove) + Cx (left AV groove). RCA (right AV groove). PDA defines dominance: from RCA in 75–90%, from Cx in 10–25%

### Embryology
- First 3 weeks: diffusion from heart tube
- Week 3: subepicardial primary plexus forms (hypoxic gradients)
- Later: vessels coalesce, penetrate aortic root into correct sinuses, arterialise with pulsatile flow
- Balance of vasculogenesis, angiogenesis, apoptosis

### Classification (International Nomenclature)
| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Anomalous pulmonary origins | ALCAPA |
| Anomalous aortic origins | Abnormal ostium, incorrect sinus, single coronary |
| Congenital atresia | Left main atresia |
| Coronary arteriovenous fistula | — |
| Coronary bridging | Intramyocardial course |
| Coronary aneurysm | — |
| Coronary stenosis | — |

### Epidemiology
- Overall incidence: 0.2–1.6% of general population
- Most common: coronary fistulae (0.8–1%), AAOCA (0.2–0.8%), ALCAPA (1:30,000–1:300,000)

### Anomalous Left Coronary Artery from Pulmonary Artery (ALCAPA)
- **Also known as:** White-Bland-Garland syndrome
- **Most common cause of myocardial ischaemia in children; mortality up to 90% in infancy**
- **Mechanism:** Postnatal drop in PA pressure → decreased perfusion of anomalous LCA → ischaemia → LV dilatation/thinning, papillary muscle dysfunction, MR. Survivors develop RCA collaterals → retrograde flow into PA (L→R shunt)
- **Presentation (infancy):** Angina with feeding, sweating, dyspnoea, FTT, heart failure
- **ECG:** ST elevation, deep Q waves in lateral leads
- **CXR:** Cardiomegaly
- **Echo (gold standard):** Hypokinetic dilated LV, MR, abnormal coronary ostium in PA with retrograde flow
- **MRI/CT:** When echo inconclusive or for anatomical detail
- **Catheterisation:** Limited role
- **Treatment:** Direct reimplantation of anomalous coronary into aorta. **No role for medical treatment**
- **Takeuchi repair:** For unfavourable anatomy (coronary far from aorta). Intrapulmonary baffle between aorta and anomalous coronary. Associated with late PA stenosis, baffle leaks, MR
- **Myocardial protection:** Snare branch PAs immediately on CPB (prevent run-off); cardioplegia into aortic root then into LCA after PA transection; adequate LV venting

### Anomalous Aortic Origin of Coronary Artery (AAOCA)
- **Definition:** Congenital abnormality of origin/course of coronary arising from aorta
- **Types:** ARCA (RCA from left sinus), ALCA (LCA from right sinus)
- **Second most common cause of SCD in children/young adults**
- **Prevalence:** ~0.7%; ARCA more prevalent, ALCA higher risk
- **Pathophysiological mechanisms (6):** Intramural compression, slit-like ostium/stenosis, acute angulation, interarterial compression (between aorta and PA), kinking at myocardial anchor, coronary spasm
- **Presentation:** 30–50% symptomatic (syncope, chest pain, dyspnoea); SCD may be first presentation; many incidental
- **Imaging:**
  - Echo: identifies sinus of origin, rules out other anomalies (insufficient detail)
  - MRI: static evaluation without radiation; function, perfusion, viability
  - CT: best spatial resolution, defines interarterial/intramural segments, ostial anatomy, 3D reconstruction, virtual angioscopy
  - IVUS: increasingly used (adults) to determine intramural course
  - Angiography: limited; for suspected atherosclerosis; doesn't define intramural course
- **Highest risk:** ALCA; interarterial course with intramural segment; symptoms or aborted SCD
- **Exercise stress testing:** Abnormal test → intervention; **negative test cannot rule out future SCD**
- **Surgical options:** Unroofing of intramural segment (± aortic valve takedown), coronary translocation, neo-ostium creation, PA translocation, CABG
- **Unroofing technique:** Open aortic root, define intramural segment with probe, incise up to exit point, debride, tacking stitches if incision beyond aorta
- **Intramural behind commissure:** Cannot standard unroof. Options: neo-ostium on correct sinus beyond commissure or coronary translocation
- **CABG:** Reserved for older patients; not ideal (competitive flow → conduit thrombosis)
- **Exercise restriction:** Recommended for at-risk and pre-intervention patients (most deaths occur during exercise)

### Coronary Artery Fistula
- **Definition:** Communication between coronary artery and cardiac chamber (most commonly RV or RA) or PA
- **~2/3 congenital** (persistent intramyocardial sinusoids)
- **Presentation:** Third decade; angina, dyspnoea, arrhythmias, heart failure; continuous "machinery" murmur; ECG: ischaemia, arrhythmias, chamber dilatation; CXR: cardiomegaly, increased PVM if high-volume shunt
- **Imaging:** Colour Doppler (sensitive), MRI/CT (adjunct), **angiography gold standard** (defines location, haemodynamics, Qp:Qs, treatment potential)
- **Management:** Treat all symptomatic; asymptomatic controversial. Goal: close fistula without compromising coronary flow
- **Transcatheter:** Coil embolisation or device closure — most now treated this way
- **Surgical (unfavourable anatomy):** Extracardiac oversewing, transcardiac closure (CPB), aneurysmorrhaphy with transarterial closure, ligation with CABG

### Coronary Aneurysm
- **Definition:** Saccular or fusiform dilatation >1.5× normal vessel size
- **Classification:** Small <5 mm, medium 5–8 mm, giant >8 mm
- **Most common sites in children:** Proximal vessels (RCA, LMCA, proximal LAD)
- **Aetiology:** Destruction of musculoelastic components. Congenital (connective tissue disorders) or acquired. Adults: atherosclerosis. **Children: Kawasaki disease (most common)**
- **Kawasaki disease:** Vasculitis of capillaries/small arteries (mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome). Fever, lymphadenopathy, rash, mucocutaneous erythema. Untreated: 25% develop coronary aneurysms. With IVIG + aspirin: lower incidence. ~50% of small aneurysms regress spontaneously; 50% persist → stenosis, thrombosis
- **Diagnosis:** Echo first-line (proximal aneurysms). **Angiography gold standard** (defines thrombosis/stenosis)
- **Management:** Aspirin + warfarin to prevent thrombosis. CABG for ischaemia or significant proximal stenosis. **IMA patency 78% at 10 years** (vs 36% saphenous vein); in situ IMA grows with patient

## 1. Definition, Normal Anatomy, and Embryology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### What are Coronary Anomalies?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Coronary anomalies** represent any variation in the origin, course, branching pattern, or termination of the arteries that supply the heart.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Normal Anatomy of the Coronary Arteries (Figure 1)</summary><div class="details-content">

- Left main coronary artery (LMCA) and right coronary artery (RCA) arise separately from perpendicular ostia located at the centre of the left and right aortic sinuses of Valsalva, approximately **1 cm below the sinotubular junction**
- **LMCA** divides into:
  - Left anterior descending artery (LAD) — anterior interventricular groove
  - Circumflex artery (Cx) — left atrioventricular groove
- **RCA** travels on the right atrioventricular groove
- **Posterior descending artery (PDA)** — defines coronary dominance:
  - From RCA in **75–90%** (right dominant)
  - From Cx in **10–25%** (left dominant)
- Throughout their course, coronary arteries travel superficially on the epicardium and give branches to underlying myocardium

![](_page_1_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1.** Coronary artery system. SVC = superior vena cava; RAA = right atrial appendage; IVC = inferior vena cava; RCS = right coronary sinus; RCA = right coronary artery; LCS = left coronary sinus; LMCA = left main coronary artery; LAA = left atrial appendage; Cx = circumflex artery; LAD = left anterior descending artery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Embryological Development</summary><div class="details-content">

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| **Beginning of gestation** | Heart derives blood supply from direct diffusion from heart tube |
| **~3rd week** | Myocardium thickens; vascular structures develop on epicardial surface → coalesce into subepicardial primary plexus (driven by hypoxic gradients) |
| **Later development** | Vessels coalesce into strands that penetrate aortic root into correct sinuses; pulsatile flow prompts arterialisation of network |
| **Overall** | Balance between **vasculogenesis, angiogenesis, and apoptosis** |

</div></details>

## 3. Anomalous Left Coronary Artery from Pulmonary Artery (ALCAPA)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Definition (Figure 2)</summary><div class="details-content">

**ALCAPA** (also known as **White-Bland-Garland syndrome**) is a condition where the left main coronary artery originates from the pulmonary artery instead of the aorta.

- Most common location: left posterior sinus of pulmonary trunk (can be anywhere within PA)
- **Most common cause of myocardial ischaemia in children**
- **Mortality up to 90% in infancy** if untreated

![](_page_3_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.** ALCAPA. SVC = superior vena cava. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Mechanism of Ischaemia</summary><div class="details-content">

| Stage | Pathophysiology |
|---|---|
| **Foetal life** | PA and aortic pressures similar → anomalous LCA receives adequate blood supply (slightly less oxygenated) |
| **After birth** | PVR drops, ductus closes → PA pressure decreases → perfusion pressure of anomalous LCA drops |
| **Consequence** | Significant ischaemia → progressive LV dilatation and thinning, papillary muscle dysfunction, mitral regurgitation |
| **Survivors** | Develop significant collaterals from RCA → retrograde flow through anomalous LCA into low-pressure PA → significant L→R shunt |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Clinical Presentation (Figures 3–4)</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Majority present in infancy** with:
  - Angina associated with feeding (food aversion)
  - Sweating, dyspnoea
  - Failure to thrive
  - Signs of congestive heart failure
- **Late presenters** (survive initial insult): coronary ischaemic symptoms or sudden death
- **Auscultation:** Systolic murmur (mitral insufficiency)
- **ECG (Figure 3):** Signs of LV ischaemia, possible prior infarction (ST elevation, deep Q waves in lateral leads)
- **CXR (Figure 4):** Cardiomegaly

![](_page_4_Figure_9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 3.** ECG of 5-month-old with ALCAPA: normal sinus rhythm with ST elevation and deep Q waves in lateral leads. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

![](_page_5_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4.** CXR of 2-month-old girl with ALCAPA: significant cardiomegaly. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Imaging (Figures 5–8)</summary><div class="details-content">

| Modality | Findings |
|---|---|
| **Echocardiography (gold standard)** | Hypokinetic dilated LV, significant MR; **diagnosis confirmed by abnormal coronary ostium in PA with retrograde flow** (Figure 5) |
| **MRI** | Origin of LCA from PA, dilated RCA (Figure 6) — when echo inconclusive or additional anatomy needed |
| **CT with 3D reconstruction** | Pulmonary origin and course of anomalous coronary (Figure 7) |
| **Catheterisation** | Limited role; reserved for special situations (Figure 8) |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Treatment (Figures 9–10)</summary><div class="details-content">

**No role for medical treatment**

**Historical palliative procedures (no longer used):**
- Ligation of anomalous coronary
- PA banding
- Creation of aortopulmonary window

**Current optimal management (Figure 9):**
- **Direct reimplantation of anomalous coronary into aorta** (based on experience with coronary transfer for TGA)
- Most coronary patterns treatable with this technique

![](_page_8_Picture_6.jpeg)

LCA = left coronary artery; RCA = right coronary artery; SVC = superior vena cava. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

**Alternative techniques (unfavourable anatomy — coronary far from aorta):**
- Takeuchi procedure
- Reconstruction of proximal coronary with aortic and PA flaps
- Coronary artery bypass surgery

![](_page_9_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 10.** CT in ALCAPA: A) LMCA originating from lateral aspect of anterior pulmonary sinus; B) 3D reconstruction 5 years after LMCA translocation using arterial flap anterior to pulmonary trunk — both coronaries remain severely dilated. Ao = aorta; MPA = main pulmonary artery; RCA = right coronary artery; LAD = left anterior descending. *Reproduced with permission from Dr. James Shambrook.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Takeuchi Repair (Figure 11)</summary><div class="details-content">

- Used for unfavourable coronary anatomy precluding direct implantation
- **Intrapulmonary baffle** created between aorta and anomalous coronary using PA tissue
- **Complications:** Late development of main PA stenosis, baffle leaks, mitral regurgitation requiring reoperation

![](_page_10_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11.** Takeuchi repair. Anterior PA flap used to create intrapulmonary baffle between anomalous coronary ostium and aorta via small aortopulmonary window. Patch reconstructs main PA. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Myocardial Protection During Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

| Principle | Description |
|---|---|
| **Snare branch PAs** | Immediately after starting CPB to prevent run-off from anomalous LCA into PAs |
| **Cardioplegia** | Initially into aortic root; additional into LCA after PA transection |
| **Venting** | Adequate LV venting to avoid distension |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 4. Anomalous Aortic Origin of Coronary Artery (AAOCA)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Definition (Figure 12)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA)** is a congenital abnormality of the origin or course of a coronary artery that arises from the aorta.

- **Types:** ARCA (RCA from left coronary sinus), ALCA (LCA from right coronary sinus)
- **Second most common cause of sudden cardiac death in children and young adults**
- Highest risk: anomalous coronaries originating from **opposite aortic sinus** with **intramural course** within aortic wall
- **Prevalence:** ~0.7%
- ARCA more prevalent; ALCA higher risk

![](_page_11_Figure_5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 12.** AAOCA arising from opposite aortic sinus. Anomalous coronary originates from opposite sinus, close to other coronary. Not unusual for these vessels to travel within aortic wall (intramural) for variable course. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Pathophysiological Mechanisms for Sudden Cardiac Death (Figure 13)</summary><div class="details-content">

Most patients with AAOCA who suffer SCD do so **during or immediately after strenuous exercise** (dynamic/aerobic type: football, basketball, athletics, swimming) — associated with ↑ HR, ↑ CO, ↑ SBP, ↓ SVR, ↓ DBP.

**Proposed mechanisms (6):**

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| **Intramural compression** | Within aortic wall |
| **Slit-like ostium/stenosis** | Coronary insufficiency (Figure 13) |
| **Acute angulation** | Of anomalous coronary |
| **Interarterial compression** | Between aorta and PA as it passes between them |
| **Kinking** | As coronary anchors into myocardium |
| **Coronary spasm** | — |

![](_page_13_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13.** Intra-operative photo: Anomalous RCA from left coronary sinus, close to LCA origin (arrow). Slit-like ostium and long intramural segment (delimited by transmural suture) before exiting aorta. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

- **30–50% have symptoms:** Syncope, chest pain, dyspnoea
- **SCD or aborted SCD** may be first presentation
- Increasing number found incidentally on echocardiography or other imaging
- Some diagnosed through screening programs

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Imaging Modalities (Figures 14–15)</summary><div class="details-content">

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **Echocardiography** | Identifies sinus of origin; **main use: rule out other intracardiac abnormalities** (insufficient detail for AAOCA) |
| **MRI** | Static evaluation of origin/course without ionising radiation; also myocardial function, perfusion, viability, other anomalies (Figure 14) |
| **CT** | **Best spatial resolution**; defines interarterial and intramural segments, ostial anatomy; 3D reconstruction, virtual angioscopy (Figure 15) |
| **Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS)** | Increasingly used (mainly adults) to determine intramural course |
| **Coronary angiography** | Reserved for suspected atherosclerosis; provides some ostial info but **does not define intramural course** |

![](_page_14_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 14.** MRI: Anomalous RCA (arrow) originating from left coronary sinus. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

![](_page_15_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15.** CT in AAOCA: A,B) Anomalous RCA (arrow) from left coronary sinus next to LCA origin (arrowhead); C) Intramural segment; D) 3D reconstruction with virtual angioscopy defining left (arrowhead) and right (arrow) coronary ostia and dynamic changes across cardiac cycle. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Risk Factors for Sudden Cardiac Death</summary><div class="details-content">

- **ALCA** higher risk than ARCA
- **Interarterial course + intramural segment** (from incorrect sinus) — believed highest risk
- **Symptoms or aborted SCD** — probably higher risk

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Exercise Stress Testing</summary><div class="details-content">

- Role not well defined
- Some patients with normal stress test developed symptoms or SCD within 6 months
- **Abnormal test → intervention**
- **Negative test cannot predict absence of future SCD**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Surgical Options</summary><div class="details-content">

| Option | Indication |
|---|---|
| **Unroofing of intramural segment** (± aortic valve takedown) | Most common |
| **Coronary translocation** | No/short intramural segment |
| **Neo-ostium creation** | Intramural behind commissure |
| **Pulmonary artery translocation** | Single coronary, interarterial compression |
| **Coronary artery bypass surgery** | Very limited, older patients |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Unroofing Technique (Figures 16–17)</summary><div class="details-content">

1. Open aortic root, inspect coronary ostia
2. Define intramural segment with coronary probe
3. Dissect anomalous coronary outside aorta; place transmural aortic suture at exit site to define length/direction
4. Incise intramural segment up to point where coronary exits aorta
5. Debride intramural segment → large ostium without intramural portion
6. Tacking stitches on unroofed segment (especially if incision taken beyond intramural coronary outside aorta)

![](_page_17_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 16.** Unroofing procedure: A) Anomalous RCA from left aortic sinus with slit-like orifice and long intramural segment; B) Intramural segment incised; C) Debrided unroofed tissue creating large ostium without residual intramural segment. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

![](_page_17_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 17.** Operative image: Unroofed anomalous RCA. Slit-like ostium and long intramural segment unroofed; edges tacked with interrupted sutures. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Intramural Segment Behind Commissure (Figures 18–19)</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Standard unroofing cannot be performed**
- Some authors: take down commissure, unroof, reattach aortic valve — **may injure aortic valve** (reports of late dysfunction)
- **Neo-ostium creation:** If long intramural segment on correct aortic sinus beyond commissure (Figure 18)
- **Coronary translocation:** Divide vessel after it exits aortic wall, reimplant on correct sinus (Figure 19)

![](_page_18_Picture_10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 18.** Neo-ostium creation: A) Coronary probe defines intramural segment; B) Separate orifice made on correct aortic sinus beyond commissure. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

![](_page_19_Picture_3.jpeg)

![](_page_19_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19.** Coronary translocation: Anomalous coronary divided as it exits aortic wall and anastomosed to aorta on correct sinus. Care to avoid distortion/kinking. *Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Other Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

| Technique | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Coronary translocation** | Also for no/short intramural segment |
| **PA translocation** | Lateral or anterior to prevent interarterial compression (especially single coronary) |
| **CABG** | Very limited, older patients. **Not ideal** — anomalous coronary has normal flow most of time → competitive flow → conduit thrombosis |
| **Coronary stenting** | Of intramural segment in older patients |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Exercise Restriction</summary><div class="details-content">

- Not clear if exercise restriction reduces SCD risk
- Most deaths from AAOCA occur during exercise → most authors support restriction
- **Recommend restriction** for at-risk patients and those awaiting surgical intervention

</div></details>

## 5. Coronary Artery Fistula</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Definition</summary><div class="details-content">

An **arteriovenous coronary fistula** is a communication between a main coronary artery or one of its branches and:
- A cardiac chamber (most commonly **right ventricle** or **right atrium**)
- The pulmonary artery

- **~2/3 of coronary fistulae are congenital** — believed related to persistence of intramyocardial sinusoids

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Pathophysiology** | Steal phenomenon (myocardial ischaemia) + L→R shunt (LV volume load) |
| **Typical age** | Third decade |
| **Symptoms** | Angina, dyspnoea, arrhythmias, congestive heart failure |
| **Auscultation** | **Continuous "machinery" murmur** |
| **ECG** | Ischaemic changes, arrhythmias, chamber dilatation |
| **CXR** | Cardiomegaly, increased pulmonary vascular markings (high-volume shunts) |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Imaging (Figure 20)</summary><div class="details-content">

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **Colour flow Doppler echocardiography** | Very sensitive |
| **MRI/CT** | Adjuvant — define anatomy |
| **Coronary angiography** | **Gold standard** — defines exact location, haemodynamics, degree of L→R shunting, treatment potential |

![](_page_21_Picture_3.jpeg)

![](_page_21_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20.** Catheterisation in 5-week-old girl: Severely enlarged LCA with fistula (4 branches) draining into RVOT. Angiographic blush = drainage into RV. Qp:Qs = 2.4:1. *Images courtesy of Dr. Dhaval Parekh. Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Management (Figure 21)</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Symptomatic patients:** Treatment indicated
- **Asymptomatic patients:** Controversial

**Goal:** Close fistula **without compromising coronary blood flow**

**Pre-intervention:** Define fistula details and relationship to coronary artery and cardiac chambers

**Treatment Options:**

| Approach | Description |
|---|---|
| **Transcatheter (most common)** | Coil embolisation or device closure (Figure 21) |
| **Surgical (unfavourable anatomy)** | Extracardiac oversewing; transcardiac closure (CPB); coronary aneurysmorrhaphy with transarterial closure; ligation with CABG |

![](_page_22_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 21.** Post-coiling occlusion of coronary fistula (same patient as Figure 20). *Images courtesy of Dr. Dhaval Parekh. Reproduced with permission from Texas Children's Hospital, © 2013.*

## 6. Coronary Aneurysm</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Definition and Classification</summary><div class="details-content">

**Coronary aneurysm:** Saccular or fusiform dilatation of a coronary artery **>1.5× normal size**

| Size | Classification |
|---|---|
| <5 mm | Small |
| 5–8 mm | Medium |
| >8 mm | Giant |

**Most common sites in children:** Proximal vessels (RCA, LMCA, proximal LAD)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Aetiology</summary><div class="details-content">

- Caused by destruction of **musculoelastic components** of coronary artery wall
- **Congenital:** Associated with connective tissue disorders
- **Acquired (most):**
  - Adults: atherosclerosis
  - **Children: Kawasaki disease (most common)**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Kawasaki Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|
| **Also known as** | Mucocutaneous lymph node syndrome |
| **Pathology** | Vasculitis of capillaries and small arteries |
| **Presentation** | Fever, lymphadenopathy, polymorphic exanthem, mucocutaneous erythema |
| **Cause** | Unknown (may be partially infectious) |
| **Coronary aneurysms** | Up to **25%** if untreated with IVIG + aspirin |
| **Spontaneous regression** | ~50% (especially small aneurysms) |
| **Persistent changes** | 50% → may advance to stenosis and aneurysm thrombosis |

</div></details>

**Clinical presentation:** Angina, myocardial infarction, or heart failure (from thrombosis or stenosis); many asymptomatic (found on follow-up imaging for Kawasaki)
