# Congenitally Corrected Transposition of the Great Arteries

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Anatomy
*   **ccTGA** is a complex congenital heart defect characterized by **both atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial discordance**. Systemic venous blood returns to the morphologic right atrium → mitral valve → morphologic left ventricle (LV) → pulmonary artery. Pulmonary venous blood returns to the morphologic left atrium → tricuspid valve → morphologic right ventricle (RV) → aorta. This results in a "corrected" and physiologically normal circulation.
*   **Segmental anatomy:** In 90% of cases, situs solitus (S,L,L); in 10%, situs inversus with dextrocardia (I,D,D).
*   **Associated anomalies** (98%): VSD (75%), pulmonary stenosis/atresia (50%), tricuspid valve abnormalities (Ebsteinoid, 50%), spontaneous heart block.

### Pathophysiology
*   The primary concern is the **morphologic right ventricle as the systemic ventricle** and the **tricuspid valve as the systemic AV valve**, which fail over time.
*   Tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is worsened by **septal shift toward the LV**. This can be reversed with pulmonary artery banding.
*   **LVOTO** (pulmonary stenosis/atresia) → early cyanosis. Large VSD or significant TR → heart failure.
*   Spontaneous **complete heart block** occurs at ~2% per year after diagnosis.

### Diagnosis
*   **Chest X-ray:** Parallel great vessels, straight left heart border, absent pulmonary knuckle.
*   **ECG:** Reversal of normal Q-wave pattern — Q waves in right precordial leads, absent in left precordial leads. Complete heart block (including neonatal period).
*   **Echocardiography** is the mainstay. Assessment includes: sequential segmental analysis, AV valve anatomy/function (especially systemic), LVOTO, VSD size/location, straddling.

### Management Principles
*   **Surgical indications:** Symptomatic patients; asymptomatic patients with declining RV function and worsening TR.
*   **Physiologic repair** (traditional): RV remains systemic. VSD closure, TV repair/replacement, LV→PA conduit for LVOTO. Survival: 77% at 5 years, 48% at 20 years. Late death from systemic ventricular failure in 50%.
*   **Anatomic repair** (contemporary): LV becomes systemic.
    *   Double-switch (atrial switch + arterial switch) — no LVOTO.
    *   Atrial switch + Rastelli — VSD + LVOTO.
    *   Early mortality ~6%, 9-year survival >90%.
*   **LV training** (PA banding) for intact septum: achieve LV pressure ≥80% systemic.

### Neonatal Palliative Interventions
*   **Modified BT shunt** — for pulmonary atresia/severe stenosis.
*   **PA banding** — for large VSD with pulmonary overcirculation.
*   **Dual-chamber pacemaker** — for congenital complete heart block with poor ventricular function.

### Late Complications After Anatomic Repair
*   RV→PA conduit dysfunction, systemic or pulmonary venous pathway obstruction, coronary obstruction/kinking, heart block, neo-aortic insufficiency, systemic LV failure (up to 25%).

## 1. Definition and Segmental Anatomy of ccTGA</summary><div class="details-content">

#### What is ccTGA?

Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (ccTGA) is a complex congenital heart defect characterized by both **atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial discordance**, in which:

- **Systemic venous blood** returns to the morphologic right atrium, which connects via a **mitral valve** to the morphologic **left ventricle (LV)**, and the LV connects to the **pulmonary artery**.
- **Pulmonary venous blood** returns to the morphologic left atrium, which connects via a **tricuspid valve** to the morphologic **right ventricle (RV)**, and the RV connects to the **aorta**.

This results in a "corrected" and physiologically "normal" circulation that remains in series (Figure 1). ccTGA represents less than 1% of all congenital cardiac lesions.

B) Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries with atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial discordance. Ao = aorta; PA = pulmonary artery; RA = right atrium; LA = left atrium; LV = left ventricle; RV = right ventricle.

#### Segmental Anatomy

**Situs solitus** is present in 90% of cases with ccTGA (Figure 2), where the segmental anatomy is **S,L,L**:
- a) situs solitus (S)
- b) ventricular loop left (L)
- c) position of the aorta to the left (L)

## 2. Associated Congenital Defects</summary><div class="details-content">

Associated anomalies (often more than one) are present in **98%** of cases of ccTGA and are the major determining factors in symptom progression and surgical management. They include:

- **Ventricular septal defect (VSD)** — 75%
- **Pulmonary stenosis or atresia**, including subpulmonary stenosis — 50% (Figure 4)
- **Tricuspid valve abnormalities**, often in the spectrum of Ebstein's malformation (Ebsteinoid valve) — 50% (Figure 5)
- **Straddling** of the mitral or tricuspid valve across the VSD
- **Spontaneous heart block**

## 3. Location of the Conduction System</summary><div class="details-content">

In **situs solitus**, the conduction system is abnormally located due to the looping pattern (Figure 6):
- The **AV node** is displaced superiorly and located anteriorly on the atrial septum, near the junction of the superior limbus of the atrial septum and the mitral valve.
- The **bundle of His** leaves the AV node and travels anteriorly toward the pulmonary valve. The bundle then travels in the **subendocardium of the morphologic LV**.

![](_page_4_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 6.** Conduction system in ccTGA: A) Right atriotomy view of the exposed mitral valve, which can be retracted to reveal the VSD (usually perimembranous). The AV node is displaced clockwise within the triangle of Koch, with the bundle of His passing anterior to the pulmonary annulus and along the morphologic LV side of the crest of the ventricular septum. B) The bundle of His courses in a superoinferior trajectory along the leftward margin of the VSD. RA = right atrium; PA = pulmonary artery; Ao = aorta; LA = left atrium; RV = right ventricle; LV = left ventricle.

In **situs inversus**, the anatomy of the conduction system is that of a normal heart but with a mirror-image pattern.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 4. Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

In the absence of associated anomalies, the physiology in ccTGA is normal, but the primary concern stems from the identity of the **morphologic right ventricle as the systemic ventricle** and the **tricuspid valve as the systemic atrioventricular valve**. Morbidity and mortality result from long-term failure of this systemic RV and/or tricuspid regurgitation (TR).

**TR** may be secondary to anatomic malformation (Ebsteinoid) or progressive annular dilatation associated with RV failure.

A major contributing factor to the malfunction of the systemic valve and ventricle is the **shift of the ventricular septum toward the morphologic LV** with progressive failure and dilatation. This causes the septal leaflet to splay open the tricuspid valve. This process can be reversed in some cases with **pulmonary artery banding**, which pressurizes the LV and shifts the septum back toward a midline position, thereby improving leaflet coaptation.

Patients with **left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO)** from pulmonary stenosis or pulmonary atresia present early in life with **cyanosis**.

Patients with significant TR or a large VSD present with **heart failure**.

**Bradycardia** secondary to spontaneous heart block contributes to heart failure symptoms. The incidence of heart block is approximately **2% per year** following diagnosis of ccTGA.

## 5. Diagnostic Findings</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Chest Radiography

- Reveals **parallel great vessels**.
- The **upper left heart border** is formed by the aorta and appears straight; the **pulmonary artery knuckle is absent** because of the rightward, posterior displacement of the artery (Figure 7).

![](_page_6_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7.** Chest radiograph in ccTGA demonstrating a straight left border with an absent pulmonary knuckle.

#### Electrocardiography (ECG)

ECG is affected by associated cardiac anomalies but commonly shows:
- **Atrioventricular block**
- **Atrial arrhythmias**
- **Abnormal initial ventricular activation** due to abnormal conduction system anatomy

**Ventricular inversion** (bundle branches are inverted, initial activation oriented from right to left) is identified by **reversal of the normal Q-wave pattern** in the precordial leads: Q waves are present in the **right precordial leads** but absent in the **left precordial leads** (Figure 8).

**Complete AV block** is not uncommon, including in the neonatal or infancy period (Figure 9).

![](_page_7_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8.** ECG in a child with ccTGA demonstrating Q waves in the right precordial leads (V2, V3R, and V4R).

![](_page_7_Figure_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9.** ECG of a neonate with ccTGA demonstrating complete heart block.

#### Echocardiography

Echocardiography is the **mainstay of diagnosis** (Figure 10) and allows complete assessment of the lesion and its associated malformations, informing surgical planning and long-term follow-up.

![](_page_8_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 10.** Transthoracic echocardiographic image in ccTGA demonstrating atrioventricular discordance.

**The assessment should include:**
- a) Standard **sequential segmental analysis** of anatomy: situs, ventricular looping, and position of the great vessels
- b) Anatomy and function of the **atrioventricular valves**, especially in the systemic position
- c) Identification of any **LVOTO**
- d) Size and location of any **VSD** (Figure 11)
- e) Presence of **straddling** of the atrioventricular valves, if a VSD is seen

![](_page_9_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11.** Transthoracic echocardiographic image in ccTGA demonstrating a moderate-sized mid-muscular VSD with a shunt from the left-sided RV to the right-sided LV.

## 6. Management Principles and Surgical Options</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Management Principles

Surgical intervention is reserved for:
- **Symptomatic patients**
- **Asymptomatic patients** with evidence of declining RV function and worsening tricuspid regurgitation (Figure 12)

Intervention in asymptomatic patients with preserved RV function and tricuspid valve function without any septal defects is **controversial**, as some patients with ccTGA have a favorable prognosis and surgical intervention carries risks and long-term issues.

![](_page_10_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 12.** Management algorithm for patients with ccTGA. *Adapted with permission from Kumar TKS. Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries. J Thorac Dis 2020; 12.*

#### Surgical Options

**Physiologic repair** (traditional approach) leaves the RV as the systemic ventricle and the TV as the systemic AV valve but addresses associated anomalies: VSD closure, tricuspid valve repair or replacement, and placement of an LV→PA conduit to bypass LVOTO. This approach is associated with significant long-term morbidity and mortality.

**Anatomic repair** — more commonly used in contemporary practice — involves restoring the LV as the systemic ventricle and the mitral valve as the systemic AV valve, using either:
- **Double-switch operation** (combined atrial and arterial switch) — in the absence of LVOTO
- **Atrial switch combined with a Rastelli operation** — in the presence of VSD and LVOTO

## 7. Neonatal Palliative Interventions</summary><div class="details-content">

## 8. Surgical Techniques (Details)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### VSD Closure (Figure 16)

- After distal ascending aortic and bicaval cannulation with cardioplegic arrest, a standard **right atriotomy** is performed, exposing the VSD by retraction of the mitral valve leaflets.
- It is important to avoid the **conduction tissue**, which is located along the **anterior and superior margin** of the defect in the subendocardium of the morphologic LV.
- Therefore, an **autologous pericardial patch** is implanted with the **superior sutures** placed on the **RV side** of the ventricular septum and the **inferior sutures** placed on the **LV side** of the ventricular septum.

![](_page_13_Picture_6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 16.** Suture placement for VSD closure in ccTGA. To avoid the conduction system (red dot and dotted line), located at the superior aspect of the VSD, the superior sutures are placed on the RV side of the ventricular septum and the inferior sutures on the LV side. PA = pulmonary artery; Ao = aorta; AV node = atrioventricular node.

#### LVOT Repair (Figure 17)

- The level of obstruction may be valvular, subvalvular, or both, but it is usually **not repairable** and requires bypass with a conduit.
- A **ventriculotomy** is performed on the morphologic LV, taking care not to injure the coronary arteries or the mitral apparatus. The VSD is closed with a patch, followed by implantation of an **LV→PA conduit**.
- The VSD can also be accessed via an atrial approach.

![](_page_14_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 17.** Physiologic repair of ccTGA with LVOTO: A) Following an anterior left ventriculotomy, the VSD is closed with a patch using sutures placed on the morphologic RV side of the septum in the area of conduction tissue to avoid heart block. B) A valved conduit is then implanted between the LV and the PA while the native pulmonary valve is oversewn. When an allograft is used, a patch extension (gusset) is frequently used for better fit. Ao = aorta; RA = right atrium.

#### Tricuspid Valve Surgery

- This is analogous to performing a mitral valve procedure in a normal heart: the **interatrial groove** is widely dissected, and the **left atrium** is entered.
- In some patients, exposure may be limited due to the looping, necessitating **transection of the superior vena cava**.
- Although **valvuloplasty** may be an option, repair of an Ebsteinoid valve can be very difficult, and **replacement** is usually required.

#### Left Ventricular Training (LV Training)

- Anatomic repair places the LV as the systemic ventricle.
- Patients with LVOTO or a large VSD are **very good candidates** for this approach, as the LV is already exposed to systemic pressure and prepared to handle systemic circulation after the operation.
- However, patients with an **intact septum** require a period of LV training prior to anatomic repair to develop LV hypertrophy.
- This is accomplished by placing a **pulmonary artery band**, with the goal of achieving at least **50% of systemic pressure in the LV** at the time of band placement. This is often associated with a shift of the septum toward the morphologic RV, which can improve often-present TR by allowing better leaflet coaptation.
- **Repeat PA band tightening** is required until LV pressure equals or exceeds **80% of systemic pressure**. At this time, the LV is adequately prepared to sustain an anatomic repair and handle the systemic circulation.

## 9. Anatomic Repair: Double-Switch and Senning/Rastelli</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Surgical Options for the Atrial Switch Component of the Double-Switch Operation

- **Senning procedure** — the preferred choice of many surgeons
- **Mustard procedure**
- **Hemi-Mustard/Glenn procedure** — eliminates superior vena caval baffle obstruction but limits future placement of a transvenous pacemaker

#### Principles of Performing a Double-Switch (Senning + Arterial Switch) Operation (Figures 18, 19)

- Although this can be performed in a neonate, it is usually deferred to months of age due to the complexity of the operation.
- Following bicaval cannulation (with distal SVC cannulation), moderate hypothermia, and cardioplegic arrest, **autologous pericardium** is harvested and treated with glutaraldehyde for later use.
- **Senning atrial switch procedure:** The interatrial groove is widely developed, followed by right and left atriotomy. The atrial septum is developed into a flap and sutured as a **baffle** to redirect pulmonary venous blood (Figure 18).
- The venous pathway is completed by folding the lateral right atrial tissue over the caval orifices, allowing caval blood to flow across the mitral valve and pulmonary venous blood to flow across the tricuspid valve.
- **Patch augmentation** of the atriotomy is performed to complete the pulmonary venous pathway.
- The **arterial switch** is performed in a standard manner as employed for dextro-transposition of the great arteries (Figure 19). Neopulmonary sinuses are constructed using the previously harvested pericardium.

![](_page_16_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 18.** Senning procedure in ccTGA: A) A right atriotomy is performed. B) The atrial septum is mobilized on a lateral (posterior) pedicle. C) The atrial septum is sutured to the floor of the left atrium around the pulmonary veins, and then a longitudinal left atriotomy is made. D) The posterior edge of the right atriotomy is folded into the right atrium and sewn along the inner atrial wall and along the divided edge of the atrial septum. This creates the systemic venous pathway. The pulmonary venous pathway is augmented using a patch sewn posteriorly to the lateral edge of the left atriotomy. E) The patch is then sewn along the outer wall of the right atrium to connect with the anterior edge of the original right atriotomy.

![](_page_17_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19.** Arterial switch procedure in the anatomic repair of ccTGA: A) The pulmonary artery is transected at its bifurcation and the aorta is divided just above the origin of the coronary arteries. Note the morphologic left coronary artery (perfuses the morphologic RV) arising from the right-facing sinus and the morphologic right coronary artery (perfuses the morphologic LV) from the left. B) The Lecompte maneuver is performed and the neo-aortic anastomosis completed. C) Coronary buttons are mobilized for transfer. D) Coronary buttons are transferred to the respective sinuses in the neo-aorta. E) Anastomosis of the coronary button to the neo-aortic root. F) A trapdoor technique using a pericardial patch if tension or distortion is likely. G) Neopulmonary sinuses are reconstructed using an autologous pericardial patch. H) To avoid distortion, the orifice of the pulmonary bifurcation is shifted to the left with the right lateral surface augmented with a patch.

#### Principles of the Senning/Rastelli Procedure (Figure 20)

- This is performed for patients with significant **unresectable LVOTO** or pulmonary atresia, where the arterial switch operation is not an option.
- In patients with significant cyanosis, an initial palliative shunt procedure is performed in the early postnatal period.
- Definitive surgical correction is usually performed at months of age.
- The **Senning procedure** is performed as previously described.
- A **right ventriculotomy** is performed, and the VSD is patched with a **baffle** to redirect blood from the LV to the aorta.
- An **RV→PA conduit** is then placed (Figure 20).

![](_page_18_Picture_9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20.** Rastelli procedure in the anatomic repair of ccTGA: A) A right ventriculotomy is performed, and an intraventricular baffle is created using a patch connecting the VSD with the aorta (red asterisk). B) A valved conduit is implanted between the right ventriculotomy and the pulmonary artery bifurcation. PA = pulmonary artery; Ao = aorta; RV = right ventricle; RA = right atrium; LV = left ventricle.

## 10. Outcomes and Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Outcomes Following Traditional Physiologic Repair

- Although historically the mortality was as high as 26%, currently it is reported as **3%**.
- Overall survival: approximately **77% at 5 years**, **67% at 10 years**, and **48% at 20 years**.
- Recurrence of tricuspid regurgitation after repair is about **50%**.
- The incidence of post-surgical heart block is **20%**.
- Systemic ventricular failure accounts for about **50% of late deaths**.

#### Outcomes Following Anatomic Repair

- Early mortality following anatomic repair is approximately **6%**, with **9-year survival >90%**.
- LV retraining is required in about **31%** of patients.
- Tricuspid regurgitation, when present, significantly improves after anatomic repair.

#### Late Complications Following Anatomic Repair

- **RV→PA conduit dysfunction**, requiring replacement
- **Systemic venous pathway obstruction** (Figure 21)

- **Pulmonary venous pathway obstruction** (Figure 22)

- **Coronary obstruction and kinking**
- **Heart block**
- **Neo-aortic valve insufficiency**
- **Failure of the systemic LV** (in up to 25% of patients)
