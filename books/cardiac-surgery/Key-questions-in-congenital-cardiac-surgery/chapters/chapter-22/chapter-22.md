# Pulmonary Atresia with Ventricular Septal Defect

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Epidemiology
*   **Pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect (PA-VSD):** A complex, cyanotic, highly heterogeneous malformation characterised by underdevelopment of the RVOT (subpulmonary infundibulum) with atresia of the pulmonary valve, a large VSD, and RV hypertrophy (Figure 1).
*   **Incidence:** ~2.5% of all congenital heart defects; ~10 per 100,000 live births. Slightly more prevalent in males.
*   **Distinction from PA-IVS:** In PA-VSD, pulmonary atresia occurs early (after conotruncal partitioning but before VSD closure); RV and TV are well-developed. In PA-IVS, atresia occurs later (after cardiac septation); RV and TV are hypoplastic with possible coronary sinusoids.

### Classification (by source of pulmonary blood flow)
*   **Type A:** Intrapericardial pulmonary arteries present; MAPCAs absent; pulmonary blood flow via patent ductus arteriosus (duct-dependent).
*   **Type B:** Both intrapericardial pulmonary arteries and MAPCAs present; flow from both ductus and MAPCAs.
*   **Type C:** Intrapericardial pulmonary arteries absent; pulmonary blood flow supplied only by MAPCAs.
*   Further categorised by presence/absence of confluent RPA/LPA and presence/absence of main pulmonary trunk.

### Major Aortopulmonary Collateral Arteries (MAPCAs)
*   Primitive intersegmental channels with muscular media that failed to involute during development.
*   Most common origin: descending thoracic aorta at carina level (also subclavian, internal thoracic, intercostal, abdominal aorta, rarely coronary artery).
*   Tortuous course; may pass anterior or posterior to oesophagus, tracheobronchial tree, and aorta.
*   Highly variable segmental distribution; associated with abnormal peripheral arborisation.

### Associated Anomalies and Syndromes
*   **Intracardiac:** DORV, TGA, complete AVSD.
*   **Extracardiac:** In 25% of patients.
*   **Syndromes:** 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge, velocardiofacial), VACTERL, Alagille, trisomy 21.

### Pathophysiology
*   Large VSD → complete mixing, cyanosis, equalisation of RV/LV pressures, compensatory RV hypertrophy.
*   **Type A (duct-dependent):** Ductus closure → hypoxia, cardiovascular collapse.
*   **Types B/C (MAPCA-dependent):** Abnormal, precarious flow; simultaneous double supply, over-circulation, and hypoperfusion of different lung segments; progressive stenosis or pulmonary vascular occlusive disease → lung segment attrition.

### Natural History
*   Unrepaired: poor prognosis; greatest attrition in infancy; survival into adult life rare.
*   Historical surgical outcomes: survival 60% at 1 year, 40% at 10 years, 6% at 35 years.
*   Long-term survivors have balanced MAPCA flow (enough to reduce hypoxia, restricted enough to limit pulmonary hypertension) but have functional limitation, polycythaemia, prone to infection and pulmonary haemorrhage.

### Symptoms
*   ~2/3 develop symptoms during infancy.
*   **Presentation modes:** Cyanosis (50%), heart failure (25%), incidental murmur with mild cyanosis/failure to thrive (25%).
*   **Type A neonates:** Acute cyanosis, hypoxia, acidosis, shock as ductus closes.
*   **Types B/C infants:** Increasing cyanosis (MAPCA stenosis or late ductal closure) or heart failure (fall in PVR with increased flow).
*   **Children:** Incidental murmur, mild cyanosis, heart failure, failure to thrive.
*   **Adults (rare):** Ubiquitously cyanotic with dyspnoea and impaired NYHA class.

### Clinical Signs
*   **Ductal closure (acute):** Shock, marked cyanosis unresponsive to O₂, tachypnoea, tachycardia, preserved peripheral pulses.
*   **Decreased pulmonary flow:** Progressive cyanosis → clubbing, polycythaemia.
*   **Increased pulmonary flow:** Heart failure signs (respiratory distress, tachypnoea, tachycardia, hepatomegaly, bounding pulse).
*   Single loud S2 (absent pulmonary component).
*   Continuous bruit (turbulent flow through MAPCAs or ductus).

### ECG
*   Right axis deviation (differentiates from tricuspid atresia — left superior axis).
*   RV hypertrophy (tall R wave >10 mm in V1; differentiates from PA-IVS where RV is diminutive) (Figure 5).
*   Left atrial enlargement and biventricular hypertrophy (increased pulmonary venous return).

### Radiology
*   **CXR:** Boot-shaped heart (small/absent LPA → left heart border concavity; RV hypertrophy → uplifted apex) (Figure 6); right aortic arch (25–50%); pulmonary vascular markings decreased or increased (reticular pattern with MAPCAs).
*   **CT/MRI:** Emerging alternatives to catheterisation — define RVOT, MPA, branch PAs and stenoses, ductus, MAPCAs (number, location, course); functional assessment (MRI) of % flow to each lung; 3D reconstruction for operative planning (Figure 7).

### Echocardiography
*   Key for intracardiac anatomy; limited for pulmonary circulation.
*   Demonstrates: PA with absent flow between RVOT and pulmonary trunk (Figure 8); variable hypoplasia of subpulmonary infundibulum and MPA.
*   **Information provided:**
    *   VSD type (large/unrestrictive, malalignment, perimembranous with outlet extension — most common)
    *   Anterior deviation of infundibular septum ± aortic override
    *   RV hypertrophy and cavity size
    *   Interatrial communication (secundum ASD, PFO in 50%)
    *   Right aortic arch and mirror branching
    *   Coronary anatomy (usually normal; rarely anomalous LAD from RCA crossing RVOT)
    *   Intrapericardial PA anatomy (presence/absence of branching, confluent/discontinuous, size/stenoses)
    *   Colour Doppler flow direction in central PAs: antegrade (from ductus) vs retrograde (from MAPCAs)

### Cardiac Catheterisation
*   Key for delineating pulmonary circulation (Figure 9).
*   **Intrapericardial PAs:** Presence of MPA, confluence ('seagull' appearance), vessel size/side disparity, number of lung segments supplied, distal stenoses. Retrograde pulmonary vein wedge injection may be needed for hypoplastic PAs.
*   **MAPCAs (Figure 10):** Number, size, origin, lung segments supplied, proximal stenoses. Elevated distal pressure (mean >20–25 mmHg) suggests pulmonary vascular occlusive disease.
*   Also delineates coronary anatomy, PVR, transpulmonary gradient. RV pressure invariably systemic (unrestrictive VSD).

### Medical Management
*   **Duct-dependent (Type A):** PGE₁ to maintain ductal patency until surgery.
*   **Acute shock after ductal closure:** Immediate resuscitation ± ventilation.
*   **Heart failure (excessive pulmonary flow):** Diuretics and ACE inhibitors.

### Principles of Surgical Repair
*   **Primary aim:** Improve on predicted natural history.
*   **Ideal outcome:** Septated biventricular circulation with low PVR and low RV pressures.
*   **Key goals:**
    *   Increase pulmonary blood flow, O₂ saturation, encourage PA growth (systemic-PA shunt, RV-PA conduit, aortopulmonary window)
    *   Unifocalisation of MAPCAs (connect as many lung segments as possible to central PAs) (Figure 11)
    *   Secure long-term RV-to-pulmonary tree source (valved conduit, preferably oversized)
    *   Close VSD (only if low PVR guaranteed; otherwise leave open to avoid RV failure)
*   **Controversy:** Different centres pursue radically different strategies; long-term outcome data awaited.

### Surgical Options by Type

**Type A (no MAPCAs) — sternotomy:**
*   **Single-stage complete repair:** Tetralogy-type repair ± PA interventions + RV-PA valved conduit + VSD closure (REV-type repair possible).
*   **Staged repair:** Initial systemic-PA shunt → later take-down + RV-PA conduit + VSD closure.

**Types B/C (with MAPCAs) — thoracotomy + sternotomy:**
*   **Single-stage complete repair (one session):** Unifocalisation of MAPCAs, central PA augmentation/reconstruction, RV-PA valved conduit, VSD closure (some measure intra-operative PVR to decide on VSD closure).
*   **Staged repair:** Initial shunt ± unifocalisation → later RV-PA conduit ± VSD closure.

### Key Anatomical Features for Surgical Decision-Making
*   Adequacy of pulmonary vascular tree, PVR, post-operative RV pressure
*   Size of intrapericardial PAs and extent of peripheral arborisation
*   Number, origin, course, and segments supplied by each MAPCA
*   Location of stenoses within PAs and collaterals
*   Post-stenotic pressure in collaterals (predicts subsequent PVR)

### Indices for Assessing Pulmonary Artery Adequacy
*   **McGoon ratio** = (Proximal RPA diameter + Proximal LPA diameter) / Descending aortic diameter above diaphragm
    *   >1.2 adequate for complete repair; <0.8 inadequate for VSD closure (may overestimate as descending aorta often smaller in PA-VSD)
*   **Nakata index** = (Distal RPA CSA + Distal LPA CSA) / BSA
    *   >150 mm²/m² adequate for complete repair without shunt; not useful with MAPCAs (does not account for unifocalisation)
*   **Total NeoPulmonary Artery Index (TNPAI)** = (Nakata index + CSA of significant MAPCAs) / BSA
    *   >200 mm²/m² correlates with low post-operative RV pressure
*   **Intra-operative functional assessment:** After unifocalisation and RV-PA conduit, perfuse via conduit; if PA pressure <25 mmHg → expected RV/LV <0.5 → may close VSD.
*   **Surrogate marker:** Recruitment of ~15 lung segments = acceptable post-operative PVR.

### Components of Complete Repair
*   **RV-PA conduit:** De-ringed Hancock® valve conduit, Medtronic Contegra® (bovine jugular vein), or cryopreserved homograft (Figure 12).
*   **VSD closure:** Patch (double velour Dacron®) via tricuspid valve or longitudinal right ventriculotomy. Leave open if pulmonary vascular bed inadequate (allows RV decompression and time for PVR to fall).
*   **Unifocalisation of MAPCAs:** Anastomose MAPCAs and intrapericardial PAs to form confluence receiving blood from single source (RV-PA conduit). Ligate MAPCAs at origin (usually descending aorta); extensively mobilise to maximise length. Via median sternotomy, lateral thoracotomy, or both.
*   **Other components:** Ligation of ductus, take-down of previous shunt, ASD fenestration, patch augmentation of PAs.
*   **If VSD not closed:** Surgical clips or bands may prevent pulmonary overflow (Figure 13); subsequently released by balloon angioplasty.
*   **Valvular atresia only:** Transannular patch repair.

### One-Stage Complete Repair Rationale
*   Early recruitment of as many lung segments as possible to low-pressure RV-PA flow → key determinant of long-term survival and function.
*   Early unifocalisation normalises cardiovascular physiology, preserves lung parenchyma (avoids pulmonary vascular occlusive disease from unrestricted systemic flow via MAPCAs).
*   Alleviates cyanosis and polycythaemia in infancy; may improve long-term cardiac function.
*   Avoids interval death from shunt complications (thrombosis, distal anastomosis stenosis).
*   **Type A (duct-dependent):** Good candidates for early repair with RV-PA conduit; no unifocalisation needed.
*   **Types B/C:** Complete repair at 3–9 months (after surgery tolerance improved, before significant occlusive disease or MAPCA stenoses). Low O₂ saturations (<70%) indicate earlier intervention.
*   Stanford group aims for one-stage complete repair during infancy in majority of patients.

### Staged Repair Rationale
*   Evolved from early surgical experience; divides complete repair into less ambitious, better-tolerated procedures.
*   Avoids neonatal right heart repair (prolonged ICU stay, increased morbidity).
*   **Stage 1:** Palliative shunt (modified BT shunt or central Melbourne shunt if confluent PAs). If central PAs diminutive → RV-PA conduit with patch augmentation (provides catheter access for inter-stage balloon angioplasty). If no mediastinal PAs → bilateral modified BT shunts via thoracotomies + unifocalisation of significant MAPCAs.
*   **Stage 2 (3–6 months):** Ligation of MAPCAs to segments with dual supply; transplant only if sole source, free of stenosis, and not hypertensive.
*   **Stage 3 (1–3 years):** Take-down of shunt + RV-PA conduit ± VSD closure.
*   **Melbourne group:** Multi-stage approach without unifocalisation; relies on inter-stage growth of pulmonary tree + patch augmentation as required.

### Complications
*   Congestive heart failure
*   Complete heart block
*   Infective endocarditis
*   Cerebral abscess (if residual VSD)
*   Delayed growth

### Outcomes
*   **Operative mortality for complete repair:** 2–6%.
*   **Single-stage unifocalisation possible:** Up to 75% of patients.
*   **Primary complete intracardiac repair achieved:** 50% of patients (delayed VSD closure in most of remainder).
*   **Inability to achieve adequate unifocalisation** → reduced survival.
*   **Intrapericardial PA morphology** not a risk factor for survival.
*   **Multidisciplinary approach** (surgeons + interventional cardiologists) essential.
*   **Early reintervention to PAs:** ~30%. Nearly all long-term survivors with RV-PA conduit will require replacement.
*   **Long-term survival after complete repair:** Up to 85% at 15 years (vs ~55% at 15 years for shunt palliation alone, with impaired functional status).
*   **Late outcomes:** Aortic regurgitation, ventricular arrhythmias (associated with higher incidence of sudden death).

### Catheter Reintervention Role
*   Pulmonary artery balloon dilatation or stenting (including surgically inaccessible distal stenoses) (Figure 14)
*   Coil occlusion of residual MAPCAs
*   Palliative dilatation or stenting of unrepaired MAPCAs
*   Dilatation or stenting of RV-PA conduit
*   Balloon dilatation to release restrictive surgical clips or PA bands
*   Pacemaker insertion

### Surgical Reintervention Role
*   Complete repair after shunt placement (following PA growth)
*   Closure of residual VSD
*   Replacement of RV-PA conduit (stenosis or outgrown) (Figure 15)
*   Pulmonary artery augmentation
*   Recruitment of additional MAPCAs (uncommon)
*   Heart-lung transplantation

## 1 What is pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Core Features</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect (PA-VSD) is a **complex, cyanotic and highly heterogeneous malformation** of the heart and pulmonary arterial tree.

It is characterised by:
*   Underdevelopment of the right ventricular outflow tract (subpulmonary infundibulum)
*   Atresia of the pulmonary valve
*   A large ventricular septal defect
*   Right ventricular hypertrophy (Figure 1)

![](_page_0_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 1. Components of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect.** MAPCAs = major aortopulmonary collateral arteries.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Morphological Variants of Intrapericardial Pulmonary Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

In its severest form, there is either partial or complete absence of the intrapericardial pulmonary arteries with pulmonary blood flow derived from major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs); although, the presence or absence of MAPCAs is **not part of the definition** (Figure 2).

![](_page_1_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Morphological variants of the intrapericardial pulmonary arteries in pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect:** A) normal size pulmonary arteries supplied by ductus, connected by MPA to RV infundibulum with atretic pulmonary valve; B) separation of MPA from RV infundibulum; C) absence of MPA with ductus supplying confluent branch PAs but stenosis at entry point; D) hypoplasia of branch PAs with absence of MPA; E) absence of intrapericardial PAs with fibrous cord connecting LPA and RPA at hilum; F) absence of intrapericardial PAs with MAPCAs supplying non-confluent (left) and confluent (right) hilar PAs.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Historical Terms and Distinction from PA-IVS</summary><div class="details-content">

Historically, the marked anatomical variability led to a number of other diagnostic labels, including **truncus arteriosus Type IV, absence of the pulmonary artery, absent 6th aortic arch and pseudotruncus**.

The term **'tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia'** remains in common use and may be applied to cases where the intracardiac malformation is consistent with tetralogy of Fallot, with or without MAPCAs.

PA-VSD is anatomically and functionally distinct from **pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum (PA-IVS)** , in which the right ventricle and tricuspid valve are hypoplastic and coronary sinusoids may be present.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 2 What is the epidemiology of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   PA-VSD is a rare congenital malformation accounting for around **2.5% of all congenital heart defects**.
*   Incidence: **10 per 100,000 live births**.
*   Slightly more prevalent in **males** than in females.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 3 What is the aetiology of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Genetic and Environmental Factors</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Genetic:** Chromosomal defects and recognised syndromes; higher incidence in siblings and children born to those with tetralogy of Fallot.
*   **Environmental:** Increased incidence with **maternal diabetes**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Developmental Timing</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **PA-VSD:** The pulmonary ostium becomes atretic **early in development**, soon after conotruncal partitioning but **before closure of the ventricular septum**.
*   **PA-IVS (contrast):** Pulmonary atresia occurs **much later**, after cardiac septation has occurred; hence, these patients consistently have well-developed main and confluent branch pulmonary arteries.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 4 What abnormalities are associated with pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intracardiac Anomalies</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Double-outlet right ventricle
*   Transposition of the great arteries
*   Complete atrioventricular septal defect

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Extracardiac Anomalies and Syndromes</summary><div class="details-content">

Extracardiac anomalies occur in **25%** of patients.

PA-VSD is associated with chromosomal defects and recognised syndromes, including:
*   **a)** 22q11.2 deletion syndromes (DiGeorge syndrome, velocardiofacial syndrome)
*   **b)** VACTERL association (vertebral anomalies, anal atresia, cardiac defects, tracheoesophageal fistula and/or oesophageal atresia, renal and radial anomalies, and limb defects)
*   **c)** Alagille syndrome
*   **d)** Trisomy 21

</div></details>

## 5 How is pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect classified?</summary><div class="details-content">

The Congenital Heart Surgery Nomenclature and Database Project provided a unified approach to classifying PA-VSD into **three types** according to the origin of the pulmonary blood flow (Figure 3):

![](_page_3_Picture_10.jpeg)

**Figure 3. Pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect (PA-VSD) classified according to the origin of the pulmonary blood flow.** PA = pulmonary artery; MPA = main pulmonary artery; DA = ductus arteriosus; MAPCAs = major aortopulmonary collateral arteries.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Type A</summary><div class="details-content">

Intrapericardial pulmonary arteries are **present**, MAPCAs are **absent**, and pulmonary blood flow is supplied via the **patent ductus arteriosus**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Type B</summary><div class="details-content">

Both intrapericardial pulmonary arteries **and** MAPCAs are **present**, and pulmonary blood flow originates from **both** the ductus arteriosus and MAPCAs.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Type C</summary><div class="details-content">

Intrapericardial pulmonary arteries are **absent**, and pulmonary blood flow is supplied **only by MAPCAs**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Further Categorisation</summary><div class="details-content">

These types are further categorised by:
*   **a)** Presence or absence of **confluent** right and left pulmonary arteries
*   **b)** Presence or absence of a **main pulmonary trunk**

Classification according to the morphology of the pulmonary circulation reflects the implications for surgical management.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Special Note</summary><div class="details-content">

Rarely, MAPCAs can occur in the absence of PA-VSD in patients with severe pulmonary stenosis or pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum. In these cases, the pulmonary circulation resembles that found in Types B and C, and the surgical management is similar.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 6 What are major aortopulmonary collateral arteries?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Definition and Origin</summary><div class="details-content">

MAPCAs are thought to be **primitive intersegmental channels** with a muscular media layer connecting the systemic and pulmonary circulations, which have **failed to involute** during development and are present from birth.

They most commonly arise from the **descending thoracic aorta at the level of the carina**, but also from:
*   Subclavian, internal thoracic or intercostal arteries
*   Abdominal aorta
*   Rarely from a coronary artery

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Course and Distribution</summary><div class="details-content">

They usually have a **tortuous course** and may pass in front of or behind the oesophagus, tracheobronchial tree and aorta to join the intrapericardial pulmonary arteries in the lung (Figure 4).

MAPCAs have a **highly variable anatomical distribution** to lung segments and are associated with abnormal peripheral arborisation of the pulmonary circulation within the lung parenchyma.

![](_page_5_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 4. Variable course of the major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs) around mediastinal structures.** RPA = right pulmonary artery; SVC = superior vena cava.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 7 What is the pathophysiology of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   A relatively large VSD produces **complete mixing of blood**, cyanosis, and **equalisation of left and right ventricular pressures** with compensatory right ventricular hypertrophy.
*   Pulmonary blood flow is invariably derived from the **ductus arteriosus, MAPCAs, or both**.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Type A (Duct-Dependent)</summary><div class="details-content">

Closure of the ductus arteriosus leads to **hypoxia and cardiovascular collapse**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Types B/C (MAPCA-Dependent)</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary blood flow is **abnormal and precarious**. Highly variable anatomical distribution results in simultaneous **double supply, over-circulation and hypoperfusion** of different lung segments.

Progressive focal stenosis or the development of **pulmonary vascular occlusive disease and pulmonary hypertension** due to unrestricted blood flow may lead to **attrition of lung segments** over time.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 8 What is the natural history of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   PA-VSD **unrepaired** has a **poor prognosis**, with the greatest attrition during infancy and survival into adult life rare.
*   Even with historical surgical management, outlook was poor with ongoing attrition during childhood. Survival at **1, 10 and 35 years** reported as **60%, 40% and 6%**, respectively.
*   **Long-term survivors** are self-selecting with a **well-balanced pulmonary blood flow via MAPCAs** — sufficient to reduce hypoxia but restricted enough to limit secondary pulmonary hypertension. They, however, have functional limitation, develop **polycythaemia**, and are prone to **infection and secondary pulmonary haemorrhage**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 9 What are the symptoms of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Approximately **two thirds** develop symptoms during infancy.
*   Modes of presentation in infants are typically:
    *   **Cyanosis (50%)**
    *   **Heart failure (25%)**
    *   Incidental finding of a heart murmur with mild cyanosis and/or failure to thrive (25%)

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Neonates (Type A)</summary><div class="details-content">

Present acutely with cyanosis (with or without murmur) as the ductus arteriosus begins to close in a duct-dependent pulmonary circulation without MAPCAs. Severe hypoxia, acidosis and shock ensue.

**Differential diagnosis:** Severe sepsis, other duct-dependent congenital heart disease, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, other severe acute illness.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Infants (Types B/C — Balanced Circulation)</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Increasing cyanosis** — as pulmonary blood flow falls due to progressive stenosis in MAPCAs or late closure of the ductus arteriosus in Type B.
*   **Heart failure** — as pulmonary blood flow increases with the physiological fall in pulmonary vascular resistance.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Children (Balanced Circulation)</summary><div class="details-content">

Presentation is usually with an incidental finding of a murmur, mild cyanosis or heart failure, or failure to thrive.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Adults</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with unrepaired or palliated PA-VSD are now **rare** but are **ubiquitously cyanotic** with dyspnoea and impaired NYHA functional class.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 10 What are the clinical signs of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

Physical signs are largely determined by the extent of pulmonary blood flow.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Acute Loss of Pulmonary Blood Flow (Ductal Closure)</summary><div class="details-content">

Shock, **marked cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen therapy**, tachypnoea, tachycardia, but **preserved peripheral pulses**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Decreased Pulmonary Blood Flow</summary><div class="details-content">

**Cyanosis** (often progressive) → may lead to **clubbing** and **polycythaemia** if untreated during infancy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Increased Pulmonary Blood Flow</summary><div class="details-content">

Signs of **heart failure**: respiratory distress, tachypnoea, tachycardia, hepatomegaly, and a **bounding pulse** (caused by large volume runoff through MAPCAs or persistent ductus).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Other Signs</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Single loud S2** — caused by absence of the pulmonary component of the 2nd heart sound.
*   **Continuous bruit** — caused by turbulent flow through MAPCAs or a ductus arteriosus.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 12 What are the radiological features of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Chest Radiograph (CXR)</summary><div class="details-content">

Chest radiograph may demonstrate:

*   **a) Boot-shaped heart** — due to small or absent left pulmonary artery creating a concavity of the left heart border below the aortic arch, and right ventricular hypertrophy leading to uplifting of the cardiac apex from the dome of the diaphragm (Figure 6).
*   **b) Right aortic arch** (25–50%).
*   **c) Pulmonary vascular markings** — may be decreased or increased, dependent on pulmonary blood flow, with a typical **reticular pattern** in the presence of MAPCAs.

#### CT and MRI</summary><div class="details-content">

Computed tomography (CT) scanning and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are emerging as alternatives to cardiac catheterisation and may provide:

*   **a) Anatomical definition** — RVOT, main pulmonary artery, branch pulmonary arteries and stenoses, ductus arteriosus, number, location and course of MAPCAs.
*   **b) Functional assessment (MRI)** — percentage of blood flow to each lung.
*   **c) 3D reconstruction (CT)** of MAPCAs, which may be useful for operative planning (Figure 7).

## 13 What are the echocardiographic features of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography is the **key investigation for determining intracardiac anatomy** but has a **limited ability to define the nature of the pulmonary circulation**.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Diagnostic Features</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography can define:
*   Pulmonary atresia with **absent flow** between the RVOT and pulmonary trunk (Figure 8)
*   Variable degree of hypoplasia of the subpulmonary infundibulum and main pulmonary artery (ranging from an intact membrane to complete absence of intrapericardial PAs)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Comprehensive Information Provided</summary><div class="details-content">

A comprehensive examination will adhere to standard segmental anatomical sequence, providing information on:

*   **a) VSD type:** Large and unrestrictive (often); malalignment VSD; perimembranous VSD with outlet extension (most often).
*   **b) Infundibular septum:** Anterior deviation with varying degree of aortic override (tetralogy type).
*   **c) RV:** Degree of hypertrophy and cavity size.
*   **d) Interatrial communication:** Secundum ASD; PFO (50%).
*   **e) Right aortic arch** and mirror branching.
*   **f) Coronary arteries:** Usually normal; in some cases, anomalous origin of LAD from RCA passing across RVOT.
*   **g) Intrapericardial pulmonary arteries:** Presence/absence of intrapericardial branching (continuous or contiguous to ventricular mass); confluent or discontinuous branches; size and possible stenoses.
*   **h) Colour Doppler flow direction in central PAs (when detectable):** Antegrade flow from ductus (joins PA in mediastinum); retrograde flow from MAPCAs (feed into pulmonary tree at hilum).

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 14 What are the cardiac catheterisation features of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

## 15 Describe the medical management for a patient with pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Neonates with a duct-dependent pulmonary circulation** require **prostaglandin E₁** to maintain patency of the ductus arteriosus until surgical intervention.
*   Those presenting **acutely with shock** following closure of the ductus arteriosus also need immediate resuscitation and may require ventilation.
*   **Medical treatment with a diuretic and an ACE inhibitor** may be required in patients presenting in congestive heart failure due to excessive pulmonary blood flow.

</div></details>

## 16 What are the principles of surgical repair of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

The primary aim of surgery is to **improve on the predicted natural history** of the disease for each patient.

Heterogeneity of the condition precludes a standardised approach to all patients, as this is determined by individual morphology, pathophysiology and presentation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Ideal Outcome</summary><div class="details-content">

The ideal outcome is the creation of a **septated biventricular circulation** with low pulmonary vascular resistance and low right ventricular pressures.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Key Goals</summary><div class="details-content">

This can be achieved by intervening on all repairable lesions:

*   **a) Increasing pulmonary blood flow, oxygen saturation, and encouraging growth of intrapericardial PAs** with:
    *   i) Systemic-pulmonary shunt
    *   ii) Right ventricular-pulmonary artery conduit
    *   iii) Aortopulmonary window
*   **b) Early or interval unifocalisation of MAPCAs** to connect as many lung segments as possible to central PAs, maximising size and run-off of the pulmonary vascular bed (Figure 11)
*   **c) Securing a long-term source of blood from RV to pulmonary tree** using, in most cases, a **valved conduit** (preferably oversized)
*   **d) Closing the VSD** to prevent shunting, avoid volume overload, and prevent/reduce pulmonary hypertension. **The VSD should NOT be closed if low PVR cannot be guaranteed**, to avoid acute and chronic right ventricular failure.

![](_page_14_Picture_3.jpeg)

![](_page_14_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 11. </div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Controversy</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical strategies should aim to minimise the need for surgical or catheter-based reinterventions. The approach for achieving these goals remains **controversial**, with different surgical centres pursuing radically different strategies. Long-term outcome data are awaited to determine the optimal approach.

</div></details>

## 17 What are the surgical therapeutic options for a patient with pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Type A (No MAPCAs) — Sternotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a) Single-stage complete repair:** Tetralogy-type repair with minor or no PA interventions + implantation of RV-PA valved conduit + VSD closure. REV-type repair (patch across RVOT) may be used.
*   **b) Staged repair:** Initial systemic-pulmonary artery shunt (increase intrapericardial PA flow, encourage growth) → later take-down of shunt + RV-PA valved conduit + VSD closure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Types B/C (With MAPCAs) — Thoracotomy + Sternotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a) Single-stage complete repair (one session):**
    *   i) Unifocalisation of MAPCAs
    *   ii) Central pulmonary artery augmentation or reconstruction
    *   iii) Implantation of RV-PA valved conduit
    *   iv) Closure of VSD (some authors advocate intra-operative PVR measurement to decide on VSD closure)
*   **b) Staged repair (several strategies):**
    *   i) **Initially:** Surgical shunt to increase PA flow and encourage growth of intrapericardial or reconstructed central PAs, or unifocalisation of MAPCAs (ligation in some cases)
    *   ii) **Later:** Implantation of RV-PA valved conduit, with concurrent or subsequent VSD closure

</div></details>

## 18 What are the key anatomical features to be considered for surgical decision-making in pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Suitability of complete repair (including VSD closure) is dependent upon **adequacy of the pulmonary vascular tree, pulmonary vascular resistance, and post-operative RV pressure**.
*   **Size of the intrapericardial pulmonary arteries** and the **extent of peripheral arborisation**.
*   **Number, origin, course, and segments supplied by each MAPCA** — may determine the approach (sternotomy vs staged thoracotomy and sternotomy).
*   **Location of stenosis** within intrapericardial PAs and collaterals.
*   **Post-stenotic pressure in the collaterals** — predictive of subsequent pulmonary vascular resistance.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 19 How is the adequacy of the intrapericardial pulmonary arteries assessed to predict the adequacy of postoperative pulmonary vascular resistance?</summary><div class="details-content">

Although several indices/ratios can be used, they are all **limited** as they do not account for the **distal pulmonary vascular bed** (a key determinant of PVR).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### McGoon Ratio</summary><div class="details-content">

$$ \text{McGoon ratio} = \frac{\text{Proximal RPA diameter} + \text{Proximal LPA diameter}}{\text{Descending aortic diameter above the diaphragm}} $$

*   Ratio **>1.2** is adequate for complete repair.
*   Ratio **<0.8** is deemed inadequate for VSD closure.
*   **Limitation:** May overestimate adequacy, as the descending aorta is often smaller in patients with PA-VSD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Nakata Index</summary><div class="details-content">

$$ \text{Nakata index} = \frac{\text{Distal RPA cross-sectional area} + \text{Distal LPA cross-sectional area}}{\text{Body surface area}} $$

*   Index **>150 mm²/m²** is adequate for complete repair without a shunt.
*   **Limitation:** Not useful in patients with MAPCAs, as it does not account for unifocalisation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Total NeoPulmonary Artery Index (TNPAI)</summary><div class="details-content">

$$ \text{TNPAI} = \frac{\text{Nakata index} + \text{Cross-sectional area of significant MAPCAs}}{\text{Body surface area}} $$

*   Index **>200 mm²/m²** correlates with low post-operative RV pressure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intra-Operative Functional Assessment</summary><div class="details-content">

After completion of unifocalisation and distal RV-PA conduit:
*   Perfusion cannula and PA catheter inserted via conduit → increased to full flow.
*   If PA pressure remains **<25 mmHg**, the expected RV/LV pressure ratio is **<0.5** → VSD may be closed.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Surrogate Marker</summary><div class="details-content">

In our practice, recruitment of approximately **15 lung segments** is a surrogate marker of acceptable post-operative PVR.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 20 What are the components of a complete repair of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### RV-PA Conduit</summary><div class="details-content">

A right ventricular-pulmonary artery conduit (e.g., de-ringed Hancock® valve conduit, Medtronic Contegra® bovine jugular vein, or cryopreserved cadaveric homograft) connects the right ventricle to the central intrapericardial or reconstructed pulmonary arteries (Figure 12).

![](_page_17_Picture_6.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Patch augmentation of the central pulmonary arteries to increase their diameter and enable placement of a valved right ventricle (RV) to pulmonary artery (PA) conduit.** RVOT = right ventricular outflow tract; MAPCAs = major aortopulmonary collateral arteries.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### VSD Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

VSD closure is performed using a patch (e.g., double velour Dacron®), either through the tricuspid valve or via a longitudinal right ventriculotomy (to which the RV-PA conduit can then be anastomosed).

**If there is concern over the adequacy of the pulmonary vascular bed, the VSD should be left open** to decompress the right ventricle and allow time for the PVR to fall.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Unifocalisation of MAPCAs</summary><div class="details-content">

Unifocalisation involves anastomosis of the MAPCAs and intrapericardial PAs to form a **confluence** to receive blood flow from a single source (RV-PA conduit).

*   Performed via median sternotomy, lateral thoracotomy on the side of the descending thoracic aorta, or both.
*   All identified MAPCAs are **ligated at their origin** (usually descending aorta) and extensively **mobilised** to maximise their length prior to unifocalisation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Other Components</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Ligation of the ductus arteriosus
*   Take-down of a previous shunt
*   ASD fenestration
*   Patch augmentation of the pulmonary arteries

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### When VSD is Not Closed</summary><div class="details-content">

When the VSD is not closed, **surgical clips or bands** may be used to prevent pulmonary overflow in the early post-operative period (Figure 13) and subsequently released by balloon angioplasty.

In patients with **valvular atresia only**, a **transannular patch repair** may be performed.

</div></details>

## 21 What is the rationale for a one-stage complete repair strategy for pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Early recruitment of as many lung segments as possible to receive **low-pressure RV-PA blood flow** is thought to be a key determinant of **long-term survival and functional outcome**.
*   Early unifocalisation **normalises cardiovascular physiology** and **preserves lung parenchyma** by avoiding the development of pulmonary vascular occlusive disease resulting from potentially unrestricted systemic blood flow via MAPCAs.
*   Alleviates **cyanosis and polycythaemia** during infancy and may improve long-term preservation of cardiac function.
*   Avoids the risk of **interval death** due to shunt complications (thrombosis, distal anastomosis stenosis).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Type A (Duct-Dependent)</summary><div class="details-content">

Neonates typically have adequately developed intrapericardial PAs without MAPCAs → good candidates for early repair with RV-PA conduit; no unifocalisation required.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Types B/C (Collateral Flow)</summary><div class="details-content">

Infants usually undergo complete repair at **3–9 months** of age — after their tolerance of surgery has improved but typically before significant pulmonary vascular occlusive disease or significant MAPCA stenoses have developed.

**Low systemic oxygen saturations (below 70%)** would be an indication for earlier intervention.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Clinical Practice</summary><div class="details-content">

Several groups with large series, including the **Stanford group**, aim to achieve a **one-stage complete repair during infancy** in the majority of patients.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 22 What is the rationale for a staged repair strategy in pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   This strategy has evolved from early surgical experience and divides the complete repair into **several less ambitious and potentially better tolerated procedures**.
*   Avoids **neonatal right heart repair**, which may be associated with prolonged ICU stay and increased morbidity.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Stage 1</summary><div class="details-content">

An initial **palliative shunt** is performed to augment pulmonary blood flow, relieve cyanosis, and encourage pulmonary artery growth.

*   **Modified Blalock-Taussig shunt** or **central Melbourne shunt** — most common in presence of confluent PAs.
*   If central PAs are **diminutive** → RV-PA conduit with patch augmentation of intrapericardial PAs (also provides catheter access for inter-stage balloon angioplasty of stenoses).
*   In absence of mediastinal PAs → **bilateral modified BT shunts** via thoracotomies along with **unifocalisation of significant MAPCAs**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Stage 2 (3–6 months)</summary><div class="details-content">

Typically involves **ligation of MAPCAs** to lung segments with a **dual blood supply** and transplantation only when they are the **sole source** of blood flow to a segment, free of stenosis, and not hypertensive.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Stage 3 (1–3 years)</summary><div class="details-content">

Involves **take-down of the shunt** and placement of an **RV-PA conduit** with or without closure of the VSD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Alternative Approach (Melbourne Group)</summary><div class="details-content">

The **Melbourne group** advocates a **multi-stage approach without unifocalisation of MAPCAs**, instead relying on inter-stage growth of the pulmonary tree with additional patch augmentation of the central and branch pulmonary arteries as required.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 23 What are the complications following surgical repair of pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Congestive heart failure
*   Complete heart block
*   Infective endocarditis
*   **Cerebral abscess** (in the presence of a residual VSD)
*   Delayed growth

</div></details>

## 24 What are the outcomes of surgery for pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Operative mortality for complete repair:** as low as **2–6%**.
*   **Single-stage unifocalisation possible:** up to **75%** of patients.
*   **Primary complete intracardiac repair achieved:** **50%** of patients (delayed VSD closure in most of the remainder).
*   **Inability to achieve adequate unifocalisation** of MAPCAs is associated with **reduced survival**.
*   **Intrapericardial PA morphology** is **not** a risk factor for survival.
*   A **multidisciplinary approach** between surgeons and interventional cardiologists is essential to anticipate potential problems and perform early reassessment.
*   Approximately **30%** require early reintervention to the PAs, and **nearly all long-term survivors with an RV-PA conduit** will require its replacement.
*   **Long-term survival after complete repair:** up to **85% at 15 years**.
*   Surgery compares favourably to those with **shunt palliation alone** (around 55% at 15 years) with impaired functional status.
*   **Late outcomes:** Aortic regurgitation and ventricular arrhythmias (associated with higher incidence of **sudden death**).

</div></details>
