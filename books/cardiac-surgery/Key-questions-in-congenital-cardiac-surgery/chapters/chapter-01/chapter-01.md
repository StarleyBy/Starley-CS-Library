# Congenital Cardiac Anatomy

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Embryological Origins

*   **Primary heart field:** Forms the linear heart tube within the pericardial cavity; responsible for forming the left ventricle and part of the ventricular septum.
*   **Secondary heart field:** Invades the venous and arterial poles; forms the atrioventricular canal, primary atrial chamber, right ventricle primordium, and outflow tract.
*   **Ballooning:** Atrial appendages balloon in parallel; ventricular pouches balloon in series (inlet → left ventricle, outlet → right ventricle).
*   **Primary vs chamber myocardium:** Primary tube walls are primary myocardium; definitive chambers form from secondary (chamber) myocardium.

### Heterotaxy and Isomerism

*   **Heterotaxy:** Abnormal arrangement of thoracic/abdominal organs across the left-right axis.
*   **Isomerism:** Right- and left-sided structures are mirror images within the same individual (e.g., bilateral right atrial appendages).
*   **Only atrial appendages** show differential response to laterality genes (Cited2, Pitx2 on left; Lefty2, Sonic hedgehog prevent right-sided expression).
*   **Atrioventricular connections are always mixed** when atrial appendages are isomeric.

### Ventricular Looping and Topology

*   **Rightward looping** (normal) → right-handed topology: palmar surface of right hand on septum (thumb in inlet, fingers in outlet).
*   **Leftward looping** → left-handed topology: palmar surface of left hand on septum.
*   **Right-handed topology** with usual atrial arrangement and concordant AV connections.
*   **Left-handed topology** with mirror-imaged atrial arrangement and concordant AV connections.
*   **Discordant AV connections** → opposite topology.

### Atrial Septum Development

*   **Septum primum:** Grows from atrial roof with mesenchymal cap; primary foramen closes as cap fuses with AV cushions.
*   **Secondary foramen:** Forms as upper edge of primary septum breaks away.
*   **Vestibular spine:** Develops from dorsal mesocardium; muscularises with mesenchymal cap to form inferoanterior buttress of oval fossa.
*   **Oval fossa floor** = primary septum; **superior rim** = infolding of atrial roof (superior interatrial fold).

### Atrial Septal Defects vs Interatrial Communications

| True ASD | Interatrial Communication (Extraseptal) |
|----------|----------------------------------------|
| Oval fossa (secundum) defects | Sinus venosus defects (with anomalous pulmonary veins) |
| Vestibular defects (rare) | Coronary sinus defects |
| — | Ostium primum defect (AVSD, not true ASD) |

### Atrioventricular Septal Defect (AVSD)

*   **Fundamental features:** Common atrioventricular junction + trifoliate left atrioventricular valve.
*   **Left valve is NOT a "cleft mitral valve"** — it is trifoliate, not bifoliate.
*   **Bridging leaflets** derived from superior and inferior AV cushions.
*   Shunting can be atrial, ventricular, or both depending on leaflet fusion.

### Ventricular Septation

*   **Membranous septum:** Formed by fusion of tubercles from AV cushions to close the tertiary interventricular communication.
*   **No muscular outlet septum** in normal heart — subpulmonary infundibulum is a freestanding sleeve.
*   **Supracristal VSD** = doubly committed juxta-arterial defect; fibrous continuity between aortic and pulmonary valves.

### Tetralogy of Fallot

*   **Four features:** Interventricular communication, biventricular aortic connection, subpulmonary stenosis, right ventricular hypertrophy.
*   **Key developmental abnormality:** Combination of anterocephalad malalignment of muscular outlet septum + anomalous (hypertrophied) septoparietal trabeculations.

### Double-Outlet Right Ventricle (DORV)

*   Defined when **larger part of both arterial roots** is supported by morphologically right ventricle.
*   Surgical determinant: Whether the right ventricular border of the overriding root can be closed without tunnelling.

### Functionally Univentricular Heart

*   **Univentricular AV connections:** Double-inlet left ventricle (DILV) or absence of right/left AV connection (e.g., tricuspid atresia).
*   **Biventricular AV connections with small ventricle:** HLHS, PA-IVS with hypoplastic RV, unbalanced AVSD.

### Tricuspid Atresia

*   Two phenotypes: **Imperforate valve** (hypoplastic RV with inlet) vs **absence of right AV connection** (incomplete RV without inlet).
*   Commonest variant: Absence of right AV connection with left atrium connected to dominant left ventricle.

### Common Arterial Trunk

*   **Essential feature:** Commonality of the ventriculo-arterial junction (persistence of undivided embryonic conus).
*   **Simplified classification:** Aortic dominance vs pulmonary dominance (latter associated with arch interruption).

### Transposition

*   **Complete transposition:** Discordant VA connections (aorta from RV, pulmonary trunk from LV) with concordant AV connections.
*   **Congenitally corrected transposition:** Discordant VA + discordant AV connections (double discordance); circulations in series.

### Coronary Artery Classification (Leiden)

*   Observer stands in **non-adjacent sinus** looking towards adjacent sinuses.
*   **Sinus #1:** Right hand of observer; **Sinus #2:** Left hand.
*   Normal: 1R, 2LCx; Transposition commonest: 1LCx, 2R.

### Aortopulmonary Septum

*   Derived from **dorsal wall of aortic sac**, protrudes into distal outflow tract.
*   Fuses with distal ends of major outflow cushions to close aortopulmonary foramen.
*   Failure of fusion → aortopulmonary window; failure of major cushion fusion → common arterial trunk.

### Vascular Rings

*   Explained by **Edwards hypothetical double aortic arch** encircling tracheo-oesophageal pedicle.
*   Derived from arteries of 3rd, 4th, and pulmonary arches (bilaterally symmetrical in early development).
*   Right-sided components normally attenuate.

### Arterial Duct

*   Derived from **left pulmonary arch artery** (previously called "6th arch").
*   Right pulmonary arch artery normally regresses.
*   **Isthmus:** Segment between left subclavian artery and duct insertion (cannot be defined until subclavian migrates cranially).

### Conduction System

*   **Sinus node:** Epicardial aspect of terminal groove at superior cavoatrial junction, inferior to right atrial appendage crest. No specialised internodal tracts — ordinary working myocardium with parallel alignment conducts preferentially.
*   **AV node:** Apex of triangle of Koch (borders: Eustachian valve extension, tricuspid septal leaflet attachment, membranous septum).
*   **AV bundle (His):** Passes through membranous septum to crest of muscular septum; branches into right and left bundle branches.
*   **Landmark:** Line from apex of triangle of Koch to medial papillary muscle (muscle of Lancisi) shows course of conduction axis.

## 1. Embryological Origins of the Definitive Heart</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Primary and Secondary Heart Fields

The primary and secondary heart fields represent the heart-forming area of the mesodermal layer of the embryonic disc.

Cardiomyocytes migrate from the **primary heart field** to produce a linear tube within the pericardial cavity (Figure 1), which is responsible for forming the left ventricle and part of the ventricular septum.

![](_page_0_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 1. Scanning electron micrograph of a mouse embryo on the 9th day of development demonstrating an essentially straight linear tube, which is eventually responsible for formation of little more than the definitive left ventricle.**

With ongoing development, new material from the **second heart field** invades the pericardial cavity at both the venous and arterial poles of the linear tube. This is responsible for:
*   Formation of the atrioventricular canal and the primary atrial chamber at the venous pole
*   Formation of the primordium of the right ventricle, along with the outflow tract, at the arterial pole (Figure 2)

![](_page_1_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Scanning electron micrograph of a mouse embryo during the 10th day of development demonstrating looping of the ventricular component of the linear tube in front of the developing atrial component, produced by ongoing addition of material from the heart-forming areas at both the arterial and venous poles. Ballooning from the cavity at the atrial level then produces the atrial appendages (white stars), while ballooning from the ventricular loop produces the apical components of the developing right and left ventricles.**

Ingrowth at both poles results in lengthening of the tube, which contributes to formation of the **ventricular loop**. Subsequent to looping, the tube retains a solitary lumen, which is lined with endocardial jelly. At this early stage, the entirety of the tube, including the outflow tract, possesses exclusively myocardial walls.

#### Primary and Chamber Myocardium

The cardiomyocytes forming the walls of the tube have their own phenotype and can be recognised as forming **primary myocardium**. The definitive atrial and ventricular chambers are formed by expansion of pouches from the cavity of the primary tube, with the newly formed walls built from phenotypically different cardiomyocytes, which give rise to **secondary, or chamber, myocardium**.

*   Outpouching or ballooning of the primary atrial chamber occurs in a symmetrical fashion and gives rise to the **atrial appendages** (Figure 2)
*   The ventricular pouches, in contrast, balloon **in series**: one from the inlet of the ventricular loop (becomes left ventricle), and the other from the outlet (becomes right ventricle) (Figure 3)

![](_page_2_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 3. Episcopic image of a mouse embryo during the 11th day of development demonstrating ballooning of the atrial appendages (large white arrows) in parallel from the atrial component of the primary tube, while the ventricular components are ballooning apically from the inlet and outlet components of the ventricular loop. At this initial stage, the atrioventricular (AV) canal opens exclusively to the inlet part of the loop.**

The pouch ballooning from the inlet of the loop will become the apical component of the left ventricle, while the ballooning from the outlet component provides the apical part of the right ventricle. The **apical muscular ventricular septum** appears concomitant with the formation of the two pouches.

#### Endocardial Cushions and Remodelling

By the time the ballooning has taken place, the endocardial jelly has itself undergone a process of **endothelial-to-mesenchymal transformation**, producing cushions in the atrioventricular canal and throughout the outflow tract. These cushions will eventually remodel to become the cardiac valves.

Additional remodelling within the lumen of the initial primary heart tube then permits the right atrium to gain access to the developing right ventricle by expansion of the atrioventricular canal (Figure 4), with the aorta subsequently being transferred to the left ventricle.

![](_page_3_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 4. Episcopic image of a mouse embryo during the 12th day of development demonstrating expansion of the atrioventricular (AV) canal, which permits the right atrium to connect directly with the cavity of the developing right ventricle (large white arrow).**

Only after this remodelling has taken place is it possible to close the **embryonic interventricular communication**, thereby completing the separation of the systemic and pulmonary ventricular blood streams.

## 2. Connection of Atrial Chambers with Ventricular Mass and Ventricles with Arterial Trunks</summary><div class="details-content">

The key part of the analysis of congenitally malformed hearts is to describe the arrangement of the **'segments'** (atrial, ventricular and arterial components) and how they are joined together.

Subsequent to remodelling of the embryonic interventricular communication:
*   The cavities of the atrial chambers are in continuity with those of the underlying ventricles
*   The cavities of the ventricles are continuous with those of the intrapericardial arterial trunks

The walls of the various components, however, are **not directly contiguous**, due to the presence of **'connecting segments'**:
*   Atrioventricular canal myocardium interposes between the walls of the atrial segment and the inlet of the ventricular loop
*   The proximal part of the outflow tract (**conus**) is supported exclusively by the developing right ventricle (Figure 5)

![](_page_4_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 5. Episcopic image of a human embryo during the 6th week of development demonstrating the developing atrioventricular junctions and the proximal part of the outflow tract (conus).**

With ongoing development:
*   The myocardium of the atrioventricular canal becomes incorporated into the atrial chambers, forming the **vestibules** of the tricuspid and mitral valves
*   The proximal myocardium of the outflow tract (conus) forms the myocardium supporting the arterial roots above their respective ventricles, and is an integral part of the ventricular myocardium

Subsequent to these changes, it is possible to recognise the definitive **atrioventricular** and **ventriculo-arterial junctions**, which delimit the extent of the ventricular mass.

Hence:
*   The cavities of the atrial chambers connect with those of the ventricles **across the atrioventricular junction**
*   The chambers of the ventricles are in **direct connection** with those of the arterial trunks

It is less accurate to use the term **'alignments'** to describe these features, since the cavities of the atrial and ventricular chambers can be aligned one to the other, as in tricuspid atresia, without being connected (Figure 6).

![](_page_5_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Macroscopic image demonstrating alignment of the right atrium with an incomplete right ventricle in a patient with tricuspid atresia but the absence of connection between the atrial and ventricular cavities.**

## 3. Heterotaxy and Isomerism</summary><div class="details-content">

**Heterotaxy** is defined as an abnormal arrangement of the internal thoracic or abdominal organs across the left-right axis of the body. Most complex congenital cardiac lesions are found in hearts with the variant of so-called heterotaxy in which there is evidence, in the thorax, of an **isomeric arrangement** of all or parts of the organs.

**Isomerism** describes the situation in which the right- and left-sided structures within the body are mirror images of each other. The hands are the perfect example of bodily isomerism. In the **mirror-imaged arrangement** (situs inversus), all organs are lateralised but positioned in mirror-imaged fashion compared to usual. In the **isomeric arrangement**, seen best in the thoracic organs, the lungs and bronchi, and the atrial appendages, are mirror images of each other in the same individual.

Both the isomeric and mirror-imaged variants represent **disorders of laterality**.

#### Genetic Basis

The right and left sides of the body develop their individual anatomical features in response to genes located on the left side of the developing embryo, such as **Cited2 and Pitx2**, and which are prevented from reaching the right side by other genes, such as **Lefty2 and Sonic hedgehog**.

Within the heart, **only the atrial appendages** respond in differential fashion to these genes, which explains why only the atrial appendages show evidence of isomerism.

The lungs and bronchi are also able to develop in isomeric or mirror-imaged fashion, but this is not the case for the abdominal organs, which can develop in a mirror-imaged or jumbled-up fashion.

#### Clinical Approach

The syndrome currently described by many as 'visceral heterotaxy' is best addressed on the basis of the **isomeric arrangement of the lungs, bronchi and atrial appendages**, although the isomeric features are not always universally present.

For appropriate description of the cardiac findings:
1.  Establish the presence of isomerism, as opposed to usually arranged or mirror-imaged atrial appendages
2.  Perform full **sequential segment analysis**, with particular attention to veno-atrial connections
3.  Veno-atrial connections can never be anatomically normal in hearts having isomeric appendages, although patterns can be quasi-usual or quasi-mirror-imaged

## 4. Significance of Ventricular Looping</summary><div class="details-content">

As new material is added to the initial heart tube by ongoing migration to the arterial and venous poles from the heart-forming area, the ventricular component of the tube elongates and turns to the right, which represents **ventricular looping**.

The next stage of ventricular development is **'ballooning'** of the apical components of the right and left ventricles from the outlet and inlet components of the ventricular loop, respectively. With normal development, this places the right ventricle to the right of the developing left ventricle.

Rightward expansion of the atrioventricular canal provides the inlet to the developing right ventricle.

#### Right-Handed Ventricular Topology

Subsequent to the formation of the right ventricular inlet, it is the **palmar surface of the right hand** that can be placed on the developing septum, such that the fingers are in the outlet and the thumb is in the inlet component. This is called **right-handed ventricular topology**, and is the consequence of rightward ventricular looping.

#### Left-Handed Ventricular Topology

Should the ventricular part of the developing heart tube loop to the left, then in association with leftward expansion of the atrioventricular canal:
*   The developing left atrium is placed into continuity with the developing right ventricle
*   The right ventricle is formed in leftward position relative to the left ventricle
*   It is the **palmar surface of the left hand**, rather than the right, which is placed on the septal surface of the developing right ventricle (thumb in inlet, fingers in outlet)
*   This produces **left-handed ventricular topology** (Figure 7)

![](_page_8_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_8_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Features of right-handed (left panel), as opposed to left-handed ventricular topology (right panel).**

#### Clinical Implications

*   In almost all patients with usual atrial arrangement and **concordant** atrioventricular connections → **right-handed** topology
*   In patients with concordant atrioventricular connections and **mirror-imaged** atrial arrangement → **left-handed** topology
*   When atrioventricular connections are **discordant**:
    *   Usual atrial arrangement → **left-handed** topology
    *   Mirror-imaged atrial arrangement → **right-handed** topology

These conventions are the basis for description of segmental anatomy in the approach promoted by Van Praagh and colleagues, with arrangements described as **(S,L,\*)** and **(I,D,\*)**, the asterisk representing the topological arrangement of the arterial segment.

For those using sequential segmental analysis, it can be presumed that the topological arrangement of the ventricular mass is in keeping with the atrioventricular connections.

In the setting of **isomerism of the atrial appendages**, however, it is necessary always to describe the ventricular topology, since the atrioventricular connections themselves are **always mixed** when the atrial appendages are isomeric (Figure 8).

![](_page_9_Figure_2.jpeg)

![](_page_9_Figure_4.jpeg)

**Figure 8. Combinations of atrial arrangement and ventricular topology that produce the various biventricular atrioventricular connections. When the atrial appendages are isomeric, the atrioventricular connections are always mixed. In this setting, therefore, it is always necessary to specify topology so as to provide a complete description.**

## 5. Origin of the Pulmonary Veins</summary><div class="details-content">

The atrial component of the developing heart tube is attached to the pharyngeal mesenchyme through the **dorsal mesocardium** (Figure 3). At this stage, neither the lungs nor any boundaries between the atrial component of the heart tube and the systemic venous tributaries have been formed.

Once the venous valves have formed, it becomes possible to recognise the boundaries of the **systemic venous sinus**. Associated with this, a strand of tissue forms in the midline of the pharyngeal mesenchyme.

Subsequent canalisation of this mid-pharyngeal strand provides a connection between the developing intraparenchymal pulmonary veins and the left atrium, with the newly formed channel opening into the left atrium between the folds showing the dorsal mesocardial connection.

#### Key Features

*   This newly formed pulmonary vein opens into the left atrium through a **myocardial portal with atrial characteristics**
*   The walls at the site of connection are **different** from the walls of the systemic venous tributaries
*   The initial opening of the pulmonary vein is adjacent to the left atrioventricular junction
*   Only at a much later stage does it become possible to recognise separate openings for the right and left pulmonary veins
*   Following formation of the separate pulmonary venous orifices, the **superior interatrial fold** develops, which provides the superior rim of the oval fossa

The walls of the pulmonary veins possess a **myocardial phenotype** and have never had any relationship with the walls of the systemic venous sinus (Figure 9).

## 6. Development of the Atrial Septum</summary><div class="details-content">

The first signs of atrial septation is the formation of a ridge in the roof of the atrial component of the heart tube. As it grows towards the orifice of the atrioventricular canal, this first septum (**septum primum**) carries a cap of mesenchyme on its leading edge.

The atrioventricular canal itself is divided into right and left parts by the fusion within its cavity of the **superior and inferior atrioventricular cushions**. As the primary atrial septum grows towards the cushions, the space between the mesenchymal cap on its leading edge and the cushions represents the **primary atrial foramen (foramen primum)**.

With ongoing growth of the primary septum and its mesenchymal cap towards the cushions, there is a concomitant decrease in the size of the primary foramen, which is eventually closed by **fusion of the cap with the cushions**.

By the time the primary foramen has been obliterated, however, the upper edge of the primary septum has broken away from the atrial roof to form the **secondary atrial foramen (foramen secundum)** (Figure 10).

![](_page_12_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 10. Initial stages of development of the atrial septum in the human heart: A) growth of the primary septum, with its mesenchymal cap, from the roof of the atrial component of the heart tube; B) growth of the primary septum towards the atrioventricular (AV) cushions, with its upper part breaking down to form the secondary atrial foramen. The primary foramen is the space between the mesenchymal cap and the atrial margins of the atrioventricular cushions. LV = left ventricle; RV = right ventricle.**

As the primary foramen closes, a new mesenchymal structure develops from the dorsal margin of the mesocardium, known as the **vestibular spine**.

With ongoing development, the vestibular spine and the mesenchymal cap **muscularise**, forming the **inferoanterior buttress of the oval fossa (fossa ovale)**.

The primary septum itself then forms the **floor** of the newly formed oval fossa. Its **superior rim** is formed by an infolding of the atrial roof between the attachments of the pulmonary veins to the left atrium and the caval veins to the right atrium.

The **oval foramen (foramen ovale)** is the space between the infolded superior interatrial fold and the cranial margin of the primary septum, with the primary septum itself forming a flap valve over the foramen (Figure 11).

![](_page_13_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 11. Later stages of development of the atrial septum in a mouse heart: A) growth of the vestibular spine; B) completion of atrial septation with infolding of the superior atrial roof that forms the cranial margin of the oval fossa (white arrow with black borders). The mesenchymal cap and the vestibular spine have muscularised to form the inferoanterior buttress of the oval fossa. SCV = superior caval vein.**

## 7. Atrial Septal Defect vs Interatrial Communication</summary><div class="details-content">

The normal atrial septum has two components:
*   **Floor of the oval fossa** — derived from the primary atrial septum
*   **Anteroinferior buttress** — formed by muscularisation of the mesenchymal cap and the vestibular spine

#### True Atrial Septal Defects

True atrial septal defects are produced either because of deficiencies or fenestrations of the floor of the oval fossa, or because of inappropriate formation of the anteroinferior buttress:

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Oval fossa (ostium secundum) defects** | Deficiencies or fenestrations of the floor of the fossa from inappropriate formation of the primary atrial septum. Distinguished from **persistent patency of the oval foramen (PFO)** , found in ~25% of normal individuals |
| **Vestibular defects** | Much rarer; secondary to improper fusion of the muscularising components of the anteroinferior buttress of the oval fossa (Figure 12) |

![](_page_15_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Atrial septal defects: A) true defects within the oval fossa (yellow oval) and a vestibular defect within the anteroinferior buttress; B) persistent patency of the oval foramen, with the flap valve of sufficient size to cover the margins of the fossa (double-headed white arrow) but fails to fuse with the anterosuperior margin, leaving a potential interatrial communication (double-headed white arrow with red borders). Shunting across the communication is only possible when right atrial pressure exceeds that in the left atrium.**

#### Interatrial Communications (Lesions Outside the Atrial Septum)

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Sinus venosus defects** | Commonest extraseptal communications. Consequence of anomalous connection of one or more pulmonary veins to a caval vein, with pulmonary veins retaining left atrial connection. Can be in openings of superior or inferior caval veins (Figure 13) |
| **Coronary sinus defect** | Rarest interatrial communication. Permits shunting across right atrial orifice of sinus due to fenestration/absence of walls interposing between coronary sinus (or persistent left superior caval vein) and left atrium |
| **Ostium primum defect** | Atrioventricular rather than atrial septal defect |

## 8. Phenotypic Features of Atrioventricular Septal Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

The **commonality of the atrioventricular junction**, along with the **trifoliate configuration of the left atrioventricular valve** are the fundamental phenotypic features of an atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD), also known as an atrioventricular canal defect.

During gestation, the bridging leaflets of the common atrioventricular valve are derived from the **superior and inferior atrioventricular cushions**, which when fused separate the atrioventricular canal into the right and left atrioventricular orifices (Figure 14).

![](_page_18_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 14. A) Atrioventricular cushions as seen in a developing mouse embryo sacrificed at embryonic day 12, just prior to fusion of the superior and inferior cushions that lie edge-to-edge within the atrioventricular canal. B) Atrioventricular septal defect with the common atrioventricular junction guarded by a common atrioventricular valve. There is an obvious similarity between the arrangements of the bridging leaflets and the superior and inferior atrioventricular (AV) cushions. The 'trifoliate' arrangement of apposition of the left ventricular cushions in the developing mouse heart (white dotted lines) parallels the configuration of the leaflets that guard the left half of the common atrioventricular junction in the specimen with deficient atrioventricular septation. Both images are viewed from the ventricular apex looking towards the base of the ventricular mass.**

#### Important Clarification

In the past, the left component of the common atrioventricular valve was usually described as a **'cleft mitral valve'** — this is **incorrect**. The left half of the persisting common atrioventricular junction is guarded by a **trifoliate valve** (Figure 14B) rather than a bifoliate valve, even when the common atrioventricular junction itself is divided into separate valvar orifices for the right and left ventricles, as in AVSD without ventricular septal defect (Figure 15).

![](_page_19_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 15. A) Atrioventricular septal defect with separate valvar orifices for the left and right ventricles ('ostium primum defect'); and B) a normal heart in mouse embryos sacrificed at embryonic day 15.5. Both short-axis images are viewed from the ventricular apex looking towards the base. The mouse with the atrioventricular septal defect has maintained the trifoliate configuration of the left half of the common atrioventricular junction despite the commitment of the aortic root to the left ventricle, whereas the normal mouse, subsequent to closure of the embryonic interventricular communication, has formed a bifoliate left valve. The black dotted line shows the line of fusion between the superior and inferior atrioventricular cushions in the normal mouse, whereas the left ventricular components of the cushions have remained unfused in the mouse with the atrioventricular septal defect.**

#### Variations in Shunting

In most cases, the bridging leaflets float more or less freely within the atrioventricular septal defect, permitting shunting at **both atrial and ventricular levels**.

| Leaflet Fusion Pattern | Result |
|------------------------|--------|
| Bridging leaflets fused to crest of muscular ventricular septum | Shunting confined to atrial level — "ostium primum" defect |
| Bridging leaflets attached to leading edge of atrial septum | Shunting confined to ventricular level (common AV junction and trifoliate left AV valve retained) |
| Spontaneous closure of AVSD | Intact septal structures with common AV junction and trifoliate left AV valve |

![](_page_20_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 16. The images show the commonality of the atrioventricular junction in hearts with an atrioventricular septal defect, when the junction is guarded by: A) a common valve; or B) when the bridging leaflets have themselves fused to produce separate valvar orifices for the right and left ventricles (star), and are additionally fused to the crest of the muscular ventricular septum. The area previously considered to represent a 'cleft' in the mitral valve (double-headed arrow in B) can be seen to be the zone of apposition between the left ventricular components of the bridging leaflets (compare with double-headed arrow in A). The dotted white lines show the location of the crest of the muscular ventricular septum.**

## 9. Origin of the Membranous Septum</summary><div class="details-content">

The normal ventricular septum is almost exclusively a muscular entity, with a small part within the left ventricular outflow tract that is fibrous (known as the **membranous septum**). Most often, it is crossed on its right ventricular aspect by the hinge of the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve, which divides it into **atrioventricular** and **interventricular** components (Figure 17).

![](_page_21_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 17. 'Four-chamber' section through the aortic root, with the non-coronary sinus and its leaflet removed. The hinge of the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve (TV) crosses the membranous septum, dividing it into its atrioventricular and interventricular components.**

#### Developmental Process

During normal development:
1.  Expansion of the atrioventricular canal allows connection of the developing right atrium with the right ventricle
2.  The ventricular outflow tract, separating to form aortic and pulmonary roots, remains supported above the cavity of the right ventricle
3.  To close the ventricular septum, the developing aortic root (still supported above the right ventricle) needs to come into continuity with the left ventricle

This is achieved by creating a **shelf within the cavity of the right ventricle** by fusion of the proximal parts of the cushions dividing the outflow tract into aortic and pulmonary components. This process begins to transfer the larger part of the persisting interventricular communication into the outflow tract for the left ventricle (Figure 18).

![](_page_22_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 18. Process of transfer of the aortic root to the left ventricle in the developing mouse heart: A) embryonic day 13.5, where the greater part of the embryonic interventricular communication is remodelling so as to connect the aortic root with the left ventricle. This can be considered to represent the secondary interventricular communication. A persisting third component of the communication is then seen between the rightward part of the aortic root and the right ventricle; B) embryonic day 14.5, where this communication is closed by fusion of so-called tubercles derived from the superior and inferior atrioventricular (AV) cushions. This produces the membranous part of the ventricular septum and commits the secondary interventricular communication to the outflow tract for the left ventricle.**

#### Three Stages of Interventricular Communication

1.  **Primary communication:** Remodelled to form the inlet of the right ventricle
2.  **Secondary communication:** Remodelled to become the subaortic outflow tract
3.  **Tertiary communication:** Persists as a channel between right and left ventricles; closed by growth of tubercles from atrioventricular cushions to produce the membranous part of the ventricular septum

When initially closed, the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve has yet to delaminate from the surface of the ventricular septum. It is only subsequent to delamination that it becomes possible to recognise the atrioventricular and interventricular components of the membranous septum.

</div></details>

## 10. Is There a Muscular Outlet Septum in the Normal Heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

**There is no muscular septal component interposed between the ventricular outflow tracts in the normal heart.**

By virtue of the **wedging of the aortic root** between the mitral valve and the ventricular septum, the inlet of the right ventricle is separated from the outlet, rather than the inlet, of the left ventricle.

This is despite the fact that the proximal parts of the outflow cushions fuse to produce a shelf in the roof of the right ventricle, providing a channel between the developing aortic root and the cavity of the left ventricle (Figure 19).

![](_page_24_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 19. Stages in development of the freestanding infundibular sleeve of the right ventricle: A) fusion of the proximal cushions at embryonic day 13.5. This process builds a shelf in the roof of the right ventricle, committing the aortic root to the left ventricle through the secondary interventricular communication; B) oblique subcostal equivalent section at embryonic day 14.5, at the stage of closure of the tertiary interventricular communication. The shell of the muscularising proximal cushion mass is transforming into the subpulmonary infundibulum (black dashed line). The core of the cushion mass (white star with red borders) is attenuating to produce an extracavitary area between the developing infundibular sleeve and the forming sinuses of the aortic root.**

As part of this process:
*   The shell of the proximal cushions achieves a myocardial phenotype
*   The core of the cushions attenuates, producing an **extracavitary area** between the muscularising shell and the developing sinuses of the aortic root
*   The muscularising shell becomes the **freestanding infundibular sleeve**, lifting the pulmonary root away from the base of the ventricular mass

**Clinical significance:** The formation of the freestanding infundibular sleeve makes it possible to remove the entirety of the pulmonary root and use it as an autograft in the Ross procedure. This would not be possible had there been formation of a muscular outlet septum.

## 11. Is a Ventricular Septal Defect (VSD) the Same as an Interventricular Communication?</summary><div class="details-content">

During development, there is extensive remoulding of the initial embryonic interventricular communication, which can be divided into three stages:

#### Primary Interventricular Communication

The portal for passage of all blood entering the developing left ventricle to reach the undivided outflow tract.

**Defects originating from primary communication:**
*   Defects in the setting of double-inlet left ventricle with double-outlet right ventricle
*   Defects seen with straddling and overriding of the tricuspid valve

#### Secondary Interventricular Communication

The embryonic interventricular channel underneath both outflow tracts during normal expansion of the atrioventricular canal and formation of the right ventricular inlet, where outflow tracts remain supported above the developing right ventricle.

**Defects originating from secondary communication:**
*   Found between the ventricles in the setting of double-outlet right ventricle

#### Tertiary Interventricular Communication

The route by which the rightward margin of the developing aortic root remains in communication with the right ventricle during connection of the aortic root with the left ventricular cavity, while the secondary communication remoulds to become the left ventricular outflow tract.

**Defects originating from tertiary communication:**
*   Failure to close → **perimembranous ventricular septal defect**

#### Conclusion

The channels found in double-outlet right ventricle, double-inlet left ventricle with DORV, and perimembranous VSD represent **different stages of remoulding** of the embryonic interventricular communication and therefore **cannot be considered the same** ventricular septal defects.

</div></details>

## 12. Defects That Open into the Inlet of the Right Ventricle</summary><div class="details-content">

There are **four phenotypically different defects** that open to the inlet of the right ventricle. The location of the atrioventricular conduction axis differs markedly in all of them.

It is now well established that the **'septum of the atrioventricular canal'** is the muscularising vestibular spine. Its formation during normal development, along with fusion with the atrioventricular cushions, divides the initial atrioventricular canal into the orifices of the tricuspid and mitral valves.

#### Type 1: Malalignment Defect (Straddling/Overriding Tricuspid Valve)

*   Results from incomplete expansion of the atrioventricular canal relative to the atrial septum
*   **Perimembranous** — fibrous continuity between mitral and tricuspid valve leaflets
*   Conduction axis arises from an **anomalous posteroinferior atrioventricular node** (Figure 20)

#### Type 2: Muscular Inlet Defect

*   Opening into the inlet of the right ventricle has **exclusively muscular margins**
*   Conduction axis courses in **cranial fashion** relative to the defect (Figure 21A)

#### Type 3: Perimembranous Inlet Defect (with Alignment)

*   Fibrous continuity between mitral and tricuspid valve leaflets forming posterior margin

## 13. Phenotypic Feature of a Supracristal Ventricular Septal Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

For some time, ventricular septal defects were categorised as supracristal or infracristal. Examination shows a lack of logic, since rather than the defects opening in different fashion to the right ventricle, it is the structure nominated as the **'crista'** that varies.

#### Supracristal Defect

*   Opens into the right ventricle **between the limbs of the septomarginal trabeculation (SMT)** , also known as the septal band (Figure 22A)
*   The 'crista' is the myocardial structure produced by fusion of the **caudal limb of the septal trabeculation** with the **ventriculo-infundibular fold** (derived from inner heart curvature)
*   **Phenotypic feature:** Fibrous continuity between the leaflets of the aortic and pulmonary valves in the roof of the defect
*   Better described as **doubly committed and juxta-arterial**

#### Infracristal Defect

*   The 'crista' is the **supraventricular crest**
*   Formed by the freestanding subpulmonary muscular infundibulum, along with the right ventricular margin of the inner heart curvature (ventriculo-infundibular fold) (Figure 22B)

![](_page_29_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 22. Ventricular septal defects defined by the 'crista', rather than location: A) in the so-called 'supracristal defect', the myocardial structure identified as the 'crista' is produced by fusion of the ventriculo-infundibular fold with the caudal limb of the septomarginal trabeculation (white star with red borders). The phenotypic feature of this defect is the fibrous continuity between the leaflets of the aortic and pulmonary valves in the roof of the defect; B) in the so-called 'infracristal defect', the structure identified as the 'crista' is the supraventricular crest.**

#### Developmental Explanation

The differences reflect the **varying maturation of the proximal outflow cushions**:
*   In infracristal defect: proximal outflow cushions **muscularise** to form the supraventricular crest (Figure 23A)
*   In supracristal defect: proximal outflow cushions **fail to muscularise** (Figure 23B)

![](_page_30_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 23. A) The defect described by some as being 'infracristal' lies inferior to the structure derived by muscularisation of the proximal outflow cushions. B) In the defect described by some as being 'supracristal', the proximal outflow cushions have failed to muscularise. The difference between the two defects, therefore, reflects the varying maturation of the proximal outflow cushions, rather than the way the defects open relative to the ventricular septum.**

## 14. Phenotypic Features of Tetralogy of Fallot</summary><div class="details-content">

Tetralogy of Fallot is a combination of **four morphological features**:
1.  Interventricular communication
2.  Biventricular connection of the aortic root
3.  Subpulmonary stenosis
4.  Right ventricular hypertrophy

There is a wide range of combinations of these features, resulting in **no two cases being morphologically identical**.

#### The Unifying Feature

For some time, it was thought that **anterocephalad malalignment of the muscular outlet septum** (or its fibrous remnant) was the unifying feature. Such a feature, however, is also found in Eisenmenger ventricular septal defect (perimembranous outlet defect with overriding of the pulmonary trunk).

The additional finding required to produce the phenotypical feature of tetralogy is the **anomalous formation of the septoparietal trabeculations**. In the tetralogy spectrum, the trabeculations are usually **hypertrophied**.

It is the **combination** of these two features that produces the characteristic narrowing at the mouth of the subpulmonary infundibulum (Figure 24).

![](_page_32_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 24. Images demonstrating malalignment of the muscular outlet septum, which in isolation, does not produce the phenotypical feature of tetralogy of Fallot: A) Eisenmenger defect where the subpulmonary outflow tract is unobstructed despite the presence of malalignment of the outlet septum, along with well-formed septoparietal trabeculations; B) tetralogy of Fallot, characterised by the phenotypical narrowing between the malaligned muscular outlet septum and the septoparietal trabeculations.**

#### Morphological Variability

Hearts with this phenotypical feature can show variability in:

| Feature | Variations |
|---------|------------|
| **Type of interventricular communication** | Perimembranous; muscular posteroinferior rim; doubly committed and juxta-arterial |
| **Extent of aortic override** | Variable |
| **Degree of subpulmonary obstruction** | Variable |
| **Right ventricular hypertrophy** | Haemodynamic consequence of subpulmonary narrowing |

## 15. Phenotypical Characteristics of Defects Associated with Systemic-to-Pulmonary Collateral Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

In pulmonary atresia, the pulmonary arterial supply is derived most frequently either through:
*   A **persistently patent arterial duct**
*   **Systemic-to-pulmonary collateral arteries**

**Systemic-to-pulmonary** is a better descriptor than major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs), since collateral arteries can arise not only from the aorta but also from coronary or brachiocephalic arteries.

#### Key Principles

These two sources **hardly ever supply the same lung**. A good working rule: if systemic-to-pulmonary collateral arteries supply one or both lungs, the arterial duct will be absent, unless it provides the sole supply to one lung through a discontinuous intrapericardial pulmonary artery.

#### Sources of Pulmonary Blood Supply

| Setting | Common Source |
|---------|---------------|
| Pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum | Persistently patent arterial duct (almost without exception) |
| Pulmonary atresia with VSD (in transposition, corrected transposition, isomerism) | Arterial duct possible |
| Tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia | Systemic-to-pulmonary collateral arteries (frequent) or arterial duct |

#### Characteristics of Collateral Supply

| Supply Type | Pattern |
|-------------|---------|
| **Arterial duct** | Unifocal supply to all bronchopulmonary segments |
| **Systemic-to-pulmonary collaterals** | Multifocal — solitary collateral artery hardly ever supplies all bronchopulmonary segments; supply is direct or through anastomoses with intrapericardial pulmonary arteries |

![](_page_34_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 25. Images demonstrating systemic-to-pulmonary collateral arteries in the setting of pulmonary atresia, with a supply through: A) a persistently patent arterial duct to confluent intrapericardial pulmonary arteries (where the intrapericardial pulmonary arteries supply all of the bronchopulmonary segments in a unifocal fashion); and B) systemic-to-pulmonary collateral arteries arising from the descending intrathoracic aorta, with coexisting confluent intrapericardial pulmonary arteries, which anastomose with the collateral arteries. In these instances, the intrapericardial arteries have a limited multifocal supply to some, but not all, of the bronchopulmonary segments.**

#### Rare Variations

*   Aortopulmonary window
*   Coronary arterial fistulous communications
*   Discontinuous pulmonary arteries fed through bilateral arterial ducts, or duct in one lung and collaterals in the other

**Note:** Arterial ducts with unusual origin were previously described as 'arteries of the 5th pharyngeal arch'. It is now known that the so-called '5th arch' does not exist.

It is a mistake to distinguish intrapericardial pulmonary arteries as 'native' — the systemic-to-pulmonary collateral arteries are equally 'native'.

## 16. Double-Outlet Right Ventricle</summary><div class="details-content">

Double-outlet right ventricle (DORV) is the situation in which the **larger part of both arterial roots** are supported by the morphologically right ventricle.

The definition of this arrangement has long been contentious. Resolution has been provided by the concept of the **'morphological method'** , which states that one variable feature in the heart should not be defined on the basis of another feature that is itself variable.

#### Determinant of DORV

Only the **proportion of the overriding arterial trunk supported by the right, as opposed to the left ventricle**, determines the precise ventriculo-arterial connection. DORV is no more than one of the possible ventriculo-arterial connections in the setting of overriding of either the aortic or the pulmonary root (or both).

In this setting, the channel overridden by the arterial root will have right and left ventricular borders.

#### Surgical Determinant

Whether the root is supported predominantly by the right ventricle (and hence in DORV) can be determined by whether the surgeon considers that they are able to **close the right ventricular border**:

| Surgical Finding | Interpretation |
|------------------|----------------|
| Surgeon can close the right ventricular border | Ventriculo-arterial connections were concordant or discordant; surgeon closed a 'ventricular septal defect' |
| Surgeon must tunnel the right ventricular border of the overriding root to the crest of the muscular ventricular septum | Ventriculo-arterial connection was DORV; leftward border of the defect was an interventricular communication, not a 'ventricular septal defect' |

![](_page_36_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 26. The patch includes a small portion or the right ventricular cavity to redirect the flow from the left ventricle to the aorta; and C) the aorta is committed to the right ventricle and the plane of the interventricular communication and that of the patch are on the opposite sides of the aortic root, as in double-outlet right ventricle. A large portion of the right ventricular cavity is required to redirect the flow from the left ventricle to the aorta.**

## 17. Functionally Univentricular Heart</summary><div class="details-content">

A functionally univentricular heart is characterised by an **incomplete ventricle** of insufficient size to support independently either the pulmonary or the systemic circulation:

*   Dominant left ventricle with incomplete right ventricle
*   Dominant right ventricle with incomplete left ventricle

#### True Solitary Ventricles

Very few congenitally malformed hearts have a solitary chamber within the ventricular mass. If found, such solitary ventricles are of **indeterminate ventricular morphology**, although occasionally hearts can be found with a dominant right ventricle when the accompanying left ventricle is so small as to escape clinical detection.

#### Hearts with Univentricular Atrioventricular Connections

The majority of hearts previously described as 'single ventricles' have a **dominant left ventricle**, with incomplete formation of the right ventricle due to absence of its inlet component. This is found either when:

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Double-inlet left ventricle (DILV)** | Both atrioventricular junctions connected to the dominant left ventricle |
| **Absence of right or left atrioventricular connection** | Most frequently tricuspid atresia |

#### Hearts with Biventricular AV Connections but Functionally Univentricular

| Condition | Mechanism |
|-----------|-----------|
| Hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) | Morphological left ventricle too small |
| Pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum (PA-IVS) | Hypoplastic right ventricle |
| Severely unbalanced AVSD | One ventricle too small |

Other hearts with complex circulatory patterns may be deemed functionally univentricular and better suited for surgical repair by construction of the **Fontan circulation**.

## 19. Developmental Background of Common Arterial Trunk</summary><div class="details-content">

The phenotypic feature of common arterial trunk is the **commonality of the ventriculo-arterial junction**, rather than the presence of a common intrapericardial arterial component or 'truncus'.

The essential feature is persistence of the **undivided embryonic 'conus'** , specifically the intermediate and proximal components of the outflow tract, rather than lack of division of its distal component (Figure 29).

![](_page_41_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 29. Embryological images demonstrating two embryonic mice genetically modified so as to produce common arterial trunk, defined on the basis of a trunk that gives rise directly to the systemic, pulmonary and coronary arteries: A) following knock-out of the Tbx1 gene, there has been no separation of the distal outflow tract, so that the trunk has an aortic dominance; B) following disruption of the Furin enzyme, the aortopulmonary septum (white arrow) has grown so as to divide the distal outflow tract disproportionately in favour of the pulmonary component of the trunk. The aortic arch is interrupted (white star with red borders), with the descending aorta fed through the persistently patent arterial duct. The left subclavian artery (LSCA) arises from the descending aorta. AV = atrioventricular; VA = ventriculo-arterial.**

#### Historical Classification

When 'persistent truncus arteriosus' was first introduced, it described the distal part of the developing outflow tract. The manner of separation of the distal outflow tract has been used to subcategorise examples of common arterial trunk:

| Classification | Basis |
|----------------|-------|
| **Collett and Edwards (4 types)** | Origin of pulmonary arteries; Type IV later recognised as solitary arterial trunk (no intrapericardial pulmonary arteries) |
| **Van Praagh and Van Praagh** | Types I and II unified as Types 1 and 2; Types 3 (discontinuous pulmonary arteries) and 4 (hypoplasia of aortic component) added |

#### Simplified Approach

A simpler approach separates variants according to **dominance of the aortic vs pulmonary component**:

| Dominance | Associated Features |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Pulmonary dominance** | Interruption of aortic arch or severe coarctation |
| **Aortic dominance** | Describe origin of pulmonary arteries precisely |

In most aortic-dominant cases, pulmonary arteries arise in close proximity from the posterior or leftward component of the trunk (frequently described as 'Type 1½'). The origin of pulmonary arteries, however, can involve one of the sinuses of the truncal root, and the right and left pulmonary arteries can cross as they extend towards the mediastinum.

## 20. Transposition</summary><div class="details-content">

Transposition literally means **'placed across'** and refers to the placement of the arterial roots across the ventricular septum, such that they arise from morphologically inappropriate ventricles.

Irrespective of the atrioventricular connections (concordant, discordant, mixed in isomerism, or univentricular), the transposition is always **'complete'** in the sense that each of the arterial trunks is completely placed across the septum.

#### Terminology

The commonest variant is simply described as **'transposition'** . The qualification 'of the great arteries' is redundant.

#### Morphological Variants

Discordant ventriculo-arterial connections can be found with:
*   Various relationships of the arterial trunks
*   Varied infundibular morphology

The combination of discordant VA connections with concordant AV connections can be found in usual or mirror-imaged variants.

| Relationship | Description |
|--------------|-------------|
| **Most usual** | Aortic root anteriorly and to the right |
| **Leftward aortic root** | Rule when transposition is found in its mirror-imaged variant |

**Note:** It is inappropriate to use 'd-transposition' as the default option for transposition.

#### Associated Malformations

The associated malformations of greatest clinical significance are:
*   Ventricular septal defect
*   Obstruction of the ventricular outflow tracts

#### Developmental Basis

The developmental defect underscoring discordant VA connections is the fusion of the major outflow cushions in a **straight** as opposed to their normal **spiralling** fashion (Figure 30).

![](_page_43_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 30. Embryological images demonstrating the arrangement of the outflow cushions in a: A) control mouse; and B) mouse with knock-out of the Ptx1 gene. Ongoing development in the mice with the knock-out gene produces either transposition or double-outlet right ventricle with subpulmonary interventricular communication. It is the fusion of the outflow cushions in a straight as opposed to spiralling fashion that underscores these changes.**

This straight fusion serves to join:
*   Rightward component of proximal outflow tract → aortic component of aortic sac
*   Leftward component of proximal outflow tract → pulmonary component

The leftward component of the outflow tract, which is closest to the embryonic interventricular communication, is transferred to the developing left ventricle, producing discordant VA connections.

## 21. How Can Transposition Be Congenitally Corrected?</summary><div class="details-content">

When discordant ventriculo-arterial connections (transposition) are associated with **concordant** atrioventricular connections, the systemic and pulmonary circulations are arranged in **parallel**, rather than in series.

Connections across the atrioventricular junctions in a **discordant** fashion, as opposed to concordant, serve to **'correct'** the discordant VA connections. By virtue of **double discordance**, the circulations are again in series, even though the arterial trunks are supported by morphologically inappropriate ventricles.

#### Developmental Mechanism

This occurs as a consequence of looping of the ventricular component of the heart tube in a **mirror-imaged fashion** compared to normal:

| Looping Direction | AV Connection Result |
|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Rightward looping** (usual) | Developing right atrium → developing right ventricle → concordant AV connections |
| **Leftward looping** | Developing left atrium → right ventricle (derived from outlet component, left-sided) → discordant AV connections |

In the setting of **mirror-imaged atrial arrangement**, it will be **rightward** looping of the ventricular component that produces discordant AV connections.

#### Associated Connections

| Association | Result |
|-------------|--------|
| Discordant AV + discordant VA | Congenitally corrected transposition (double discordance) |
| Discordant AV + double-outlet right ventricle | Possible variant |
| Discordant AV + concordant VA | Haemodynamic picture of regular transposition (rare) |

</div></details>

## 22. Classification System for Origin of the Coronary Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

Almost always, the major coronary arteries arise from one or other (usually both) of the sinuses of the aortic root that are **adjacent to the pulmonary root**.

#### Embryological Origin

Endothelial stems grow out from the developing aortic root during separation of the intermediate part of the outflow tract. The stems initially develop **distal** to the myocardial turret surrounding the intermediate outflow tract. They are incorporated into the sinuses as the sinuses themselves expand proximally relative to the myocardial boundary.

#### Normal Development

*   Leftward aortic valvar sinus (adjacent to pulmonary root) → anterior interventricular and circumflex arteries
*   Rightward adjacent sinus → right coronary artery

#### Leiden Classification

As viewed by an observer **standing upright in the sinus that is not adjacent to the pulmonary trunk**:

| Sinus | Position | Nomenclature |
|-------|----------|--------------|
| One sinus | To observer's right hand | #1 |
| Other sinus | To observer's left hand | #2 |

This arrangement remains constant irrespective of the relationship of the arterial roots to each other (Figure 31).

![](_page_46_Figure_2.jpeg)

## 23. Aortopulmonary Septum</summary><div class="details-content">

In the postnatal heart, each of the aortic and pulmonary pathways can be considered as possessing:
*   Ventricular outflow tract
*   Arterial root
*   Intrapericardial and extrapericardial arterial components

#### A Better Approach to Outflow Tract Development

The outflow tract should be considered as possessing **proximal, intermediate, and distal components**, with the cavity of the distal component becoming continuous with the cavity of the aortic sac at the margins of the pericardial cavity.

The **intermediate component** can be recognised anatomically by the appearance of **intercalated swellings** within its lumen. The distal ends of these swellings, along with the major outflow cushions, will cavitate to produce the leaflets of the arterial valves, with the arterial roots forming within the intermediate component (Figure 32).

![](_page_48_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 32. Long axis of the developing outflow tract of the mouse heart, subsequent to the formation of the so-called intercalated swellings (white stars with red borders). The proximal part will become separated into the ventricular outflow tracts, with the intermediate part being transformed into the arterial roots, and the distal part forming the intrapericardial arterial trunks. The dotted white line shows the site of fusion between the aortopulmonary septum and the major cushions within the outflow tract. This obliterates the embryonic aortopulmonary foramen.**

#### Aortopulmonary Septum Formation

Earlier in development, the **aortic sac** gives rise to the arteries of the pharyngeal arches:
*   Arteries of 3rd and 4th arches → systemic pathways
*   Arteries of pulmonary arches → right and left pulmonary arteries

At this stage, it is the **dorsal wall of the aortic sac** that represents the aortopulmonary septum (Figure 33).

With ongoing development, the dorsal wall of the aortic sac grows and protrudes into the cavity of the distal outflow tract, separating it into the intrapericardial components of the aorta and the pulmonary trunk. This protrusion is the **aortopulmonary septum**.

#### Fusion and Malformations

| Process | Result if Successful | Result if Failed |
|---------|---------------------|------------------|
| Protrusion fuses with distal ends of major outflow cushions | Obliterates embryonic aortopulmonary foramen | Persistence of aortopulmonary window |
| Major cushions fuse with each other | Separates arterial roots distally and ventricular outflow tracts proximally | Common arterial trunk |

The white arrows with red borders show the junction between the distal outflow tract and the aortic sac at the margins of the pericardial cavity; B) the dorsal wall of the aortic sac has protruded into the cavity of the distal outflow tract, separating the intrapericardial components of the developing aorta and pulmonary trunk. ## 24. Embryological Origin of Vascular Rings</summary><div class="details-content">

Multiple arrangements of the extrapericardial branches of the aorta encircle the tracheo-oesophageal pedicle and produce **'dysphagia lusorum'** . All of these patterns are well explained on the basis of the **hypothetical double arch** as proposed by Edwards (Figure 34).

![](_page_50_Picture_6.jpeg)

Each arch gives rise to a common carotid artery and a subclavian artery from its cranial surface, and an arterial duct from its dorsal surface. The various vascular rings, along with isolation of the brachiocephalic arteries, are all explained on the basis of attenuation and disappearance of the different components of the double arch, including the arterial ducts.**

#### Validation from Development

Although six sets of pharyngeal arch arteries tend to be shown in classical diagrams, it is only the arteries of the **3rd, 4th, and pulmonary arches** that provide the vessels shown by Edwards in the hypothetical model (Figure 34).

During early development, these arteries are **bilaterally symmetrical** and encircle the developing tracheo-oesophageal pedicle (Figure 35).

![](_page_51_Figure_7.jpeg)

With ongoing normal development, the **right-sided components** of these bilateral symmetrical primordiums undergo attenuation and the components encircling the pedicle to join the descending aorta disappear. ## 25. Origin of the Arterial Duct</summary><div class="details-content">

The arterial duct (ductus arteriosus) is an integral part of the foetal circulation, ensuring that deoxygenated systemic venous return, having passed through the right ventricle, is returned to the placenta, bypassing the lungs.

#### Developmental Origin

From a developmental standpoint, the arterial duct is derived from the artery of the pharyngeal arch previously described as '6th'. **There is never a 5th arch**. The so-called '6th arch' is better described as the **pulmonary arch**.

#### Development of Pharyngeal Arch Arteries

During early development, the arteries of the pharyngeal arches are **bilaterally symmetrical**. By the time the arteries of the pulmonary arches are recognisable:
*   Arteries of the 1st and 2nd arches have effectively disappeared, becoming incorporated into arteries supplying the head and face
*   Symmetrical arteries extend through the **3rd, 4th, and pulmonary arches** (Figure 36)

![](_page_53_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 36. Arrangement of the arteries of the pharyngeal arches in the developing mouse at embryonic day 10.5. The arteries of the 3rd, 4th and pulmonary arches are bilaterally symmetrical. They extend through the pharyngeal mesenchyme before merging dorsally to form the descending aorta. The 7th cervical intersegmental arteries, which will become the subclavian arteries, arise from the dorsal aorta at this early stage of development.** *Image created and reproduced with permission by Dr. Simon Bamforth, Newcastle University, UK.*

With ongoing development, the **right-sided components** of the bilateral symmetrical arrangement largely disappear, leaving:
*   **Left pulmonary arch artery** → arterial duct
*   **Left 4th arch artery** → transverse component of the aortic arch (Figure 37)

![](_page_54_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 37. Reconstruction of the cavities of the right (blue) and left (brown) ventricles, along with the extent of the pericardial cavity (green) in the developing mouse at the end of embryonic day 12.5. The artery of the left pulmonary pharyngeal arch has become the arterial duct, while the artery of the left 4th arch has become the transverse aortic arch. Note that the right and left pulmonary arteries develop within the pharyngeal mesenchyma, taking their origin from the caudal component of the aortic sac. The developing subclavian artery (white arrow with red borders) still retains its origin from the descending aorta at this stage of development.** *Image created and reproduced with permission by Dr. Simon Bamforth, Newcastle University, UK.*

#### Subclavian Artery Migration

When first formed, subclavian arteries (derived from the 7th cervical intersegmental arteries) take origin from the descending aorta. Only later in development do they migrate cranially to arise from the transverse aortic arch, crossing the insertion of the duct to the descending aorta during this process.

The **isthmus of the aortic arch** is the component between the origin of the left subclavian artery and the junction with the arterial duct. It cannot be defined until the left subclavian artery achieves its definitive position.

The right and left pulmonary arteries develop within the pharyngeal mesenchyme, taking origin from the caudal component of the aortic sac, which gives rise initially to the bilaterally symmetrical arteries of the pulmonary arches.

## 26. Is There an Artery of the 5th Pharyngeal Arch?</summary><div class="details-content">

It is frequent to find aberrant arterial channels in patients with congenitally malformed hearts interpreted in terms of **'5th arch arteries'** . The classical diagram for developing pharyngeal arch arteries does, indeed, illustrate six pairs of bilaterally symmetrical channels (Figure 38).

![](_page_55_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 38. Classical 'Rathke' diagram showing the developing arteries of the pharyngeal arches. The purported 5th arch arteries are shown with dotted lines, since their existence remains contentious. The 7th cervical intersegmental arteries (white arrows with red borders) originate from the descending aorta (white star with red borders) and later in development become the subclavian arteries.**

#### Evidence for 5th Arch Arteries

To the best of the authors' knowledge, there is but a **solitary example** thus far identified of an attenuating artery of the 5th arch, enclosed within its own segment of pharyngeal mesenchyme (Figure 39).

![](_page_56_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 39. Reconstruction from the left side of the pharyngeal region of a development human embryo at Carnegie stage 15. It demonstrates attenuation of the left-sided artery that initially coursed between the aortic sac and the descending aorta parallel to the arteries of the left 4th and left pulmonary arches. This arterial channel was embedded within its own segment of pharyngeal mesenchyme. Although initially interpreted as a '5th arch artery', we now consider it better described as a persisting collateral channel.**

Arteries extending between the aortic sac and the descending aorta at the site of the postulated 5th pharyngeal arch are **exceedingly rare**.

#### Alternative Explanation

**Collateral channels** extending between the posterior terminations of the arteries of the 4th and pulmonary arches are found in up to half of all developing mouse embryos, and in a comparable number of human embryos.

The majority of vascular channels interpreted as persistence of the hypothetical 5th pharyngeal arch artery are better explained on the basis of:
*   Presence of such collateral channels, or
*   Remodelling of the walls of the aortic sac

## 27. Anatomy of the Sinus Node and Atrial Conduction</summary><div class="details-content">

To this day, no evidence has ever been presented to show that postulated **internodal atrial conducting tracts** or **'specialised internodal pathways'** extending through the atrial myocardium to join the sinus and atrioventricular nodes have been identified and isolated from the remainder of the atrial myocardium.

#### Sinus Node Anatomy

Histological examination demonstrates the sinus node to be a **well-defined anatomical entity**. The sinus node can be recognised as occupying the **epicardial aspect of the terminal groove** at the superior cavoatrial junction, usually located inferior to the crest of the right atrial appendage. Its borders are well demarcated, with no extensions of nodal cardiomyocytes identifiable as extending into the adjacent atrial myocardium (Figure 40).

![](_page_57_Picture_6.jpeg)

![](_page_57_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 40. A) Operative image demonstrating the superior cavoatrial junction, with terminal groove (yellow dotted line) and anticipated location of the sinus node (yellow oval). B) Histological section (taken from the site of the yellow solid line) demonstrating the sinoatrial node aggregated around a prominent artery. It has a discrete boundary (blue dashed line) from the adjacent myocardium of the terminal crest and superior caval vein. There are no insulated tracts identifiable extending from the node into the adjacent atrial wall.**

#### Atrial Conduction

The specialised cardiomyocytes activate the adjacent atrial myocardium at the **margins of the node**. The prominent myocardial bundles within the right atrium, such as:
*   Terminal crest
*   Bachman's bundle
*   Margins of the oval fossa

serve to conduct the impulse generated within the sinus node towards the atrioventricular node at the apex of the triangle of Koch.

The pathways within the major myocardial atrial bundles are all composed of **ordinary working atrial cardiomyocytes**. There is nothing 'specialised', either histologically or electrophysiologically, regarding these pathways. Rather, the **preferential conduction** through the major pathways is dictated by the **parallel alignment** of the working cardiomyocytes making up the bundles.

## 28. Anatomical Location of the Atrioventricular Conduction Axis</summary><div class="details-content">

Although the specialised conduction tissues themselves are invisible to the cardiac surgeon, the landmarks regarding their anatomical disposition are sufficiently robust to permit recognition in all patients with **concordant atrioventricular connections**.

#### Triangle of Koch

The atrioventricular node is located at the **apex of the triangle of Koch**. The borders of the triangle are:
*   **Atrial border:** Extension from the Eustachian valve into the myocardium separating the inferior border of the oval fossa from the orifice of the coronary sinus
*   **Ventricular border:** Attachment of the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve
*   **Apex:** Atrioventricular component of the membranous septum (where the borders come together)

#### Course of the Conduction Axis

The atrioventricular bundle (bundle of His) passes through the atrioventricular component of the membranous septum to reach the crest of the muscular ventricular septum. It branches on the crest of the septum, or just below it, into the right and left bundle branches.

The right bundle branch courses through the muscular ventricular septum, emerging on the right ventricular surface in relation to the **medial papillary muscle** (muscle of Lancisi, or conal papillary muscle).

A line drawn from the **apex of the triangle of Koch to the medial papillary muscle** shows the anticipated course of the atrioventricular conduction axis (Figure 41).

![](_page_59_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 41. Macroscopic image depicting the location of the atrioventricular conduction axis in a heart with concordant atrioventricular connections. The right atrioventricular junction is shown, in an attitudinally appropriate orientation, from the right side, having opened the junction and reflected its parietal wall. The location of the axis is shown by the red line joining the apex of the triangle of Koch with the medial papillary muscle.**

#### Surgical Implications

Providing the surgeon keeps all operative manoeuvres **outside the boundaries of the triangle of Koch**, no damage will be inflicted to the atrioventricular node. The landmarks indicating the site of the axis remain consistent in all hearts with deficient ventricular septation when the atrioventricular connections are concordant.

#### Variations in Abnormal Hearts

| Setting | Location of Connecting AV Node |
|---------|-------------------------------|
| Malalignment between atrial and ventricular septal components | At site of insertion of malaligned ventricular septum to inferior AV junction (e.g., straddling tricuspid valve) |
| Double-inlet left ventricle or discordant AV connections | Anterior, beneath the mouth of the right atrial appendage |
