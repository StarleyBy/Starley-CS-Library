# Aortopulmonary Window

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Embryology
- **Aortopulmonary window (APW):** Communication between ascending aorta and main pulmonary artery with **two distinct semilunar valves** (distinguishes from truncus arteriosus)
- **Embryology:** Failure of complete fusion of conotruncal ridges during 5th week of gestation → persistent communication

### Classification
| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| I (proximal) | Just above sinus of Valsalva, little inferior rim |
| II (distal) | Uppermost ascending aorta, little superior rim |
| III (total) | Majority of ascending aorta, little superior/inferior rims |
| IV (intermediate) | Similar to total but with well-formed rims |

### Epidemiology
- Incidence: **0.1–0.2%** of all cardiac malformations; M:F **~2:1**

### Associated Anomalies (25–60% of patients)
- Most common: PDA (21%), IAA/coarctation (21%), VSD (19%), ASD/PFO (19%)
- Coronary anomalies (2%; RCA most common; mortality 16% if present)
- **VACTERL** association (2–10%); **no 22q11 association**

### Pathophysiology and Natural History
- Foetal: minimal flow (equal pressures)
- Postnatal: PVR drops → unrestrictive L→R shunt → pulmonary congestion, heart failure
- Untreated: ~40% mortality in first year; does not close spontaneously

### Diagnosis
- **Echo (gold standard):** Deficiency between aortic and PA walls
- ECG: non-specific (tachycardia, biventricular hypertrophy)
- CXR: cardiomegaly, prominent MPA, increased PVM

### Treatment
- **Closure indicated in all patients** as soon as diagnosed
- **Surgical repair:** Median sternotomy, CPB; direct suture or patch; single-stage repair of all defects
- **Coronary management:** Leave ostium on aortic side; reimplant if needed
- **Device closure:** Rare, selected patients (defect size 3–8 mm)

### Outcomes
- Simple APW: mortality **near 0%**; no late deaths; no reinterventions
- APW + IAA: mortality 0–15%; survival 84% at 10 years; reintervention ~57% at 5 years

## 1. Definition and Embryology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. What is aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Aortopulmonary window** is a communication between the ascending aorta and the main pulmonary artery in the presence of two distinct ventriculo-arterial or semilunar valves.

The presence of **two separate semilunar valves** distinguishes aortopulmonary window from truncus arteriosus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. Describe the embryological formation of the conotruncus</summary><div class="details-content">

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| **3rd week** | Tubular heart elongates → sinus venosus, atrium, ventricle, bulbus cordis |
| **Later** | Bulbus cordis → common outflow tract (truncus arteriosus); together = **conotruncus** |
| **5th week** | Mesenchymal cell proliferation → swellings: wedge between 4th and 6th aortic arches; bulbar ridges; truncal ridges (collectively = conotruncal ridges) |
| **Development** | Conotruncal ridges run in **spiral course** (not straight line) — required for RV → pulmonary and LV → systemic connections |
| **Fusion** | Wedge projects inferiorly and fuses with conotruncal ridges; fusion occurs distally → proximally → forms **aortopulmonary septum** |
| **Result** | Aorta and pulmonary trunk twist around each other in **helical arrangement** |
| **Fate** | Truncus arteriosus → proximal ascending aorta + pulmonary trunk; bulbus cordis → RV infundibulum |
| **Semilunar valves** | Develop from three swellings around each orifice; completed by **9th week** |

## 2. Classification</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. Describe the classification of aortopulmonary window</summary><div class="details-content">

**Mori & Ho / STS Classification (Figure 2):**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Type I (proximal defect)** | Window just above sinus of Valsalva (aortic sinus), few mm above semilunar valve; superior rim present, little inferior rim |
| **Type II (distal defect)** | Window in uppermost portion of ascending aorta; well-formed inferior rim, little superior rim |
| **Type III (total defect)** | Window involving majority of ascending aorta; little superior or inferior rims |
| **Type IV (intermediate defect)** | Similar to total defect but with well-formed superior and inferior rims |

![](_page_3_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.** Classification of aortopulmonary window: A) Type I (proximal); B) Type II (distal); C) Type III (total); D) Type IV (intermediate).

**Richardson Classification (1979) (Figure 3):**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Type I** | 'Typical' APW between posteromedial wall of ascending aorta and MPA |
| **Type II** | Between posterior wall of ascending aorta and origin of RPA |
| **Type III** | Anomalous origin of RPA from posterolateral wall of ascending aorta (often referred to as "hemitruncus arteriosus" — **misnomer**) |

![](_page_4_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 3.** Richardson classification: A) Type I; B) Type II; C) Type III.

The term **'hemitruncus'** is a misnomer — it implies that the anomalous vessel arises from a common arterial trunk, whereas it actually arises from the ascending aorta.

**Correct terminology:** Anomalous origin of the right pulmonary artery from the ascending aorta with or without concomitant aortopulmonary window (Figure 4).

![](_page_4_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4.** A) Anomalous origin of RPA from ascending aorta with concomitant APW; B) Without concomitant APW.

## 3. Epidemiology and Associated Anomalies</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. What is the epidemiology of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Aortopulmonary window accounts for **0.1–0.2%** of all cardiac malformations
- Male-to-female prevalence: approximately **2:1**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. What cardiovascular abnormalities are associated with aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

Approximately **25–60%** of patients with APW have at least one concomitant cardiovascular anomaly (Table 1).

**Table 1. Cardiovascular anomalies associated with aortopulmonary window**

| Cardiovascular anomaly | Published literature (average, %) | Melbourne RCH (n=43, %) |
|---|---|---|
| Patent ductus arteriosus | 21.3 | 27.9 |
| Interrupted aortic arch (mostly Type A)/coarctation | 20.8 | 20.9 |
| Ventricular septal defect | 19.4 | 20.8 |
| Atrial septal defect/patent foramen ovale | 19.3 | 34.9 |
| Tetralogy of Fallot | 5.0 | 4.7 |
| Right aortic arch | 5.0 | 4.7 |
| Persistent left superior vena cava | 3.7 | 9.3 |
| Anomalous origin of coronary artery | 2.0 | 2.3 |
| Origin of RPA from ascending aorta | 2.0 | 2.3 |
| Transposition of the great vessels | 1.9 | 0 |
| Aortic valve stenosis | 1.7 | 0 |
| Pulmonary atresia | 1.6 | 0 |
| Pulmonary valve stenosis | 1.3 | 4.7 |

**Key points:**
- Most frequent associations: **PDA** and **interrupted aortic arch**
- 6–25% have APW with PDA but without IAA
- Average IAA prevalence 21% (can reach 50% in some series); when present, **85% Type A**, **15% Type B** (Figure 5)
- APW is **not particularly prevalent** in patients with IAA
- Most VSDs are **perimembranous** (78% perimembranous, 22% muscular in Melbourne series)

![](_page_6_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5.** APW with concomitant Type A or B interrupted aortic arch (Richardson classification).

**Coronary anomalies (Figure 6):**
- Uncommon but vital to delineate (mortality 16% in 25 reported cases)
- RCA most commonly involved
- Anomalous arteries tend to arise from MPA or from APW itself

![](_page_7_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 6.** A) APW with normal coronary origins; B) Anomalous RCA origin from APW; C) Anomalous RCA origin from MPA with APW. LCA = left coronary artery; RCA = right coronary artery.

**Less frequent associations:** Tricuspid atresia, subaortic stenosis, pulmonary artery stenosis, aberrant subclavian artery, absent left pulmonary artery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 6. Which extracardiac anomalies are associated with aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **VACTERL association** (2–10% of APW patients; 2.3% in Melbourne series):
  - Vertebral anomalies
  - Imperforate anus
  - Tracheo-oesophageal fistula with oesophageal atresia
  - Cardiac anomalies
  - Renal dysplasia
  - Limb defects
- APW **does not seem to be associated** with 22q11 anomalies or DiGeorge syndrome

</div></details>

## 4. Pathophysiology and Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 7. What is the pathophysiology of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

| Period | Pathophysiology |
|---|---|
| **Gestation** | Equal aortic and PA pressures (high PVR) → minimal flow across APW |
| **Postnatal (first 5–7 days)** | PVR decreases progressively → **unrestrictive L→R shunt** |
| **Consequences** | Sustained ↑ pulmonary blood flow → severe pulmonary congestion + LV volume overload → biventricular congestive heart failure |
| **Long-term** | Antegrade pressure overload → pulmonary vascular changes → pulmonary hypertension → **Eisenmenger syndrome** (irreversible) |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 8. What is the natural history of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Untreated APW:** Poor outcome
- **~40% mortality within first year of life** (usually from congestive heart failure sequelae)
- Small defects may not manifest clinically, but APW **does not close or contract over time** → disease progression likely
- Symptom progression and timing of deterioration depend on defect size and development of heart failure or Eisenmenger syndrome

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 5. Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 9. What are the symptoms of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Depend on degree of restriction to shunting and pulmonary overflow/cardiac failure
- **Small APW:** Minimal or no symptoms
- **Large APW (typical):** Presentation within first weeks of life with congestive heart failure symptoms:
  - Dyspnoea
  - Diaphoresis
  - Poor feeding
  - Failure to thrive
  - Frequent respiratory infections

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 10. What are the signs of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

Physical signs determined by defect size and magnitude of L→R shunt:

- Tachypnoeic infant using accessory muscles
- Chest wall retraction
- Enlarged heart
- **Systolic murmur** best heard along left sternal border
- **Loud S₂** (pulmonary hypertension)
- Cyanosis usually absent (unless severe pulmonary vascular disease)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 11. What are the differential diagnoses of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Patent ductus arteriosus
- Large ventricular septal defect
- Atrioventricular septal defect with significant ventricular component
- Persistent truncus arteriosus
- Ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm

</div></details>

## 6. Diagnostic Imaging</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 12. What are the electrocardiographic features of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **No specific findings** for APW
- Findings vary with disease progression:
  - Tachycardia
  - Univentricular or biventricular hypertrophy
  - Signs of atrial enlargement (Figure 7)

![](_page_9_Figure_13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7.** ECG of 5-month-old with APW demonstrating biventricular hypertrophy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 13. What are the features of aortopulmonary window on chest radiograph?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Cardiomegaly
- Prominent main pulmonary artery
- Increased pulmonary vascular markings (increased pulmonary blood flow) (Figure 8)

![](_page_10_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8.** CXR of 5-month-old with APW: prominent MPA, enlarged heart, congested hilar regions.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 14. What are the echocardiographic features of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Foetal echocardiography:** Principal diagnostic tool, but APW may be overlooked (over-reliance on Doppler shunt interrogation — ineffective in foetus).

**Postnatal diagnosis:** Relies on recognition of **deficiency between adjacent walls of aorta and pulmonary trunk**. Use multiple views: praecordial, subcostal, suprasternal (Figure 9).

![](_page_11_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9.** Echocardiogram of 5-month-old with APW: A) 2D image — communication between ascending aorta and MPA (green arrow); B) Colour Doppler — L→R shunt (white arrow). Ao = aorta; MPA = main pulmonary artery.

**Important measurements:**
- Distance between semilunar valves and coronary arteries from proximal end of defect
- Distance between branch PA and distal end of APW

**Other common findings:**
- Enlargement of left and/or right heart chambers
- Increased pulmonary venous return
- Increased flow across mitral and aortic valves
- Enlarged pulmonary arteries
- Reversal of diastolic flow in descending aorta
- Prominent pulsatility of thoracic aorta

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 15. Describe the use of other imaging modalities for assessing aortopulmonary window</summary><div class="details-content">

| Modality | Role |
|---|---|
| **CT** | Better delineation of great arteries and coronary anatomy than echo; seldom required for diagnosis; usually for concomitant cardiac/extracardiac anomalies |
| **Cardiac catheterisation** | Not often performed; may be considered in patients >6 months to evaluate PVR and reversibility of pulmonary hypertension |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 7. Treatment</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 16. What are the therapeutic options for a patient with aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Indication for closure:** **All patients** as soon as diagnosis made (prevent heart failure or irreversible pulmonary hypertension)

**Contraindication only:** Demonstrated irreversible pulmonary hypertension (suspected by reversal of shunt flow)

**Goal:** Improve systemic circulation, limit pulmonary blood flow

**Pre-operative stabilisation:**
- Diuretics, anti-failure medications
- Inotropes (in some cases)
- Intubation: mechanical ventilation allows hyperpnoea → ↑ PVR → ↓ L→R shunt → improves systemic circulation
- For concomitant interrupted aortic arch: **Prostaglandin infusion** to maintain ductal patency

**Closure:** Nearly always surgical; rarely transcatheter

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 17. What are the principles of surgery for repair of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

**General approach:**
- Division of APW with subsequent closure of aorta and PA
- Defects closed by direct suture or patch repair (when appropriate)
- **Single-stage repair** of all defects (reduces overall mortality)

**Operative technique:**
- Median sternotomy, CPB, mild hypothermia
- **Aortic cannulation:** As distal as possible in ascending aorta (cross-clamping should not interfere with repair)
- Occlude both PAs with tourniquets at start of CPB
- Ligation of PDA if present
- **Complex cases/IAA:** Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest ± regional perfusion for arch repair before APW repair
- **Bicaval cannulation** for concomitant intracardiac lesions

**Defect exposure:**
- Through window itself, through aorta, or through PA
- **Must provide good visualisation of both coronary arteries and RPA**

**Closure:**
- Primary or patch closure
- Ensure closure does not compromise coronary arteries or semilunar valves

**Coronary management (Figure 10):**
- If coronary artery originates from PA or APW → leave ostium on aortic side of repair
- With patch closure: place patch to include coronary on aortic side
- If not possible → reimplant coronary directly to aorta

**Avoid:** Direct ligation of APW (obsolete)

![](_page_13_Picture_6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 10.** Surgical correction of anomalous coronary origin from APW (A) or PA (B). Patch on aorta fashioned to ensure unobstructed coronary flow. LCA = left coronary artery; RCA = right coronary artery; PA = pulmonary artery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 18. What structures are at risk during surgical repair of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

| Structure | Risk |
|---|---|
| **Coronary arteries** | Most important; especially in proximal defects (Figure 11). Direct suturing or patch distortion can compromise flow |
| **Pulmonary and aortic valve leaflets** | Distortion → valve dysfunction |
| **RPA and LPA ostia** | Kinking or obstruction if located very close to defect |
| **Conduction system** | With concomitant VSD closure |
| **Thoracic duct, phrenic nerve, recurrent laryngeal nerve** | With concurrent aortic arch repair |

![](_page_14_Picture_8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11.** APW in close proximity to A) left or B) right coronary artery orifice — must be identified before closure to avoid compromising coronary flow. LCA = left coronary artery; RCA = right coronary artery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 19. What are the complications of surgery following repair of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Residual defect** → persistent shunt → poorly tolerated → early reintervention
- **Coronary distortion** → life-threatening (emphasises importance of intra-operative visualisation)
- **Aortic or PA stenosis** from direct suturing
- **Branch pulmonary artery stenosis** (requires long-term follow-up)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 8. Outcomes and Prognosis</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 20. What are the risk factors for operative mortality following repair of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Simple APW:** Excellent outcomes, mortality **approaching 0%**
- Operative mortality related to **concomitant anomalies** or **poor pre-operative state**, not APW repair itself
- **Interrupted aortic arch:** Independent risk factor (mortality 0–15% depending on centre quality/volume)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 21. What are the long-term outcomes following surgical repair of aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

| Parameter | Isolated APW | APW with IAA |
|---|---|---|
| **Early mortality (modern)** | ~0% | 0–15% |
| **Late mortality** | Excellent; no late deaths in most series (median follow-up 2–11 years) | 91% (1y), 86% (5y), 84% (10y) |
| **Reintervention rate** | 0% (median follow-up up to 7 years; range 3 months–25 years) | 15–20%; ~57% at 5 years (51% arch, 6% PA); only 43% alive without reintervention |

**Historical trends:**
- **Pre-2000:** Average mortality ~14% (0–54%) — later intervention, irreversible PH or heart failure
- **Post-2000:** Average mortality ~8% (0–20%)

Isolated reports of late mortality have occurred in setting of **persistent pulmonary hypertension**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 22. What are the principles of device closure for aortopulmonary window?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Rare, selected patients only**

**Limitations:**
- Large defect size
- Small femoral vessels for access
- Associated cardiovascular anomalies
- Coronary anatomy difficult to appreciate by echo alone before closure

**Devices used (successful reports):**
- Double-umbrella occluder system
- Button device closure
- Amplatzer™ device closure

**Defect size:** Mostly 3–6 mm (one report 8.7 mm)

**Follow-up:** Limited (<12 months); no residual leaks reported in successful cases

</div></details>
