# Paediatric Cardiac Intensive Care

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Principles of Practice

Cardiac intensive care units manage children with cardiac conditions admitted for:
*   Acute medical problems (stabilisation prior to cardiotomy)
*   Post-operative care (cardiac operations, catheter-based procedures, electrophysiology)
*   Post-non-cardiac surgery care

### Monitoring Tools (Table 1)

| Tool | Key Uses |
|------|----------|
| **ECG** | Heart rate, rhythm, ST changes |
| **Invasive arterial pressure** | Beat-to-beat BP, pulse pressure (SVR), pulse pressure variation (volume), contractility (upstroke), CO calculation, sampling |
| **CVP** | Right atrial pressure, RVEDP, preload; waveform for arrhythmias/tricuspid disease |
| **LAP** | LVEDP (surrogate), preload/contractility/afterload; risk of air embolus |
| **SpO₂** | Oxygen saturation (<80% less accurate), tissue perfusion (waveform) |
| **CO monitors** | CO, SV, SVR, contractility; trends more useful than absolute values |
| **ETCO₂** | Gas exchange, ventilation adequacy, ETT position, pulmonary blood flow |
| **NIRS** | Regional tissue oxygenation (cerebral, renal); marker of perfusion |
| **aEEG** | Electrocortical activity (seizures, encephalopathy) |
| **BIS** | Depth of sedation/anaesthesia |

### Duct-Dependent Circulations

| Type | Lesions | Presentation | Management |
|------|---------|--------------|------------|
| **Systemic** | Critical AS, interrupted aortic arch, coarctation, HLHS | Cardiogenic shock, acidosis, multiorgan failure | Prostaglandin (5–100 ng/kg/min), intubation, inotropes, correct electrolytes, organ support |
| **Pulmonary** | Critical PS, PA ± VSD, TOF with severe RVOTO | Cyanosis, hyperoxia test negative | Prostaglandin, intubation if shock/acidosis/profound cyanosis/apnoea |

**Prostaglandin effects:** Dose-dependent (low: maintain, high: reopen); side effects: apnoea, pyrexia, vasodilation (hypotension)

### Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension of Newborn (PPHN) vs Cyanotic CHD

| Feature | PPHN | Cyanotic CHD |
|---------|------|--------------|
| Response to 100% O₂ | Minor response | No response |
| PaO₂ | Occasionally normal/high | Never normal |
| Response to iNO | Usually good | No/minimal response |
| Response to ↓PaCO₂ | Improved oxygenation | No response |

**Gold standard:** Echocardiography (exclude CHD, especially obstructed TAPVC)

### Single-Ventricle Pre-operative Management

**Goal:** Balanced Qp:Qs (0.7–1:1), target SpO₂ 75–80%

**Requirements:**
*   Unrestrictive interatrial communication
*   Patent duct of adequate size
*   Balanced PVR/SVR (Figure 2)

**High Qp:Qs (excessive pulmonary flow):**
*   ↑PVR: ↑PaCO₂ (pulmonary vasoconstrictor), avoid O₂, ↓prostaglandin
*   ↓SVR: sedation/analgesia, normothermia, afterload reduction (milrinone)

**Qp:Qs calculation:**
$$\frac{Qp}{Qs} = \frac{\text{Aortic sats} - \text{Mixed venous sats}}{\text{Pulmonary venous sats} - \text{Pulmonary artery sats}}$$

(Assumptions: mixed venous = CVP sats, pulmonary venous ≈100%, pulmonary artery = aortic sats in true single ventricle)

### Left-to-Right Shunt Pre-operative Management

**Causes:** Large VSD, AVSD, PDA, AP window

**Deterioration triggers:** Falling PVR with age, intercurrent infections, pulmonary hypertensive episodes

**Management:**
*   Treat intercurrent illness
*   Reduce pulmonary oedema: diuretics, PEEP
*   Reduce pulmonary blood flow (↑PVR): ↑PaCO₂, minimise O₂
*   Afterload reduction: ACE inhibitors, milrinone, positive pressure ventilation

### Right-to-Left Shunt Pre-operative Management (Cyanotic)

**Causes:** Tetralogy of Fallot, pulmonary atresia ± VSD

**Aim:** Improve pulmonary blood flow

**Strategies:**
*   Reduce R→L shunt: ↓PVR (O₂, sedation, ventilation, iNO); ↑SVR (phenylephrine, noradrenaline, vasopressin, cooling); ↓RVOTO (avoid acidosis, β-blockers, sedation, fluids)
*   Interventional cardiology: RVOT/PDA stenting, balloon valvuloplasty
*   Surgery: BT shunt, transannular patch, primary repair, ECMO

### Transposition of Great Arteries (TGA) Pre-operative Management

**Mixing required at:** Atrial (PFO/ASD), ventricular (VSD), or arterial (PDA) level

**Causes of profound cyanosis:** Associated PPHN, restrictive/intact atrial septum, delayed diagnosis, associated lesions

**Strategies to improve mixing:**
*   Maintain ductal patency (prostaglandin)
*   Unrestrict atrial shunting: balloon atrial septostomy (BAS), ↑LA pressure (prostaglandin)
*   ↓PVR: O₂, sedation, ventilation, iNO
*   ↑SVR: noradrenaline, vasopressin

### Left Ventricular Failure and Dilated Cardiomyopathy

**Assessment:**
*   Echocardiography: LV function, structural disease, coronary origins
*   ECG: tachyarrhythmias, ischaemia/infarction
*   Bloods: end-organ function, troponin, lactate, SvO₂
*   Cardiomyopathy screen: genetics, metabolic, endocrine

**Medical management (Figure 4):**
*   Optimise preload, contractility, afterload
*   Diuretics, inotropes (adrenaline, dopamine), inodilators (milrinone), vasopressors
*   Mechanical support (ECMO, VAD) if medical management fails → bridge to recovery/transplant

### Post-cardiotomy Complications

**Airway:** Endobronchial intubation, airway oedema, vocal cord palsy, central airway compression

**Breathing:** Acute lung injury, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, chylothorax, atelectasis, pulmonary oedema, diaphragm palsy

**Circulation:** SIRS, LCOS, myocardial dysfunction, arrhythmias, coagulopathy/bleeding, tamponade, residual/iatrogenic lesions, thrombosis

**Disability:** Pain, stroke, anxiety, delirium/withdrawal

### Postoperative Bleeding (Table 3, Figure 5)

| Severity | Chest Drain Losses |
|----------|-------------------|
| Nil | 0 |
| Minimal | 1–2 mL/kg/hr |
| Moderate | 2–5 mL/kg/hr |
| High | 5–10 mL/kg/hr |
| Very severe (assume surgical) | >10 mL/kg/hr |

**Management:**
*   Resuscitation: warmed blood products (RBCs, platelets, FFP, cryoprecipitate)
*   Correct coagulopathy: tranexamic acid (antifibrinolytic), normothermia, ionised calcium, protamine (heparin reversal), acidosis correction
*   TEG® (Figure 5) guides product selection: ↑R time (factors) → FFP; ↓MA (platelets) → platelets; ↑LY30 (fibrinolysis) → tranexamic acid
*   rFVIIa only in extreme circumstances (thrombosis risk)
*   Consider surgical bleeding/tamponade → re-exploration

### Low Cardiac Output State (LCOS)

**Incidence:** ~25% post-cardiotomy, peak 6–12 hours post-op

**Causes:** Inflammatory response, myocardial ischaemia, reperfusion injury, residual lesions, hypovolaemia, tamponade, pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmias (JET)

**Clinical features:** Cool peripheries, weak pulses, tachycardia, hypotension, ↓urine output, ↓NIRS, ↑lactate, ↓SvO₂

**Oxygen delivery (DO₂):** $DO_2 = CO \times CaO_2$, where $CaO_2 = (1.34 \times Hb \times SaO_2) + (0.003 \times PaO_2)$

**Management strategies (Figure 6):**
*   Sedation/muscle relaxation (↓metabolic demand)
*   Optimise ventilation/oxygenation
*   Optimise preload: correct bleeding, fluid boluses
*   Optimise contractility: inotropes (adrenaline, dopamine), inodilators (milrinone), calcium
*   Optimise afterload: normothermia, inodilators (if high SVR), vasopressors (if low SVR)
*   Renal replacement therapy, steroids (inotrope-resistant shock), ECMO

### Junctional Ectopic Tachycardia (JET) (Figure 7)

**Incidence:** 5–15% post-cardiotomy; occurs 24–72 hours post-op, resolves in 7–10 days

**Risk factors:** Younger age, low weight, long CPB/cross-clamp times, DHCA, complex surgery, TOF/AVSD, genetic syndromes, electrolyte disturbances, acidosis, inotropes

**ECG features:** Non-sinus tachycardia, QRS similar to sinus, AV dissociation (ventricular rate > atrial rate), P waves lost in QRS, regular R-R intervals

**Management:**
*   Sedation/muscle relaxation
*   Correct electrolytes (Mg²⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺), acidosis
*   Reduce temperature (aim 35°C)
*   Reduce inotropes (especially dopamine)
*   Antiarrhythmics: α-agonists (clonidine, dexmedetomidine), amiodarone, β-blockers (esmolol)
*   Overdrive pacing (AAI/DDD at 10–15 bpm above JET rate)
*   ECMO if refractory

### Renal Replacement Therapy (RRT)

**Incidence:** 2–7% post-cardiotomy; associated with increased mortality

**Indications:** Fluid overload, severe AKI, oliguria/anuria, profound acidosis, hyperkalaemia, acute-on-chronic renal failure

| Modality | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|----------|------------|---------------|
| **Peritoneal dialysis (PD)** | Safe, no vascular access, no anticoagulation, simple equipment | Limited clearance, haemodynamic instability with intermittent shifts, limited to neonates/infants |
| **CRRT (haemodialysis)** | Predetermined fluid removal, consistent clearance, continuous (less instability), works for all ages | Large venous catheter, anticoagulation required, complex |

### Chylothorax

**Diagnosis:** Pleural fluid triglycerides >1.1 mmol/L (if enterally fed), lymphocytes >80%, chylomicrons

**Causes:** Thoracic duct injury (arch surgery), elevated systemic venous pressures (Glenn/Fontan, SVC obstruction, RV failure), congenital lymphatic abnormalities

**Management:**
*   Chest drain
*   Treat reversible causes (e.g., SVC thrombosis)
*   Nutritional: continue normal enteral feed (breast milk) if tolerated; if losses increase → medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) feed (not absorbed via lymphatics); if persistent → TPN
*   Octreotide
*   Surgery: thoracic duct ligation, pleurodesis, pleurectomy (if recurrent)

### Postoperative Management by Procedure

| Procedure | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **PA band** | Loose band: minimise L→R shunt, pulmonary oedema; tight band: ↑PVR, inotropes, possible recalibration |
| **BT shunt** | Anticoagulation (heparin → aspirin), balance circulations, rapid extubation; shunt dependency critical (failure → no pulmonary flow → rapid arrest) |
| **Arterial switch (TGA)** | Monitor coronary perfusion: Left CAPP = Ao diastolic BP – LAP; Right CAPP = Ao diastolic BP – CVP. Avoid diastolic hypotension, avoid large fluid boluses |
| **TAPVC** | Obstructed = surgical emergency; post-op: pulmonary hypertension, residual PV obstruction, pleural effusion/chylothorax |
| **ALCAPA** | High-risk (pre-op LV dysfunction, ischaemia); monitor for coronary ischaemia (ST changes, regional wall motion); low threshold for ECMO |
| **L→R shunt repair** | Low-pressure shunts (ASD) low risk; pressure-loading shunts (VSD, AVSD, truncus) require milrinone; pulmonary hypertension risk |
| **R→L shunt repair (TOF)** | Restrictive RV physiology: ↓preload tolerance, ↑CVP, poor end-organ perfusion; manage with minimal PEEP, fluids, vasopressors, milrinone, evacuate effusions |
| **Norwood (Stage I)** | Balance Qp:Qs (<1 post-op). Sano modification: systolic pulmonary flow (preserves diastolic pressure) → more stable haemodynamics; open chest common |
| **BCPS (Glenn)** | Passive SVC flow → passive PBF; head elevation (30°), permissive hypercapnia (↑cerebral flow → ↑SVC return), minimal ventilation pressures, early extubation |
| **TCPC (Fontan)** (Figure 8) | Passive flow from SVC and IVC; fenestration may maintain CO at expense of desaturation; manage as BCPS + avoid ↑PVR, maintain preload, consider fenestration closure if stable |

### Cardiac Arrest Post-cardiotomy (Table 4)

**Reversible causes (4Hs and 4Ts):**
*   Hypoxia, Hypovolaemia, Hypothermia, Hyper/hypokalaemia
*   Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade, Thromboembolic, Toxins

**Specific interventions:**
*   Activate ECMO team
*   Early chest opening → internal cardiac compressions
*   Inhaled nitric oxide (pulmonary hypertension)
*   Epicardial pacing (bradycardia)
*   Heparin bolus (blocked shunt)

**Tamponade:** Assume in all post-cardiotomy arrests (even without echo evidence); emergency chest re-opening may be necessary

### Outcomes (RACHS-1 Categories, Table 5)

| Category | Example Surgeries |
|----------|-------------------|
| 1 (lowest risk) | ASD repair, PAPVD, PDA, coarctation >30 days |
| 2 | VSD repair, TOF repair, Glenn, AP window, coarctation <30 days |
| 3 | Ross, valve replacement, ALCAPA repair, Fontan, PA band, arterial switch (only) |
| 4 | Arterial switch + VSD, TAPVD <30 days, Rastelli, truncus, hypoplastic arch |
| 5 | Truncus + interrupted arch, Ebstein's <30 days |
| 6 (highest risk) | Norwood |

30-day survival ~98% (UK/Ireland data); long-term morbidity: neurodevelopmental (autism, cognitive impairment, behavioural difficulties, motor delay, seizures)

### Positive Pressure Ventilation (PPV) Effects (Table 6)

| | Preload | Afterload |
|---|---|---|
| **Pulmonary ventricle** | ↓ (↓venous return, ↓RAP, ↓RVEDV) | ↑ (↑RVEDP, ↓pulmonary blood flow) |
| **Systemic ventricle** | ↓ (↓pulmonary venous return, ↓LAP, ↓LVEDV) | ↓ (↓LVEDP, ↓aortic transmural pressure) |

Extubation (return to negative pressure) has opposite effects: ↑systemic afterload, ↑pulmonary ventricle volume loading — high-risk period

### Pulmonary Hypertensive Crisis (Figure 9, Table 7)

**Pathophysiology:** Acute ↑PA pressure → ↑RV pressure → ↓LV preload (no shunt) or R→L shunt (if present) → ↓CO → bradycardia → arrest

**Management:**
*   ↓PVR: hyperventilation (↓PaCO₂), FiO₂ 1.0, iNO, milrinone, sedation, muscle relaxation, correct acidosis
*   ↑SVR: vasopressin, noradrenaline, inotropes (adrenaline)
*   Fluid bolus (↑RV preload)
*   Magnesium replacement
*   ECMO if refractory

### Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS) (Figure 11)

**Binary questions:**
*   Cardiac: Is ventricle contracting well? Empty/full? Pericardial effusion?
*   Respiratory: Pulmonary oedema? Pleural effusion? Pneumothorax? Consolidation?
*   Abdominal: Ascites?

**Advantages:** Immediate availability, real-time changes, repeatable, improves procedural safety

### Nutrition (Table 8)

**Effects of poor nutrition:** Impaired immunity, infection, poor wound healing, constipation/ileus, vitamin/mineral deficiency, hypoproteinaemia

**Effects of overfeeding:** Fluid overload, infection, diarrhoea, obesity

**High-risk for ischaemic colitis (feed cautiously):** Low systemic output lesions (HLHS, critical AS, interrupted arch, coarctation), prolonged CPB, ECMO, previous abdominal pathology, long-term poor nutrition (refeeding syndrome risk)

**Enteral feeding preferred:** Contraindications: NEC, ischaemic colitis, GI bleeding, mechanical obstruction. Multiple inotropes NOT an absolute contraindication

### End-of-Life Care

**Process:**
1.  MDT consensus that treatment no longer in child's best interest
2.  Family discussions (time, understanding)
3.  Palliative care involvement
4.  Consider organ donation, memory boxes, religious support
5.  Withdrawal: location (home/hospice/hospital), who present, symptom management
6.  Post-death: team support, debrief, documentation, family follow-up

## 1. Principles of Practice and Monitoring</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Practice for a Cardiac Intensive Care Unit</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac intensive care units are highly specialised units providing intensive care to a wide range of clinical scenarios and needs. Some hospitals operate specialised cardiac intensive care (paediatric and adult) and some care for patients with cardiac disease as part of general intensive care practice.

Regardless of the structural model, specialists in cardiac intensive care are called to deal with a child or adult with a cardiac condition admitted:
*   With an acute medical problem including stabilisation prior to cardiotomy
*   Following cardiac operations and/or catheter-based procedures (diagnostic or therapeutic, electrophysiology interventions)
*   After non-cardiac surgery

This chapter focuses on children with cardiac conditions; principles for adult congenital heart disease are described in the following chapter.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### TABLE 1: Common Monitoring Tools Used in the Paediatric Intensive Care Unit</summary><div class="details-content">

| Monitoring Tool | Description |
|-----------------|-------------|
| **ECG** | All patients have a rhythm strip displayed on their monitor, allowing determination of heart rate and rhythm. Many machines allow for a variety of leads to be selected and can determine ST segment changes from baseline. |
| **Invasive arterial pressure** | Invasive arterial lines measure arterial blood pressure beat-by-beat. Pulse pressure gives an indication of systemic vascular resistance or diastolic run-off. Pulse pressure variation (change in peak amplitude of systolic pressure, known as a "swing") can suggest volume depletion. The trace indicates myocardial contractility; a steep upstroke implies greater change in pressure per unit of time (good contractility). Cardiac output (CO) can also be calculated from the arterial waveform. Two arterial lines can monitor residual gradients across a vessel (e.g., pre- and post-ductal lines following hypoplastic aortic arch repair). Allows ease of sampling for arterial blood gases. Risks include distal perfusion problems, particularly in smaller infants. |
| **Central venous pressure (CVP)** | Internal jugular central venous lines can be transduced to measure CVP, which estimates right atrial pressure and (in absence of tricuspid valve disease) right ventricular end-diastolic pressure (RVEDP). CVP is often used as an indirect measure of preload and volume status. Tricuspid stenosis/regurgitation and RV disease (e.g., restrictive RV physiology) affect CVP. The normal CVP waveform consists of waves (3 in systole, 2 in diastole), altered in arrhythmia or tricuspid valve disease. Allows safer administration of irritant drugs (e.g., vasopressors, TPN). |
| **Left atrial pressure (LAP)** | LAP lines are left *in situ* following certain congenital cardiac surgical procedures (e.g., arterial switch). The pressure reading is a surrogate marker for LV end-diastolic pressure, giving an indicator of LV preload, contractility, and afterload. Normal LAP is slightly higher than RAP (5–10 mmHg). LAP lines are not routinely left *in situ* due to risks of air entrainment or thromboembolic events directly to the systemic circulation, and bleeding on removal. If an air bubble is seen in the line, it must be clamped immediately. |
| **Pulse oximeter saturation levels (SpO₂)** | Standard monitoring using transmission of light at two frequencies through pulsatile tissues to assess oxygen saturation of haemoglobin. Original calibration means SpO₂ accuracy is less with extremes of hypoxia (<80%). The pulse oximeter waveform informs tissue perfusion; a poor waveform implies poor pulsatility in distal tissues. Direct arterial blood gas analysis is most accurate for oxygen saturation and is important for Qp:Qs assessment in single-ventricle cyanotic children. |
| **Cardiac output (CO) monitors** | Helpful to measure CO and tissue oxygen delivery. Traditionally used pulmonary artery catheters, but this technique is rarely used in PICU. Modern CO monitors use pulse pressure analysis, bioimpedance, Doppler measurements, or the applied Fick principle. Machines often give (indexed) recordings of CO, stroke volume, SVR, and contractility. These machines can be inaccurate; trends are more helpful and must be used with clinical assessment. |
| **End-tidal CO₂ (ETCO₂)** | Mandatory monitoring for any ventilated patient. Measures CO₂ concentration at end of expiration (normally 35–45 mmHg or 4.6–5.9 kPa), approximately correlating to PaCO₂. ETCO₂ is helpful for: monitoring effectiveness of gas exchange and adequacy of mechanical ventilation; confirming and monitoring ETT position; assessing cardiac output by monitoring pulmonary blood flow; monitoring metabolic demand. |
| **Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS)** | Method of monitoring regional tissue oxygenation using infrared probes placed over the tissue of interest. Typically measured on both cerebral hemispheres to ensure adequate cerebral oxygenation (particularly helpful if on VA ECMO with carotid artery cannulation). Probes can also be placed on the kidney, giving cerebral-renal NIRS difference, helpful to ensure adequate cardiac output and detect falling CO. |
| **Amplitude integrated electroencephalography (aEEG)** | Cerebral function monitors measure a reduced number of EEG channels. Data is processed, filtered, and presented on a semi-logarithmic scale as an aEEG, with one trace for each cerebral hemisphere. Allows bedside team to monitor changes in electrocortical activity (e.g., seizures, encephalopathy). |
| **Bispectral index (BIS)** | Monitors depth of sedation or anaesthesia. The lower the number, the more sedated the patient. |

## 2. Stabilisation and Pre-operative Management</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Stabilising a Neonate with a Duct-Dependent Systemic Circulation</summary><div class="details-content">

**Lesions requiring duct to supply systemic circulation:** Critical aortic stenosis, interrupted aortic arch, coarctation of the aorta, hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS).

**Presentation:** As duct closes (day 5–14 of life), systemic cardiac output and tissue oxygen delivery are critically impaired. Neonates present with various degrees of cardiogenic shock: poor peripheral pulses, heart murmur, hepatomegaly, respiratory distress, acidosis, lacticaemia, multiorgan failure.

**Monitoring:** Pre-ductal (right arm) and post-ductal (lower limb) blood pressure and pulse oximetry differences. During resuscitation, pre-ductal measurements are of interest as they measure perfusion to coronary arteries and brain.

**Management:**
*   **Prostaglandin:** Key intervention to reopen duct. Dose-dependent: low doses (5–10 ng/kg/min) maintain open duct; higher doses (20–50 ng/kg/min) reopen closing duct; up to 100 ng/kg/min may be needed short-term. Side effects: apnoea, pyrexia, vasodilation (hypotension).
*   **Other interventions:** Intubation and ventilation with sedation; full monitoring with adequate venous and arterial access; inotropic support (adrenaline or dopamine); correction of electrolyte disturbances; support of end-organ function (e.g., RRT); consideration of neurological injury; assessment for associated pathologies (sepsis, genetic syndromes).

The duct will almost always reopen, providing time to optimise physiology before surgery. If duct cannot be reopened, urgent surgery may be indicated.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Stabilising a Neonate with a Duct-Dependent Pulmonary Circulation</summary><div class="details-content">

**Lesions requiring duct to supply pulmonary circulation:** Critical pulmonary stenosis, pulmonary atresia (with or without VSD), tetralogy of Fallot with severe RVOTO.

**Presentation:** As duct closes (day 5–14), reduced blood flow to lungs causes inadequate oxygenation and R→L shunt → cyanosis. Hyperoxia test (high O₂ ventilation) shows minimal improvement; neonate remains cyanotic.

**Management:**
*   **Prostaglandin:** Key intervention to reopen duct. Will not normalise saturations but ensures adequate pulmonary blood flow and mixing.
*   **Intubation and ventilation** indicated if: signs of shock; acidosis and lacticaemia; profound cyanosis; apnoea (prostaglandin side effect).
*   **Other interventions:** Good general intensive care measures and organ support.

If duct cannot be reopened, urgent surgery indicated (full correction or systemic-to-pulmonary shunt, e.g., modified Blalock-Taussig shunt).

PPHN reflects failure of foetal circulation to transition, with elevated PA pressures, ongoing R→L shunt through duct and foramen ovale → persistent hypoxia.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Pre-operative Management of a Neonate with Single-Ventricle Physiology</summary><div class="details-content">

**Goal:** Balance between pulmonary and systemic perfusion (Qp:Qs = 0.7–1:1), monitored by oxygen saturation (target 75–80% with good clinical signs of systemic perfusion).

**Requirements for balanced Qp:Qs:**
*   Unrestrictive interatrial communication (secundum ASD or PFO)
*   Patent duct of adequate size for body surface area
*   Balance between PVR and SVR (Figure 2)

![](_page_9_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Single-ventricle physiology with HLHS and balanced Qp:Qs. SVC = superior vena cava; IVC = inferior vena cava; SVR = systemic vascular resistance; PVR = pulmonary vascular resistance; size of arrows = volume of flow; SaO₂ = arterial oxygen saturation; SvO₂ = venous oxygen saturation; Qs = systemic blood flow; Qp = pulmonary blood flow.**

During period of high PVR, maintaining duct patency with PGE₂ and careful monitoring is usually sufficient. As PVR falls in the first days after birth, excessive pulmonary blood flow (elevated Qp:Qs) can occur (Figure 3): high SpO₂, respiratory distress, poor peripheral perfusion, lacticaemia, low central venous saturation.

![](_page_9_Figure_4.jpeg)

**Figure 3. Single-ventricle physiology with HLHS and high pulmonary blood flow. SVC = superior vena cava; IVC = inferior vena cava; SVR = systemic vascular resistance; PVR = pulmonary vascular resistance; size of arrows = volume of flow; SaO₂ = arterial oxygen saturation; SvO₂ = venous oxygen saturation; Qs = systemic blood flow; Qp = pulmonary blood flow.**

**Management of high Qp:Qs:**
*   Increase PVR: ventilate to higher PaCO₂ (pulmonary vasoconstrictor), avoid oxygen (pulmonary vasodilator), reduce prostaglandin infusion if possible (while maintaining duct patency)
*   Decrease SVR: adequate sedation and analgesia, normothermia, afterload reduction (e.g., milrinone)

**Qp:Qs calculation at bedside:**
$$\frac{Qp}{Qs} = \frac{\text{Aortic sats} - \text{Mixed venous sats}}{\text{Pulmonary venous sats} - \text{Pulmonary artery sats}}$$

Assumptions:
*   Mixed venous sats = central venous sats (internal jugular line)
*   Pulmonary venous sats ≈ 100% (or 95–98% if pulmonary oedema/V/Q mismatch)
*   Pulmonary artery sats = aortic sats (in true single-ventricle system)

Surgery is usually performed following natural fall in PVR (around 7–14 days).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Pre-operative Management of an Infant or Child with Heart Failure Due to a Large Left-to-Right Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

**Causes:** Large VSD, AVSD, PDA, aortopulmonary (AP) window. ASDs do not usually lead to heart failure until very late in life.

**Presentation:** Breathlessness, respiratory distress, failure to thrive, hepatomegaly, cardiomegaly, murmur. Deterioration precipitated by:
*   Falling PVR with increasing age
*   Intercurrent infections (e.g., bronchiolitis)
*   Pulmonary hypertensive episodes

**Management:**
*   Treat intercurrent illness
*   Reduce pulmonary oedema: diuretics, positive end-expiratory pressure (non-invasive or invasive ventilation)
*   Reduce pulmonary blood flow (increase PVR): reduce minute ventilation to increase PaCO₂, minimise oxygen usage
*   Promote aortic forward flow and reduce L→R shunt by afterload reduction: ACE inhibitors, inodilators (e.g., milrinone), positive pressure ventilation
*   Optimise nutrition and growth

**Timing for intervention:** Palliative surgery (e.g., PA banding) or complete surgical/interventional correction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Pre-operative Management of an Infant or Child with Cyanosis Due to a Right-to-Left Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

**Typical lesions:** Tetralogy of Fallot, pulmonary atresia with or without VSD.

**Pathophysiology:** R→L shunt → reduced pulmonary blood flow → systemic desaturation, negative hyperoxia test. Shunt varies with SVR/PVR relationship and degree of RVOTO. Children with TOF can have hypercyanotic episodes ("spells") with dynamic worsening of RVOTO.

**PICU care required when:** Pulmonary blood flow reaches critical level → significant cyanosis, impaired oxygen delivery, possible neurological symptoms.

**Aim:** Improve pulmonary blood flow.

**Strategies:**
*   **Reduce R→L shunt:**
    *   ↓PVR: oxygen therapy, sedation, muscle relaxation, ventilation to normal/lower PaCO₂, inhaled nitric oxide
    *   ↑SVR: phenylephrine, noradrenaline, vasopressin, cooling to normothermia
    *   ↓RVOTO: avoid acidosis, β-blockers (e.g., esmolol), sedation, analgesia, adequate preload (fluids)
*   **Interventional cardiology:** Stent insertion (RVOT or PDA), balloon valvuloplasty
*   **Cardiac surgery:** Temporary shunt (modified BT shunt), partial correction (transannular patch), primary correction, ECMO

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Pre-operative Management of a Neonate or Infant with Transposition of the Great Arteries (TGA)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Pathophysiology:** Parallel circulations (morphological RV → aorta, morphological LV → PA). Without mixing, circulation is incompatible with life. Mixing occurs at atrial (interatrial communications), ventricular (VSD), or arterial (PDA) level.

**Causes of profound cyanosis requiring intervention:** Associated pulmonary hypertension, restrictive or intact atrial septum, delayed diagnosis with PDA closure, associated cardiac lesions.

**Aim:** Improve oxygen saturation by increasing mixing between circulations and treating additional pathology.

**Strategies to improve mixing:**
*   Ensure ductal patency with prostaglandin
*   Ensure unrestricted atrial shunting: percutaneous balloon atrial septostomy (BAS) to remove mechanical obstruction; prostaglandin to increase pulmonary venous return (elevates LA pressure, encouraging mixing through atrial septum)
*   Reduce PVR: oxygen therapy, sedation and muscle relaxation, ventilation to normal/lower PaCO₂, inhaled nitric oxide
*   Increase SVR: noradrenaline, vasopressin
*   Treat other problems (e.g., sepsis)

Acute surgery rarely indicated; neonate ideally extubated after BAS to allow normal PVR reduction in first week of life before surgical repair.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Management of a Child Presenting with Left Ventricular Failure and Dilated Cardiomyopathy</summary><div class="details-content">

**Presentation:** Varying degrees of low cardiac output states and cardiogenic shock. Acute decompensation from worsening LV function or intercurrent illness.

**Assessment:**
*   **Echocardiography:** LV function, structural heart disease, coronary artery origins (exclude ALCAPA)
*   **ECG:** Tachyarrhythmias, signs of ischaemia/infarction (Q waves, ST/T changes)
*   **Blood results:** End-organ perfusion (renal function), troponin, lactate, mixed venous oxygen saturation (SvO₂)
*   **Cardiomyopathy screen:** Genetic testing, metabolic assessment, endocrine evaluation

**Medical management (Figure 4):**
*   Aim: ensure adequate oxygen delivery to tissues while treating reversible/underlying causes
*   Factors determining cardiac output: preload, contractility, afterload, heart rate
*   Specific strategies: diuretics (pulmonary oedema), inotropes (adrenaline, dopamine), inodilators (milrinone), afterload reduction (ACE inhibitors)

![](_page_15_Figure_7.jpeg)

**Figure 4. Intensive care unit medical management strategies for the failing left ventricle. ACE = angiotensin-converting enzyme.**

Rapid resolution of LV function with good critical care usually implies secondary cardiac cause (e.g., sepsis). Ongoing LV dysfunction without structural CHD suggests primary cardiomyopathy/myocarditis.

If medical management fails, consider mechanical support (ECMO, VAD) as bridge to recovery, definitive treatment, or transplant. Early discussion with cardiac transplantation centre recommended.

</div></details>

## 3. Postoperative Complications and Management</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Main Complications Seen Post-cardiotomy and Thoracotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

**Airway:**
*   Iatrogenic pathology from airway management (endobronchial intubation, airway oedema)
*   Vocal cord palsy (recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy)
*   Iatrogenic compression of central airways (especially main bronchi and trachea, from arch surgery)

**Breathing:**
*   Acute lung injury, pneumothorax, pleural effusion, chylothorax (thoracic duct injury)
*   Atelectasis/collapse, pulmonary oedema, diaphragm palsy (phrenic nerve injury)

**Circulation:**
*   Systemic inflammatory response, low cardiac output state, myocardial dysfunction
*   Arrhythmias and heart block, coagulopathy and bleeding, cardiac tamponade (bleeding or myocardial oedema)
*   Residual untreated cardiac lesions, new iatrogenic lesions, thrombosis

**Disability:**
*   Pain, stroke (ischaemic or haemorrhagic), anxiety, delirium/withdrawal

**Miscellaneous:**
*   Sepsis (line sepsis, mediastinitis, wound infection), scarring, pyrexia
*   Hypoglycaemia, fluid overload, electrolyte disturbances, acid-base disturbances
*   Renal impairment, multi-organ dysfunction, ischaemic colitis, feed intolerance

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Management Strategy for Patients with Postoperative Bleeding (Table 3, Figure 5)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Common causes:**
*   Coagulopathy from inflammatory/immunological response to surgery and CPB (bypass >90 minutes associated with greater risk)
*   Major intra-operative transfusion, consumption of platelets and coagulation factors during CPB
*   Inadequate heparin reversal, haemodilution, hypothermia, surgical bleeding

**Severity classification (chest drain losses):**

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### TABLE 3: Classification for Severity of Bleeding Post-Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

| Severity of Bleeding | Chest Drain Losses |
|----------------------|-------------------|
| Nil — check drains are not blocked | 0 |
| Minimal | 1–2 mL/kg/hr |
| Moderate | 2–5 mL/kg/hr |
| High | 5–10 mL/kg/hr |
| Very severe — assume surgical bleeding | >10 mL/kg/hr |

</div></details>

**Management:**
*   **Resuscitation:** Ensure adequate oxygen delivery despite blood loss
*   **Replacement:** Warmed blood products (red blood cells, platelets, FFP, cryoprecipitate) to replace whole blood lost; pump blood or cell-saved blood can also be used
*   **Correct residual coagulopathy:** Early antifibrinolytic treatment (tranexamic acid) significantly reduces need for transfusion; ensure normothermia; restore ionised calcium; further reversal of heparin with protamine; correct acidosis
*   **Consider surgical bleeding or tamponade** → chest re-exploration
*   **rFVIIa:** Only in extreme circumstances and after all surgical sources corrected (increased adverse events, including arterial thrombi)

**Coagulation testing:**
*   **ACT (activated clotting time):** Bedside test of fibrin formation in whole blood; normal range 100–140 seconds; used to monitor heparin anticoagulation
*   **TEG® (thromboelastogram):** Detailed whole blood clotting test to determine cause of coagulopathy and guide product administration

![](_page_19_Figure_5.jpeg)

**Figure 5. Thromboelastogram (TEG®) and its interpretation.**

| Parameter | Description | Clinical Implications |
|-----------|-------------|----------------------|
| **R time** (reaction time) | Time to first significant clot formation/fibrin initiation | ↑R time = ↓coagulation factors → FFP or protamine |
| **K value** | Time to clot firmness of 20 mm amplitude | ↑K time = ↓platelets and/or fibrinogen |
| **α-angle** | Speed of fibrin accumulation | ↓α-angle = ↓fibrinogen and/or platelets |
| **MA** (maximum amplitude) | Maximum clot strength | ↓MA = ↓platelet function → platelet transfusion |
| **LY30** | Degradation of clots 30 min after MA | ↑LY30 (>7.5%) = excess fibrinolysis → antifibrinolytics (e.g., tranexamic acid) |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Characteristic Findings and Management of Post-cardiotomy Low Cardiac Output State (LCOS) (Figure 6)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Definition:** Cardiac output falls, causing significant impairment of oxygen delivery to tissues. Occurs in ~25% of children following cardiac surgery; peak 6–12 hours post-admission to PICU, returning to baseline after 24 hours.

**Causes:** Inflammatory response from CPB, myocardial ischaemia (aortic cross-clamp), reperfusion injury, ventricular injury (e.g., ventriculotomy), residual cardiac lesions, hypovolaemia, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary hypertension, arrhythmias (e.g., JET), drug delivery issues.

**Clinical features:**
*   Examination: cool peripheries, weak pulses, grey/mottled appearance
*   Observations: tachycardia, hypotension, reduced urine output, fall in cerebral/renal NIRS
*   Investigations: raised lactate, widening arteriovenous O₂ saturation difference (low SvO₂), end-organ injury, fall in cardiac index

**PRIMACORP study:** Milrinone use compared to placebo reduced incidence of LCOS; higher dose more effective.

**Oxygen delivery (DO₂):** $DO_2 = CO \times CaO_2$, where $CaO_2 = (1.34 \times Hb \times SaO_2) + (0.003 \times PaO_2)$

![](_page_21_Figure_6.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Factors determining oxygen delivery. CaO₂ = arterial content of oxygen; Hb = haemoglobin; SaO₂ = oxygen saturation; PaO₂ = partial pressure of oxygen; DO₂ = oxygen delivery; CO = cardiac output; HR = heart rate; SV = stroke volume; EDV = end-diastolic volume; ESV = end-systolic volume.**

**Key points:**
*   Interventions affecting PaO₂ (factor 0.003) are not significant
*   Hb and SaO₂ interventions are important for increasing oxygen transport
*   Heart rate is most important factor regulating CO in infants (small ventricular dimensions, poor compliance)
*   Preload and afterload manipulations are important given reduced contractility of young heart

**Systematic management approach:**
*   Sedation and muscle relaxation (reduce metabolic demand)
*   Optimise ventilation and oxygenation
*   Optimise preload: correct bleeding, ensure adequate Hb, fluid boluses according to response
*   Optimise contractility: inotropes (adrenaline, dopamine), inodilators (milrinone), calcium infusion
*   Optimise afterload: normothermia, inodilators (if high SVR) or vasopressors (noradrenaline, vasopressin) depending on SVR
*   Support end-organ function: RRT
*   Consider steroid therapy (hydrocortisone) for inotrope-resistant shock (may improve BP but not mortality; increases morbidity)
*   Extracorporeal life support if LCOS refractory to medical management

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Management of Postoperative Junctional Ectopic Tachycardia (JET) (Figure 7)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Incidence:** 5–15% of children undergoing congenital cardiac surgery; most common problematic arrhythmia. Occurs within first 24–72 hours; self-limiting, often terminating within 7–10 days.

**Risk factors:** Younger infants/neonates, low body weight for age, longer CPB or cross-clamp times, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest, complex surgery, TOF/AVSD, genetic syndromes, electrolyte disturbances, acidosis, use of inotropes.

**Haemodynamic compromise:**
*   Tachycardia reduces diastolic filling time
*   Loss of atrioventricular synchrony
*   Increased myocardial oxygen consumption → worsening ventricular function

**ECG features (Figure 7):**
*   Non-sinus tachycardia
*   QRS morphology similar to sinus rhythm
*   AV dissociation (ventricular QRS rate faster than atrial P wave)
*   Difficulty identifying P waves (lost within QRS complex unless using atrial ECG)
*   Regular QRS complexes with fixed R-R interval (occasional sinus beat may make rhythm appear irregular)

![](_page_24_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Typical electrocardiographic rhythm strip of a patient with junctional ectopic tachycardia.**

**Management (cannot be cardioverted):**
*   Adequate sedation and muscle relaxation (reduce metabolic demand, intrinsic catecholamine release)
*   Optimise electrolytes (especially magnesium, potassium, calcium), correct acidosis
*   Reduce body temperature (aim 35°C) with antipyretics, environmental measures, cooling devices
*   Reduce inotropes, especially dopamine (balance with ventricular dysfunction/LCOS)
*   Antiarrhythmic drugs:
    *   α-agonists (clonidine, dexmedetomidine): reduce intrinsic heart rate, provide excellent sedation
    *   Amiodarone: rate control (prolongs action potential and refractory period); negative inotropic effect
    *   β-blockers (esmolol): rate control (increases AV delay); powerful negative inotropic effect → mainly used prior to ECMO
*   Overdrive pacing (AAI or DDD) at 10–15 bpm above intrinsic JET rate to promote return of AV synchrony
*   ECMO if refractory (supports CO until JET resolves spontaneously)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles for Renal Replacement Therapy Post-cardiotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

**Incidence:** Acute kidney injury in 20–60%; RRT required in 2–7% (associated with increased mortality).

**Risk factors:** Younger age, low body weight, complex surgery (higher RACHS-1 category), univentricular or cyanotic defects, longer CPB and cross-clamp times, pre-operative pulmonary hypertension, known CKD, pre-operative ventilation/inotropes/PICU admission.

**Indications for RRT:** Fluid overload, severe AKI, oliguria/anuria, profound acidosis, hyperkalaemia, acute-on-chronic renal failure.

| Modality | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|----------|------------|---------------|
| **Peritoneal dialysis (PD)** | Relatively safe (ultrasound guidance); no vascular access; no anticoagulation; simple equipment; efficient fluid removal | Limited control over clearance; haemodynamic instability with intermittent fluid shifts; limited to neonates/infants in acute setting; limited electrolyte/waste clearance; avoid if GI pathology |
| **Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT, haemodialysis)** | Predetermined fluid removal; consistent electrolyte/waste clearance; continuous (less haemodynamic instability once established); works in all ages | Large venous dialysis catheter; anticoagulation required; potential instability on initiation; complex prescription/management; risk of recirculation |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Managing an Infant with Postoperative Chylothorax</summary><div class="details-content">

**Diagnosis:** Pleural chest drain losses persisting >48 hours. Definitive diagnosis by pleural fluid analysis:
*   Triglycerides >1.1 mmol/L (when enterally fed)
*   Lymphocytes >80%
*   Chylomicrons present

**Causes:**
*   Damage to central lymphatic system (thoracic duct) — especially aortic arch surgery
*   Elevated systemic venous pressures (Glenn/Fontan, SVC obstruction/thrombosis, RV failure)
*   Congenital lymphatic abnormalities

**Management:**
*   Chest drain insertion
*   Treat reversible causes (e.g., SVC thrombosis)
*   Monitor biochemical markers, serum proteins, immunoglobulins
*   **Nutritional management:** Continue normal enteral feed (especially breast milk) if tolerated; if losses increase → change to medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) feed (not absorbed via lymphatic system); if losses remain high → intravenous parenteral nutrition. Involve dietician.
*   IV octreotide if losses persistently high
*   **Surgical options** for recurring chylothorax: thoracic duct ligation, pleurodesis, pleurectomy

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 4. Postoperative Management by Procedure</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management for a Neonate with Biventricular Anatomy Following a Palliative Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

**Pulmonary artery (PA) banding:** Limits pulmonary blood flow in patients with large L→R shunt until child is large enough for full correction.

**Management depends on band tightness:**
*   **Loose PA bands** (mild to moderate obstruction): Acceptable if planned for long period; child will grow and cardiac output increase will progressively tighten band. Postoperative management: minimise L→R shunt, reduce pulmonary oedema. If unresponsive → surgical recalibration.
*   **PA bands with moderate to severe obstruction:** May cause RV failure, hypoxia, LCOS (especially post-bypass with myocardial stunning). May require PVR reduction (↑pulmonary blood flow), inotropic support. If unresponsive → surgical recalibration.

Isolated PA banding patients can generally be extubated quickly after a period to assess clinical effect.

**Aortopulmonary shunts (modified BT shunt):** Provides pulmonary blood flow when full biventricular repair cannot be performed immediately. Key question: is BT shunt the only source of pulmonary blood flow? Shunt failure would lead to no pulmonary blood flow and rapid arrest.

**Post-BT shunt management:**
*   Appropriate anticoagulation (heparin → aspirin)
*   Balance circulations
*   Rapid extubation if only procedure performed
*   Support nutrition and growth prior to definitive surgery

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management Following Surgical Repair of Transposition of the Great Arteries (Arterial Switch)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Key aspects:** Neonate undergoing CPB; reimplantation of coronary arteries.

**Postoperative complications:** Standard CPB complications, LCOS (6–12 hours post-bypass), coronary artery complications, arrhythmia, pulmonary hypertension.

**Coronary artery perfusion pressure (CAPP):**
$$ \text{Left CAPP} = \text{Aortic diastolic BP} - \text{Left atrial pressure} $$
$$ \text{Right CAPP} = \text{Aortic diastolic BP} - \text{CVP} $$

(In absence of mitral lesions, LVEDP ≈ LAP; in absence of tricuspid lesions, RVEDP ≈ CVP)

Coronary artery problems more likely with single coronary systems or intramural course.

**General management:**
*   Manage general CPB complications
*   Monitor for cardiac ischaemia: ST segments on serial ECGs, regional wall motion on echocardiography
*   Manage LCOS: milrinone with adrenaline/dopamine; reduce metabolic demand (ventilation, sedation, normothermia); consider mechanical support (ECMO)
*   Maintain coronary perfusion pressure: low-dose vasopressor (noradrenaline/vasopressin) to prevent diastolic hypotension; avoid fast or large fluid boluses (increase LAP and CVP, reducing coronary perfusion)
*   Manage arrhythmias
*   Diuretics, fluid restriction, consider RRT
*   Consider cardiac catheterisation for coronary angiography if clinical deterioration or ventricular arrhythmias

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management for a Neonate with Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Drainage (TAPVC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Pre-operative:** Assess presence and severity of obstruction (at pulmonary veins, veno-atrial connection, or interatrial communication). Obstructed TAPVC = paediatric cardiac surgical emergency (medical management is temporising). Non-obstructed TAPVC managed with conventional heart failure treatment until surgery.

**Postoperative problems:**
*   Standard CPB complications
*   Pulmonary hypertension crisis
*   Persistent residual pulmonary venous obstruction
*   Pleural effusion or chylothorax
*   Arrhythmias

**Management:**
*   Manage general CPB complications
*   Careful monitoring and treatment of pulmonary hypertension
*   Manage arrhythmias
*   Diuretics, fluid restriction, RRT
*   Manage LCOS

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management Following Surgical Repair of Anomalous Left Coronary Artery from the Pulmonary Artery (ALCAPA)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Pathophysiology:** Chronic ischaemia during gestation (myocardium perfused with poorly oxygenated blood). LV dilated and poorly contracting at birth. Elevated PVR may prevent coronary flow steal initially; when PVR drops, myocardium is poorly oxygenated and perfused → acute ischaemia/infarction, dilated poorly functioning LV, mitral regurgitation (physiological or papillary muscle infarction).

**Postoperative issues:**
*   General CPB complications
*   LCOS (due to poor pre-operative LV function + CPB effects)
*   Coronary artery spasm or anatomical issues with reimplantation
*   Mitral valve regurgitation
*   Arrhythmias (often atrial or nodal rhythms; VF/VT suggests underlying coronary perfusion problems)

**Management:**
*   Manage general CPB complications
*   Monitor for cardiac ischaemia: ST segments on serial ECGs, regional wall motion on echocardiography
*   Manage LCOS: milrinone with adrenaline/dopamine; reduce metabolic demand (ventilation, sedation, normothermia); consider mechanical support (ECMO)
*   Maintain coronary perfusion pressure
*   Manage arrhythmias
*   Diuretics, fluid restriction, RRT
*   Consider cardiac catheterisation for coronary angiography if clinical deterioration or ventricular arrhythmias

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management for a Neonate or Infant with a Fully Repaired Left-to-Right Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

Following closure of L→R shunt, circulation should return to normal. Postoperative concerns relate to consequences of pre-operative high pulmonary blood flow.

**Expected complications:**
*   Standard CPB complications
*   Pulmonary hypertension (persistent or crisis) — increased risk with: high pre-operative Qp:Qs, smaller neonates/infants, delayed shunt closure, pre-operative heart failure, trisomy 21, pulmonary hypoplasia/upper airway obstruction, Eisenmenger syndrome
*   Arrhythmias and heart block
*   Residual lesions

**Management:**
*   All children require diuresis
*   **Low-pressure, high-volume shunts** (ASD, AVSD with no ventricular component, unobstructed TAPVC, PAPVC): low risk, can often be extubated quickly
*   **Pressure-loading shunts** (VSD, multiple VSD, complete AVSD, AP window, truncus arteriosus): often require milrinone post-CPB
*   High-risk pulmonary hypertension patients may require prolonged ventilation for observation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management for a Patient with a Fully Repaired Right-to-Left Shunt and Restrictive Right Ventricular Physiology</summary><div class="details-content">

**Restrictive RV physiology:** Stiff, non-compliant RV with small intracavitary volume. Characterised by end-diastolic antegrade flow during atrial contraction in MPA on Doppler echocardiography. Typically seen after TOF repair (underlying RV hypertrophy + CPB effects).

**Consequences:**
*   Impaired diastolic filling → reduced stroke volume and forward CO
*   Central venous hypertension (high RA pressures, RVEDP)
*   Poor end-organ perfusion (reduced pressure gradient, especially kidneys and gut)

**Complications:**
*   Standard CPB complications
*   Residual lesions: pulmonary regurgitation (especially with transannular patch), residual RVOTO, residual shunts, tricuspid valve dysfunction
*   Arrhythmias (especially JET)
*   LCOS (worsened by restrictive RV, residual lesions, reduced RV systolic function)
*   Pulmonary hypertension
*   Pleural effusion or chylothorax

**Management:**
*   Manage general CPB complications
*   Observe for and rapidly treat arrhythmias (tachycardia poorly tolerated due to diastolic impairment)
*   Ventilation: lowest possible mean airway pressure to maintain gas exchange (positive pressure may exacerbate RV afterload)
*   Optimise preload (important due to restrictive RV): judicious fluids and vasopressors (noradrenaline, vasopressin)
*   Inodilators (milrinone) to improve LCOS and provide lusitropic properties for RV
*   Evacuate any chylothorax or pleural effusions
*   Adequate diuresis, including early RRT

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management Following Norwood Operation (Stage I) with Modified BT Shunt or Sano Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

**Norwood operation components:** Atrial septectomy, reconstruction of aortic arch, creation of neo-aorta, systemic-to-pulmonary shunt (modified BT or Sano).

**Key principle:** Ensure adequate CO with balanced Qp:Qs, manage standard CPB complications.

**Sano modification:** Pulmonary flow is systolic and does not affect diastolic pressure in systemic circulation → more stable haemodynamic behaviour postoperatively (coronary flow not affected by drop in mean arterial pressure). However, not immune to Qp:Qs balancing problems.

**Issues with either shunt:**
*   Standard CPB complications: LCOS, tamponade (chest often left open), myocardial dysfunction
*   High pulmonary blood flow: high SVR (insufficient inodilation, pain, hypothermia), low PVR (hypocarbia, high FiO₂, BTS/BSA mismatch, excessive pulmonary vasodilation)
*   Low pulmonary blood flow: shunt obstruction/blockage, pulmonary hypertension episodes
*   Anatomical complications: pulmonary venous drainage obstruction, residual arch narrowing, AV valve regurgitation, neo-aortic valve regurgitation
*   Non-cardiac: ischaemic colitis, sepsis

**Multimodal monitoring:** ECG, BP, CVP, SpO₂, ETCO₂, NIRS (cerebral), urinary output, lactate, arteriovenous saturation gradient (allows Qp:Qs calculation assuming normal lung oxygenation).

**Causes of isolated arterial desaturation:**
*   Decreased pulmonary blood flow and reduced O₂ pick-up
*   Reduced mixed venous saturation
*   Pulmonary venous desaturation (poor lung function)

**General management:**
*   Manage general CPB complications
*   Balance Qp:Qs (maintain adequate PVR-SVR gradient; ideally Qp:Qs <1 postoperatively)
*   Reduce metabolic demand (ventilation, sedation, normothermia)
*   Leave chest open postoperatively (delayed closure)
*   Adequate inodilator (milrinone → later ACE inhibitor)
*   Heparin to prevent shunt blockage (later → aspirin)
*   Maintain adequate haemoglobin
*   Avoid jugular central venous access (preserve central vessels for later surgery)
*   Delay feeding (risk of ischaemic colitis); early parenteral nutrition if enteral feeds not tolerated or GI abnormalities
*   Consider repeat echocardiography, ECG, diagnostic cardiac catheter if difficulties managing physiology
*   VA ECMO support if inadequate oxygen delivery cannot be medically optimised

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management Following Bidirectional Cavopulmonary Shunt (BCPS) (Glenn)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Stage II single-ventricle palliation:** Redirect upper body systemic venous return (unilateral or bilateral SVCs) to pulmonary circulation, bypassing the heart. Also includes takedown of BT or Sano shunt, repair of branch PAs (if required), and repair of additional pathology.

**Aim:** Reduce volume loading of RV. Performed in first 3–6 months of life. Pre-operative diagnostic cardiac catheter ensures low PVR (SVC→PA drainage driven by minimal pressure gradients and inspiratory passive flow).

**Postoperative issues:**
*   Standard CPB complications
*   Sinoatrial node injury
*   SVC syndrome (mechanical obstruction or physiological due to pulmonary hypertension)
*   Pleural effusion or chylothorax
*   Arteriovenous malformations (systemic steal or pulmonary overflow)

**General management:**
*   Manage general CPB complications
*   Appropriate pacing (often AAI) for sinoatrial node dysfunction
*   Head elevation (30°) to promote passive venous drainage through SVC
*   Anticoagulation (heparin or aspirin) — especially bilateral BCPS (turbulent flow may cause thrombi at pulmonary artery section between anastomoses)
*   Milrinone (significant inotropic support often not required)
*   Ensure adequate preload to maintain venous drainage to PAs
*   **Permissive hypercapnia** (mild respiratory acidosis): hypercapnia causes cerebral vasodilation → increased blood flow to head and neck → increased venous return (preload) to SVC. Balance against possible increase in PVR.
*   Minimal ventilation pressures (without causing atelectasis): lowest peak inspiratory pressure, PEEP, shortest inspiratory times; spontaneous breathing modes as soon as possible; minimise intrathoracic pressure to promote SVC→PA flow
*   Early extubation (when clinically ready) — promotes passive cavopulmonary flow during inspiration, though loses benefits of positive pressure (e.g., ventricular support)
*   Remove jugular central venous lines as soon as possible (reduce thrombosis risk)
*   Delay chest drain removal until no longer draining

**Management of persistent hypoxia:**
*   Increase FiO₂
*   Avoid hyperventilation; ensure higher PaCO₂
*   Ensure Glenn shunt and branch PAs patent (echocardiography or cardiac catheter)
*   Ensure no pathologies affecting ventilation (atelectasis, pleural effusion)
*   Consider inhaled nitric oxide
*   Consider diagnostic cardiac catheterisation for collateral vessels or AVMs that may benefit from coiling

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Management Following Total Cavopulmonary Connection (TCPC) (Fontan) (Figure 8)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Final stage of univentricular palliation:** Connect IVC directly to PAs via extracardiac conduit (or less commonly intracardiac conduit). Fenestration (communication between conduit and adjacent atrial wall) may be opened to allow preload to increase to systemic ventricle if PVR rises (maintains CO at expense of systemic desaturation).

![](_page_40_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 8. Angiogram of a completed Fontan circulation, with bilateral bidirectional cavopulmonary shunts and an intracardiac total cavopulmonary connection in a child with situs ambiguous, right atrial isomerism, dextrocardia, and functional single ventricle. The arrows indicate direction of flow, with potential for slow flow, thrombus formation, and reduced CO due to competing and turbulent flow at the confluence. Early opacification of atria indicates patency of fenestration. Rt BCPS = right bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt; Lt BCPS = left bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt; LPA = left pulmonary artery; RPA = right pulmonary artery; IC TCPC = intracardiac total cavopulmonary connection; Rt HV = right hepatic veins; Lt HV = left hepatic veins; IVC = inferior vena cava.** *Image courtesy of Dr. Andrew Ho, University Hospital Southampton, UK.*

**Postoperative complications:**
*   Standard CPB complications
*   Systemic inflammatory response and hypovolaemia (more profound impact due to full venous return depending on passive flow) → low PA pressures (CVP), low common atrial pressure
*   Arrhythmias: sinoatrial node dysfunction or nodal tachycardias
*   High PVR → high PA pressures (CVP), low common atrial pressure; if fenestration present, increasing hypoxia
*   Ventricular dysfunction → high PA pressures and high common atrial pressure
*   Pleural effusions
*   Persistent hypoxia (large fenestration, collateral vessels, high PVR)
*   Fontan circuit failure

**General management (similar to BCPS):**
*   Manage CPB complications
*   Appropriate pacing to maintain AV synchrony
*   Head elevation
*   Appropriate anticoagulation (heparin, aspirin, or warfarin)
*   Ensure adequate preload
*   Vasopressors (noradrenaline) to manage inflammatory response and promote systemic venous return to PAs
*   Inodilator (milrinone) if ventricular impairment, LCOS, or AV valve regurgitation
*   Minimal ventilation pressures (lowest PIP, PEEP, shortest inspiratory times); spontaneous breathing as soon as possible
*   Early extubation when clinically ready
*   Remove jugular central venous lines as soon as possible
*   Delay chest drain removal until no longer draining

**Long-term complications requiring PICU care (failing Fontan):**
*   Systemic venous hypertension
*   Pleural effusions
*   Ascites
*   Protein-losing enteropathy
*   Thromboembolic events
*   Ventricular failure

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 5. Cardiac Arrest, Outcomes, and Supportive Care</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Management of Cardiac Arrest in the Post-cardiotomy Neonatal or Paediatric Patient (Table 4)</summary><div class="details-content">

Standard advanced paediatric life support should be initiated (early CPR, rapid defibrillation if appropriate). Reversible causes ("4Hs and 4Ts") must be considered quickly.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### TABLE 4: Potentially Reversible Causes of Cardiac Arrest Post-cardiotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

| Reversible Cause | Relevance to Post-cardiotomy Patients |
|------------------|---------------------------------------|
| **Hypoxia** | Airway/ventilation issues; residual lesion or blockage to pulmonary blood flow (e.g., clot in BT shunt). Correction with FiO₂ 1.0 should correct majority; consider underlying anatomy and surgery. |
| **Hypovolaemia** | Blood loss common post-cardiac surgery; may need volume replacement. Also 3rd space losses from systemic inflammatory response. |
| **Hypothermia** | Rewarming from bypass should occur prior to return to PICU; if on extracorporeal circuits, ensure temperature set correctly; avoid pyrexia post-arrest. |
| **Hyper/hypokalaemia/metabolic disturbances** | Unlikely cause but quickly excluded with blood gas (K⁺, Ca²⁺, glucose). Correct as needed. |
| **Tension pneumothorax** | Many children have pleural drains; ensure drains not blocked and remain *in situ*. Ultrasound can help during arrest. |
| **Tamponade (cardiac)** | Common cause. Typical features: hypotension, raised CVP, tachycardia, echo evidence. In neonates/smaller children, tamponade can occur without pericardial fluid (myocardial swelling against posterior sternum). **Assume in all post-cardiotomy cardiac arrests.** Tamponade not an echo diagnosis; emergency chest re-opening may be necessary even without echo findings. |
| **Thromboembolic** | Consider in children with coagulation disorders or shunt insertion (thrombosis risk). Venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis should be considered. |
| **Toxins** | Unlikely; ensure any infusions/drugs were administered correctly. |
| **Pulmonary hypertension** | More common in neonates/smaller infants or those with previous L→R shunts. Triggered by rising CO₂, acidosis, pain/agitation, waking up. Without residual shunt, CVP increases → hypotension → signs of LCOS → cardiac arrest without intervention. |
| **Arrhythmias** | Any arrhythmia can occur. Profound sinus bradycardia/heart block → LCOS. Tachyarrhythmia → loss of CO. Ventricular arrhythmias (VF/VT) → question coronary artery pathology. JET may cause all degrees of cardiovascular compromise and may require ECMO while resolving. |

</div></details>

**Specific interventions in post-cardiotomy patient:**
*   Activate extracorporeal life support team for ECMO cannulation
*   Early opening of chest → internal cardiac compressions
*   Inhaled nitric oxide into ventilation circuit
*   Attempt pacing with epicardial wires for bradycardia
*   Fix cardiac lesions (e.g., heparin bolus for blocked shunt)
*   Low threshold for early diagnostic interventions (CT scan or cardiac catheterisation) once stability obtained

</div></details>

*   Immediate postoperative: excellent PICU care, early recognition and management of complications
*   Longer-term: ongoing cardiology follow-up, psychological support, neurodevelopmental care, nutritional support

*   Impaired communication (e.g., autism)
*   Cognitive impairment
*   Behavioural difficulties
*   Motor developmental delay
*   Seizure disorders

#### Effects of Positive Pressure Ventilation on the Cardiovascular System (Table 6)</summary><div class="details-content">

Mechanical ventilation changes respiration from negative pressure (spontaneous breathing) to positive pressure, impacting more than just the lungs.

**Right-sided cardiac lesion management principles:**
*   Limit intrathoracic pressure
*   Lower peak and mean airway pressures, lower PEEP
*   Shorten inspiratory times
*   Optimise tidal volumes (ventilate to FRC)
*   Avoid hypoxia and respiratory acidosis (to control PVR)

**Left-sided cardiac lesion management principles:**
*   Ventilation to optimise afterload reduction (reduced transmural pressures)
*   Higher PEEP
*   Positive pressure to reduce venous return and volume loading

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### TABLE 6: Summary of the Effect of Positive Pressure Ventilation on the Heart</summary><div class="details-content">

| | Preload | Afterload |
|--|---------|-----------|
| **Pulmonary ventricle** | REDUCED<br>• Reduced venous return<br>• Decreased RAP<br>• Decreased RVEDV | ELEVATED<br>• Increased RVEDP<br>• Decreased pulmonary blood flow |
| **Systemic ventricle** | REDUCED<br>• Reduced pulmonary venous return<br>• Decreased LAP<br>• Decreased LVEDV | REDUCED<br>• Decreased LVEDP<br>• Decreased aortic transmural pressure |

</div></details>

Extubation (return to negative pressure) has opposite effects: increases systemic afterload, increases volume loading of pulmonary ventricle — high-risk period for all children with congenital heart disease.

</div></details>

#### Principles of Management of a Child with a Pulmonary Hypertensive Crisis (Figure 9, Table 7)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Pathophysiology:** Acute rise in PA pressure → RV pressure rise → (if intracardiac shunt present) R→L shunt causing systemic desaturation; (if no shunt) preload to LV reduces while pressure-overloaded RV distends, altering ventricular interdependence → ↓CO → bradycardia → arrest.

**Common features:** Sharp rise in CVP (elevated RVEDP, tricuspid regurgitation); compensatory tachycardia (early) → bradycardia (rapid progression of ventricular failure) → ↓CO → arrest.

#### Principles of Monitoring the Neurological Status of Children on the PICU</summary><div class="details-content">

**Standard neurological monitoring:**
*   Age-specific regular neurological observations (GCS, pupillary responses)
*   Sedation, COMFORT-B, and pain scores
*   Sedation breaks to assess neurology
*   Clinical examination of neurological system

**Additional bedside tools:**
*   **Bispectral index (BIS):** Depth of anaesthesia
*   **Cerebral function-analysing monitor (CFAM):** Amplitude-integrated EEG (aEEG)
*   **Formal EEG:** Requires specialist neurophysiology interpretation
*   **Cranial ultrasound:** Useful in infants with open fontanelle; rapid detection of intraventricular haemorrhage, can suggest severe ischaemia (may miss smaller changes)

**Other imaging modalities:**
*   **CT:** Quick, good for bleeding/infarction; may miss subtle pathologies; radiation exposure
*   **MRI:** Detailed information about brain matter (especially hypoxic brain injury); no radiation; longer acquisition time, less patient access; contraindications (e.g., certain pacemakers)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS) (Figure 11)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Definition:** Portable bedside ultrasound performed by intensivist to scan relevant body systems, answering a predefined binary question (yes/no), not intended for detailed anatomical/physiological assessment.

**Advantages:** Immediately available, real-time changes, repeatable, improves safety/accuracy of interventional procedures.

**Binary questions:**
*   **Cardiac POCUS:** Is ventricle contracting well? Is ventricle empty or full? Is there a pericardial effusion?
*   **Respiratory POCUS:** Is there pulmonary oedema? Pleural effusion? Pneumothorax? Consolidation?
*   **Abdominal POCUS:** Is there ascites?

**Particularly useful in cardiac population:** Assessment of extravascular lung water and pulmonary oedema (prior to extubation or on presentation). Pulmonary oedema shows extensive B-lines with normal pleural line (Figure 11); B-lines resolve in real time with oedema resolution (unlike CXR changes, which lag).

![](_page_55_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 11. Lung point-of-care ultrasound taken from anterior chest wall demonstrating: A) normal lung; B) pulmonary oedema.**

</div></details>

#### Principles of Nutrition for Cardiac Patients on the PICU (Table 8)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Importance:** Malnutrition associated with increased mortality, morbidity, longer PICU stay; prevents growth, makes further surgery more difficult. Regular dietician review essential.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### TABLE 8: Complications of Malnutrition and Overfeeding</summary><div class="details-content">

| Effects of Poor Nutrition | Effects of Overfeeding |
|---------------------------|------------------------|
| Impaired immunity | Fluid overload and oedema |
| Increased risk of infection | Increased risk of infection |
| Impaired wound healing | Diarrhoea |
| Constipation/ileus | Increased gastric losses/poor absorption |
| Specific vitamin/mineral deficiency | Obesity |
| Hypoproteinaemia and oedema | |

</div></details>

**Challenges:** Fluid restrictions, feed interruptions (procedures), gastric intolerances (ileus), gastrointestinal pathology (ischaemic colitis).

**Factors affecting nutrition:** Increased metabolic requirements (pyrexia, sepsis), poor intake (fluid restriction), increased losses (poor absorption, sweating), increased exertion (respiratory effort).

**Enteral feeding:** Preferable despite critical illness (maintains gut integrity, reduces ileus risk). Contraindications: necrotising enterocolitis (NEC), ischaemic colitis, GI bleeding, mechanical bowel obstruction. Multiple inotropes NOT an absolute contraindication.

**High-risk children for abdominal pathology/feeding complications (feed cautiously):**
*   Cardiac lesions with low systemic output: HLHS, critical AS, interrupted aortic arch, coarctation
*   Prolonged CPB and aortic cross-clamp times (especially small infants)
*   Children on ECMO
*   Previous abdominal pathology
*   Long-term poor nutrition (refeeding syndrome risk)

**Management:** Feed cautiously after period of stability. If enteral nutrition suspended for 7 days, consider parenteral nutrition. Low threshold for stopping feeds, abdominal X-ray/ultrasound, general surgical review for early detection of ischaemic colitis.

**Parenteral nutrition disadvantages:**
*   Venous thrombosis, difficulty obtaining long-term IV access
*   Line sepsis
*   Metabolic abnormalities, electrolyte imbalances
*   Regular blood sampling (renal/liver function, lipids, amino acids, triglycerides)
*   Imbalance of trace elements
*   Financial implications

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Considerations for the Child Who Is Dying</summary><div class="details-content">

**Process:**
1.  Obtain MDT agreement that treatment no longer in child's best interest
2.  Involve second opinions, ethical/legal reviews, senior management as needed
3.  Family discussions: ensure understanding; time is important
4.  Involve palliative care services
5.  Consider organ donation if clinically indicated
6.  Memory boxes (handprints, footprints, photographs) to support parents
7.  Support religious beliefs
8.  Consider post-mortem
9.  Record all phases, routine clinical practice, and communication (legal investigations may arise)
10. Perform relevant investigations for diagnosis (e.g., skin and muscle biopsies)

**Withdrawing life-sustaining treatment considerations:**
*   Location: home, hospice, or hospital
*   Timing
*   Who will be present (family, PICU team)
*   Symptom management plans

**After death:**
*   Emotional support for team, debriefs
*   Reporting and legal documentation (death certification)
*   Ongoing follow-up and support for family
*   Outstanding investigation results (e.g., genetic reports)

</div></details>
