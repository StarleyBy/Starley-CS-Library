# Cardiopulmonary Bypass

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Aims of Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB)

CPB facilitates open heart surgery by:
*   Completely replacing heart and lung function
*   Providing a clear operating field
*   Maintaining O₂ delivery and CO₂ removal
*   Emptying the heart → minimising myocardial O₂ demand
*   Providing myocardial and end-organ protection: cardioplegia (stops cellular metabolism) + hypothermia (↓cellular O₂ demand)

### CPB Circuit Components (Figure 1)

| Component | Function |
|-----------|----------|
| **Venous line (blue)** | Drains blood from SVC/IVC (or RA/hepatic veins) |
| **Reservoir** | Hard-shell plastic container with filters and volume markings |
| **Pump** | Roller pump (flow set regardless of afterload) or centrifugal pump (flow dependent on preload/afterload) |
| **Oxygenator** | Microporous fibres; gas exchange across micropores |
| **Heat exchanger** | Incorporated into oxygenator; precise thermoregulation |
| **Arterial line (red)** | Returns blood to arterial circulation (antegrade or retrograde) |
| **Cardiotomy suction (green)** | Removes blood from operative field |

### Safety Features

*   **Level detector** (reservoir) — stops arterial pump if venous return drops (prevents air entrainment)
*   **Ultrasonic bubble alarm** (arterial line) — stops pump if bubbles detected
*   **One-way valves** (suckers) — prevent air being blown into patient
*   **Arterial line pressure monitors** — alarm if cannulae obstructed (prevents circuit rupture)
*   **Filters** (reservoir, oxygenator) — prevent gaseous/particulate microemboli
*   **In-line monitors** — Hct, K⁺, lactate, O₂
*   **Hand crank** — for electrical failure

### CPB Pumps

| Pump Type | Characteristics |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Roller pump** (Figure 2) | Flow set regardless of afterload; requires adequate venous return; risk of spallation (tubing breakdown) |
| **Centrifugal pump** (Figure 3) | Non-occlusive; flow dependent on preload/afterload; backflow possible; more common in ECMO/VAD |

### Priming Constituents for Congenital Heart Surgery

*   **Crystalloid prime** (Hartmann's, Plasma-Lyte™) — maintains tonicity
*   **Packed red blood cells** — added in neonates/infants (Hct >24% on CPB associated with better outcomes; Hct >40 in single-ventricle neonates)
*   **Colloids** (gelatins, hetastarch, albumin) — theoretical oedema reduction (outcome benefit unproven)
*   **Mannitol** — osmotic diuretic, membrane stabiliser, free radical scavenger
*   **Sodium bicarbonate/THAM** — corrects base deficit
*   **FFP** — improves fibrinogen post-CPB (benefit inconclusive)
*   **Aprotinin** — for redo surgery (bleeding risk)

### Parameters Monitored During CPB

**Circuit parameters:**
*   Reservoir volume, venous return, arterial flow
*   Line pressures (arterial, cardioplegia) — high pressures (300–350 mmHg) stop pump

**Fluid parameters:**
*   Total gas flow, FiO₂
*   PaO₂ (target 20–30 kPa) — hyperoxic bypass may increase myocardial injury in cyanotic patients
*   SvO₂ — indicator of systemic perfusion (complicated by collaterals)
*   PaCO₂ — CO₂ addition prevents respiratory alkalosis
*   Hct — higher Hct strategy in infants associated with better outcomes
*   pH, K⁺, lactate — acidosis/lactate suggests inadequate perfusion
*   Blood glucose — rises during CPB (inflammatory response)

**Patient parameters:**
*   Arterial pressure — adjusted by pump flow or vasoactive drugs
*   RAP — should be zero on CPB (optimal venous drainage)
*   Core/peripheral temperature — avoid large gradients (<10°C)
*   Cerebral NIRS — indication of cerebral perfusion (especially in paediatrics)

### Differences: Congenital vs Acquired Cardiac Disease

| Feature | Congenital | Acquired |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| Patient age range | Preterm neonates to adults | Mostly adults |
| Circuit size | Smaller circuits; greater haemodilution | Larger circuits |
| Repeat procedures | Common (multiple staged/redo surgeries) | Less common |
| Sternotomy risk | Increases with number of procedures; femoral cannulation may be needed | Lower risk |
| Systemic-pulmonary collaterals | Common (difficult to assess tissue O₂ delivery) | Rare |
| DHCA requirement | Greater | Less |

### Anticoagulation

*   **Heparin** — added to CPB prime + IV bolus
*   **Target ACT:** 400–480 seconds
*   **Neonates/infants:** Less predictable efficacy/duration (variable clotting factors, immature metabolism)
*   **HIT:** Rare but possibly under-recognised

### Arterial Cannulation (Figures 4, 5)

*   Cannula must be large enough for adequate flow but small enough to avoid vessel obstruction
*   **Restenotomy:** Alternative sites (femoral, subclavian) for CPB prior to sternotomy
*   **Arterial switch procedure:** Distal placement to avoid flow directed away from arterial branches
*   **Stage I HLHS:** Cannula in pulmonary artery (aorta small/absent)
*   **Complex arch reconstructions:** Two cannulae for upper and lower body perfusion

### Venous Cannulation

*   Blood drains by gravity (siphon effect)
*   Separate IVC and SVC cannulae for optimal drainage
*   **Paediatric challenges:** Smaller vessels, smaller tubing reduces gravity siphon → may require vacuum-assisted drainage
*   **Restenotomy:** Femoral route may require vacuum assistance
*   **Unusual anatomy:** Left/bilateral SVC, azygos/hemiazygos continuation → >2 venous cannulae

### Venting the Heart

**Purposes:** Clear operative field, prevent heart warming, avoid distension (↑wall tension, ↓subendocardial perfusion), de-air heart

**Sources of blood return:**
*   Thebesian/bronchial veins (normal)
*   Systemic-pulmonary shunts (collaterals, PDA)
*   Abnormal anatomy (persistent left SVC)
*   Surgically implanted shunts (BT, Potts)
*   Coronary sinus (cardioplegia)

**Vent sites:** Left ventricle (antegrade via RSPV/LA or retrograde via aortic valve), right ventricle (antegrade)

### Flow Rate Calculation

$$\text{Flow (LPM)} = \text{BSA} \times \text{CI}$$

**DuBois formula:**
$$BSA = \sqrt{\frac{\text{Height (cm)} \times \text{Weight (kg)}}{3600}}$$

**Cardiac index (CI):** Adults 2.4; Paediatrics 2.6–2.8 (higher metabolic rate)

### Gas Exchange (Figure 6)

*   Gases diffuse across micropores (concentration gradients)
*   Microporous fibres orientated perpendicular/counter-current to blood flow → ↑efficiency
*   **Fick's law:** ↑concentration gradient → ↑diffusion
    *   ↑O₂ transfer: ↑O₂ concentration in fibres (blender)
    *   ↑CO₂ removal: ↑gas flow rate ("sweep")

### Myocardial Protection

**Match O₂ supply with demand:**
*   Adequate aortic root pressure
*   Empty ventricle (minimal wall tension)
*   Optimal Hct (high for O₂ carriage)
*   Adequate flow through small coronary vessels (low viscosity)

**Reduce O₂ demand:**
*   Hypothermia (↓metabolic rate)
*   Cardioplegia (↑extracellular K⁺ → paralysis) (Figure 7)

**Cardioplegia types:**
*   Blood cardioplegia (older children/adults) — ratios 4:1 or 1:1 (blood:cardioplegia)
*   Crystalloid (neonates/infants) — avoids viscous blood at low temperatures

### Cardiac Arrest Indications

**Arrest required for:**
*   Complex surgery on still heart
*   Surgery on very young myocardium
*   Risk of systemic air (intracardiac shunt, left-sided access, single ventricle)

**Arrest not required for:**
*   Extracardiac shunt (BT shunt)
*   Right-sided abnormality without intracardiac communications

### Myocardial Arrest Principles

**High-concentration solutions (St. Thomas'):** Depolarisation
*   K⁺ (high concentration) — alters resting membrane potential; side effects: vasoconstriction, Ca²⁺ loading
*   Mg²⁺ — inhibits myosin phosphorylase, protects ATP

**Depleted solutions (Bretschneider):** Hyperpolarisation
*   Na⁺ depletion — eliminates Na⁺ differential
*   Ca²⁺ depletion — prevents contractile force
*   Lidocaine — Na⁺ channel block (vasodilation)
*   Adenosine — enhances recovery

**Delivery routes:**
*   Antegrade via aortic root (requires competent aortic valve)
*   Antegrade directly into coronary vessels (caution with small ostia) (Figure 8)
*   Retrograde via coronary sinus (uncommon in CHD)

**Hypothermia:**
*   Local (ice, cooling jacket) — ↓50% O₂ consumption per 10°C
*   Systemic — decreases heat delivered to heart

**Hypothermic fibrillation (<24°C):** Still field, myocardium perfused if systemic pressure >70 mmHg; useful for redo surgery (avoids coronary injury). Problems: impaired coronary flow (compression), incompetent aortic valve (distension).

### Complications of CPB

**Paediatric:**
*   Immature organs more vulnerable
*   Lung injury (high PVR + inflammation + ischaemia-reperfusion)
*   Neurological: CVA, seizures, intracranial haemorrhage, delayed choreoathetoid syndrome
*   DHCA-related: NEC, perforation, bowel ischaemia
*   Cyanosis/hypertrophy → more sensitive to ischaemia-reperfusion injury
*   Acute kidney injury (common)

**ACHD:**
*   Increased catastrophic bleeding (redo procedures)
*   Pre-existing coagulopathy (liver failure + dilution)
*   Concurrent renal abnormalities

### Myocardial Injury Causes

*   **Cross-clamp ischaemic energy depletion** — may be reversible; hypoxic hearts less tolerant
*   **Reperfusion injury** — Ca²⁺ overload, free radicals, complement activation, endothelial injury, oedema, ↓NO → vasospasm, PHT, RV failure, air embolism
*   **LV distension** (inadequate venting)
*   **Failed myocardial protection** — long intervals between cardioplegia, impaired delivery (AR, anomalous coronaries, absent coronary sinus, coronary sinusoids)

### Temperature Control (Figure 9)

*   CPB heater-cooler with closed water circuits
*   Maintain thermal gradient <10°C (prevents microbubble formation during rewarming)
*   Children: smaller core-peripheral gradients (ineffective temperature regulation); large gradients → rapid heat loss from core to periphery

### Cerebral Protection (Figure 10)

**Optimise O₂ delivery:**
*   Adequate Hb (children 90 g/L, adults 80 g/L, cyanotic 120 g/L)
*   Adequate arterial saturations
*   Cerebral blood flow (↑CPB flow or ↑CO₂ → vasodilation)

**Reduce O₂ demand:**
*   Hypothermia (ice packs useful in infants with unfused fontanelles)
*   Sedation/analgesia

**Monitoring:**
*   Internal jugular venous saturation (>95% → minimal extraction)
*   NIRS (continuous transcranial)

**Drugs (uncertain benefit):** Corticosteroids (↓inflammation but ↑infection risk), mannitol (reduces fluid overload but may damage intracellular neuronal volume)

**Acid-base management during hypothermia:**

| Strategy | Method | Advantages |
|----------|--------|------------|
| **Alpha stat** | Allow pCO₂ to drift (blood becomes alkalotic as patient cools) | Maintains cerebral autoregulation, normal protein function, intracellular neutrality |
| **pH stat** | Add CO₂ to maintain pCO₂ 5.3 kPa, pH 7.4 at patient temperature | ↑cerebral blood flow (better cooling, ↑embolic load), minimises left shift of ODC |

pH stat may be better with DHCA (cerebral vasodilation provides more O₂ during cooling); alpha stat used for rewarming.

### Indications for Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA)

*   Aortic arch surgery (coarctation with arch hypoplasia, interrupted arch, Norwood, PHACES complex, descending aortic surgery for spinal protection)
*   Complex neonatal/paediatric surgery with multiple systemic-pulmonary collaterals
*   Difficult obstructed TAPVC
*   Unifocalisation of MAPCAs

### Principles of DHCA (Table 1)

**Cooling:** 15–20°C (cerebral metabolism ↓6–7% per 1°C)

**Risks:**
*   Impaired microcirculation (↑viscosity, vasoconstriction) — minimise with haemodilution (Hct 20% at 20°C)
*   Neurological injury, reduced neurocognitive function
*   Coagulopathy, platelet dysfunction
*   SIRS (capillary leak, oedema, organ dysfunction)

**Safety (time- and temperature-dependent):**

**Selective cerebral perfusion:**
*   **Antegrade** (Figure 11): Cannulate right axillary/subclavian arteries → clamp brachiocephalic artery → flow to right common carotid; additional cannula in left common carotid. Flow 10–30 mL/kg/min, pressure 30–70 mmHg
*   **Retrograde** (Figure 12): Attach arterial line/shunt to SVC. Flow 250–400 mL/min, pressure 25–40 mmHg; deoxygenated blood in aortic arch indicates cerebral O₂ uptake

### Renal Protection

*   Kidneys at risk during low flow/pressure
*   **Haemofiltration techniques:**
    *   CUF (continuous ultrafiltration) — during CPB
    *   ZBUF (zero-balance ultrafiltration) — during rewarming
    *   MUF (modified ultrafiltration) — post-CPB (caution: hypotension)
*   **Goal-directed perfusion:** DO₂ target 270–280 mL/min/m² + high Hct → significantly reduces postoperative acute renal dysfunction

### Anticoagulation Reversal (Protamine)

**Protamine reactions:**

| Class | Type | Mechanism | Treatment |
|-------|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Class 1** | Hypotension | Histamine | Fluids, vasopressors |
| **Class 2a** | True anaphylaxis | IgE antibodies | Stop protamine, adrenaline, hydrocortisone, chlorphenamine, bronchodilators |
| **Class 2b/c** | Anaphylactoid (immediate/delayed) | Complement cascade | Usually less severe |
| **Class 3** | Catastrophic pulmonary vasoconstriction | Complement + thromboxane A₂ → PHT, RV failure, shock, bronchospasm | Stop protamine, adrenaline, milrinone, often reinstitute CPB (with heparin) |

### Blood Conservation Challenges

**Paediatric:**
*   Small blood volume
*   Higher Hct requirement during CPB
*   Increased cyanotic heart disease
*   Immature coagulation ± congenital factor deficiency
*   Increased platelet dysfunction
*   Profound hypothermia more common

**ACHD:**
*   Redo operations (pre-emptive femoral CPB → prolonged dissection → cumulative blood loss)
*   Abnormal coagulation
*   Difficult cross-matching (antibodies from previous transfusions)

**Conservation techniques:**
*   Retrograde/antegrade autologous priming
*   Miniature circuits with vacuum-assisted venous drainage
*   Whole blood from venous line given before protamine (after MUF)

### Strategies to Limit Blood Loss

**Antifibrinolytics:**
*   **Tranexamic acid:** 100 mg/kg bolus + 10 mg/kg/hr infusion
*   **Aprotinin:** Test dose 1 mL (10,000 KIU), loading 4 mL/kg (40,000 KIU/kg, max 2M KIU), pump prime 4 mL/kg, infusion 1–2 mL/kg/hr. BART trial showed increased death/stroke/renal impairment → temporary suspension; reinstituted 2012 (greater benefits than risks)

**Topical haemostatic agents:**
*   Fibrin sealants (Tisseel, Evicel)
*   Tissue adhesives (BioGlue®) — NOT for children (prevents tissue growth)
*   Gelatin matrices (Floseal, Surgiflo, Surgicel, Gelfoam)
*   Platelet-rich fibrin (Vivostat PRF®) — autologous

**Other:**
*   Viscoelastic tests (TEG®, ROTEM®) — guide transfusion
*   Filtration — concentrates platelets/clotting factors
*   Erythropoietin — pre-op for low Hb
*   Autologous blood donation — age >3 years, weight >15 kg

### CPB Management with Aortopulmonary Shunt

**Examples:** Ductus arteriosus, MAPCAs, surgical shunts (BT, central Gore-Tex®), multiple small aortopulmonary vessels

**Challenge:** Cessation of systemic ejection → excessive pulmonary run-off → systemic hypoperfusion

**Management:**
*   Shunt-dependent pulmonary circulations → control shortly after commencing bypass
*   Shunt-dependent systemic circulations → cooling period to preserve end-organ function
*   Ejection during cooling helps maintain systemic perfusion
*   May require elevated bypass flows and vasopressors
*   If shunt cannot be controlled: hypothermia reduces O₂ demand → temporary reduction of bypass flow
*   DHCA may be necessary for intracardiac repair (excessive left atrial return)
*   Once heart stops ejecting: adequate left atrial drainage mandatory (prevents lung congestion)

## 1. Aims and Components of Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Aims of Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB)</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is a complex circulation and respiratory device that facilitates open heart surgery by:
*   Completely replacing the function of the heart and lungs
*   Providing a clear operating field for the surgeon
*   Maintaining oxygen (O₂) delivery to, and carbon dioxide (CO₂) removal from, the patient's tissues
*   Reducing the workload of the heart by emptying, thus minimising myocardial oxygen demand
*   Providing additional myocardial and end-organ protection through:
    *   Cardioplegia solution to stop myocardial cellular metabolism
    *   Reducing the patient's temperature to decrease cellular oxygen demand

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Critical Components of a Cardiopulmonary Bypass Circuit (Figure 1)</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_1_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 1. Components of the cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) circuit, including the venous return component of the circuit (blue), arterial component (red) and suction/venting system (green).**

*   **Venous line (blue):** Drains blood from the systemic venous circulation, usually the superior and inferior vena cavae (SVC and IVC), or less commonly in congenital surgery, the right atrium (RA) or hepatic veins
*   **Reservoir:** Usually a hard-shell plastic container with filters (to remove debris) and volume markings (to visualise changes in venous return)
*   **Pump:** Can be either a roller or centrifugal pump
*   **Oxygenator:** Contains thousands of microporous fibres inside a plastic housing. Air and oxygen are circulated within the fibres, whilst blood flows around them. Gas exchange occurs across micropores, replacing the function of the lungs
*   **Heat exchanger:** Incorporated into the oxygenator housing. Water is pumped through the exchanger and allows precise thermoregulation of the circulating blood
*   **Arterial line (red):** Returns blood to the arterial circulation (usually the aorta) providing antegrade flow. A variety of other externally or internally accessible sites may be used, including the subclavian artery, brachiocephalic artery and femoral artery (which results in retrograde flow). This is particularly common in redo surgery, which occurs more frequently in congenital procedures
*   **Cardiotomy suction (green):** Controlled by roller pumps to remove blood from the operative field and return it to the reservoir, thereby keeping the surgical field clear

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Cardiopulmonary Bypass Components Materials</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The CPB circuit is single use and made of a variety of synthetic polymers, with most tubing sets manufactured from polyvinyl chloride (PVC) and plasticisers to increase flexibility
*   Tubing contained in the raceway of the roller pumps and suckers are usually made of silicone, which produces less haemolysis (in comparison to PVC) when blood is repeatedly compressed by rollers
*   Oxygenator fibres are made of polypropylene, stretched and heated to form micropores
*   The oxygenator accounts for the largest surface area within the CPB circuit and is often the only component of the circuit to be coated with biocompatible substances, such as para-methoxy-N-ethylamphetamine (PMEA) or heparin
*   The rest of the circuit is less commonly coated, due to cost, though evidence suggests fully coated circuits cause less inflammatory response

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Important Safety Features Incorporated into a CPB Circuit</summary><div class="details-content">

**Intra-circuit air detectors:**
*   **Level detector attached to reservoir:** Stops the arterial pump if venous return is reduced to the point of emptying the reservoir (prevents gross air in circuit). Most likely with surgical manipulation (blocking venous cannulae) or cannula displacement
*   **Ultrasonic bubble alarm:** Attached to the arterial line after the oxygenator. Stops the pump if bubbles are detected (e.g., negative pressure draws gas across oxygenator or cavitates air)
*   **One-way valves:** Often incorporated into suckers on neonatal and paediatric CPB circuits; prevent air being blown into patient if suckers are wrongly placed or pump operated in wrong direction

**Other safety features:**
*   **Arterial line pressure monitors:** Alarm if cannulae obstructed (reduces risk of circuit rupture)
*   **Filters:** Incorporated in reservoir and oxygenator; prevent gaseous or particulate microemboli reaching the patient
*   **In-line monitors:** Allow constant monitoring of haematocrit, potassium, lactate and O₂ levels
*   **Small-volume circuits:** Reduce foreign material exposure (reduces inflammatory response) and haemodilution
*   **Hand crank:** Available on every CPB machine for electrical failure

Perfusionist vigilance is necessary to act upon safety features.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Different Types of Cardiopulmonary Bypass Pumps (Figures 2, 3)</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_4_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2. A roller pump, which is repeatedly compressed to propel the blood and consists of tubing manufactured from silicone to reduce the risk of spallation.**

![](_page_4_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 3. A centrifugal pump with red arrows that demonstrate the inlet (A) and outlet (B) pipes.**

**Roller pumps:**
*   Allow desired flow to be set regardless of resistance to flow (afterload)
*   Constraints: adequate venous return, excessive arterial line pressure
*   With prolonged use, may be associated with spallation (breakdown of tubing) and particulate embolisation
*   More common in neonatal/infant patients

**Centrifugal pumps:**
*   Non-occlusive; more common in ECMO and VAD circuits
*   Vortex generated by spinning magnets creates pressure gradient between inlet (A) and outlet (B)
*   Flow generated is entirely dependent on preload and afterload
*   Backflow may occur, particularly at low flows
*   In children with small CO and high afterload, disproportionately large drop in flow may occur

## 2. Priming, Monitoring, and Differences from Acquired Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Typical Priming Constituents for CPB Circuits in Congenital Heart Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Crystalloid prime** (Hartmann's solution, Plasma-Lyte™): Mimics physiological conditions, maintains tonicity, minimises red cell lysis and fluid shifts
*   **Packed red blood cells:** Added in neonates/infants (even minimised CPB volumes risk excessive haemodilution). Hct >24% on CPB associated with reduced lactate and better psychomotor development; higher Hct not proven to improve outcomes. In cyanotic patients with high Hct, reduction may not be tolerated if mixed circulation remains
*   **Colloids** (gelatins, hetastarch, albumin): Increase osmotic pressure, theoretically reducing oedema (outcome benefit unproven)
*   **Mannitol:** Osmotic diuretic, stabilises cell membranes, scavenges oxygen free radicals, reduces cerebral oedema (outcome benefit inconsistent)
*   **Sodium bicarbonate or tromethamine (THAM):** Corrects base deficit
*   **Fresh frozen plasma (FFP):** Added in some paediatric/neonatal centres; thought to improve fibrinogen levels post-CPB (benefit inconclusive)
*   **Aprotinin:** Frequently added to prime for redo surgery (increased bleeding risk); use has varied over time

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Parameters Monitored During CPB</summary><div class="details-content">

**Within the CPB circuit:**
*   **Reservoir volume:** Ensure pump output matches return
*   **Venous return:** Reduction (snaring, kinking) may cause cerebral hypoperfusion (↑CVP, ↑ICP)
*   **Arterial flow:** Ensure adequate cardiac output delivered
*   **Line pressures (arterial, cardioplegia):** Avoid circuit rupture (kinked/obstructed lines); high pressures (300–350 mmHg) stop pump. Monitoring cardioplegic line pressure critical in neonates (avoid hypertensive flow)

**Fluid within the bypass circuit:**
*   **Total gas flow and FiO₂:** Manipulated to maintain O₂ and CO₂ within physiological values
*   **PaO₂:** Usually maintained 20–30 kPa; hyperoxic bypass associated with increased myocardial injury and systemic stress in cyanotic patients
*   **SvO₂:** Important indicator of systemic perfusion; complicated by systemic-pulmonary collaterals (high venous saturation despite organ hypoperfusion). During deep hypothermic CPB, higher proportion of O₂ delivery from plasma (Bohr effect)
*   **PaCO₂:** Allows appropriate CO₂ addition; excessively efficient removal can cause respiratory alkalosis
*   **Haematocrit (Hct):** Higher Hct strategy during low-flow hypothermic CPB in infants associated with better short- (lower lactate, higher post-op CI) and long-term (higher psychomotor development) outcomes. Hct >40 in neonates with single-ventricle circulation associated with better outcomes (especially stage I)
*   **pH, potassium, lactate:** Acidosis and ↑lactate suggest inadequate perfusion; potassium may rise after cardioplegia delivery
*   **Blood glucose:** Tends to rise during CPB (inflammatory response)

**Patient parameters:**
*   **Arterial pressure:** Adjusted mechanically (pump flow) or pharmacologically (metaraminol, phenylephrine, GTN) to ensure tissue perfusion
*   **RAP:** Should be zero on CPB (optimal venous drainage); helps guide filling when weaning
*   **Core and peripheral temperature:** Ensure even cooling/warming; avoid large gradients (<10°C) between heat exchanger and blood. Inadequate cooling = inadequate protection; over-warming linked to neurological damage
*   **Cerebral NIRS:** Indication of cerebral perfusion, particularly in paediatric patients

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Major Differences in CPB Between Congenital and Acquired Cardiac Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Patients range from preterm neonates to adults; ACHD population increasing but much smaller than acquired disease
*   **Circuit size:** Even small circuits for infants result in greater haemodilution than adults
*   **Repeat procedures:** Many congenital conditions require multiple staged or repeat procedures; risk of catastrophic injury to mediastinal structures increases with number of procedures. CPB may be initiated via femoral cannulation (though femoral cannulae size may be inadequate in children)
*   **Systemic-pulmonary collaterals:** Common in congenital patients; shunt blood away from systemic circulation (difficult to assess tissue O₂ delivery) and increase blood in operative field (requires venting)
*   **Complicated anatomy:** Extensive open-heart access, long CPB and cross-clamp durations associated with higher morbidity
*   **DHCA:** Greater need in congenital patients; associated with worse outcomes

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Venous Cannulation for CPB in Congenital Cardiac Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Blood drains by gravity from patient to reservoir (siphon effect)
*   Separate cannulae in IVC and SVC provide optimum drainage and empty heart for surgical access
*   **Paediatric challenges:** Drainage more likely compromised (smaller vessels, cannulae, blood volumes); small tubing reduces gravity siphon drainage → may require vacuum-assisted suction
*   Resternotomy cases: CPB may be initiated via femoral route (may not provide adequate venous return) → vacuum-assisted drainage
*   Unusual venous anatomy (left/bilateral SVCs, azygos/hemiazygos continuation of IVC) may necessitate >2 venous cannulae

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Venting the Heart During CPB</summary><div class="details-content">

**Purposes of cardiac vent (using one or more suckers):**
*   Clear the operative field
*   Prevent warming the heart (↑myocardial O₂ consumption)
*   Avoid distending the arrested heart (↑wall tension, ↑metabolic rate, ↓subendocardial perfusion → more vulnerable to ischaemic damage)
*   De-air the heart at procedure completion

**Sources of blood return to heart (venting required more frequently than in acquired disease):**
*   Thebesian and bronchial veins (normal circulation)
*   Pathophysiological systemic-pulmonary shunts (collateral vessels, PDA)
*   Abnormal anatomy (persistent left SVC)
*   Surgically implanted shunts (BT shunt, Potts shunt)
*   Coronary sinus (following cardioplegia)

**Vent sites:**
*   Left ventricle: antegrade via right superior pulmonary vein and left atrium, or retrograde via aortic valve (if aorta opened)
*   Right ventricle: antegrade

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Calculation of Required Flow Rate (Cardiac Output) in Adult and Paediatric Patients</summary><div class="details-content">

$$\text{Flow (LPM)} = \text{BSA} \times \text{CI}$$

**DuBois formula:**
$$BSA = \sqrt{\frac{\text{Height (cm)} \times \text{Weight (kg)}}{3600}}$$

*   **Adults:** Cardiac index (CI) 2.4
*   **Paediatrics:** CI 2.6–2.8 (higher metabolic rate)

</div></details>

## 4. Gas Exchange, Myocardial Protection, and Cardiac Arrest</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Gas Exchange in CPB Circuits (Figure 6)</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_13_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Membrane oxygenator with a hydrophobic membrane that presents a large surface area for gas exchange.**

*   Gases diffuse in and out of blood across micropores in oxygenator fibres according to concentration gradients
*   Microporous fibres orientated perpendicular (counter-current) to blood flow → increases gas transfer efficiency
*   **Fick's law of diffusion:** Amount of gas that diffuses increases as concentration gradients increase
    *   To increase O₂ transfer into blood: increase O₂ concentration in fibres (via blender)
    *   To increase CO₂ removal from blood: increase gas flow rate ("sweep") through fibres

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Myocardial Protection in Congenital Cardiac Surgery (Figure 7)</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_14_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Antegrade crystalloid cardioplegia delivered under pressure, usually via a roller pump.**

**Match O₂ supply with demand:**
*   Adequate aortic root (driving) pressure
*   Minimal ventricular wall tension (empty ventricle)
*   Optimal Hct for O₂ carriage (high)
*   Adequate flow (low viscosity) through small coronary vessels

**Reduce O₂ demand:**
*   **Temperature:** Reduction associated with decreased metabolic rate
*   **Pharmacological (cardioplegia):** Paralyse myocardium in flaccid state by increasing extracellular K⁺ concentration (blocks contraction, minimises cellular metabolism)

**Cardioplegia types:**
*   Mixed with arterial blood (older children/adults): ratios 4:1 or 1:1 (blood:cardioplegia)
*   Crystalloid solutions (neonates/infants): avoid highly viscous blood failing to perfuse tiny coronary arteries at low temperatures

**Additional considerations:**
*   ECG must be monitored at all times (collateral circulation may cause washout of cardioplegia)
*   Ventricular hypertrophy (e.g., aortic stenosis) may require higher doses of cardioplegia

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### When Is Cardiac Arrest Required for Congenital Cardiac Surgery?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Arrest required (cardioplegia represents myocardial ischaemic time):**
*   Complex surgery made easier on a still heart
*   Surgery needed on the myocardium of the very young
*   Risk of generating systemic air (intracardiac shunt e.g., ASD/VSD, left-sided access, single ventricle)

**Arrest not required for:**
*   Surgery for extracardiac shunt (e.g., Blalock-Taussig shunt)
*   Right-sided abnormality (e.g., RVOT obstruction) provided intracardiac communications excluded

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Myocardial Arrest in Congenital Cardiac Surgery (Figure 8)</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_16_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 8. Cardioplegia delivery in neonatal surgery: A) cardioplegia cannula inserted at angle caudally to aorta to avoid perforation of posterior wall and injury to aortic valve; B) following cross-clamp, avoid LV distension and high aortic root pressure.** *Images courtesy of Mr. Antonio Ravaglioli, University Hospital Southampton, UK.*

**High-concentration solutions (St. Thomas' solution) — induce membrane depolarisation:**
*   **Potassium chloride (high concentration):** Alters resting membrane potential (-90 mV) and ionic gradients; reduces availability of rapid opening Na⁺ channels → maintains diastolic arrest. Side effects: vasoconstriction (variable myocardial delivery), myocyte Ca²⁺ loading (↑repolarisation injury)
*   **Magnesium:** Inhibits myosin phosphorylase (protects ATP reserves), reduces vasoconstriction and intracellular Ca²⁺ loading

**Depleted solutions (Bretschneider solution) — induce membrane hyperpolarisation:**
*   **Sodium depletion:** Eliminates Na⁺ differential across cell membrane → diastolic cardiac arrest
*   **Calcium depletion:** Prevents contractile force generation
*   **Lidocaine:** Blocks Na⁺ channels on cytoplasmic side of membrane (local anaesthetic action); associated vasodilation promotes even distribution (overcomes K⁺ vasoconstriction)
*   **Adenosine:** Believed to enhance recovery (small doses)

**Delivery routes:**
*   Antegrade via aortic root (requires competent aortic valve)
*   Antegrade directly into coronary vessels (caution to avoid trauma, especially with small coronary ostia) (Figure 8)
*   Retrograde via coronary sinus (uncommon due to possible malformations)

**Hypothermia:**
*   **Local hypothermia** (ice, cooling jacket): Reduces phosphate utilisation; decreases basal O₂ consumption ~50% per 10°C drop
*   **Systemic cooling:** Decreases heat delivered to heart by blood returning via: normal body drainage (venae cavae — congenital patients may have persistent left SVC), normal heart drainage (coronary sinus, Thebesian veins), lungs (bronchial veins, anomalous PV drainage), collateral vessels (may require active venting)

**Hypothermic fibrillation (<24°C):**
*   Provides still field; if systemic pressure maintained >70 mmHg, myocardium remains perfused via antegrade coronary flow (protection for ≥1 hour)
*   Useful in redo surgery (avoids coronary injury risk)
*   Problems: impaired coronary flow (fibrillating myocardium compresses coronary arteries), incompetent aortic valve (ventricular distension, coronary malperfusion)

**Cross-clamp fibrillation:**
*   Heart electrically fibrillated (reduces myocardial load but induces global ischaemia and anoxic arrest)
*   Hypothermia (32–34°C) reduces myocyte O₂ demand
*   Decompress ventricles intermittently with aortic cross-clamp (avoid distension)
*   Limit each fibrillating episode to 10–15 minutes; at least 3–4 minutes coronary reperfusion between clamping periods

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 5. Complications and Organ Protection</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Important Complications of CPB in Congenital Cardiac Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

**General paediatric:**
*   Immature organs more vulnerable to damage
*   **Lung injury:** Foetal lungs have high PVR that drops in first weeks of life; CPB in neonates (especially with persistently high PVR) increases lung injury (inflammation from circuit surface contact + ischaemia-reperfusion injury)
*   **Neurological complications:** CVA, seizures, intracranial haemorrhage, delayed choreoathetoid syndrome
*   **DHCA-related:** Major GI complications (NEC, perforation, bowel ischaemia)
*   **Cyanosis/hypertrophy:** Particularly challenging to protect; more sensitive to ischaemia-reperfusion injury
*   **Acute kidney injury:** Common in children following CPB
*   **Chronic hypoxia and high blood viscosity** (older severely cyanotic patients): May further impact organ function

**ACHD:**
*   Increased risk of catastrophic surgical bleeding (redo procedures)
*   Pre-existing coagulopathy (varying degrees of liver failure + dilution of clotting factors/platelets during CPB → increased haemorrhage risk)
*   Concurrent renal abnormalities (repeated CPB + chronic hypoxia → increased AKI and long-term damage)

Pre-existing abnormalities make it difficult to attribute these solely to CPB; hypoxia, temperature-related cerebral flow changes and embolism are potential mechanisms.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Causes of Myocardial Injury During CPB for Congenital Cardiac Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

**Cross-clamp ischaemic energy depletion:**
*   May be reversible or irreversible depending on ischaemia length and protection efficacy
*   Damage results from: ischaemia with lactic acidosis, depletion of high-energy phosphates, altered intracellular calcium, direct trauma
*   Chronically hypoxic hearts (common in CHD) less able to tolerate ischaemia
*   Late reperfusion activates inflammatory response and apoptosis
*   Intermediate duration ischaemia → myocardial stunning with delayed recovery

**Reperfusion injury (after cross-clamp removal):**
*   Intracellular calcium overload and leucocyte activation
*   Release of oxygen free radicals → complement activation → endothelial injury, ↑microvascular permeability → oedema, cell death
*   Superoxide anion production (↑O₂ availability) → damages endothelial barriers, promotes neutrophil adhesion/activation
*   Inhibition of endothelial NO production → exacerbates coronary artery vasospasm, pulmonary hypertension, RV failure
*   Intra-coronary air embolism during reperfusion → further ischaemia (especially in neonates)

**Left ventricular distension (inadequate venting):** Reduced ability to eject against high afterload

**Failure of myocardial protection:**
*   Long intervals between cardioplegia (especially complex cases)
*   Impaired cardioplegia delivery due to:
    *   Aortic regurgitation
    *   Anomalous coronary arteries arising from pulmonary artery (do not receive cardioplegia)
    *   Absent coronary sinus (may be associated with persistent left SVC)
    *   Anomalous connections of coronary sinus with left atrium or coronary arteries
    *   Coronary sinusoids (fistulous connections between coronary arteries and RV, e.g., pulmonary atresia) → shunting of blood away from myocardium

**Surgical issues:** Direct myocardial injury, coronary injury, prolonged bypass or cross-clamp times

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Temperature Control During CPB (Figure 9)</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_20_Figure_6.jpeg)

**Figure 9. Heater cooler unit attached to the CPB circuit (red arrows), with heat exchange mat (zigzag lines) separating heater-cooler water flow from patient blood flow.**

*   Heat loss occurs from skin and mucous membranes by radiation, conduction, convection and evaporation
*   CPB heater-cooler provides temperature-controlled closed water circuits (isolated from patient) to two heat exchangers (cardioplegia circuit and oxygenator circuits)
*   Monitor venous and arterial blood temperatures; maintain thermal gradient <10°C (prevents microbubble formation during rewarming)
*   **Children:** Lose heat more readily than adults (large body surface area relative to body mass); smaller core-peripheral gradients used (infants regulate body temperature ineffectively). Large gradients → rapid heat loss from core to peripheral tissues
*   Children more often have shunts/collaterals, requiring DHCA for clear surgical field

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Measures to Optimise Cerebral Protection During Congenital Cardiac Surgery (Figure 10)</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_22_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 10. Operative image demonstrating set-up for DHCA and regional antegrade perfusion to brain and heart. Flow to circle of Willis via distal arm of arterial line to right brachiocephalic artery (via interposition graft, clamped proximally). Flow to coronaries via proximal arm to aortic root (clamped distal to cannula). LCCA and LSCA individually snared. Arch accessible beyond isthmus on beating heart with brain perfusion. RBCA = right brachiocephalic artery; Ao = aorta; RA = right atrium.** *Image courtesy of Mr. Antonio Ravaglioli, University Hospital Southampton, UK.*

**Optimise O₂ delivery (particularly important in children with higher O₂ demand):**

$$\text{O}_2 \text{ delivery} = \text{Cardiac output} \times \text{O}_2 \text{ content}$$

$$\text{O}_2 \text{ content} = (\text{Hb} \times 1.34 \times \text{SpO}_2) + (\text{PaO}_2 \times 0.03)$$

*   Adequate Hb (transfusion triggers: children 90 g/L, adults 80 g/L, cyanotic disease 120 g/L); minimise CPB circuit volumes
*   Adequate arterial oxygen saturations
*   Cerebral blood flow (↑CPB flow or ↑CO₂ → vasodilation)

**Reduce O₂ demand:**
*   Reduced temperature (ice packs more useful in infants with unfused fontanelles)
*   Effective sedation/analgesia

**Tissue oxygenation monitoring:**
*   Internal jugular venous saturation (>95% suggests minimal cerebral O₂ extraction)
*   NIRS (continuous transcranial)

**Drugs (uncertain benefit):**
*   Corticosteroids: reduce inflammation but may increase infection risk
*   Mannitol: reduces fluid overload but may affect intracellular neuronal volume (severely damaging effects)

**Antegrade or retrograde cerebral perfusion** (Figure 10)

**Acid-base management during hypothermia:**
*   **Alpha stat strategy:** Allow pCO₂ to drift as dictated by solubility (blood becomes more alkalotic as patient cools). Advantages: maintains cerebral autoregulation (couples CBF to O₂ demand → reduced flow minimises microemboli damage), buffering capacity of alpha imidazole group, intracellular electromechanical neutrality
*   **pH stat strategy:** Add CO₂ to maintain pCO₂ 5.3 kPa and pH 7.4 at patient temperature. Advantages: increased cerebral blood flow (better cerebral cooling at cost of increased embolic load), minimises left shift of O₂ dissociation curve (O₂ more easily unloaded to tissues)

Best strategy unclear; pH stat may be better with DHCA (cerebral vasodilation provides more O₂ during cooling). Some evidence of advantage in paediatric patients (less so in adults). Alpha stat used for rewarming.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 6. Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest and Organ Protection</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Indications for Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest in Congenital Cardiac Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

DHCA use is uncommon and generally limited to certain operations:

**Aortic arch surgery (regional perfusion of carotid/coronary arteries can minimise injury):**
*   Coarctation with aortic arch hypoplasia requiring full arch reconstruction
*   Interrupted aortic arch requiring complex reconstruction (long segments of interruption)
*   Norwood procedure
*   Rare aneurysms (PHACES complex)
*   Descending aortic surgery (redo coarctation repair, complications) to protect spinal cord

**Other indications:**
*   Complex neonatal/paediatric surgery with multiple systemic-pulmonary collaterals returning blood to left heart/pulmonary arteries
*   Difficult cases of obstructed TAPVC
*   Unifocalisation of MAPCAs

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (Table 1, Figures 11, 12)</summary><div class="details-content">

During total DHCA, no tissues are perfused and O₂ debt is inevitable despite reduced basal metabolic rate.

**Cooling:** Patient usually cooled to 15–20°C before initiating circulatory arrest (cerebral metabolism decreases 6–7% per 1°C temperature fall)

**Risks:**
*   Impaired microcirculation (↑plasma viscosity, vasoconstriction, reduced erythrocyte plasticity) — minimised by haemodilution (Hct 20% at 20°C) at risk of inadequate O₂ delivery during rewarming
*   Neurological injury and reduced postoperative neurocognitive function
*   Coagulopathy and platelet dysfunction
*   SIRS (capillary leak, oedema, organ dysfunction)

**Safety is both time- and temperature-dependent:**

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### TABLE 1: Safe Duration of Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest at Different Temperatures</summary><div class="details-content">

| Temperature (°C) | 'Safe' DHCA Time (minutes) |
|------------------|---------------------------|
| 36 | 1 |
| 32 | 5 |
| 28 | 10 |
| 24 | 20 |
| 20 | 30–40 |
| 16 | 45–60 |

</div></details>

Major changes in mortality: low mortality at 18°C for 45 minutes; 90 minutes DHCA associated with 90% mortality

**Selective cerebral perfusion enhances brain protection:**

![](_page_25_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 11. Antegrade cerebral perfusion circuit with blood returned to patient via left common carotid artery and right common carotid artery (via right subclavian artery).**

*   **Antegrade flow:** Cannulate right axillary/subclavian arteries, clamp brachiocephalic artery (directs flow to right common carotid). Additional cannula in left common carotid. Recommended flows 10–30 mL/kg/min, perfusion pressure 30–70 mmHg

![](_page_26_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Retrograde cerebral perfusion circuit with blood returned to patient via superior vena cava.**

*   **Retrograde flow:** Attach arterial line or shunt to SVC. Recommended flows 250–400 mL/min, perfusion pressure 25–40 mmHg. Presence of deoxygenated blood in aortic arch indicates cerebral O₂ uptake

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Renal Protection During CPB</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Kidneys particularly at risk during low flow or low pressure → retention of excess fluid and solutes, protein loss
*   **Haemofiltration techniques:**
    *   **CUF (continuous ultrafiltration):** Whole circuit (including patient) filtered during CPB
    *   **ZBUF (zero-balance ultrafiltration):** High-volume circuit ultrafiltration during rewarming
    *   **MUF (modified ultrafiltration):** Post-CPB, blood removed from arterial line, passed through haemofilter, returned via venous cannula. Careful volume management required (avoid hypotension from reduced preload and CO)
*   **Goal-directed perfusion:** Measuring DO₂ (target 270–280 mL/min/m²) with high Hct significantly reduces risk of postoperative acute renal dysfunction

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of Anticoagulation Reversal Following Congenital Cardiac Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

**Protamine:** Cation derived from salmon sperm; strongly alkaline, attracts strongly acidic heparin from its binding site on anti-thrombin III. Inadequate doses leave free heparin circulating.

**Protamine reactions:**

| Class | Type | Mechanism | Treatment |
|-------|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Class 1** | Hypotension | Histamine | Fluids, vasopressors |
| **Class 2a** | True anaphylaxis | IgE antibodies | Stop protamine, adrenaline, hydrocortisone, chlorphenamine, bronchodilators |
| **Class 2b/c** | Anaphylactoid (immediate/delayed) | Complement cascade | Usually less severe |
| **Class 3** | Catastrophic pulmonary vasoconstriction | Complement activation + thromboxane A₂ → PHT, RV failure, circulatory shock, severe bronchospasm | Stop protamine, adrenaline, milrinone, often reinstitute CPB (with heparin) |

</div></details>

## 7. Blood Conservation and Special Situations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Main Features of Blood Conservation and Bleeding in Congenital Cardiac Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

**Paediatric challenges:**
*   Small blood volume
*   Requirements for higher Hct (to deliver O₂) during CPB
*   Increased rate of cyanotic heart disease
*   Immature coagulation system and association with congenital deficiency of coagulation factors
*   Increased risk of platelet dysfunction
*   Profound hypothermia used more commonly

**ACHD bleeding risk:**
*   Redo operations (pre-emptive femoral CPB may be necessary → prolonged mediastinal dissection during anticoagulation → significant cumulative blood loss)
*   Abnormal coagulation systems
*   More challenging cross-matching (antibody production from previous transfusions)

**Conservation techniques (smaller blood volume):**
*   Retrograde autologous priming (RAP) and antegrade autologous priming (displace bypass prime with patient's own blood)
*   Miniature circuits with vacuum-assisted venous drainage (reduce haemodilution)
*   Using whole blood (high Hct) from venous line given directly to anaesthetist before protamine (after MUF)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Strategies to Limit or Prevent Blood Loss in Congenital Cardiac Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

**Antifibrinolytic agents:**
*   **Tranexamic acid:** Binds to plasminogen → inhibits fibrinolysis. 100 mg/kg bolus + 10 mg/kg/hr infusion
*   **Aprotinin:** Plasmin and kallikrein inhibitor (slows fibrinolysis, inhibits factor XIIa formation); reduces post-op mediastinal blood loss and transfusion requirements. Anti-inflammatory effects (↓IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, Mac-1). BART trial showed increased death/stroke/renal impairment/heart failure → temporary suspension; reinstituted 2012 (results not replicated, greater benefits than risks). Dosing: test dose 1 mL (10,000 KIU), loading 4 mL/kg (40,000 KIU/kg, max 2M KIU), pump prime 4 mL/kg, infusion 1–2 mL/kg/hr (10,000–20,000 KIU/kg/hr)

**Topical haemostatic agents:**
*   **Fibrin sealants** (Tisseel, Evicel): Combinations of fibrinogen, thrombin, aprotinin (emulates final clotting cascade stages)
*   **Tissue adhesives** (BioGlue®): Bovine serum albumin + glutaraldehyde → protein hydrogel mechanical seal (independent of coagulation). **NOT for children** (does not allow tissue growth)
*   **Gelatin matrices** (Floseal, Surgiflo, Surgicel, Gelfoam): Swell and control bleeding (mechanical tamponade + thrombin-induced clot formation)
*   **Platelet-rich fibrin** (Vivostat PRF®): Autologous fibrin sealant from patient's own blood (120 mL taken pre-op); effective for suture line bleeding, supports tissue regeneration and wound healing

**Other strategies:**
*   **Viscoelastic tests** (TEG®, ROTEM®): Guide transfusion policy, optimise blood product usage
*   **Filtration:** Removes excess water and small molecules (<12,000 Da) → concentrates platelets and clotting factors
*   **Erythropoietin therapy:** Pre-operative use reduces transfusion requirements in those with low Hb
*   **Autologous blood donation:** Only useful age >3 years, weight >15 kg

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Principles of CPB Management in the Presence of an Aortopulmonary Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

**Examples of aortopulmonary shunts:**
*   Ductus arteriosus
*   Major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs)
*   Surgical aortopulmonary shunts (BT shunt, central Gore-Tex® shunt)
*   Multiple small aortopulmonary vessels in mediastinum of severely cyanosed patients

**Challenge:** Cessation of systemic ejection with CPB results in excessive pulmonary run-off from shunt → systemic hypoperfusion

**Management:**
*   Ideally control aortopulmonary shunts prior to commencing CPB (may not be possible in duct-/shunt-dependent circulations, if shunt difficult to access, or multiple small collaterals)
*   **Shunt-dependent pulmonary circulations:** Control shortly after commencing bypass
*   **Shunt-dependent systemic circulations:** Cooling period to help preserve end-organ function until surgical repair completion
*   Ejection of heart during cooling helps maintain systemic perfusion pressures
*   May require elevated bypass flows and vasopressors
*   Once shunt controlled, bypass and surgical repair conducted in usual fashion
*   If shunt cannot be controlled: hypothermia reduces tissue O₂ demand → temporary reduction of bypass flow → reduced pulmonary run-off
*   DHCA during intracardiac repair may be necessary to manage excessive left atrial return and provide adequate visibility
*   Once heart stops ejecting: adequate left atrial drainage mandatory (prevents severe lung congestion from significant pulmonary venous return)

</div></details>
