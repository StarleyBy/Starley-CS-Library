# Congenital Abnormalities of the Mitral Valve

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Function and Anatomy
- **Mitral valve function:** Opens in diastole (LA → LV), closes in systole (prevents regurgitation).
- **Embryology:** Development begins 4th–5th week, complete by 15th week. Endocardial cushions fuse at 6th week → lateral cushion → posterior leaflet, superior cushion → anterior leaflet. Papillary muscles form by delamination (completes ~17 weeks).
- **Normal components:** Annulus, two leaflets (anterior semi-circular, posterior quadrangular with 3 scallops P1–P3), two papillary muscles (anterolateral, posteromedial), multiple chordae tendineae.

### Classification (Carpentier)
| Type | Leaflet motion | Aetiology |
|---|---|---|
| I | Normal | Posterior leaflet defect, cleft MV, annular dilatation |
| II | Excessive | Elongated/absent cords, elongated papillary muscle, excess tissue |
| III | Restricted | Lack of tissue, papillary muscle–commissure fusion, parachute MV, hammock MV |

### Specific Anomalies
**Leaflet:**
- Double-orifice MV (Types I and II) — associated with AVSD
- Imperforate leaflet (mitral atresia) — with aortic atresia (HLHS)
- Isolated cleft MV — anterior leaflet, failure of AV cushion fusion
- Straddling leaflets, posterior leaflet hypoplasia

**Papillary muscle/chordal:**
- Parachute MV — single papillary muscle, associated with Shone syndrome (ASD, VSD, coarctation, LVOT obstruction)
- Asymmetric dominant papillary muscles (Types I–III)
- Hammock MV (mitral arcade) — papillary muscles extend to leaflet edges

**Annular:**
- Supramitral/intramitral rings (band-type or membrane-type)
- Isolated annular dilatation (with LV dilatation or ALCAPA)

### Epidemiology and Associated Defects
- **Incidence:** 0.4% of CHD, 5/100,000 general population
- **Male predominance:** 1.5–2.2:1
- **Associated defects (96%):** PDA, VSD, coarctation, conotruncal abnormalities, Shone syndrome

### Pathophysiology
- **Stenosis:** ↑ LAP → LA dilatation/hypertrophy → pulmonary oedema → RV overload → pulmonary hypertension
- **Regurgitation:** ↑ LV and LA volumes → chamber dilatation/hypertrophy → elevated pulmonary vascular pressures
- Concomitant defects: ASD alleviates stenosis manifestations; VSD worsens them

### Clinical Presentation
**Symptoms:** Dyspnoea, poor feeding, failure to thrive, wheezing/recurrent infections, syncope/aphonia (from LA compression)
**Signs:** Normal peripheral exam, LV dilatation (palpable in MR), blowing pansystolic murmur (MR) or mid-diastolic murmur (MS), RV heave (if PH), tachycardia/poor perfusion (if failure)

### Diagnosis
- **ECG:** Sinus rhythm, LA enlargement, 1° AV block, RVH (PH), LV enlargement (MR)
- **CXR:** Increased bronchial angle (LA enlargement), pulmonary oedema, ventricular enlargement
- **TTE:** Primary modality — 2D for anatomy, Doppler for stenosis/regurgitation
- **TOE:** When TTE windows suboptimal; 3D echo for enhanced imaging
- **MRI:** Ventricular volumes/function (valve anatomy less detailed)
- **Catheterisation:** Rarely for isolated disease; useful for associated anomalies and haemodynamics
- **Fluoroscopy:** Assess implanted mechanical valves

### Mitral Valve Prolapse (MVP)
- **Definition:** Leaflet bows into LA (usually posterior leaflet)
- **Aetiology:** Myxomatous proliferation of spongy layer, acid mucopolysaccharide deposition
- **Incidence:** 2.4% general population; autosomal dominant in non-syndromal cases
- **Associated syndromes:** Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos, osteogenesis imperfecta, cutis laxa, pseudoxanthoma elasticum
- **Symptoms:** Dyspnoea, fatigue, dizziness, palpitations, non-exertional chest pain
- **Signs:** Late systolic murmur following mid-systolic click
- **ECG:** Inverted T waves (inferior/lateral), prolonged QTc, SVT/VT
- **Prognosis:** Usually benign; complications include endocarditis, heart failure, sudden death

### Indications for Surgery
**Stenosis:**
- Poorly tolerated symptoms or evidence of pulmonary hypertension
- Delay intervention in neonates/infants if possible (valve grows in first months)
- 5× greater mortality than MR → often requires earlier surgery
- Repair can be palliative until child is old enough for replacement

**Regurgitation:**
- Symptoms present or progressive
- Secondary effects: arrhythmia, progressive ventricular dilatation, pulmonary hypertension

### Non-Surgical Interventions
- **Medical:** Diuretics (stenosis and regurgitation), ACE inhibitors (MR)
- **Balloon angioplasty:** Limited experience in congenital MS (used in rheumatic disease)

### Surgical Repair — Stenosis
- **Exposure:** Vertical/transverse transatrial septal incision, or left atrial incision at interatrial groove (± SVC division)
- **Techniques:**
  - Papillary muscle splitting/fenestration (parachute MV)
  - Patch augmentation of posterior leaflet (detach from annulus, augment with pericardium)
  - Leaflet thinning (dissect fibroelastic layer)
  - Excision of supramitral/intramitral rings
- **Avoid:** Supra-annular valve replacement (worse immediate/long-term results)

### Surgical Repair — Regurgitation
- **Annuloplasty:** Pericardium (preferred in children), partial annuloplasty (allows growth), prosthetic ring (only in older children)
- **Chordal reconstruction:** PTFE
- **Cleft repair:** Running/interrupted sutures + annuloplasty if residual regurgitation
- **Elongated chordae:** Shortening or transfer
- **Elongated papillary muscles:** Shortening or reconstruction

### Replacement Options
| Type | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Bioprosthetic/homograft** | No anticoagulation | Accelerated degeneration (≈1 decade) |
| **Mechanical** | Durable, few thromboembolic events | Requires lifelong anticoagulation; reoperation for growth |
| **Melody valve** | Percutaneously dilatable as child grows | Originally for pulmonary position; adapted for MV |
| **Ross II** | No anticoagulation; potentially less structural deterioration | Does not grow; replaces two valves |

### Risks and Complications
**Common to all MV surgery:** Heart block (pacemaker), endocarditis, stroke

**Repair:** Residual stenosis/regurgitation (may progress, require reoperation)

**Replacement:**
- Prosthetic valve thrombosis, haemolysis, perivalvular leak, thromboembolism
- Anticoagulation complications (mechanical valves)
- Reoperation for growth and structural deterioration
- Patient-prosthesis mismatch (predictor of early/late death)

### Long-Term Outcomes
- **Stenosis vs regurgitation:** MS has worse prognosis; parachute MV is risk factor for worse outcome
- **Repair within first year of life:** Risk factor for persistent MV dysfunction
- **MR repair vs replacement:** Repair → lower rates of heart block, thromboembolism, endocarditis
- **10-year survival (MS):** 40% (medical), 56% (surgical)
- **At age 19 (after repair):** Freedom from MVR 82%, freedom from reintervention 91%

### Emerging Technologies
- **Melody valve:** Catheter-based, expandable as child grows
- **Tissue-engineered valves:** Potential for growth, avoiding anticoagulation and frequent replacements
- **Advanced imaging:** Better operative planning, earlier characterisation

## 1. Function, Embryology and Normal Anatomy</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Function of the Mitral Valve

The mitral valve is one of the two atrioventricular heart valves lying between the atria and ventricles:
- **Diastole:** Opens to allow unobstructed blood flow from left atrium (LA) to left ventricle (LV)
- **Systole:** Closes to allow forward ejection and prevent regurgitation into LA

#### Embryology

- **Timing:** Development begins during 4th–5th week of gestation, mostly complete by 15th week
- **Endocardial cushions:** Fuse during 6th week, partitioning left and right AV canals
  - Lateral cushion → posterior leaflet
  - Superior cushion → mainly anterior leaflet
- **8th week:** Mitral orifice takes shape; trabecular ventricular myocardium present
- **Papillary muscles:** Form by delamination of trabecular tissue (takes ~17 weeks)
- **Chordae and leaflets:** Cushion tissue separates and thins out during this time

#### Normal Anatomy

The mitral valve is a **bicuspid valve** with four main components:

| Component | Description |
|---|---|
| **Annulus** | Ring-like fibrous structure, continuous with fibrous skeleton of heart and left/right fibrous trigones |
| **Two leaflets** | Posterior (mural) leaflet: attached to 3/5 of annulus, quadrangular, 3 scallops (P1 lateral, P2 middle, P3 medial); Anterior (aortic) leaflet: semi-circular, attached to 2/5 of annulus, edges A1–A3 oppose posterior scallops |
| **Two papillary muscles** | Anterolateral and posteromedial |
| **Chordae tendineae** | Connect papillary muscles to leaflets; classified as marginal (free edge), rough zone, or strut (basal posterior leaflet); or primary (free edge), secondary, and tertiary |

Both leaflets are anchored at common points: the **anterolateral** and **posteromedial commissures**.

## 2. Classification of Congenital Mitral Valve Abnormalities</summary><div class="details-content">

Mitral valve disease can be broadly categorised into **stenotic** and **regurgitant** lesions.

#### Carpentier Classification of Mitral Regurgitation (Table 1)

| Type | Leaflet motion | Aetiology |
|---|---|---|
| **Type I** | Normal | Posterior leaflet defect, cleft mitral valve, annular dilatation |
| **Type II** | Excessive | Elongated cords, absent cords, elongated papillary muscle, excess tissue |
| **Type III** | Restricted | Lack of valvular tissue, papillary muscle–commissure fusion, parachute mitral valve, hammock mitral valve |

#### Leaflet Anomalies

**Double-orifice mitral valve (Figure 1):**
- Commonly associated with atrioventricular septal (canal) defects
- **Type I:** Bridge between two leaflets with otherwise normal subvalvular apparatus
- **Type II:** Two distinct orifices with their own chordal attachments

![](_page_2_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1.** 3D echocardiography of double-orifice mitral valve, LV apex view, two orifices in late diastole (white asterisks). Ao = aorta. *Image courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

**Imperforate leaflet (mitral atresia):** Usually associated with aortic atresia (as in HLHS)

**Isolated cleft mitral valve (Figure 2):**
- Usually involves anterior (aortic) leaflet
- Leaflet components dysplastic and thickened from failure of AV cushion fusion
- Results in mitral insufficiency

![](_page_3_Figure_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.** Cleft in anterior MV leaflet: A) Apical four-chamber: mild prolapse, reduced coaptation; B) Parasternal short-axis: defect on leaflet edge (white asterisk); C) Colour Doppler: posteriorly directed jet; D) Short-axis colour Doppler: jet origin from defect. *Images courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

**Straddling leaflets:** One leaflet overrides the other

**Posterior leaflet hypoplasia:** Valvular insufficiency from failure of coaptation

#### Papillary Muscle or Chordal Anomalies

**Secondary chords or additional papillary muscles:** Tether leaflets → relatively immobile (Type III dysfunction)

**Parachute mitral valve (Figures 3, 4):**
- Chordae attach to single centralised papillary muscle → stenosis and/or regurgitation
- Caused by failure of ventricular trabecular muscle to properly delaminate into two distinct papillary muscles
- Often associated with ASD, VSD, coarctation, or multiple left heart obstructive lesions (Shone syndrome)
- Presents as spectrum of stenosis/regurgitation depending on valve architecture

![](_page_4_Figure_5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 3.** Parachute MV: A) Enlarged, thickened single papillary muscle with tethered leaflets; B) Single papillary muscle (red asterisk) on short-axis; C) Reduced diastolic inflow (red flow); D) Moderate MS on CW Doppler. *Images courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

![](_page_5_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4.** Parachute MV in Shone complex: A) Diastole: inflow MV obstruction + aortic regurgitation; B) Systole: MR + aortic stenosis. *Images courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

**Asymmetric dominant papillary muscles:**
- Two papillary muscles, one contains majority of chordal fixation (usually to posteromedial leaflet)
- **Type I:** Elongated papillary muscle, normal chordae
- **Type II:** Elongated papillary muscle with few shortened chordae
- **Type III:** Elongated papillary muscle with chordae attached adjacent to annulus

**Mitral arcade / hammock mitral valve (Figure 5):**
- Papillary muscles extend to leaflet edges with short or absent chordae
- Results in stenosis and regurgitation

![](_page_6_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5.** 3D echocardiography of hammock MV: Two fused papillary muscles forming arcade (black asterisks), thickened leaflets (white asterisk). *Image courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

#### Annular Anomalies

**Mitral rings (Figure 6):**
- Fibrous growths that can be supravalvular or intravalvular
- Vary in thickness and circumferential involvement
- Cause mitral stenosis; may be associated with underlying anatomical abnormality causing turbulent flow
- Commonly grouped with congenital MV abnormalities (may be secondary lesions)

![](_page_6_Figure_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 6.** Supramitral ring: A) Ring visible (red arrow) above posterior MV leaflet attachment (white arrow); B) Flow acceleration at SMR level above true valve orifice (TVO). Associated multiple VSDs (red asterisks). *Images courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

**Isolated annular dilatation:**
- Secondary lesion occurring with:
  - LV dilatation (e.g., foetal dilated cardiomyopathy)
  - Congenital coronary abnormalities (e.g., ALCAPA)

## 3. Epidemiology and Associated Defects</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Incidence

- Mitral valve anomalies are **rare**: 0.4% of congenital heart disease
- Occurrence: 5/100,000 of general population
- Congenital mitral stenosis: 1.2% of autopsied CHD patients; 0.42% of all cardiac patients (one study)
- Another study: 0.6% of post-mortem patients had congenital MV deformities
- **Male predominance:** 1.5–2.2:1

#### Associated Congenital Heart Defects

In the **majority (96%)** of patients with MV anomalies, associated defects are identified:

- Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)
- Ventricular septal defect (VSD)
- Coarctation of the aorta
- Conotruncal abnormalities
- Shone syndrome

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 4. Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Mitral Stenosis

- Increased left atrial pressure → LA dilatation and hypertrophy
- Pressure reflected to pulmonary circulation → pulmonary oedema → right heart overload → pulmonary hypertension
- Severely stenotic valves may develop failure of leaflet coaptation → concomitant regurgitation

#### Mitral Regurgitation

- Increased LV and LA volumes → chamber dilatation and hypertrophy to accommodate increased flow
- Structural changes contribute to elevated pulmonary vascular pressures and ventricular sequelae

#### Effect of Concomitant Defects (Figure 7)

- **ASD:** Alleviates manifestations of elevated LAP in congenital MS (pop-off)
- **VSD:** Worsens manifestations of elevated LAP in congenital MS

## 5. Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Symptoms

- Dyspnoea
- Poor feeding
- Failure to thrive
- Wheezing and recurrent respiratory infections
- Syncope and aphonia (secondary to compression from enlarged LA)
- **Note:** Clinical manifestations are usually overshadowed by concurrent complex anomalies

#### Signs

- **Peripheral vascular examination:** Usually normal (including jugular venous pulses)
- **Palpation:** LV dilatation may be palpable in MR (LV enlargement)
- **Auscultation:**
  - MR: Blowing pansystolic murmur at apex radiating to axillae (different quality from VSD)
  - MS: Mid-diastolic murmur
- **If severe pulmonary hypertension develops:**
  - RV heave (RV hypertrophy)
  - Accentuated S₂ or diminished S₁
- **If heart failure present:** Tachycardia, poorly perfused extremities

|---|---|
| **ECG** | Sinus rhythm (usually); LA enlargement, 1° AV block, RVH (if PH), LV enlargement (if MR) |

## 7. Mitral Valve Prolapse (MVP)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Definition and Aetiology

**Mitral valve prolapse (MVP)** is a congenital defect in which a portion of the mitral leaflet bows outward into the LA. Usually involves the **posterior leaflet** (entire leaflet or portion).

**Aetiology:** Myxomatous proliferation of spongy layer of leaflet + increased deposition of acid mucopolysaccharides → parachute-like shape.

#### Epidemiology and Genetics

- **Incidence:** 2.4% of general population
- Usually undetected; symptoms may emerge after years as stress on valve produces anatomical deformity
- **Non-syndromal cases:** Likely autosomal dominant trait

#### Associated Syndromes

- Marfan syndrome
- Ehlers-Danlos syndrome
- Osteogenesis imperfecta
- Dominant cutis laxa
- Pseudoxanthoma elasticum

#### Clinical Features

- Prolapsing leaflet segment crosses coaptation line → regurgitation → heart failure symptoms
- **Symptoms:** Dyspnoea, fatigue, dizziness, palpitations, non-exertional chest pain
- **Signs:** Late systolic murmur following **mid-systolic click**

#### Diagnosis

- **ECG:** Inverted T waves (inferior ± lateral leads), prolonged QTc; associated dysrhythmias: SVT, VT
- **CXR:** Usually normal
- **TTE:** Demonstrates prolapsing valve ± regurgitation
- **Catheterisation:** No role

#### Prognosis and Complications

- Usually **benign**; managed by surveillance based on rhythm disturbances or symptoms
- **Complications:** Endocarditis, heart failure, sudden cardiac death

## 8. Indications for Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Mitral Stenosis

- Intervention indicated when **symptoms poorly tolerated** or **evidence of pulmonary hypertension**
- **Delay intervention in neonates/infants if possible** — MV continues to increase in size in first months of life
- Mitral stenosis carries **5× greater mortality** than mitral regurgitation → often requires earlier surgery
- MV repair (even if not normal/near-normal) can be **palliative** until child is old enough for formal replacement

#### Mitral Regurgitation

- Intervention indicated when:
  - Symptoms present or progressive
  - Secondary effects present: arrhythmia, progressive ventricular dilatation, or pulmonary hypertension

## 10. Surgical Repair of Congenital Mitral Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

#### General Principles

- Congenital MS is **more difficult** to manage surgically than acquired MS (unusual and variable anatomy)
- **Repair strongly preferred over replacement** if adequate result can be obtained
- Strategy selected based on individual patient anatomy

#### Exposure

- Vertical transatrial septal incision
- Transverse atrial septal incision
- Left atrial incision at interatrial groove (± division of SVC)

#### Repair Techniques

**Papillary muscle splitting/fenestration (Figure 9):**
- For parachute mitral valve
- Incise papillary muscles across the body → mobile leaflets, unobstructed inflow

![](_page_13_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9.** Papillary muscle splitting in parachute MV: A) Thickened, crowded chordae restricting leaflet opening; B) After incision across papillary muscle body (red dotted line) → mobile leaflets, unobstructed inflow.

**Patch augmentation of posterior leaflet (Figures 10, 11):**
- Detach posterior leaflet from annulus and posterior papillary muscle from posterior wall
- Avoid damage to ventricular wall, coronary sinus, and circumflex artery (red arrow)
- Augment gap with pericardial patch (yellow)

![](_page_13_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 10.** Patch augmentation: A) Detachment along red dotted line; B) Avoid circumflex artery (red arrow); C) Pericardial patch augmentation (yellow).

![](_page_14_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11.** Postoperative: A) TOE long-axis — good coaptation depth; B) TTE apical three-chamber — competent, unobstructed valve.

**Leaflet thinning:** Sharply dissect fibroelastic layer off remaining leaflet (similar to endarterectomy)

**Ring excision (Figures 12, 13):**
- Supramitral and intramitral rings directly excised
- Intramitral rings have additional difficulty of direct leaflet involvement

![](_page_14_Picture_5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 12.** Supramitral rings: A) Band-type — adherent to leaflets, restrictive effect; B) Membrane-type — above annular level, clearly visible, obstructive effect.

![](_page_15_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13.** Excision of membrane-type supramitral ring with sharp and blunt dissection.

#### Technique to Avoid

**Supra-annular valve replacement** (prosthetic valve placed in smaller annulus) — associated with **worse immediate and long-term results**.

## 11. Surgical Repair of Congenital Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

#### General Principles

- **Repair strongly preferred over replacement** if possible, even if reoperation required later
- Specific techniques vary by aetiology and valve morphology (less standardised than acquired MR repair)

#### Repair Techniques

**Annuloplasty (Figure 14):**
- Frequently required
- **Pericardium** avoids prosthetic material
- **Prosthetic ring annuloplasty** may reduce reoperation need but **cannot be used in infants/small children** (restricts growth)
- **Partial annuloplasty** preferred in growing children; support with pericardium, xenograft, PTFE, or felt

![](_page_16_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 14.** Surgical MV repair: A) Bilateral isolated commissuroplasty — reduces AP diameter; B) Posterior annuloplasty — reduces both lateral and AP diameters.

**Chordal reconstruction:** PTFE

**Cleft repair (Figure 15):**
- Suture leaflet tissue together with running or interrupted sutures
- Add annuloplasty if residual regurgitation after cleft closure

![](_page_16_Picture_6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15.** Repair of cleft anterior MV leaflet using interrupted sutures.

**Elongated chordae:** Shortening or transfer

**Elongated papillary muscles:** Shortening or reconstruction

#### Concomitant Repairs

Concomitant congenital repairs can safely be performed at the same operation.

## 13. Risks and Complications of Mitral Valve Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Common to All MV Surgery

- Heart block (requiring pacemaker)
- Endocarditis
- Stroke

#### Mitral Valve Repair

- Residual stenosis or regurgitation (can progress, may require reoperation)
- Intraoperative TOE helps guide immediate re-repair and improve results

#### Mitral Valve Replacement

- Prosthetic valve thrombosis
- Haemolysis
- Perivalvular leak
- Thromboembolism
- Endocarditis
- Anticoagulation complications (mechanical valves)
- Need for redo replacement due to growth and structural valve deterioration
- **Patient-prosthesis mismatch** (predictor of early and late death)
- Prosthetic valves too large for annulus also negatively impact survival

#### Advantages and Disadvantages Summary (Table 2)

| Type of procedure | Pros | Cons | Difficulty | Durability |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **MVr — MS** (commissurotomy, papillary splitting, fenestration, leaflet thinning, augmentation) | Maintains growing valve; no treatment needed; allows reinterventions | Uneven durability; likely reinterventions; anatomy/material-dependent; not suitable in hypoplasia | Demanding (small size, complex techniques) | More complex repairs less durable; seldom "one-off" |
| **MVr — MR** (leaflet resuspension, commissuroplasty, Alfieri stitch, annuloplasty, leaflet repair) | Maintains growing valve; no treatment needed; allows reinterventions | Possible reinterventions; anatomy/material-dependent; functional status-dependent | Demanding complex techniques | Large variability in reported outcomes |
| **MVR — Autograft/homograft** (Ross II, bicuspidalised homograft) | Growing valve; no treatment needed; excellent dynamics; great versatility | Very difficult in neonates; two valves replaced; risk of autograft failure; technique-dependent | Demanding, especially after previous interventions | Potentially as long as mechanical valves |
| **MVR — Tissue (bioprosthetic)** | Very good dynamics; no treatment needed | Limited durability; patient-related durability | Straightforward | Average ≈1 decade |
| **MVR — Mechanical** | Ever-improving dynamics; virtually life-lasting | Requires anticoagulation; high risk of thromboembolism; risk of acute failure | Straightforward | New devices more durable |

## 14. Long-Term Outcomes</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Key Findings

- Results vary significantly with underlying anatomy
- **Mitral stenosis** (vs regurgitation) is risk factor for late heart failure and persistent MV dysfunction
- **Parachute MV** is risk factor for worse long-term outcome
- **Repair within first year of life** is risk factor for persistent MV dysfunction

#### Repair vs Replacement (for MR)

- Repair results in **lower rates** of heart block, thromboembolism, and endocarditis

#### Survival

| Lesion | 10-year survival |
|---|---|
| Mitral stenosis (medical management) | 40% |
| Mitral stenosis (surgical management) | 56% |
| Mitral regurgitation | Better overall prognosis than MS |

#### Freedom from Reintervention

At age 19 (after MV repair for congenital and acquired disease):
- Freedom from mitral valve replacement: **82%**
- Freedom from repeat interventions: **91%**

## 15. Emerging Technologies</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Less Invasive Alternatives

**Melody® valve (Medtronic):**
- Catheter-based technology (originally for percutaneous pulmonary valve replacement)
- Adapted for mitral valve replacement via open surgical approach
- **Theoretical advantage:** Can be dilated percutaneously as child grows → fewer redo operative valve replacements
