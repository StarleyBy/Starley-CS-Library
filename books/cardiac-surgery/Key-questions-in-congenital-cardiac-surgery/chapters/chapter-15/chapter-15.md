# Atrioventricular Septal Defects

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Classification
*   **Atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD):** A spectrum of endocardial cushion defects defined by variable absence of the atrioventricular septum. Accounts for **3% of all congenital heart defects**.
*   **Complete AVSD:** Primum ASD + inlet VSD + common AV valve (left and right components with bridging leaflets).
*   **Intermediate AVSD:** Primum ASD + inlet VSD but with two separate AV valve orifices.
*   **Transitional AVSD:** Primum ASD + restrictive VSD + commonly a cleft in the left AV valve.
*   **Partial AVSD:** Primum ASD + usually a cleft in the left AV valve (no VSD).
*   **Rastelli classification (complete AVSD):** Type A (75%, divided superior bridging leaflet with chordal attachments), Type B (rarest, straddling chords, unbalanced), Type C (25%, undivided leaflet, no chordal attachments, associated with TOF).

### Associated Anomalies
*   Tetralogy of Fallot, LVOTO, Down syndrome (70–80% of complete AVSD), double-orifice left AV valve, additional VSDs, PDA (10%), DORV, TGA.

### Pathophysiology and Natural History
*   Left-to-right shunting at atrial level (partial/intermediate) → pulmonary volume overload → right-sided chamber dilatation → AV valve insufficiency.
*   Complete AVSD → shunting at both atrial and ventricular levels → volume and pressure overload of pulmonary circulation + left-sided chamber dilatation → heart failure within first months of life.
*   Irreversible pulmonary vascular changes as early as **6 months**, accelerated in Down syndrome.
*   Untreated → Eisenmenger complex (right-to-left shunt, cyanosis).
*   80% of untreated complete AVSD survive into 2nd–3rd decade; survival beyond 4th decade is rare.

### Diagnosis
*   **ECG:** Left axis deviation (inferior displacement of bundle of His), RV hypertrophy, occasionally prolonged PR interval.
*   **Chest radiograph:** Cardiomegaly, increased pulmonary vascular markings.
*   **Echocardiography:** Diagnostic — evaluates ASD/VSD size, AV valve regurgitation, valve/ventricular balance, LVOTO, associated anomalies. 3D echo useful for regurgitation mechanism.
*   **Catheterisation:** Not routine pre-op except in older patients for PVR calculation. Post-op for residual lesions (LVOTO, VSD).
*   **MRI:** Post-operative assessment of residual LVOTO.

### Surgical Indications and Timing
*   Diagnosis of AVSD is an indication for surgical repair.
*   **Complete AVSD:** Safe repair as early as **2 months**; waiting beyond 8 months risks obstructive pulmonary vascular disease.
*   **Partial/transitional AVSD:** After **1 year** of age (pulmonary circulation is protected).

### Surgical Principles
*   Complete repair: close all shunts (ASD, VSD, PDA), obtain two competent and non-stenotic AV valves, treat LVOTO potential, avoid conduction system.
*   Coronary sinus can be left on right or left side of patch.
*   Pulmonary artery banding (PAB) rarely performed (tendency to develop left AV valve regurgitation).

### Surgical Techniques for Complete AVSD
*   **Double patch technique:** Separate patches for VSD (Dacron® or autologous pericardium) and ASD (autologous pericardium). Cleft closure after VSD repair but before ASD repair.
*   **Single patch technique:** One large autologous pericardial patch — sutured to crest of ventricular septum, divided AV valves resuspended on patch, cleft closed, remaining patch closes ASD.
*   **Modified single patch technique (Nunn procedure):** AV valve tissue sutured directly to crest of ventricular septum with pledgeted sutures passed through septum, valve tissue, and patch.

### Partial and Transitional AVSD Repair
*   **Partial:** Left AV valve cleft closure + ASD closure with autologous pericardium.
*   **Transitional:** Restrictive VSD closure (1–2 pledgeted sutures) + left AV valve cleft closure + ASD closure with autologous pericardium.

### Complications
*   **Heart block:** Minimised by suturing VSD patch on right side of crest at posteroinferior border; closing coronary sinus into left atrium may also reduce risk.
*   **Residual ASD/VSD:** Assess by intra-op echo and saturation run. Qp:Qs formula: $\frac{\text{Aortic sats} - \text{Mixed venous sats}}{\text{Pulmonary vein sats} - \text{Pulmonary artery sats}}$
*   **LVOTO:** Usually from accessory chordal attachments requiring division.
*   **AV valve regurgitation:** Reoperation in up to 15%. Risk factors: double-orifice left AV valve, absence of Down syndrome, significant pre-op left AV valve regurgitation.

### Management of Left AV Valve Dysfunction
*   **Regurgitation (more common):** Cleft reclosure, annuloplasty at commissures, leaflet extension with autologous pericardium. Annuloplasty rings often too large for small patients.
*   **Stenosis:** Partial takedown of cleft repair (risks regurgitation), papillary muscle splitting.
*   **Valve replacement:** Last resort (smallest mechanical prosthesis 15mm — risk of heart block, coronary injury, outgrowth).

### Outcomes
*   **Early mortality (complete AVSD):** 2–5% (single institution); STS database: **2.2%**.
*   **Most common reasons for reoperation:** Left AV valve dysfunction and LVOTO → lifelong follow-up required.
*   **Unbalanced AVSD with single-ventricle palliation:** Subject to Fontan complications (protein-losing enteropathy, plastic bronchitis, liver dysfunction, diminished ventricular function).

## 1 What is an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Complete AVSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Complete AVSD comprises a **primum atrial septal defect (ASD)** and an **inlet ventricular septal defect (VSD)**. There is a **common atrioventricular (AV) valve** (not distinct mitral and tricuspid valves), with left and right components and superior and inferior portions that 'bridge' the crest of the VSD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intermediate AVSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Intermediate AVSD is comprised of a primum ASD and inlet VSD but has **two atrioventricular valve orifices**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transitional AVSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Transitional AVSD is comprised of a primum ASD and a **restrictive VSD**. There is commonly a cleft in the left AV valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Partial AVSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Partial AVSD is comprised of a primum ASD and usually a **cleft in the left AV valve** (no VSD component).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

*   **Rarest subtype**
*   Has **straddling chordal attachments**
*   Commonly seen in an **unbalanced AVSD**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rastelli Type C</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **25%** of complete AVSD
*   Has an **undivided superior bridging leaflet** and **no chordal attachments** to the crest of the ventricular septum
*   Commonly seen in patients with AVSD associated with **tetralogy of Fallot**

## 3 What is the embryology of an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Primum ASD</summary><div class="details-content">

After looping of the cardiac tube, septation and formation of the AV valves occurs, with the endocardial cushions located at the crux of the heart. **Failure of proper endocardial cushion growth into the posterior atrium** results in a primum ASD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Inlet VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

**Abnormalities of growth toward the ventricular apex** result in an inlet VSD, located under what normally would be the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Common AV Valve</summary><div class="details-content">

The endocardial cushions also grow to the left and right to form the AV valves. **Abnormal AV valve formation** results in a common AV valve with left and right components and superior and inferior portions that bridge the ventricular septum.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 4 Describe the anatomical features of an atrioventricular septal defect</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### ASD and VSD Components</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Although a primum defect is the commonest type of ASD found in AVSD, a **patent foramen ovale (PFO) or secundum ASD** is also often present.
*   The VSD is usually an **inlet defect**, which is commonly very large in complete AVSD defects.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Conduction System Anatomy</summary><div class="details-content">

As the conduction system is located **posteriorly and inferiorly near the crest of the ventricular septum**, it is important when closing the VSD that care is taken to place sutures on the **right ventricular side of the septum** in this area.

It is also important when closing the ASD to avoid the conduction system near the **coronary sinus** (Figure 3). In this area, sutures should be placed in the mouth of the coronary sinus and/or slightly leftward of the atrial septum. Alternatively, the coronary sinus can be closed to the left atrium to ensure the conduction system is avoided.

![](_page_3_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 3. Anatomical relations of the conduction system to a complete atrioventricular septal defect.** SVC = superior vena cava; IVC = inferior vena cava; SAN = sinoatrial node; AVN = atrioventricular node; CS = coronary sinus; RA = right atrium; LA = left atrium; RV = right ventricle; LV = left ventricle; PV = pulmonary vein; VS = ventricular septum; BoH = bundle of His; RAVV = right atrioventricular valve; LAVV = left atrioventricular valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Gooseneck Deformity (LVOT)</summary><div class="details-content">

The LVOT in an AVSD is described as a **'gooseneck'** (Figure 4). Although intervention on the LVOT is not common during repair in infancy, the LVOT requires **long-term echocardiographic evaluation** to screen for subaortic obstruction.

![](_page_3_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 4. A) Echocardiographic and B) angiographic images of a patient with an atrioventricular septal defect demonstrating the typical 'gooseneck' deformity of the left ventricular outflow tract.**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Left AV Valve Cleft</summary><div class="details-content">

The left AV valve commonly has a **cleft that runs perpendicular to the ventricular septum**. Cleft closure is recommended, when possible, to avoid left AV valve regurgitation. This can develop when the heart is septated, even if not present pre-operatively.

Care should be taken to **not 'purse-string' the suture** when closing the cleft. An interrupted technique may prevent purse-stringing and foreshortening of the leaflets.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Valve Tissue Assignment and Reintervention</summary><div class="details-content">

While all components of AVSD repair are important, **reintervention is most common for left AV valve regurgitation and/or stenosis**. When deciding how to best septate the superior and inferior bridging leaflets, it is critical to assign the proper amount of leaflet tissue to each side. Sometimes it is prudent to septate in such a way that assigns **slightly more leaflet tissue to the left side**.

A **deficient or poorly formed mural leaflet** is a risk factor for left AV valve regurgitation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Other Left AV Valve Abnormalities</summary><div class="details-content">

Left AV valve abnormalities in AVSD include:
*   Parachute type of left AV valve with a single LV papillary muscle
*   Rotated papillary muscles
*   **Double-orifice left AV valve**

In patients with a double-orifice left AV valve, the bridging tissue between the two orifices **should not be divided** during repair, as this will cause significant left AV valve regurgitation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Unbalanced AVSD</summary><div class="details-content">

**Unbalanced AVSD** refers to the morphology of the ventricle over which the AV valve predominantly empties (Figure 5).

*   **Unbalanced to the right:** right ventricle is large, left ventricle is underdeveloped
*   **Unbalanced to the left:** left ventricle is large, right ventricle is underdeveloped

Unbalanced AVSD occurs along a spectrum. Some unbalanced AVSD can be septated while others are palliated with a **single-ventricle approach**.

When the AVSD is unbalanced to the right and septated, the left AV valve cleft is sometimes **left open** to prevent left AV valve stenosis.

**Balanced AVSD** can be safely septated.

![](_page_4_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 5. Complete atrioventricular septal defects, with:** A) balanced defect; B) right-dominant unbalanced defect; and C) left-dominant unbalanced defect.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 5 What anomalies are associated with atrioventricular septal defects?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Tetralogy of Fallot.** If a transannular patch is necessary for repair of the TOF component, any subsequent pulmonary regurgitation can be problematic if the right AV valve has significant insufficiency after repair of the AVSD component.
*   **Left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO).** The apically displaced common hinge point of the AV valves creates an elongated LVOT ('gooseneck'). Abnormal chordal attachments from the left AV valve to the ventricular septum increase the risk of LVOTO.
*   **Down's syndrome.** The majority (70–80%) of patients with complete AVSD have Down's syndrome, whereas 10% of patients with partial AVSD have Down's syndrome. Overall, **50% of all children with Down's syndrome have a complete AVSD**. It is not clearly understood why patients with Down's syndrome rarely develop LVOTO.
*   **Double-orifice left AV valve.** Rarely seen, usually in the inferior bridging leaflet. The smaller orifice is usually not addressed at the time of repair (creating a single orifice may cause significant left AV valve insufficiency).
*   **Additional ventricular septal defects** can be present in any location.
*   **Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)** is seen in approximately **10%** of patients with AVSD and is more common in those with a VSD.
*   **Double-outlet right ventricle (DORV)** and **transposition of the great arteries (TGA)** occur rarely with AVSD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 6 What is the pathophysiology of an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Shunt Patterns by Type</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The anatomy of an AVSD results in **left-to-right shunting**.
*   **Complete AVSD:** non-restrictive VSD and single or multiple ASD.
*   **Partial AVSD:** similar pathophysiology to a simple ASD, with shunting dependent on defect size and ventricular compliance.
*   **Transitional AVSD:** restrictive VSD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Role of Pulmonary Vascular Resistance</summary><div class="details-content">

In all subtypes, the amount of shunting is dependent on the **pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)**. As the PVR decreases over the first month of life, the left-to-right shunt increases.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Atrial-Level Shunting (Partial/Intermediate)</summary><div class="details-content">

Left-to-right shunting at the atrial level will cause mainly **pulmonary volume overload**, with consequent dilatation of the right-sided chambers. Over time, the dilatation will cause suboptimal coaptation of the leaflets of the left and right AV valves and subsequent **AV valve insufficiency**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Complete AVSD Haemodynamics</summary><div class="details-content">

In complete AVSD, shunting occurs at **both the atrial and ventricular level**, resulting in:
*   Pulmonary circulation burdened with **volume and pressure overload**
*   Left-sided chamber dilatation

This can lead to **congestive heart failure within the first few months of life**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Down Syndrome and Pulmonary Vascular Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

**Irreversible pulmonary vascular changes** can be seen as early as **6 months of age** and are **accelerated in patients with Down's syndrome**.

Patients with Down's syndrome have an anatomical predisposition to:
*   Recurrent respiratory infections
*   Development of pulmonary vascular occlusive disease
*   Increased bronchial secretions
*   Decreased number of distal bronchi
*   Thick tongue (contributing to airway obstruction, tracheobronchomalacia, and CO₂ retention)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Eisenmenger Complex</summary><div class="details-content">

In untreated patients with AVSD, **Eisenmenger's complex** will eventually develop as the pulmonary vascular disease progresses. The shunt will become **right-to-left** and the patient will become **cyanotic**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 7 What is the natural history of an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Many patients (**80%**) with an untreated complete AVSD can survive into the **second and third decades of life** without operative correction. Survival beyond the third or fourth decade is rare.
*   The natural history of a partial or intermediate AVSD is similar to that of a simple ASD and depends on the degree of any residual left AV regurgitation.
*   **Heart failure and recurrent respiratory infections** cause the majority of early mortality, whereas **pulmonary vascular disease** accounts for the majority of late mortality.

</div></details>

## 8 What are the symptoms of an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Complete AVSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with a complete AVSD become symptomatic when the PVR decreases, causing an increase in pulmonary blood flow. This usually occurs after the **first few weeks of life**.

Common symptoms:
*   Poor feeding
*   Tachypnoea
*   Diaphoresis
*   Weight loss

Patients with significant left AV valve regurgitation and/or LVOTO usually have a **more dramatic presentation**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Partial AVSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with a partial AVSD with minimal AV valve regurgitation will be **asymptomatic**, similar to patients with a simple secundum ASD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### AVSD with Tetralogy of Fallot</summary><div class="details-content">

Although patients with AVSD and associated tetralogy of Fallot may remain balanced with respect to pulmonary and systemic blood flow, they may also develop **right-to-left shunting** across the VSD and cyanosis if severe right ventricular outflow tract obstruction is present.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 9 What are the signs of an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Complete AVSD:** Presence of a VSD results in a **harsh, holosystolic murmur** audible at the left sternal border. P2 is loud and there is **fixed splitting of the 2nd heart sound**.
*   **Partial AVSD:** A **systolic pulmonary flow murmur** and a **fixed, widely split 2nd heart sound** is found, similar to patients with a secundum ASD.

</div></details>

## 12 What are the echocardiographic features of an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Diagnostic Evaluation</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography is diagnostic and should include a thorough evaluation of:

*   **a)** size of the ASD and VSD (Figure 8)
*   **b)** presence or absence of an additional VSD
*   **c)** degree of AV valve regurgitation
*   **d)** balance of the valves and ventricular cavities
*   **e)** evaluation for left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO)
*   **f)** presence or absence of:
    *   i) double-orifice left AV valve
    *   ii) tetralogy of Fallot
    *   iii) double-outlet right ventricle (DORV)
    *   iv) transposition of the great arteries (TGA)
    *   v) patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)

## 14 What are the principles of managing a patient with an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   The diagnosis of an AVSD is an **indication for surgical repair**.
*   A **complete AVSD** can routinely and safely be repaired with good results as early as **2 months of age**. Waiting until 8 months and beyond risks the development of obstructive pulmonary vascular disease.
*   Surgery for **partial or transitional AVSD** can be performed **after the age of 1 year**, as the pulmonary circulation is protected and there is minimal risk of developing pulmonary vascular disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 15 What is the surgical approach for repair of an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Partial or complete median sternotomy** is the standard approach for patients with a complete and transitional AVSD.
*   For patients with a partial AVSD, some groups have advocated a **submammary right anterior thoracotomy** as an alternative approach to sternotomy.
*   As standard, the patient is placed on cardiopulmonary bypass using **bicaval cannulation, aortic cross-clamping and administration of cardioplegia**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 16 What are the principles of surgery for a patient with an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Goals of Complete Repair</summary><div class="details-content">

The primary goal is **complete repair**, which should include:

*   **a)** closure of all intracardiac and extracardiac shunts (ASD, VSD, and PDA when present)
*   **b)** obtaining two competent and non-stenotic AV valves
*   **c)** treating any potential for LVOT obstruction
*   **d)** avoiding lesions to the conduction system

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Coronary Sinus Management</summary><div class="details-content">

The interatrial communication can be closed leaving the coronary sinus to the **right or left side of the patch**, thereby allowing it to drain into the right or left atrium, respectively.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Associated Anomalies and Palliation</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Although associated anomalies should be treated at the time of repair, these can become exceptionally difficult operations to perform.
*   **Palliation of an AVSD by pulmonary artery banding (PAB)** is rarely performed due to the tendency to develop left AV valve regurgitation. It is usually only considered when severe comorbidities prevent the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (e.g., intracranial bleed or ongoing sepsis).

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 17 What are the surgical options for repair of a complete atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Double Patch Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

This involves using **one patch to close the VSD** and a **second patch to close the ASD** (Figure 11B):

*   **a)** The VSD repair is performed using Dacron® or autologous pericardium, with a continuous or interrupted suturing technique.
*   **b)** The ASD repair should be performed with **autologous pericardium**, as the use of synthetic material close to the ASD may result in haemolysis if any AV valve regurgitant jet is directed at the synthetic patch.
*   **c)** The **cleft in the left AV valve** should be closed to prevent later development of significant AV valve regurgitation. This is usually done after VSD repair but before ASD repair. An interrupted or continuous suturing technique can be used, but care should be taken to **not purse-string the cleft repair**.
*   **d)** Prior to closing the right atrium, the **right AV valve** should be evaluated for significant regurgitation and corrected if present.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Single Patch Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

This involves **one large autologous pericardial patch** (Figure 11A):

*   **a)** The superior and inferior bridging leaflets are incised over the crest of the septum.
*   **b)** The pericardial patch is initially sutured to the crest of the ventricular septum, with care taken to place sutures slightly more rightward along the posteroinferior border to avoid the conduction tissue.
*   **c)** The divided AV valves are resuspended to an appropriate level on the pericardial patch.
*   **d)** The cleft in the left AV valve is then closed.
*   **e)** The remaining pericardial patch is used for ASD repair.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Modified Single Patch Technique (Nunn Procedure)</summary><div class="details-content">

This involves **primary closure of the VSD by suturing the AV valve tissue directly to the crest of the ventricular septum** (Figure 11C):

*   Pledgeted sutures are passed through the septum, AV valve tissue, and finally the autologous pericardial patch.
*   The sutures are then tied to bring the AV valve tissue and patch down to the level of the ventricular septum.
*   The cleft is then closed and the remaining patch is used to close the ASD.

![](_page_12_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 11. Surgical repair of a complete atrioventricular septal defect, using a:** A) single patch technique; B) double patch technique; or C) modified single patch technique. RA = right atrium; LA = left atrium; RV = right ventricle; LV = left ventricle; RAVV = right atrioventricular valve; LAVV = left atrioventricular valve.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 21 What are the most common complications following repair of an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Heart Block</summary><div class="details-content">

Heart block can be minimised by suturing the VSD patch on the **right side of the crest of the septum at the posteroinferior border**. **Closing the coronary sinus into the left atrium** may also decrease the risk of post-operative heart block.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Residual ASD or VSD</summary><div class="details-content">

Residual defects should be identified by intra-operative echocardiographic assessment and a **saturation run** (from the SVC, IVC and pulmonary artery) that can help to quantify the degree of shunting, using the formulae:

$$Qp:Qs = \frac{\text{Aortic saturations} - \text{Mixed venous saturations}}{\text{Pulmonary vein saturations} - \text{Pulmonary artery saturations}}$$

$$\text{Mixed venous saturations} = \frac{(3 \times \text{SVC saturations}) + (1 \times \text{IVC saturations})}{4}$$

In the post-operative period, patients that fail to wean from the ventilator, have continued/worsening cardiomegaly or require significant anti-congestive medical therapy should be evaluated for residual shunts. A **large residual VSD** is more likely to cause a problem than a residual ASD. Cardiac catheterisation is often necessary to characterise the severity of the shunt.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction (LVOTO)</summary><div class="details-content">

LVOTO is usually caused by **accessory chordal attachments to the septum** that require division. In some cases, the anomalous position or attachment of the left portion of the superior bridging leaflet can cause LVOTO or occasionally a recurrent subaortic membrane.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### AV Valve Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

AV valve regurgitation will require reoperation in up to **15%** of patients that undergo repair of AVSD.

**Risk factors:**
*   Double-orifice left AV valve
*   Absence of Down's syndrome
*   Significant pre-operative left AV valve regurgitation

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 22 What are the strategies for treating left atrioventricular valve regurgitation or stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Left AV Valve Regurgitation (More Common)</summary><div class="details-content">

The left AV valve is the **most common reason for reoperation** after repair of the AV canal, with regurgitation more common than stenosis.

*   The previous cleft closure frequently breaks down — **reclosure of the cleft** reduces regurgitation in many cases.
*   When regurgitation is due to a **deficient mural leaflet or annular dilation**, the repair is more complex and unlikely to be durable.
*   Various techniques of **annuloplasty at the commissures** is sometimes helpful, along with valvuloplasty of the leaflets.
*   **Leaflet extension** using autologous pericardium or other commercially available patches can reduce regurgitation in some cases.
*   Commercially available **annuloplasty rings** are too large for smaller patients, and circumferential techniques restrict growth potential.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Left AV Valve Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Left AV valve stenosis is a challenging problem:

*   One strategy involves **taking down part of the cleft repair** — but this risks increasing regurgitation.
*   **Splitting the papillary muscle** and other parts of the subvalvar apparatus may be helpful in some cases of patients with a single left AV valve papillary muscle.
*   **Valve replacement** is a last resort in children. The smallest mechanical prosthesis available is **15mm**. There is a real risk of **heart block and coronary artery injury**. In addition, children will outgrow these small prostheses, necessitating re-replacement.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 23 What are the long-term outcomes of surgery for an atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Early mortality for repair of complete AVSD is **2–5%** in single institution studies. The recent Society of Thoracic Surgeons Congenital Heart Surgery Database analysis reported an operative mortality for complete AVSD of **2.2%**.
*   The most common reasons for reoperation are **left AV valve dysfunction** and **LVOT obstruction**. For these reasons, AVSD patients require **lifelong follow-up**.
*   Reoperation on older children or adults with AVSD can be challenging as patch material used in previous repairs is often **calcified**, which does not permit suture placement for valve repair or replacement. This can necessitate taking down previous patch material.
*   **Unbalanced AVSD patients** that undergo single-ventricle palliation are subject to the same long-term complications of other Fontan patients, including (but not limited to) protein-losing enteropathy, plastic bronchitis, liver dysfunction/cirrhosis, and diminished ventricular function. This population is often referred for heart or combined heart/liver transplantation.

</div></details>
