# Cardiac Tumours

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Classification
- **Definition:** "Abnormal growth of tissue in or on the heart, demonstrating partial or complete lack of structural organisation, and no functional coordination with normal cardiac tissue"
- **Primary tumours:** Arise from heart or pericardium (myxoma, papillary fibroelastoma, rhabdomyoma, fibroma, lipoma, teratoma, haemangioma, lipomatous hypertrophy of IAS, mesothelioma of AV tissue, pheochromocytoma, sarcoma, cardiac lymphoma) (Figure 1)
- **Secondary tumours:** Metastatic lesions (seeding via IVC to RA)
- **Benign vs malignant** (Table 1)

### Epidemiology
- **Primary tumours:** 0.02–0.32% (autopsy/foetal echo); ~14% occur in patients <16 years
- **Secondary tumours:** ~6% in children (non-Hodgkin's lymphoma, neuroblastoma, Wilms' tumour, sarcomas, hepatoma)

### Signs and Symptoms
- Related to size, location, growth rate (not histology)
- Asymptomatic → life-threatening (obstruction, valvular dysfunction, impaired myocardial function, embolisation)
- Most common signs: tachycardia, cardiac murmur, systemic embolisation
- Left atrial tumours responsible for 25% of embolisation (cerebral/retinal arteries, mesenteric branches, renal arteries, limb arteries)

### Diagnosis
- **Foetal scanning:** Tumours developing during early gestation detected (Figure 2)
- **Echocardiography:** Mainstay; can be diagnostic (e.g., myxoma)
- **ECG:** Very common but non-specific (arrhythmias, ST-T changes, strain, ventricular hypertrophy)
- **CXR:** Cardiomegaly, mediastinal widening, pleural effusion, pulmonary oedema, mediastinal calcification (Figures 3–4)
- **MRI/CT:** Define anatomy, tissue definition, borders (Figures 8–9); useful in follow-up

### Echocardiographic Features (Figures 5–7)
- Size, location (intracavitary/intramyocardial), attachment (sessile/pedunculated), relation to valves/outflow tract, growth rate, blood flow obstruction, associated lesions, pericardial/pleural effusions (often with malignant tumours)
- Differential diagnosis: vegetations, cysts, thrombi

### Primary Malignant Tumours (10% of primary tumours in children)
- Angiosarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, fibrosarcoma
- Originate from any chamber or pericardium; asymptomatic until metastases
- Prognosis very poor (untreated survival <1 year)
- Treatment: chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgical excision
- Resection limited by need to preserve conduction system and coronary arteries (Figure 10) → local recurrence and disease progression frequent

### Primary Benign Tumours
- Usually successfully treated by surgical resection (non-invasive, slow growth)
- Complete resection with wide margins to avoid recurrence
- Extensive involvement may require complex reconstruction (e.g., valve replacement with pulmonary autograft)

### Secondary Tumours
- Management depends on primary lesion
- Most common: lymphomas, neuroblastomas, extracardiac sarcomas
- Surgical options limited for incurable malignancies
- Wilms' tumour: resection of intracaval/intracardiac extension safe and reliable (Figure 11)

### Surgical Principles (by tumour location)
| Location | Approach |
|---|---|
| Extrapericardial/pericardial masses | Usually without CPB/DHCA (except great vessels/veins) |
| Atrial lesions | Right atriotomy; left atrial lesions require additional IAS incision |
| RV and outflow tract lesions | Usually via RA; may require right ventriculotomy |
| LV tumours | Via aortic or mitral valves (avoid left ventriculotomy); severe compromise/extensive resection → Norwood or heart transplant |

### Specific Tumours

**Rhabdomyoma (most common primary tumour in children):**
- Spontaneous regression in 50–100%
- Asymptomatic → follow-up with serial echocardiography
- Surgical resection for refractory arrhythmias or obstruction

**Myxoma (most common primary tumour in adults; rare in children):**
- 50% of adult benign cardiac tumours
- Multi-lobulated, frail consistency (prone to embolisation); attach to atrial wall (pedicle or wide base); 75% in left atrium (Figure 12)
- Diagnosis → surgical removal (complete excision due to recurrence risk) (Figure 13)

**Fibroma (second most common primary tumour in children):**
- Asymptomatic → conservative with serial echocardiography
- Complete resection for refractory arrhythmias or haemodynamic compromise
- If compromising conduction system → partial resection
- Extensive infiltration → heart transplant

**Angioma (rare):**
- Often diagnosed incidentally
- Symptomatic, small, well isolated → surgical resection
- Asymptomatic → conservative with serial echocardiography

**Intrapericardial Teratoma (rare):**
- Benign neoplasm with endodermal, mesodermal, neuroectodermal elements (Figure 14)
- Early diagnosis often antenatal
- Pericardial effusions common (2nd–3rd trimester); hydrops in ~77% (predicts fate)
- **In utero pericardiocentesis** → life-saving (relieves venous return obstruction, delays hydrops progression)
- **Early neonatal surgical removal** → treatment of choice (generally curative)

## 1. Definition and Classification</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 1. What are cardiac tumours?</summary><div class="details-content">

The most recent publication of the Congenital Heart Surgery Nomenclature and Database Project defines cardiac tumours as **"abnormal growth of tissue in or on the heart, demonstrating partial or complete lack of structural organisation, and no functional coordination with normal cardiac tissue"** .

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 2. How are cardiac tumours classified?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Primary cardiac tumours** (Figure 1) — arise from tissues of the heart itself or the pericardium, include:
- Myxoma
- Papillary fibroelastoma
- Rhabdomyoma
- Fibroma
- Lipoma
- Teratoma
- Haemangioma
- Lipomatous hypertrophy of the interatrial septum
- Mesothelioma of the atrioventricular (AV) tissue
- Pheochromocytoma
- Sarcoma
- Cardiac lymphoma

**Secondary cardiac tumours** are considered metastatic lesions, with seeding of neoplastic cells onto cardiac tissues usually occurring via the inferior vena cava to the right atrium.

**Alternatively, lesions can be categorised as benign or malignant (Table 1).**

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 2. Epidemiology and Signs</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 3. What is the incidence of primary cardiac tumours?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Primary cardiac tumours are **rare**
- Published incidence in autopsy series and foetal echocardiograms: **0.02–0.32%**
- Wide range due to: improvement in non-invasive imaging techniques, more intensive routine echocardiographic screening, increased application of foetal ultrasonography
- About **14%** of all cardiac tumours occur in patients who are less than 16 years of age

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 4. What is the incidence of secondary cardiac tumours?</summary><div class="details-content">

- In autopsy series, incidence of secondary cardiac tumours in children is approximately **6%**
- Most common tumours causing secondary involvement in children:
  - Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma
  - Neuroblastoma
  - Wilms' tumour
  - Soft tissue and bone sarcomas
  - Hepatoma

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 5. What are the signs of cardiac tumours?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Symptoms and signs related to **size, location and growth rate** (not histology type)
- Range: asymptomatic → life-threatening cardiac events (obstruction to flow, valvular dysfunction, impaired myocardial function, embolisation)

**Most common signs:**
- Tachycardia
- Cardiac murmur
- Evidence of systemic embolisation

**Left atrial tumours** are responsible for **25%** of all tumour embolisation to:
- Cerebral and retinal arteries
- Mesenteric branches
- Renal arteries
- Limb arteries (occasionally)

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 3. Diagnostic Imaging</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 6. What are the principles of diagnosis of a cardiac tumour?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Tumours developing during early gestation should be detected during **foetal scanning** (Figure 2)
- Current standards expect full cardiac consultation at first diagnosis (though tissue diagnosis and gestational course may not be predictable at that time)

![](_page_3_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.** Foetal echocardiogram at 24 weeks demonstrating left ventricular mass with no signs of heart failure or hydrops. LV = left ventricle; MV = mitral valve; LA = left atrium; RA = right atrium; TV = tricuspid valve; RV = right ventricle. *Image courtesy of Dr. Tara Bharucha.*

- **Echocardiography** remains mainstay of diagnosis; in some cases can provide definitive diagnosis (e.g., myxoma). More often, detection of intracardiac mass is not enough to provide specific tissue diagnosis
- **ECG changes** are very common (especially in paediatric presentation) but usually non-specific
- **MRI, CT and angiography** are useful in refining anatomical characteristics of the mass and its effects

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 7. What are the chest radiographical features of cardiac tumours?</summary><div class="details-content">

Chest radiograph may detect:
- Cardiomegaly (due to cardiac enlargement and/or pericardial fluid) (Figure 3)
- Mediastinal widening (Figure 4)
- Pleural effusion
- Evidence of congestive heart failure (e.g., pulmonary oedema)
- Mediastinal calcification

![](_page_4_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 3.** Chest radiograph of neonate (1 week) with cardiac fibroma — large left ventricular mass (diagnosed prenatally), enlarged left margin occupying majority of left chest (*). *Image courtesy of Dr. Shankar Sadagopan.*

![](_page_4_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4.** Chest radiograph of neonate (day 2) with cardiac teratoma — widened mediastinum and right pleural effusion. *Reproduced with permission from Manoli I, et al.*

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 8. What are the electrocardiographic features of cardiac tumours?</summary><div class="details-content">

ECG changes are very common but generally non-specific:
- **Arrhythmias** — every type reported in association with cardiac tumours
- **ST-T segment changes** — can occur as focal, acute events following coronary embolisation or as diffuse, long-lasting changes with myocardial infiltration
- Evidence of strain and ventricular hypertrophy (secondary to blood flow obstruction) may also be present

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 9. What are the echocardiographic features of cardiac tumours?</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography is the **gold standard** — 2D, 3D, Doppler interrogation and tissue Doppler indices (TDI) all useful to identify:

- Size
- Location (intracavitary, intramyocardial)
- Attachment (sessile, pedunculated)
- Relation to other structures (valve (Figure 5), outflow tract)
- Growth rate (with serial examinations)
- Blood flow obstruction (Figure 6)
- Associated lesions
- Pericardial or pleural effusions (often associated with malignant tumours) (Figure 7)

**Differential diagnosis:** Vegetations, cysts, thrombi

In some cases (e.g., myxomas), echocardiographic characteristics can be diagnostic.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 10. What are the features of cardiac tumours on cross-sectional imaging?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **CT chest and cardiac MRI** are important adjuncts to echocardiography in defining anatomy, tissue definition and borders (Figure 8)
- **MRI** can be quite sensitive and specific in diagnosis of intracavitary tumours, often providing tumour type in majority of cases (except intramural masses, which are generally less well defined)

**MRI complements echocardiography in assessing:**
- Location, size and boundaries
- Relation to myocardium, valvular tissue and blood vessels
- Involvement of pericardium, pericardial cavity, great vessels and nerves
- Involvement of airways, and mediastinal and hilar structures
- Effects on blood flow, cardiac function and cardiac output

- Also useful in **follow-up management** (whether or not surgical resection has taken place) (Figure 9)

## 4. Management of Primary and Secondary Cardiac Tumours</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 11. What are the principles of management for a primary cardiac tumour?</summary><div class="details-content">

**Primary malignant cardiac tumours** (angiosarcomas, rhabdomyosarcomas, leiomyosarcomas, fibrosarcomas):
- Very rare — represent **10%** of primary cardiac tumours in children
- Can originate from any chamber of the heart and pericardium
- Usually remain asymptomatic until they produce metastases
- Prognosis very poor; expected survival in untreated patients <1 year
- Treatment options: chemotherapy, radiotherapy, surgical excision

![](_page_11_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 10.** Rhabdomyosarcoma of right atrium: A) Neoplastic process involves RA wall, AV junction (including triangle of Koch), AV groove, CS ostium; B) Following tumour debulking, pericardial patch inserted, but extent of resection limited to preserve CS, posterior descending artery, conduction system. RCA = right coronary artery; TV = tricuspid valve; RV = right ventricle; RA = right atrium; SAN = sinoatrial node; AVN = atrioventricular node; CS = coronary sinus.

**Primary benign cardiac tumours:**
- Usually successfully treated by surgical resection (tumour growth tends to be non-invasive and slow)
- Depending on histology type, treatment can vary from conservative approach to aggressive surgical resection
- Principles of resection comparable to general oncology surgery — **complete resection with wide margins** to avoid recurrence should be sought in all cases

**Malignant tumours:** Extent of resection often limited by need to preserve specialised tissues (conduction system, coronary arteries) (Figure 10) → local recurrence and disease progression frequent, may necessitate repeat procedures

**Extensive involvement:** Complex surgical reconstruction may be required (e.g., valve replacement using pulmonary autograft)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 12. What are the principles of management for a secondary cardiac tumour?</summary><div class="details-content">

- By definition, secondary cardiac tumours are **metastatic lesions**; management depends on primary lesion
- In children, metastatic cardiac lesions are **more common** than primary cardiac tumours
- Most common malignancies with metastatic spread to heart: lymphomas, neuroblastomas, extracardiac sarcomas
- Surgical options for incurable malignancies with cardiac invasion are limited

**Wilms' tumour:** Resection of intracaval and intracardiac extension of tumour and removal of thrombus extension has proven safe and reliable in the long term (Figure 11)

**FIGURE 11.** Secondary cardiac tumour from Wilms' tumour of femur: A) Single metastatic lesion contained within interventricular septum, abutting LVOT; B) Lesion approached through aortic valve and enucleated from surrounding muscle fibres, followed by primary marsupialisation (*). IVS = interventricular septum; RV = right ventricle; RA = right atrium; LA = left atrium; LV = left ventricle.

</div></details>

## 5. Surgical Principles and Specific Tumour Treatments</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 13. What are the principles of surgery for cardiac tumours?</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical strategy and approach depend on location and extent of tumour:

| Location | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Extrapericardial and pericardial masses** | Usually excised without CPB/DHCA (except lesions involving great vessels and veins) |
| **Atrial lesions** | Usually accessed via right atriotomy; left atrial lesions require additional incision in interatrial septum |
| **Right ventricular and outflow tract lesions** | Usually resected via right atrium; may require right ventriculotomy in certain circumstances |
| **Left ventricular tumours** | Accessed via aortic or mitral valves (avoid left ventriculotomy if possible, especially in small children). If ventricular mass severely compromised or extensive resection required → Norwood procedure or heart transplant may be warranted |

Reconstruction follows general principles of surgical reconstruction for non-neoplastic congenital lesions.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 14. What are the principles of treatment for a rhabdomyoma?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Most common type of primary cardiac tumour in children**
- Spontaneous regression reported in **50–100%** of patients
- Asymptomatic children → follow-up with regular echocardiographic evaluation
- Surgical resection indicated for **refractory arrhythmias** and **obstruction to blood flow**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 15. What are the principles of treatment for a myxoma?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Most common primary cardiac tumour in adults; represents **50%** of all adult benign cardiac tumours
- **Rare in children**
- Multi-lobulated masses with frail consistency (prone to embolisation)
- Usually attach to atrial wall with pedicle or wide base; **75% originate in left atrium** (Figure 12)

![](_page_13_Picture_12.jpeg)

![](_page_13_Picture_13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 12.** Cardiac myxoma with typically multi-lobulated appearance and sessile base. *Images courtesy of Mr. Pietro Malvindi and Mr. Theo Velissaris.*

- **Diagnosis of myxoma is indication for surgical removal**
- Tumour should be excised **completely** because of risk of recurrence (Figure 13)

![](_page_14_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13.** Surgical management of pedunculated left atrial myxoma: A) After CPB and cardiac arrest, access via right atriotomy (Ra), left atriotomy (La) via Waterston's groove, or left atrial roof incision (Lar). Small portion of atrial septum resected along circumference 3–4 mm around root of pedicle, tumour carefully extracted; B) Interatrial communication closed primarily or with pericardial patch after wide septal resection (*). IVS = interventricular septum; RV = right ventricle; RA = right atrium; LA = left atrium; LV = left ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 16. What are the principles of treatment for a fibroma?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Second most common primary cardiac tumour in children**
- Asymptomatic children → treated conservatively, followed up with serial echocardiography
- **Complete surgical resection** indicated for refractory arrhythmias or haemodynamic compromise
- If tumour compromising conduction system → **partial resection** should be considered
- Extensive infiltration of cardiac walls → **heart transplant** should be considered

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 17. What are the principles of treatment for an angioma?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Rare**; often diagnosed incidentally
- Surgical resection indicated for **small, well isolated, symptomatic tumours**
- Asymptomatic patients → treated conservatively, followed up with serial echocardiography

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### 18. What are the principles of treatment for an intrapericardial teratoma?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Generally **benign neoplasms** containing elements of endodermal, mesodermal and neuroectodermal germinal layers (Figure 14)
- **Rare**
- Early diagnosis often obtained during antenatal scan; counselling required at diagnosis

![](_page_15_Picture_12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 14.** Intrapericardial teratoma in neonate: A) Macroscopic image — encapsulated solid and cystic mass originating from adventitia of ascending aorta; B) Histopathology — scattered islets of hepatocytes (endoderm) with associated extramedullary haemopoiesis and bile pigment among immature tissues (suspicion of yolk sac tumour). *Reproduced with permission from Manoli I, et al.*

- **Pericardial effusions** common; may be diagnosed as early as 2nd–3rd trimester
- Compression effect of tumour mass and pericardial fluid often results in severe foetal cardiovascular compromise → **hydrops** in approximately **77%** of affected foetuses
- Prenatal development of foetal hydrops accurately predicts fate of foetuses with teratoma
- **In utero pericardiocentesis** and evacuation of pericardial fluid → life-saving approach (relieves venous return obstruction, delays progression of hydrops)
- **Early surgical removal of mass in neonatal period** → treatment of choice (generally curative)

</div></details>
