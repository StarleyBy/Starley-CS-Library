# Adult Congenital Cardiac Intensive Care

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Where Should ACHD Patients Be Managed?

AHA and ESC advise that ACHD patients are managed in **specialist centres** with specific resources and expertise. If transfer is not possible due to clinical status, expert advice should be sought until transfer is possible.

### Most Common Indications for ICU Admission

| Type | Indications |
|------|-------------|
| **Post-surgical** | Recovery post-cardiac surgery (commonest) |
| **Medical acute/emergency** | Arrhythmia/cardiac arrest, LV failure, endocarditis, RV failure, haemoptysis, syncope |

### Risk Factors for Increased Perioperative Mortality

- Complex congenital lesions (higher mortality than simple lesions despite similar APACHE II scores; Figure 1)
- Deranged thyroid function tests
- Elevated serum creatinine and bilirubin

![](_page_1_Figure_4.jpeg)

### Main Differences from Non-Congenital Cardiac Intensive Care

ACHD patients often have significantly different normal physiological status. Key differences:
- High incidence of RV dysfunction (associated with renal/hepatic impairment, GI dysfunction, prolonged respiratory support)
- Certain ICU trends may not be well tolerated: volume resuscitation, systemic oxygen delivery, sedation, vasoactive drugs, positive airway pressure ventilation

### Principles of Managing Critically Ill ACHD Patient

In addition to standard principles:
1. Know the underlying cardiopulmonary anatomy
2. Understand the patient's normal physiology
3. Appreciate how interventions may affect the circulation
4. Anticipate potential pitfalls in monitoring, investigations, and interventions

### Baseline Information Required on ICU Arrival

- Primary cardiac lesion(s)
- Surgical/catheter interventions undertaken
- Important residual haemodynamic lesions (static or dynamic)
- Optimal intra-operative and post-operative haemodynamics and ventilatory settings
- Post-operatively: whether surgeon achieved all goals, unexpected challenges

### Minimal Baseline Investigations

**Compare admission values with last well values for:**
- Haemoglobin
- Oxygen saturations
- Systemic and pulmonary pressures
- ECG (beware: massive atrial enlargement may cause atrial tachycardia mimicking sinus tachycardia)
- Cardiac function (most recent echo/CMR)

**Additional investigations:**
- CXR, routine labs, haematinics (if cyanotic), coagulation screen (citrate-adjusted if haematocrit >60), pregnancy test, blood cultures, serum/faecal alpha-1 antitrypsin (Fontan with ascites)

### Challenging Situations

**Blood pressure monitoring inaccuracies:**
- Previous/existing BT shunt → use contralateral arm; bilateral shunts → lower body readings may be more accurate
- Coarctation, previous femoral bypass, multiple catheterisations → lower limb pressure may underrepresent central pressure
- Radial line cannulation/surgical cutdown → may have ulnar-dominant or absent radial artery (cuff pressure usually accurate)

**Volume status:** Cyanotic and univentricular patients tolerate hypovolaemia poorly. CVP monitoring not possible in Fontan/TCPC.

**Cardiac output measurement:** Challenging in complex CHD. PA catheterisation not possible in tricuspid/pulmonary atresia, Fontan, or TCPC. Intracardiac/extracardiac shunts make PA catheter data unreliable.

### Pacing Differences

- Some circulations make transvenous pacing impossible (e.g., tricuspid atresia) → alternative access needed (Figure 2)
- Restrictive ventricular disease with limited stroke volume may require high pacing rates (up to 120 bpm)
- Pacing optimisation using echocardiography can be helpful for borderline cardiac output

![](_page_5_Picture_6.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Access for pacing in tricuspid atresia. A = atrial or proximal pulmonary vein; B = epicardial; C = transaortic access; TA = tricuspid atresia; AoV = aortic valve; RV = rudimentary right ventricle; LV = left ventricle; MV = mitral valve.**

### Respiratory Support Differences

- Craniofacial abnormalities may complicate intubation and make NIV impossible
- Tracheostomy may be complicated by collateral vessels or abnormal airway anatomy
- Associated congenital pulmonary disease (hypoplastic lung, severe V/Q mismatch)
- Pulmonary hypertension may or may not need treating — compare with previous investigations
- Consider phrenic nerve injury (previous cardiac surgery) and congenital musculoskeletal deformities when weaning

### Non-Cardiopulmonary Organ Dysfunction

| System | Considerations |
|--------|----------------|
| **GI** | Asplenia/polysplenia, GI/renal dysfunction |
| **Renal** | Cyanotic CHD → common renal dysfunction; poor tolerance of IV contrast (rapid K⁺ rise post-procedure) |
| **Hepatic** | Abnormal LFTs common (high venous pressure, low CO, or medication) |
| **Thyroid** | Dysfunction common; manage in context of critical illness |
| **Feeding** | Severe RV dysfunction → low enteral feeding rates to avoid ileus |

### Specific Postoperative Considerations

| Procedure | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Coarctation repair** | Rebound hypertension, mesenteric ischaemia, spinal neurological damage (rare). Role of CSF drainage unknown in ACHD |
| **Interventional ASD closure** | High incidence of arrhythmias; elderly patients may have restrictive LV and RV disease; if CO suboptimal despite pacing/inotropes/volume → consider fenestration (at expense of cyanosis) |
| **AVSD repair** | If inadequate CO post-op with increasing inotropes → exclude tamponade, LV/RV failure, arrhythmia, LVOT obstruction (echocardiography) |
| **Tetralogy of Fallot (re-do)** | Most have uncomplicated course; severe pre-op RV dysfunction managed as usual; acute lung injury possible with increased pulmonary blood flow |
| **Ebstein's anomaly** | Care: maintain sinus rhythm, avoid elevated venous pressure, avoid RV over-distension, measure filling pressures and CO, maintain normoxia/normocarbia, avoid aggressive ventilation. Worsening LFTs = inadequate CO with high venous pressure (not drug reaction). Consider fenestration or mechanical circulatory support if CO inadequate |

### Cyanotic Heart Disease — ICU Management

**Adaptive mechanisms:** Increased CO, increased haematocrit, rightward shift of oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve

**Key principles:**
- Do not normalise O₂ saturations to non-cyanotic levels — target patient's "well" saturations (unless intervention intended to restore normoxia)
- Haemostatic abnormalities correlate with erythrocytosis — usually do not need treating
- Use citrate bottles adjusted for haematocrit when measuring INR
- Intolerant of hypovolaemia — keep well hydrated
- Transfusion threshold unclear — some intensivists have lower threshold
- Routine venesection NOT indicated; iron deficiency common
- High suspicion for cerebral abscess or haemorrhage with abnormal neurology/headache/postictal
- Hyperviscosity → renal hypoperfusion → renal failure. Hydrate well before IV contrast; those with significant kidney injury at risk of marked hyperkalaemia post-contrast

### Failing Right Ventricle (Subpulmonary) — Management (Table 1)

**Optimise preload:**
- Diuretics (overfilling increases RV filling pressure, size, wall stress)
- Cautious vasoconstriction (noradrenaline) with volume resuscitation (excessive vasodilation may reduce RV preload, especially in restrictive RV)

**Reduce afterload:**
- Optimise airway pressures (balance against atelectasis/hypoxaemia)
- Manage pleural collections aggressively
- Treat bronchoconstriction with bronchodilators
- Elevated PVR → inhaled or systemic pulmonary vasodilators
- Aim for normoxia and normocarbia
- Early extubation recommended (positive pressure reduces venous return)

**Pulmonary hypertension treatment (if CO inadequate in context of subpulmonary RV failure):**
- Inhaled nitric oxide
- Nebulised prostacyclin
- Oral/IV sildenafil (caution: systemic effects)

**Mechanical circulatory support** if progressive/worsening haemodynamics

### Failing Systemic Morphologically Right Ventricle

**Seen in:** ccTGA, TGA repaired with atrial switch

**Management:**
- Seek and treat reversible causes (arrhythmia, volume overload)
- Coronary arteries usually unobstructed; if ECG suggests ischaemia → coronary angiography
- IABP may be helpful in older patients (use appropriately sized balloon for aortic diameter)
- Role of levosimendan uncertain
- Multi-site pacing may help; even simple HR optimisation and AV delay modification may alter haemodynamics
- Early mechanical circulatory support if progressive/worsening

### Fontan Circulation — ICU Management

**Challenges:** Univentricular circulation + systemic venous hypertension

**Key principles:**
- Passive flow depends on adequate preload and avoidance of pulmonary hypertension
- Positive pressure ventilation significantly reduces CO → keep intrathoracic pressure low, drain pleural effusions, avoid pulmonary vasoconstrictors, treat bronchoconstriction
- Aim for early extubation
- Very poor tolerance of hypovolaemia
- High risk of atrial arrhythmias (can be lethal) and thrombi
- Some patients (e.g., TCPC) may not have transvenous access for pacing → external pacing available
- Always involve an expert

### Univentricular Heart (Non-Fontan) — ICU Management

- Nature of pulmonary connections must be considered when using inotropes/vasoactive agents
- Unprotected pulmonary circulation (with/without Eisenmenger) → minimise further increase in PA pressures
- Protected pulmonary circulation (PA stenosis/banding) → relative effects of pulmonary vs systemic constriction/dilatation may differ
- Absent pulmonary connection with systemic-pulmonary collaterals/shunts:
    - Small ↑PVR → significant ↓pulmonary blood flow → dramatic desaturation
    - ↑SVR and/or ↓PVR → ↑systemic-pulmonary shunting → ↓CO
- Oxygen administration may alter balance between pulmonary and systemic circulations
- Any vasoactive drug should be used with care — results may be unpredictable

### Systemic-Pulmonary Shunt — Specific Considerations

- Patients with central or BT shunt depend on systemic arterial pressure to maintain pulmonary blood flow
- Desaturation may require systemic vasoconstriction
- Aggressive ventilatory manoeuvres may reduce pulmonary blood flow
- Blood pressure measurement: previous classic/modified BT shunt → reduced arterial pressure in ipsilateral arm

## 1. Where Should Critically Ill ACHD Patients Be Managed?</summary><div class="details-content">

The American Heart Association (AHA) and European Society of Cardiology (ESC) advise that adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) patients are managed in **specialist centres** where they have access to specific resources and expertise.

The physiology of the critically ill ACHD patient can be complex and expert input is essential when managing these individuals. Where transfer/admission to a specialist centre is precluded due to the clinical status of the patient, expert advice should be sought until transfer is possible, as they may need surgical intervention (for example in endocarditis), but their management prior to transfer may still require ACHD expertise.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 2. Most Common Indications for ICU Admission</summary><div class="details-content">

The commonest indication for admission to the ICU is **recovery post-cardiac surgery**.

Medical acute/emergency admissions are similar to those in acquired cardiac disease, although there are additional indications that reflect the higher incidence of right-sided pathology in this patient population. The most common indications for medical admission include:

*   Arrhythmia/cardiac arrest
*   Left ventricular failure
*   Endocarditis
*   Right ventricular failure
*   Haemoptysis
*   Syncope

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 3. Risk Factors for Increased Perioperative Mortality and Morbidity</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with complex congenital lesions carry a significantly higher postoperative mortality than those with simple lesions despite having similar APACHE II scores (Figure 1).

Perioperative mortality is increased in the presence of pre-operative abnormalities, including deranged thyroid function tests, and elevated serum creatinine and bilirubin levels.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 4. Main Differences from Non-Congenital Cardiac Intensive Care</summary><div class="details-content">

The normal physiological status of an ACHD patient is often significantly different from those with acquired cardiac disease.

The relatively high incidence of **right ventricular dysfunction** presents a particular challenge, as it is associated with renal and hepatic impairment, gastrointestinal dysfunction, and the need for prolonged respiratory support and intensive care admission.

Whilst standard management principles do apply to many acutely unwell ACHD patients, certain trends in intensive care management may not be well tolerated by this patient population, including:

*   Volume resuscitation
*   Systemic oxygen delivery
*   Sedation
*   Use of vasoactive drugs
*   Application of positive airway pressure ventilation

Considering how supportive and therapeutic interventions may affect the patient, anticipating potential pitfalls is important in every case.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 5. Principles of Managing a Critically Ill ACHD Patient</summary><div class="details-content">

The first step in assessing a critically ill ACHD patient (as with every critically ill patient) is to **know the indication for admission**.

In addition to the standard principles relating to the assessment and management of the critically ill patient, there are a number of additional factors when assessing the ACHD patient that should be considered in turn:

1.  Knowledge of the underlying cardiopulmonary anatomy
2.  Understanding the normal physiology of the patient
3.  Appreciation of how any supportive or therapeutic interventions may affect the circulation
4.  Anticipating the potential pitfalls related to critical care monitoring, investigations and interventions

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 6. Baseline Information Required on ICU Arrival</summary><div class="details-content">

The following baseline information regarding cardiopulmonary anatomy and function is required when assessing an ACHD patient on arrival in the ICU:

*   Primary cardiac lesion(s)
*   Surgical or catheter interventions that have been undertaken
*   Any important residual haemodynamic lesions (static or dynamic)
*   Optimal intra-operative and postoperative haemodynamics and ventilatory settings for the patient
*   In cases admitted postoperatively: whether the surgeon achieved all they had aimed to, or whether there were any unexpected intra-operative challenges

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 7. Minimal Baseline Investigations</summary><div class="details-content">

The normal physiological status of the patient may be very abnormal when compared with that of a patient with acquired cardiac disease. In this patient population, trying to correct what may be **adaptive pathophysiological processes** can potentially be harmful.

It is important to have knowledge of when the patient was last well, and most recent (prior to admission) values of:

*   Haemoglobin
*   Oxygen saturations
*   Systemic and pulmonary pressures
*   Electrocardiogram (beware in patients with massive atrial enlargement, where atrial tachycardia may mimic sinus tachycardia)
*   Cardiac function on the most recent echocardiogram or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging scan

These should be compared with values obtained on admission to the intensive care.

**Additional investigations** (depending upon the clinical situation):

*   Plain chest radiograph
*   Routine laboratory investigations
*   Haematinics (if cyanotic, with automated electronic particle counts)
*   Coagulation screen (citrate adjusted if erythrocytotic with haematocrit >60)
*   Pregnancy test (if female of childbearing age)
*   Blood cultures
*   Serum or faecal alpha-1 antitrypsin (Fontan circulation with ascites)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 8. Challenging Situations When Managing ACHD Patients</summary><div class="details-content">

All ACHD patients have the potential to have very abnormal cardiopulmonary physiology; however, any anatomy that leaves a patient with an effectively **univentricular circulation**, the presence of **systemic-pulmonary shunts**, the potential to shunt **bidirectionally**, and the **Fontan circulation** are particularly challenging in the critical care setting.

#### Blood Pressure Monitoring Inaccuracies

A number of diagnoses and previous interventions may make blood pressure monitoring inaccurate:

| Situation | Implication |
|-----------|-------------|
| Previous or existing classic or modified Blalock-Taussig (BT) shunt | Blood pressure will under-read; catheter or cuff should be placed on **contralateral arm**. With bilateral shunts, lower body blood pressure readings may be more accurate |
| Coarctation, previous femoral bypass or multiple cardiac catheterisation | Lower limb pressure may underrepresent the central pressure |
| Radial line cannulation or surgical cutdown (especially neonatal) | May have ulnar-dominant or absent radial artery. Cuff pressure is usually accurate; ulnar cannulation should be avoided |

#### Volume Status

Patients with cyanotic congenital heart disease and those with univentricular circulation tolerate **hypovolaemia poorly**. Assessment of volaemic status is further complicated by coexisting impairment of ventricular function and/or atrioventricular valve regurgitation.

Monitoring central venous pressure in patients with Fontan or total cavo-pulmonary connection (TCPC) circulation is **not possible**.

#### Interpreting Clinical Signs

The presence of basal crepitations in a patient with pulmonary vein stenosis may not indicate systemic ventricular failure or volume overload but rather **pulmonary vein congestion**, and hence, should be interpreted with caution.

#### Cardiac Output Measurement

Measuring cardiac output in congenital heart disease (particularly where complex) can be challenging:

*   Pulmonary artery catheterisation is **not possible** in the presence of tricuspid or pulmonary atresia, a Fontan circulation or TCPC
*   The presence of intracardiac and extracardiac shunts make pulmonary artery catheter data **unreliable**
*   If non-invasive measures are used, the presence of a chronically low cardiac output state and correspondingly small aorta mean that oesophageal Doppler measures may be **invalid**

</div></details>

## 9. Differences in Pacing</summary><div class="details-content">

When considering the pacing route (transvenous vs. epicardial), certain circulations make traditional transvenous routes impossible, such as **tricuspid atresia**. In this situation, alternative access must be used (Figure 2) or in an emergency, transcutaneous pacing may be required.

![](_page_5_Picture_6.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Access for pacing in a patient with tricuspid atresia. A = atrial or proximal pulmonary vein; B = epicardial; C = transaortic access; TA = tricuspid atresia; AoV = aortic valve; RV = rudimentary right ventricle; LV = left ventricle; MV = mitral valve.**

Where cardiac output is borderline, **pacing optimisation using echocardiography** can be helpful, although there is little evidence in the literature to date.

In patients with restrictive ventricular disease and limited stroke volume, high pacing rates may be required (occasionally up to **120 bpm**) in order to maximise cardiac output.

Although indications for biventricular pacing are in general the same as in acquired cardiac disease, where the patient is critically ill due to limited cardiac output, expert advice should be sought.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 10. Differences in Respiratory Support</summary><div class="details-content">

Associated **craniofacial abnormalities** seen in some syndromes may complicate both the process of intubation and ventilation, and make non-invasive ventilation impossible.

**Tracheostomy** may be complicated due to the presence of collateral blood vessels or abnormal airway anatomy.

In patients with associated congenital pulmonary disease, there may be a hypoplastic lung or severe V/Q mismatch. Associated pulmonary hypertension may or may not need treating. **Comparison with previous investigations is crucial**, and interpretation of values must be in the context of right ventricular function.

Finally, when planning weaning from mechanical ventilation, previous cardiac surgery (possibility of **phrenic nerve injury**) or associated congenital musculoskeletal deformities must be taken into consideration.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 11. Differences in Non-Cardiopulmonary Organ Dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

Associated **gastrointestinal anatomical defects** are not uncommon, and may include asplenia/polysplenia, GI or renal dysfunction.

In patients with cyanotic congenital heart disease, associated **renal dysfunction** is common, and tolerance of intravenous contrast agents may be poor, with a rapid rise in serum potassium seen post-procedure.

**Abnormal liver function tests** are common, and may be a reflection of high venous pressure, low cardiac output, or as a result of medication.

**Thyroid dysfunction** is common in ACHD patients, and should be managed in the context of critical illness in the standard manner.

In a patient with severe right ventricular heart dysfunction, **low rates of enteral feeding** may be required to avoid ileus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 12. Specific Considerations Following Coarctation Repair</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients following a coarctation repair are closely monitored for:

*   **Rebound arterial hypertension**
*   **Mesenteric ischaemia**

Spinal neurological damage is a rare but reported complication. There is literature discussing the use of cerebrospinal fluid drainage following thoracoabdominal aortic surgery and a reduction in the incidence of neurological injury; however, its role in the ACHD population is unknown.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 13. Specific Challenges Following Interventional ASD Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

The incidence of **arrhythmias**, both tachycardia and bradycardia, are high in the immediate post-procedural period.

In the more elderly patient population, there may be significant restrictive left ventricular (as well as right ventricular) disease. If the cardiac output remains suboptimal despite pacing, inotrope and volume resuscitation, **fenestration** should be considered to maintain cardiac output (but at the expense of cyanosis).

Pre-operative pulmonary hypertension may take time to resolve post-procedure.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 14. Principles of Managing AVSD with Inadequate Cardiac Output</summary><div class="details-content">

Although atrioventricular septal defects (AVSDs) tend to be repaired in childhood, adults do present with partial defects, which can be repaired.

If the cardiac output is inadequate postoperatively and particularly in the setting of increasing inotropic support, in addition to exclusion of tamponade, **left and/or right ventricular failure, arrhythmia and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction** should be specifically excluded using echocardiography.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 15. Challenges Following Surgery for Tetralogy of Fallot</summary><div class="details-content">

Adults with tetralogy of Fallot are most likely to have undergone **re-do surgery**, although primary repair may be undertaken in a small number of cases.

Most patients undergoing redo surgery for tetralogy have an **uncomplicated postoperative course**.

Severe right ventricular dysfunction pre-operatively should be managed in the usual way.

In patients who have had either primary repair, or where there has been relief of significant proximal pulmonary artery stenosis, the increase in pulmonary blood flow can significantly increase lung capillary permeability, and **acute lung injury** can ensue.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 16. Specific Considerations Following Surgery for Ebstein's Anomaly</summary><div class="details-content">

The immediate postoperative care strategy for patients with repair of Ebstein's anomaly is largely driven by their **residual right ventricular function**.

In addition, there is a significant incidence of postoperative **atrial arrhythmias**, and **restrictive left as well as right ventricular function**.

**Care must be taken to:**

*   Maintain sinus rhythm and optimise pacing using echocardiography
*   Avoid elevation in venous pressure
*   Avoid over-distension of the right ventricle by excessive fluid administration
*   Measure filling pressure and cardiac output with care — and when inadequate, take action early
*   Maintain normoxia and normocarbia, whilst avoiding aggressive ventilation

Worsening liver function tests in such patients are not usually related to a drug reaction but rather an indication of **inadequate cardiac output in the context of a high venous pressure**. Where the cardiac output is inadequate, interatrial fenestration may be considered (if not already done) or, alternatively, percutaneous/surgical acute mechanical circulatory support.

</div></details>

## 17. Factors for Managing Patients with Underlying Cyanotic Heart Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

Over years, these patients will have developed adaptive mechanisms to increase oxygenation to tissues, including:

*   Increase in cardiac output and haematocrit
*   Rightward shift in the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve

**Key principles:**

*   It is **not necessary** to try to normalise their oxygen saturations to those of non-cyanotic patients, but rather to those they have when well, unless the intervention was intended to restore normoxia
*   Patients are prone to **haemostatic abnormalities**, which correlate with the level of erythrocytosis:
    *   Abnormalities of prothrombin time, partial thromboplastin time, factors V, VII, VIII, IX and thrombocytopenia have been documented but do not usually need treating
    *   **Citrate bottles adjusted for the haematocrit must be used when measuring the INR**
*   Determining volaemic status and appropriate level of haemoglobin can be challenging. These patients are **intolerant of hypovolaemia** and must be kept well hydrated
*   Whether the transfusion parameters for intensive care are appropriate for these patients is not clear; some intensivists will have a **lower threshold for transfusion** than in the non-cyanotic patient population
*   **Routine venesection is not indicated**, and iron deficiency is relatively common
*   In any patient admitted with abnormal neurology, headache or postictal, a high index of suspicion should exist for **cerebral abscess or haemorrhage**
*   Hyperviscosity and arteriolar vasoconstriction can lead to hypoperfusion of the kidneys and subsequent renal failure. Any investigation that requires intravenous contrast must be performed with the patient **well hydrated** and using appropriate precautions to prevent further renal injury. Those with significant kidney injury are at risk of developing **marked hyperkalaemia** following administration of intravenous or intra-arterial contrast media

</div></details>

## 18. Principles of Managing a Failing Right Ventricle (Subpulmonary)</summary><div class="details-content">

When in the subpulmonary position, the failing right ventricle can be supported using standard inotropic agents whilst avoiding pulmonary vasoconstrictors.

Although no one positive inotropic agent has been shown to be superior to another, there are theoretical benefits in using **levosimendan**, in terms of its effects on VA coupling.

Any potentially treatable underlying cause should be addressed, such as volume overload or arrhythmia, including heart rate and pacing optimisation, guided by echocardiography.

Cardiac output may be maintained or improved by optimising preload and minimising right ventricular afterload (Table 1).

| **Pressors:** Excessive systemic vasodilatation may reduce RV preload to a level that is detrimental, particularly in the presence of a restrictive RV. Cautious vasoconstriction (with noradrenaline) may be indicated, together with volume resuscitation | **Pleural collections:** Should be managed aggressively in the context of RV failure |

| | **Positive pressure ventilation:** Will reduce venous return, and generally early extubation is recommended; however, this must be balanced against the risk of basal atelectasis and abnormal arterial blood gases |

**Pulmonary arterial hypertension**, even if only moderate, should be treated if the cardiac output is inadequate in the context of subpulmonary right heart failure. Additional treatment options include:

*   Inhaled nitric oxide
*   Nebulised prostacyclin
*   Oral or intravenous sildenafil (caution: may have systemic effects)

In the event of progressive or worsening haemodynamic status, early implementation of **acute mechanical circulatory support** should be considered.

</div></details>

## 19. Principles of Managing a Failing Systemic Morphologically Right Ventricle</summary><div class="details-content">

This may be seen in a number of conditions, including:

*   Congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (ccTGA)
*   TGA repaired with an atrial switch procedure

**Management:**

*   Reversible causes (arrhythmia, volume overload) should be sought and treated
*   In these patients, the coronary arteries are usually angiographically unobstructed. If an electrocardiogram suggests ischaemia, however, **coronary angiography** is indicated
*   Interventions used for systemic ventricular failure in the non-congenital patient (pharmacological and mechanical support) may be less useful in this patient population, although **intra-aortic balloon counterpulsation** can be helpful (taking care to use an appropriately sized balloon for the individual patient aortic diameter) in older patients
*   The role of newer inodilators, such as levosimendan, is uncertain
*   In some centres, **multi-site pacing** has been used with good effect; however, even simple heart rate optimisation and modification of atrioventricular (AV) delay may significantly alter haemodynamics
*   In the event of progressive or worsening haemodynamic status, early implementation of **acute mechanical circulatory support** should be considered

</div></details>

## 20. Considerations for Managing Patients with Fontan Circulation</summary><div class="details-content">

The combination of univentricular circulation and systemic venous hypertension in a Fontan circulation is challenging to manage.

**Key principles:**

*   The passive flow of blood from the systemic venous circulation to the pulmonary circulation depends upon **adequate preload** and **avoidance of pulmonary hypertension**
*   Positive pressure ventilation considerably reduces systemic cardiac output and can cause significant haemodynamic instability. If mechanical ventilation is unavoidable:
    *   Keep intrathoracic pressure low
    *   Drain pleural effusions
    *   Avoid pulmonary vasoconstrictors
    *   Treat bronchoconstriction
    *   Titrate ventilator settings accordingly
    *   Aim for **early extubation**, if possible
*   Patients with Fontan circulation have **very poor tolerance of hypovolaemia**
*   High risk of **atrial arrhythmias** (can be lethal) and **thrombi**. Atrial arrhythmias should be prevented; standard practice such as optimisation of electrolytes should not be underestimated
*   Some patients may not have transvenous access to the heart if pacing is indicated (e.g., those with TCPC); in this case, **external pacing** should be available
*   It is essential to **always involve an expert** in the care of such patients

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 21. Considerations for Managing a Univentricular Heart (Non-Fontan)</summary><div class="details-content">

When considering the use of inotropic and vasoactive agents, the **nature of the pulmonary connections** must be considered.

| Circulation Type | Management Consideration |
|------------------|--------------------------|
| **Unprotected pulmonary circulation** (with or without Eisenmenger) | Manage to minimise any further increase in pulmonary artery pressures |
| **Protected pulmonary circulation** (pulmonary artery stenosis/banding) | Relative effects of pulmonary vs systemic constriction or dilatation may be different |
| **Absent pulmonary connection with systemic-pulmonary collaterals/shunts** | Small increase in PVR → significant reduction in pulmonary blood flow → dramatic desaturation. Increase in SVR and/or fall in PVR → increase in systemic-pulmonary shunting → fall in cardiac output |

**Additional considerations:**

*   Oxygen administration may itself alter the balance between pulmonary and systemic circulations
*   Any vasoactive drug should be used with care as the results may be **unpredictable**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 22. Specific Considerations for a Patient with a Systemic-Pulmonary Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with either a central or Blalock-Taussig type aortopulmonary shunt depend on **systemic arterial pressure** to maintain pulmonary blood flow via the shunt.

In such patients:

*   Ensuing desaturation may require **systemic vasoconstriction**
*   Any aggressive ventilatory manoeuvres may **reduce pulmonary blood flow**
*   Blood pressure measurement can be challenging: patients who have undergone a full repair following a previous classic or modified Blalock-Taussig shunt will have a **reduced arterial pressure in the ipsilateral arm**

</div></details>
