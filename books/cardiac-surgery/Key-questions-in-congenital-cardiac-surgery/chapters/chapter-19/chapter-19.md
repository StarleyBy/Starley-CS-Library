# Hypoplastic Right Heart

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Spectrum
*   **Hypoplastic right heart (HRH):** Umbrella term for congenital cardiac defects with underdevelopment of the right heart where the majority of ventricular mass for single-ventricle circulation is morphologically left ventricle.
*   **Common feature:** Functionally univentricular circulation due to severe RV hypoplasia or inability to septate into two ventricles.
*   **Lesions included:**
    *   Tricuspid atresia (TA) with RV hypoplasia
    *   Double-inlet left ventricle (DILV) or straddling tricuspid valve (sTV)
    *   Pulmonary atresia/stenosis with intact ventricular septum (PA-IVS) with severe RV hypoplasia or RV-dependent coronary circulation
    *   Severe Ebstein's anomaly (EA) with hypoplastic RV or severe TR unsuitable for repair
    *   Unbalanced AVSD to the left (uAVSD)
    *   Congenitally corrected TGA (ccTGA) with severe RV hypoplasia

### Embryology
*   Underdevelopment related to **low flow through the right heart** during 7th–9th week of gestation.
*   **TA/DILV/sTV:** Limited blood entry to foetal RV → failure of cavity development. RV development dependent on VSD.
*   **PA-IVS with intact septum:** RV hypertension → maldevelopment of epicardial coronaries → sinusoids or coronary fistulae. RV-dependent coronary circulation → decompression causes ischaemia.
*   **Isomerism/heterotaxy:** Single common AV valve with RV hypoplasia → uAVSD to the left.
*   **ccTGA:** Discordant AV and VA connections → high predilection for heart block (spontaneous or post-intervention).

### Aetiology and Associations
*   Multifactorial, largely unknown. Up to 1/3 of univentricular patients have genetic abnormalities.
*   **Specific associations:** Transposition more common in boys; Ebstein's associated with maternal lithium; maternal viral infections and ACE inhibitors.
*   **Syndromes:** VACTERL, Apert, Noonan, Rubinstein-Taybi, Holt-Oram, Down, DiGeorge (22q11).
*   **Genetic mutations:** TBX5, NKX2-5, GATA4, NOTCH1.
*   **Extracardiac malformations:** Exomphalos, gastroschisis, diaphragmatic hernia, renal dysplasia, etc.

### Diagnosis
*   **Foetal ultrasound (2nd trimester):** Assess RV apex formation, wall thickness, TV inflow/regurgitation, valve Z-scores, sinusoids, forward flow through PV and ductus.
*   **Postnatal:** Echocardiography is sufficient for most lesions; MRI/CT for volume assessment. Angiography seldom required.
*   **CXR:** Small right heart shadow with decreased vascular markings (low flow) or cardiomegaly with plethora (high flow).
*   **Catheterisation:** Confirms anatomy, RV volume/function, coronary anatomy (PA-IVS), PA size, PVR, MAPCAs.

### Z-Score Classification (Table 1)
| TV Z-score | RV composition | Likely destination |
|------------|----------------|-------------------|
| > –2 | Tripartite | Biventricular repair |
| –2 to –5 | Tripartite ± bipartite | 1½-ventricle repair |
| < –5 | Bipartite | Univentricular (Fontan) |

### Symptoms
*   **Low pulmonary blood flow:** Cyanosis (deoxygenated Hb >5g/dL), clubbing with chronic hypoxia.
*   **High pulmonary blood flow:** Dyspnoea, tachypnoea, recurrent RTIs.
*   Some patients asymptomatic with incidental murmur or arrhythmia.

### Specific Lesions

**Double-Inlet Left Ventricle (DILV) and Straddling TV (sTV)**
*   Atrioventricular malalignment → valves shared between ventricles, overriding the septum.
*   Both AV valves open into LV (DILV) or predominantly into LV (sTV). Hypoplastic RV gives origin to aorta (if VA discordance).
*   Blood to aorta must pass through VSD → **bulboventricular foramen (BVF)**. Restrictive BVF → systemic outflow obstruction.
*   Treatment: Damus-Kaye-Stansel anastomosis + aortopulmonary shunt (Norwood-type) for restrictive BVF.

**Tricuspid Atresia (TA)**
*   Complete absence of right AV valve, unrestrictive ASD, variable RV hypoplasia.
*   Often associated with VA discordance, VSD, subpulmonary stenosis.
*   Definitive palliation: Staged Fontan (BCPS → TCPC). Rarely, if adequate RV cavity, atrioventricular valved conduit for biventricular repair.

**Ebstein's Anomaly (EA)**
*   Dysplasia of TV with apical displacement of posterior/septal leaflets → atrialised RV.
*   Redundant 'sail-like' anterior leaflet attached normally or rotated.
*   Associated with ASD and accessory AV conduction pathways (WPW).
*   Severe neonatal form with hypoplastic RV or severe TR → single-ventricle palliation.

**Pulmonary Atresia with Intact Ventricular Septum (PA-IVS)**
*   Severe PV stenosis/atresia, intact VSD, variable RV hypoplasia. Pulmonary arteries normal size. Lung blood supply solely via ductus (PDA-dependent).
*   RV hypertension → RV-dependent coronary circulation (widespread fistulae with proximal coronary stenosis) in ~1/3. Decompression of RV causes ischaemia.
*   TV dysplasia mirrors RV underdevelopment. Z-score <-3 or RV-dependent coronaries → univentricular strategy.

**Congenitally Corrected TGA (ccTGA) with Pulmonary Stenosis**
*   Discordant AV and VA connections. Frequently associated with PS, VSD, RV hypoplasia.
*   Heart block very common (spontaneous or post-intervention).
*   Severe desaturation as neonate → aortopulmonary shunt. Some achieve biventricular or 1½-ventricle repair.

**Unbalanced AVSD to the Left (uAVSD)**
*   ~10% of endocardial cushion defects. Common AV valve sits over one ventricle more than the other → contralateral ventricular hypoplasia.
*   Minority unbalanced to the left → hypoplastic RV. Pulmonary outflow obstruction from mild stenosis to atresia.
*   Often part of isomerism/heterotaxy with abnormal systemic/pulmonary venous drainage.

### Principles of Palliation
*   **Neonatal management:** Ensure adequate but not excessive pulmonary blood flow. Prostaglandin if ductal-dependent. Interventions: BAS, ductal stenting, balloon valvuloplasty, atrial septectomy, PA banding, aortopulmonary shunt, Damus-Kaye-Stansel.
*   **Staged Fontan pathway:**
    *   **Stage 1 (neonatal):** Balance circulation (shunt or band)
    *   **Stage 2 (4–6 months):** Superior cavopulmonary connection (SCPC) — BDG or hemi-Fontan
    *   **Stage 3 (2–6 years):** Total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) — extracardiac conduit or lateral tunnel

### Surgical Options
*   **Modified Blalock-Taussig (mBT) shunt:** Synthetic conduit from subclavian to PA. Easy construction/takedown; size limited by subclavian artery.
*   **PA banding:** ePTFE or nylon tape around mid-PA. Trusler's rule: 20 mm + 1 mm/kg. Target distal PA pressure with O₂ sat 80–85%.
*   **Bidirectional Glenn (BDG):** SVC disconnected from RA, end-to-side anastomosis to PA.
*   **Hemi-Fontan (HF):** SVC-RA junction incised and laid onto RPA with patch closure of junction.
*   **Extracardiac conduit (ECC):** IVC disconnected from RA, tube graft to underside of PA. Can be performed on warm beating heart.
*   **Lateral tunnel (LT):** IVC baffled within RA to PA.

### Medical Management of Single-Ventricle Circulation
*   Thromboprophylaxis (antiplatelet or anticoagulation)
*   Dietary modification or steroids for protein-losing enteropathy
*   Childhood and seasonal flu vaccines

### Complications
*   **Death:** From staged operations or inter-stage (neurological injury, myocardial dysfunction, sepsis). Earlier SCPC reduces inter-stage mortality.
*   **Arrhythmias:** Transient (respond to medication/pacing) or permanent heart block (requires pacemaker).
*   **Long-term:** Atrial arrhythmias (almost inevitable — sinus node dysfunction, heart block, junctional/atrial ectopic/re-entrant tachycardia), myocardial dysfunction (VAD or transplant).

### Structures at Risk During Surgery
*   **mBT shunt:** Recurrent laryngeal nerve, thoracic duct, phrenic nerve; innominate/subclavian/PA narrowing.
*   **PA banding:** PV incompetence (if too proximal); branch PA impingement (if too distal, especially right).
*   **Glenn/hemi-Fontan:** Right phrenic nerve, SA node/artery (transient or permanent junctional rhythm).
*   **Fontan:** Right phrenic nerve (during caval dissection).

### Outcomes
*   **Untreated:** Ductal-dependent circulations → death after ductal closure; pulmonary over-circulation → PVD and death; worsening cyanosis → mortality.
*   **Long-term survival ('perfect Fontan'):** 86% at 5 years, 74% at 10 years. Many survive into third decade.
*   **Determinants of outcome:** LV function, AV valve function, maintenance of sinus rhythm. Morphological RV as systemic ventricle not a risk factor.

## 1 What is hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

Hypoplastic right heart (HRH) is an **umbrella term** for congenital cardiac defects where there is underdevelopment of the right heart and where the majority of the ventricular mass for the single-ventricle circulation is comprised, morphologically, of the left ventricle.

Although there are a number of lesions within this heterogeneous group, each with their own distinct morphological and surgical characteristics, they have in common a **functionally univentricular circulation**, due to either severe hypoplasia of the right ventricle (RV) or the inability of the ventricular mass to septate into two ventricles.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Lesions Included in HRH</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** Tricuspid atresia (TA) with hypoplasia of the right ventricle (HRV)
*   **b)** Double-inlet left ventricle (DILV) or straddling tricuspid valve (sTV)
*   **c)** Pulmonary atresia/stenosis with intact ventricular septum (PA-IVS), with severe hypoplasia of the RV and/or RV-dependent coronary blood flow
*   **d)** Severe forms of Ebstein's anomaly (EA) or severe forms of dysplasia of the tricuspid valve with hypoplasia of the RV and/or severe tricuspid regurgitation deemed not suitable for repair
*   **e)** Unbalanced atrioventricular septal defects (uAVSD) to the left (Figure 1)
*   **f)** Congenitally corrected transposition of the great vessels (ccTGA) with severe hypoplasia of the RV

![](_page_1_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 1. A) Echocardiographic apical view; and B) cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) four-chamber view demonstrating an unbalanced atrioventricular septal defect.** RV = right ventricle; LV = left ventricle; RAVV = right atrioventricular valve; LAVV = left atrioventricular valve; IVS = interventricular septum.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Common Features</summary><div class="details-content">

Common to all is an underdevelopment of the RV myocardium that variably affects the tricuspid valve, RV inlet, main RV and RV outflow tract. In the setting of ventriculo-arterial concordance, this may be associated with abnormalities of the pulmonary valve and pulmonary arteries, whereas in the presence of ventriculo-arterial discordance, the aortic valve or aorta can be affected.

As with any valvular lesion in congenital heart disease, the poorly developed annulus or leaflets can result in a valve that is incompetent (regurgitant), narrowed (stenotic) or imperforate (atretic).

In some of these conditions, the atrial and ventricular septa will be intact, but in many lesions of HRH, there will be an atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect or both.

</div></details>

## 2 Describe the embryology of hypoplastic right heart</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Low Flow Hypothesis</summary><div class="details-content">

In many lesions of HRH, underdevelopment of the RV is thought to be related to **low flow through the right heart** during the early stages of ventricular development from the **7th to 9th week of gestation**. Around the same time, the ventricular septum develops.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### TA, DILV and sTV</summary><div class="details-content">

In TA, DILV and sTV, entry of blood to the foetal RV is limited, causing a failure of the ventricular cavity to develop. Any development of the RV is dependent on the presence of a defect in the ventricular septum. Lesions where inflow into the foetal RV is compromised are seldom isolated and are often associated with RV hypoplasia. At the same time, the pulmonary valve is often affected, being stenotic or atretic.

Importantly, any defect where inflow into the foetal RV is compromised can be accompanied with ventriculo-arterial discordance, so the hypoplastic RV in these cases may be associated with **aortic valvular and arch hypoplasia** rather than pulmonary stenosis or atresia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### PA-IVS</summary><div class="details-content">

If there is an intact ventricular septum and pulmonary stenosis or atresia, consequent **RV hypertension** is believed to contribute to the maldevelopment of the epicardial coronary arteries and the characteristic sinusoids or coronary fistulae of PA-IVS.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Isomerism and Heterotaxy</summary><div class="details-content">

In isomerism or heterotaxy syndrome, characteristically, there is a single, common atrioventricular (AV) valve. In the unusual situation where a common AV valve occurs with RV hypoplasia, this is termed an **unbalanced AVSD to the left**, often associated with a stenotic or atretic pulmonary valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### ccTGA</summary><div class="details-content">

The unusual arrangement of discordant atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial connections seen in ccTGA has a strong predilection to **heart block**, either spontaneously or around the time of intervention, with high rates of permanent pacemaker insertion. This is thought to be related to the abnormal morphology of the conduction system seen with these discordant connections.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Persistent Left Superior Vena Cava</summary><div class="details-content">

Although a persistent left superior vena cava (LSVC) can occur with almost any congenital abnormality, other forms of anomalous systemic or pulmonary venous return are uncommon in patients without isomerism or heterotaxy.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 3 What is the aetiology of hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   As with many lesions in congenital heart disease, the aetiology of HRH is **multifactorial and largely unknown**.
*   In large cohort studies of univentricular patients subjected to genetic analysis, up to **one third have genetic abnormalities** — this may underestimate the contribution of genetic abnormalities inherited in an autosomal fashion, X-linked or through spontaneous intra-uterine mutations.
*   **Specific associations are rare**, but transposition lesions are more common in boys and Ebstein's anomaly is associated with maternal lithium exposure.
*   Maternal viral infections and the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEI) are also felt to be important in the genesis of many congenital defects, including HRH.
*   Reported associations with univentricular lesions include parental cigarette, alcohol and drug use, as well as advanced maternal age and diabetes.

</div></details>

## 5 How is hypoplastic right heart diagnosed?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Foetal Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

In the modern era, screening foetal ultrasound during the **second trimester** often identifies structural and developmental abnormalities. When the possibility of congenital heart disease has been raised, HRH can be further diagnosed on a focused scan.

**Foetal diagnostic criteria for HRH include assessment of:**
*   **a)** Right ventricular apex formation
*   **b)** Right ventricular wall thickness
*   **c)** TV inflow or the presence of tricuspid regurgitation
*   **d)** Size and Z-score of the tricuspid and pulmonary valves
*   **e)** Presence of sinusoids
*   **f)** Forward flow through the pulmonary valve and ductus arteriosus

Serial scanning will usually delineate any heart failure due to HRH as evidenced by foetal hydrops or poor growth (Figure 2). Early diagnosis allows for parental education and full discussion about the severity of the diagnosis and possible therapeutic options, including interruption and intervention.

![](_page_4_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Foetal ultrasound four-chamber view with the apex pointing to the left demonstrating a hypoplastic bipartite right ventricle with normal-sized left-sided structures and right atrium.**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postnatal Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

In the absence of an antenatal diagnosis, HRH may be diagnosed in the setting of immediate postnatal examination or even later, due to collapse or desaturation.

RV hypoplasia is difficult to quantify, as the shape of the right ventricle precludes accurate assessment of right ventricular volume by 2D echocardiography.

Although echocardiography is sufficient to delineate most congenital lesions associated with HRH, there is an increasing trend to use **MRI or CT** to complement echocardiography. MRI and 3D echocardiography result in more accurate assessment of ventricular volume, but the lower limit of right ventricular functional adequacy remains unknown. Moreover, even a small RV can work efficiently to contribute to lung perfusion as part of a **one-and-a-half ventricle repair**, where a superior cavopulmonary connection allows for a proportion of the stroke volume to perfuse the lungs directly.

In contemporary practice, **angiography is seldom required**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 6 What are the symptoms of hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   In some conditions, the baby may not show any signs of distress and may present with an incidental heart murmur, a new atrial arrhythmia or a ventricular arrhythmia.
*   When present, the symptoms of HRH will largely be dictated by whether there is **reduced pulmonary blood flow**, in which case **cyanosis** will be the predominant symptom, or **excessive pulmonary blood flow**, in which case **dyspnoea, tachypnoea and recurrent respiratory tract infections** will dominate the clinical picture.
*   **Cyanosis** is defined as a plasma concentration of deoxygenated haemoglobin greater than **5 g/dL**. The blue tinge is most noticeable in the skin, lips and fingernails, but in more severely affected individuals will be present in the central veins such as on the underside of the tongue.
*   The compensatory polycythaemia often seen in patients with chronic hypoxia makes cyanosis more likely. Longstanding cyanosis can be associated with **finger clubbing**.

</div></details>

## 7 What are the echocardiographic features of hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Diagnostic Features</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography can usually demonstrate:
*   Hallmark absence of the right atrioventricular valve in tricuspid atresia
*   Variable degrees of RV hypoplasia associated with straddling tricuspid valve, double-inlet left ventricle or ccTGA
*   Abnormalities of atrioventricular connection
*   Ventricular displacement of the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve with underdeveloped annulus, RV hypoplasia and varying TR (severe Ebstein's — mandates neonatal intervention, precludes biventricular repair)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Additional Information Required</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **a)** Relationship of the great vessels
*   **b)** Size and location of any atrial or ventricular communications
*   **c)** Size and function of the RV and LV
*   **d)** Presence of atrioventricular valvular stenosis or regurgitation
*   **e)** Degree and level of both right and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Z-Score Classification (Table 1)</summary><div class="details-content">

Z-scores have been used extensively to inform the management of HRH. This scoring system assesses the tricuspid valve annulus and RV volumes in relation to weight-based means to give important information about whether a biventricular circulation is possible (Table 1 and Figure 3).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

**Table 1. Z-score of the tricuspid valve and composition of the right ventricle (RV) used to determine the feasibility of a biventricular or one-and-a-half ventricle repair**</summary><div class="details-content">

| Z-score of the tricuspid valve | Composition of the RV | Likely destination |
|--------------------------------|----------------------|--------------------|
| Larger than –2 | Tripartite | Biventricular repair |
| –2 to –5 | Tripartite ± bipartite | 1½-type repair |
| Smaller than –5 | Bipartite | Univentricular repair (Fontan pathway) |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Other Z-Scores</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary valve and pulmonary artery (or in the case of ventriculo-arterial discordance, aortic valve and aorta) Z-scores can also guide the need for neonatal intervention. In uAVSD, inflow to the hypoplastic RV has been shown to predict suitability for biventricular repair.

*   **a)** Confirm anatomy
*   **b)** Assess volume and function of the RV
*   **c)** Delineate coronary anatomy in patients with PA-IVS
*   **d)** Assess the size of the pulmonary arteries

*   Measure pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR)
*   Assess additional sources of pulmonary blood flow, collateral vessels and MAPCAs

*   Diagnose areas of multiple perfusion (native + collateral) and areas perfused by one system only

## 9 What are the contraindications for intervention on hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   In the modern era of management of congenital heart disease, care should be **patient-centred and tailored to the individual**. As such, there is **no absolute contraindication** to intervention on HRH.
*   Multidisciplinary teams, in conjunction with the caregivers, normally assess the impact of **relative contraindications**, which usually relate to coexistent syndromes, genetic abnormalities or non-cardiac disease, to single-ventricle palliation.
*   Many centres are reluctant to offer a single-ventricle pathway to patients with genetic abnormalities such as **trisomy 13 or 18**, or syndromes with limited life expectancy.
*   Some caregivers decide to pursue **in utero termination** or a **non-interventional course** after childbirth. In those cases, end-of-life care should be provided in the manner that best suits the child's needs and parent's wishes.

</div></details>

## 10 What is double-inlet left ventricle and straddling tricuspid valve?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Definition and Anatomy</summary><div class="details-content">

Double-inlet left ventricle (DILV) and straddling tricuspid valve (sTV) represent a spectrum of congenital cardiac defects characterised by **atrioventricular malalignment** causing the atrioventricular valves to be shared between the ventricles and overriding the ventricles (Figure 6).

When the valvular apparatus of the right atrioventricular valve straddles the ventricular septum, there is necessarily a defect of the ventricular septum and therefore an interventricular communication. It is very common for there to be ventriculo-arterial discordance with this spectrum of lesions.

![](_page_9_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Double-inlet left ventricle (DILV) with ventriculo-arterial discordance and hypoplasia of the right ventricle (RV), which require all blood flow to the aorta (Ao) to go through a potentially restrictive bulboventricular foramen (BVF).** LV = left ventricle; PA = pulmonary artery; LA = left atrium; RA = right atrium.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Spectrum of Severity</summary><div class="details-content">

These lesions vary from:
*   Both atrioventricular valves open into the LV (DILV) — hypoplastic RV gives origin to the aorta
*   Straddling tricuspid valve — mitral and tricuspid valves open predominantly into the LV, with much less flow into a hypoplastic RV

In DILV and extreme forms of sTV with ventriculo-arterial discordance, all blood to the aorta must go through the VSD, and the septal defect is termed a **bulboventricular foramen (BVF)**.

Although some centres have described repair of sTV with septation of the ventricular septum in cases of an adequate-sized RV, this condition often requires single-ventricle palliation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Bulboventricular Foramen and Obstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

The presence of a BVF means that **systemic outflow obstruction** can develop, particularly if banding of the pulmonary artery is required, as secondary RV hypertrophy can partially obstruct the interventricular communication.

A restrictive BVF may require a single-ventricle type of palliation to ensure adequate systemic blood flow. This is achieved by construction of a **Damus-Kaye-Stansel anastomosis** between the aorta and PA and an aortopulmonary shunt as the initial palliation to ensure an unobstructed systemic outflow tract (Figure 7).

![](_page_10_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 7. A) Illustration; and B) operative image demonstrating a double-inlet left ventricle treated with a Norwood procedure involving a left Blalock-Taussig shunt (1), division of the pulmonary trunk (2) and Damus-Kaye-Stansel anastomosis (3).** The hypoplastic right ventricle communicates with the left ventricle through the bulboventricular foramen.

</div></details>

## 11 What is tricuspid atresia?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Definition and Anatomy</summary><div class="details-content">

Tricuspid atresia (TA) is characterised by the **complete absence of the right atrioventricular or tricuspid valve**, an unrestrictive ASD and variable degrees of RV hypoplasia (Figure 8).

![](_page_11_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 8. Tricuspid atresia with a ventricular septal defect (VSD) restricting blood flow to the lungs.** If there is atresia of the pulmonary valve, blood gets to the lungs via the patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) — ductal-dependent pulmonary blood flow. ASD = atrial septal defect; RA = right atrium; Ao = aorta; PA = pulmonary artery; LA = left atrium; LV = left ventricle; RV = right ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Associated Features and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Tricuspid atresia is often associated with:
*   Ventriculo-arterial discordance
*   Defects in the ventricular septum
*   Subpulmonary stenosis

The degree of obstruction to pulmonary blood flow, the relationship of the great arteries and the aortic valve-VSD diameter ratio determine the exact pathophysiology and clinical sequelae (Figure 9).

An aortopulmonary shunt, with or without surgical or interventional relief of any interatrial flow limitation, can be required in the neonatal period to ensure adequate pulmonary blood flow.

![](_page_12_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 9. Cardiac magnetic resonance image (MRI) demonstrating absence of the right atrioventricular (AV) valve and a hypoplastic right ventricle (RV), characteristic of tricuspid atresia.**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Surgical Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Subsequent interventions will depend on the effective size of the right ventricular cavity.

Surgical intervention for tricuspid atresia has represented the hallmark of intervention for the development of the **'right heart bypass' strategy** (Figure 10). Such interventions have led to the conceptualisation of staged single-ventricle palliation:
*   Initially a superior or unidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (Glenn)
*   Then a total cavopulmonary shunt (Fontan)

This remains the treatment of choice in most cases.

![](_page_13_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 10. Evolution of right heart bypass strategies used for tricuspid atresia and other causes of hypoplastic RV.**

Current accepted practice includes:
*   **Bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (BCPS)** — SVC anastomosed to the pulmonary bifurcation to perfuse both lungs (Figure 11A)
*   **Total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC)** — with extracardiac or intracardiac conduit (Figure 11B)

![](_page_13_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 11. Current strategies for right heart bypass, including:** A) bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (BCPS); and B) total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) with extracardiac conduit and fenestration between the conduit and the right atrium (RA). SVC = superior vena cava; IVC = inferior vena cava.

In rare cases where an adequate ventricular cavity can be obtained (either due to spontaneous growth or following aggressive RV muscle band resection), atrioventricular valved conduits (Figure 12) and closure of interventricular communication have been used to reinstate a biventricular circulation.

![](_page_14_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Atrioventricular valved conduit used in the management of tricuspid atresia with an adequate-sized right ventricle.**

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 12 What is Ebstein's anomaly?</summary><div class="details-content">

Ebstein's anomaly (EA) is a rare malformation characterised by **dysplasia of the tricuspid valve**.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Anatomical Features</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Ventricular **apical displacement** of the annular attachments of the posterior and septal leaflets into the RV, producing an **atrialised section** of the RV (Figure 13)
*   The leaflets are dysplastic and tethered to the ventricular wall by shortened chordae
*   The redundant and **'sail-like' anterior leaflet** is attached to the tricuspid annulus in the normal or slightly rotated position

![](_page_15_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 13. Ebstein's anomaly with a hypoplastic right ventricle (RV), characterised by ventricular apical displacement of the attachments of the tricuspid valve producing an atrialised section of RV.** RA = right atrium; Ao = aorta; PA = pulmonary artery; LA = left atrium; LV = left ventricle; RV = right ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Associations and Clinical Course</summary><div class="details-content">

Ebstein's anomaly is associated with:
*   Atrial septal defect
*   Accessory atrioventricular conduction pathways (WPW) in many cases

Although many cases require no intervention in infancy and can achieve good biventricular repairs later in life, a proportion present in infancy with **cyanosis, heart failure and haemodynamic instability** due to a hypoplastic RV or severe tricuspid regurgitation. In this situation, **single-ventricle palliation is often the only option**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 13 What is pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Definition and Anatomy</summary><div class="details-content">

The combination of severe stenosis or atresia of the pulmonary valve with an **intact ventricular septum** and varying degrees of hypoplasia of the right ventricle is a rare abnormality.

Unlike pulmonary atresia associated with tetralogy of Fallot:
*   The ventricular septum is intact
*   The pulmonary arteries are usually of normal size
*   The aorta is almost always left-sided and of normal size
*   Aortopulmonary collaterals are unusual

The **sole blood supply to the neonatal lungs is via the ductus arteriosus**. If prostaglandin is not commenced soon after birth, the ductus arteriosus will close, resulting in death.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### RV Hypertension and Coronary Fistulae</summary><div class="details-content">

As there is either limited or no blood ejected by the RV via the pulmonary valve and there is no VSD, **RV hypertension and hypertrophy** is a prominent feature of this condition. There is invariably a generous interatrial communication with right-to-left shunting and consequent cyanosis.

A particular aspect of PA-IVS is a **RV coronary fistula**. This can be an important feature when there are widespread fistulae with proximal native coronary stenosis resulting in a **RV-dependent coronary circulation**. In this situation, decompression of the RV by valvuloplasty can lead to severe coronary ischaemia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Management Implications</summary><div class="details-content">

The tricuspid valve can be dysplastic, and the degree of annular hypoplasia of the tricuspid frequently mirrors the degree of RV underdevelopment and the presence of coronary fistulae.

In about **a third of cases**, the presence of a RV-dependent coronary circulation, or a **Z-score of < –3** of the tricuspid valve or RV, mandate a **univentricular strategy**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 14 What is congenitally corrected transposition with pulmonary stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

The combination of atrioventricular and ventriculo-arterial discordance is also known as **congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (ccTGA)**.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Associated Features</summary><div class="details-content">

ccTGA is frequently associated with:
*   Pulmonary stenosis
*   VSD
*   Varying degrees of RV hypoplasia

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Surgical Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

Over the years, many ingenious procedures have been devised to achieve an anatomical **biventricular** or a **one-and-a-half ventricle repair**, where the LV becomes the systemic ventricle. Single-ventricle palliation is still used in some cases where the RV is deemed unsuitable to sustain the pulmonary circulation completely.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Neonatal Presentation and Heart Block</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Some patients with the HRH variant of ccTGA will be well balanced in the neonatal period and can have definitive palliation in later childhood.
*   Those who are severely desaturated as a neonate may require an **aortopulmonary shunt** as their first procedure, often in the first year of life.
*   **Heart block is very common** in ccTGA, either post-intervention or spontaneously.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 15 What is an unbalanced atrioventricular septal defect?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Definition and Prevalence</summary><div class="details-content">

In approximately **10% of endocardial cushion defects**, the common atrioventricular valve sits over one ventricle more than the other, with hypoplasia of the contralateral ventricle — this entity is described as an **unbalanced atrioventricular septal defect**.

A minority of these lesions are **unbalanced to the left** and produce a hypoplastic RV (Figure 14).

![](_page_17_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 14. Unbalanced AVSD to the left with a primum atrial septal defect, common atrioventricular (AV) valve and hypoplastic right ventricle (RV) with an inlet ventricular septal defect.** RA = right atrium; Ao = aorta; PA = pulmonary artery; LA = left atrium; LV = left ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Outflow and Associations</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Pulmonary outflow obstruction varies from mild stenosis to complete pulmonary atresia — this will often dictate the necessity of aortopulmonary shunting in infancy prior to staged palliation.
*   Good outcomes have been reported with biventricular repairs, but it is necessary to pursue single-ventricle palliation in a proportion of these patients.
*   It is not unusual for uAVSD to be part of a spectrum of congenital heart disease described variably as **isomerism or heterotaxy**. In these conditions, there is a laterality disturbance of the abdominal viscera, as well as frequent abnormalities of systemic and pulmonary venous drainage into the atria, below the common atrioventricular valve. Abnormalities of the systemic or pulmonary outflow tracts are also common.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 16 What are the principles of palliation of hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Postnatal management will often be informed by antenatal assessment, with many parents choosing to have the birth in a **tertiary congenital centre** where access to neonatal cardiology and surgery is readily available.
*   If there is concern for **ductal-dependent pulmonary blood flow or arch obstruction**, prostaglandin will be started in the neonatal intensive care unit while accurate imaging is used to delineate a complete cardiac diagnosis.
*   With improved outcomes of cardiac surgery, many units are pushing the boundaries of **biventricular repair** where cardiac repairs are performed as a single or staged procedure in the presence of hypoplasia of one of the ventricles or difficult anatomy for septation. However, in the process of trying to achieve a biventricular repair, the infant can develop pulmonary hypertension, which may preclude single-ventricle palliation at a later date.
*   In the neonatal period, it is essential to make sure there is **sufficient but not excessive pulmonary blood flow** to ensure viability but also that the pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) remains low for staged palliation.
*   The ultimate goal for children with HRH and single-ventricle physiology is the **Fontan circulation**. This is most commonly achieved in a staged manner. Central to the circulation is that all ventricular tissue is utilised for systemic perfusion, while pulmonary perfusion is dependent on passive systemic venous return directly to the pulmonary arteries. Most contemporary surgical techniques aim to achieve a **total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC)** rather than the original atriopulmonary (AP) Fontan.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 17 What are the principles of neonatal management of hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

In some cases, the circulation will be nicely balanced with flow limitation through the pulmonary vasculature, whilst in others, surgical or catheter-based intervention is required to ensure adequate intracardiac mixing of blood, unobstructed systemic outflow and an adequate but not excessive source of pulmonary blood flow.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Catheter or Hybrid Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Ductus arteriosus stenting
*   Balloon atrial septostomy (BAS)
*   Stent or balloon valvuloplasty of the pulmonary or aortic outflow

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Surgical Interventions</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Atrial septectomy
*   Pulmonary artery banding
*   Aortopulmonary shunting
*   Systemic outflow or arch reconstruction

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Damus-Kaye-Stansel Anastomosis</summary><div class="details-content">

It is sometimes necessary to perform a **Damus-Kaye-Stansel anastomosis** of the main pulmonary artery and ascending aorta where systemic outflow tract obstruction can occur, particularly in conditions with a restrictive bulboventricular foramen (BVF). This is a particular issue in DILV and extreme cases of sTV.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Timing of Second Stage</summary><div class="details-content">

Once the circulation has been optimised for palliation in the neonatal period, the child is usually discharged with a plan for cardiac imaging to assess suitability for the second stage of palliation with a superior cavopulmonary connection (SCPC), usually performed at **4–6 months**.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 18 What is the rationale for superior cavopulmonary connection?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Superior cavopulmonary connection (SCPC), particularly in the setting of aortopulmonary shunts, is a **more efficient and reliable source of pulmonary blood flow**, and because of the parallel nature of aortopulmonary shunted circulation, results in **significant volume unloading of the single ventricle**.
*   This second, interim stage of palliation, popularised in the early era of hypoplastic left heart palliation, allows for stabilisation of the circulation after the pulmonary vascular resistance has dropped, at a time when TCPC would still be high risk.
*   SCPC redirects the systemic venous drainage of the upper body directly to the lungs and allows for a circulation **in series rather than in parallel**.
*   Controversy exists as to the optimal management of **antegrade pulmonary blood flow** during single-ventricle palliation. Too much flow can lead to raised pulmonary artery pressures, and practice differs between centres whether to disconnect the antegrade flow at the time of SCPC, to disconnect at the time of TCPC or to leave some antegrade blood flow long term.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 19 What is the rationale for a total cavopulmonary connection?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Fontan completion is generally left until the child is **ambulating** and the cardiac structures are sufficiently developed to accommodate a large enough conduit (in the case of an extracardiac conduit).
*   By completing the Fontan circulation and ensuring all the IVC blood goes directly to the lungs rather than entering and mixing in the heart, a completion Fontan should **limit the effect of chronic cyanosis**.
*   With ongoing cyanosis after a SCPC, many centres advocate performing a TCPC **sooner rather than later**.
*   The improvement in arterial saturations after a TCPC can be limited by the presence of collaterals from the systemic venous system to the pulmonary venous system or pulmonary arteriovenous fistulae in the lung. Aortopulmonary collaterals should not result in desaturation but do lead to **over-circulation** of blood by the heart and can contribute to accelerated myocardial dysfunction.
*   After TCPC and completion of the Fontan circulation, most patients do well. The long-term effect of single-ventricle palliation, however, results in a number of complications that limit the durability of the circulation and the survival of these patients.
*   **Atrial arrhythmias** are a common late finding in palliated patients and can affect cardiac output as well as causing symptoms. The cause is unclear — potentially secondary to surgery on the atrium at the time of staged palliation or diastolic dysfunction of the systemic ventricle. Sinus node dysfunction resulting in bradyarrhythmias or tachyarrhythmias has led to aggressive use of medical management, EP ablation and surgical ablation procedures.
*   **Myocardial dysfunction** is a common late complication of single-ventricle circulations, and treatment options are limited to palliative care with symptom control, ventricular assist devices or transplantation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 20 What are the surgical options in the management for hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Modified Blalock-Taussig (mBT) Shunt</summary><div class="details-content">

A modified Blalock-Taussig shunt uses a synthetic conduit to create an aortopulmonary connection. It is considered by most centres to be the shunt of choice because of its ease of both construction and takedown, as well as the fact that it can be performed by thoracotomy or sternotomy.

*   Graft size selected according to patient size
*   Graft anastomosed to the side of the subclavian artery (preserved)
*   The size of the subclavian artery controls the amount of blood going into the shunt (at least until the artery grows to a size greater than the shunt)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Artery Banding</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary artery banding reduces excessive pulmonary blood flow and can be performed by left thoracotomy or median sternotomy.

*   Most common materials: ePTFE and nylon tape
*   Band placed circumferentially around the **mid-PA**
*   **Proximal placement** → pulmonary valve dysfunction
*   **Distal placement or migration** → obstructs the right PA
*   **Trusler's rule** for unidirectional mixing lesions: circumference of 20 mm + 1 mm for each kilogram of body weight
*   **Target:** reduction in distal main PA pressure to the lowest acceptable pressure with oxygen saturation of **80–85%**
*   As the patient undergoes somatic growth, band tightness increases, further decreasing distal PA pressure and oxygen saturation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Superior Cavopulmonary Connection (SCPC)</summary><div class="details-content">

Two principal operations have been popularised to achieve SCPC (Figure 15):

*   **a) Bidirectional Glenn (BDG) procedure** — simple disconnection of the SVC from the RA and an end-to-side anastomosis of the SVC to the pulmonary arteries
*   **b) Hemi-Fontan (HF) procedure** — the *in situ* SVC to RA junction is incised posteromedially and laid onto a reciprocating incision in the right PA with patch closure of the SVC to RA junction to separate the hemi-Fontan anastomosis from the heart

![](_page_21_Picture_6.jpeg)

**Figure 15. Superior cavopulmonary connections (SCPC), including:** A) bidirectional Glenn (BDG) procedure; and B) hemi-Fontan (HF) procedure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Total Cavopulmonary Connection (TCPC)</summary><div class="details-content">

Two principal surgeries have been popularised to achieve TCPC (Figure 16):

*   **a) Extracardiac conduit (ECC)** — IVC disconnected from the right atrium and connected to the underside of the pulmonary arteries with a tube graft, often after a prior bidirectional Glenn procedure
*   **b) Intracardiac lateral tunnel (LT)** — IVC baffled within the right atrium to the pulmonary arteries, often by removal of the SVC to RA patch of the hemi-Fontan procedure

Many surgeons now prefer the **extracardiac conduit** because Fontan completion can be performed on a **warm, beating heart** without cardiopulmonary bypass and may have a lower risk of inducing arrhythmias.

![](_page_22_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 16. Total cavopulmonary connection (TCPC) using an:** A) extracardiac conduit; and B) intracardiac lateral tunnel conduit. Both have a fenestration between the conduit and the right atrium (RA).

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 21 What are the principles of medical management of a patient with a single-ventricle circulation?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Thromboprophylaxis** — using an antiplatelet agent or anticoagulation
*   **Dietary modification or steroids** — for patients with protein-losing enteropathy
*   **Childhood and seasonal flu vaccines**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 22 What are the complications of surgery following intervention on hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Death** — either from any of the staged operations or between stages, from neurological injury, myocardial dysfunction, multi-organ dysfunction or sepsis. To reduce inter-stage mortality, a SCPC can be performed earlier in patients who have had an aortopulmonary shunt.
*   **Transient arrhythmias** after surgery are usually short-lived and respond well to medication or epicardial pacing.
*   **Heart block** will require placement of a permanent transvenous or epicardial pacing system.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 23 What structures are at risk during surgical intervention on hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   **Modified Blalock-Taussig shunt (via thoracotomy or sternotomy):** Recurrent laryngeal nerve, thoracic duct, phrenic nerve. During construction of the anastomosis, the innominate, subclavian or pulmonary arteries can be narrowed.
*   **PA banding:** Pulmonary valve incompetence if the band is sited incorrectly or migrates proximally. Siting the band distally can cause impingement of the branch PAs, most commonly on the right.
*   **Glenn anastomosis or hemi-Fontan procedure:** Right phrenic nerve, sinoatrial (SA) node or SA nodal artery (resulting in transient or permanent junctional rhythm).
*   **Fontan procedure:** Right phrenic nerve, principally at the time of dissecting out the cavae for cannulation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 24 What are the short-term outcomes for hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

The natural history of HRH is variable, depending on the different lesions and also the severity of each lesion.

*   Ductal-dependent systemic circulation or pulmonary circulations will result in **death after ductal closure**.
*   Untreated pulmonary over-circulation will lead to **pulmonary vascular disease and death**.
*   Prolonged and worsening cyanosis will also eventually lead to **mortality**.

</div></details>

---

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 25 What are the long-term outcomes of surgery on hypoplastic right heart?</summary><div class="details-content">

*   Only a small percentage of patients with HRH with the optimal balance between the pulmonary and systemic circulations survive into adulthood without intervention.
*   For patients who have undergone single-ventricle palliation, the long-term outcomes are, in the absence of any residual obstructing lesions in the Fontan pathway, dictated by **left ventricular function, AV valve function and the maintenance of sinus rhythm**.
*   Though many centres have pondered whether having a morphological right ventricle as the systemic pumping chamber will be a risk factor for poor survival, **this does not appear to be the case**.
*   The long-term survival from the original cohort of patients with the 'perfect Fontan' was **86% at 5 years** and **74% at 10 years**.
*   While surgical techniques improve all the time, many patients with a Fontan circulation survive into the **third decade**.
*   Further intervention for residual lesions within the circuit, repair of a leaking AV valve or EP intervention to re-establish sinus rhythm can improve symptoms.
*   **Atrial arrhythmias** are almost inevitable with a Fontan circulation and include:
    *   Bradyarrhythmia caused by sinus node dysfunction or heart block
    *   Tachyarrhythmia (junctional excitatory tachycardia, atrial ectopic tachycardia, re-entrant tachycardia)
*   **Ventricular assist devices or heart transplantation** represent the end-stage management of patients with HRH and a failing systemic ventricle.

</div></details>
