# Chapter 8. Techniques in Type I Dysfunction

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Annular Remodeling vs. Annular Narrowing
* **Historical Background:** Early surgical repairs (Davila, Kay, Wooler, McGoon) relied on circumferential suture annuloplasty, fabric bands, or localized plication, constricting the orifice arbitrarily to 2 finger breadths. Recurrent regurgitation and stenosis were frequent due to persistent disease, annular distortion, and fibrous tissue reaction around non-absorbable sutures ([Fig. 8-1](#fig-08-01)).
* **The Remodeling Concept (Carpentier, 1968):**
  1. Restores normal systolic annular size and geometry without impairing leaflet mobility.
  2. Ring selection is governed by precise anatomic measurements of the leaflets (not orifice size).
  3. Stabilizes the reconstructed annulus, permanently halting recurrent dilatation.

### The 2 Golden Relationships
* **1st Relationship:** The surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the surface area of the posterior leaflet ($S_{AL} = S_{PL}$) ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), a).
* **2nd Relationship:** The surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the orifice area of the mitral valve in systole ($S_{AL} = \text{Systolic Orifice Area}$) ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), b).
* **Systolic Geometry:** In normal systole, the annulus assumes a kidney/ovoid shape where the transverse diameter exceeds the anteroposterior diameter in a 4:3 ratio ($T > A$). In mitral regurgitation, this ratio is inverted ($A > T$) ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), c).

### Disease-Specific Remodeling Prosthetic Rings
* **Carpentier-Edwards Classic Ring:** Rigid titanium core providing maximum remodeling force for highly fibrotic/calcified rheumatic annuluses, open configuration with axial flexibility to adapt to the saddle shape ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03), a).
* **Carpentier-Edwards Physio Ring:** Semirigid structure composed of alternate layers of Elgiloy and Mylar plastic. Designed for degenerative disease; incorporates selective anteroposterior flexibility to absorb systolic shocks while preserving transverse rigidity and a 3D saddle shape matching aorto-mitral dynamics ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03), b).
* **Carpentier-McCarthy-Adams IMR Ring:** Rigid titanium construction with a reduced anteroposterior diameter and asymmetrical contour specifically designed for ischemic Type IIIb dysfunction to overcome P2/P3 tethering ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03), c).
* **Ring Dimensions & Specifications:** Defined by orifice area ($\text{cm}^2$), transverse external/internal diameter ($\text{mm}$), and vertical external diameter ($\text{mm}$) across sizes 24 to 40 ([Table 8-1](#table-08-01)).

### Surgical Technique: Sizing, Suture Placement, and Ring Implantation
* **Sizing Methodology:** Based strictly on the anterior leaflet:
  1. Placement of landmark commissural sutures at the true commissures identified by fanlike chordae, visual extension of commissural margins, or traction on main chordae ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04)).
  2. Measurement of intercommissural distance using dedicated sizers with notches matching the landmark sutures: selection between too small, too large, and perfect fit ([Fig. 8-5](#fig-08-05)).
  3. Verification of anterior leaflet height against the sizer surface after unfurling with nerve hooks ([Fig. 8-6](#fig-08-06)) or right-angle clamp ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07)).
* **Posterior Leaflet Assessment & SAM Prevention:** Normal posterior leaflet height is 10 to 15 mm. If posterior leaflet height exceeds 20 mm, height reduction is mandatory to prevent systolic anterior motion (SAM) ([Fig. 8-8](#fig-08-08)).
* **Systematic Suture Placement:**
  * Annular identification at the junction of atrium and leaflet hinge ([Fig. 8-9](#fig-08-09)).
  * Anterior annulus: horizontal mattress sutures placed with a backhand grip, strictly avoiding the aortic valve leaflets, circumflex artery, and AV node ([Fig. 8-10](#fig-08-10), [Fig. 8-12](#fig-08-12), [Fig. 8-13](#fig-08-13), [Fig. 8-14](#fig-08-14)).
  * Posterior annulus: deep bites into fibrous tissue 2 mm outside the hinge ([Fig. 8-11](#fig-08-11), [Fig. 8-15](#fig-08-15)).
  * Commissural sutures: downward forehand sutures at the posterior commissure ([Fig. 8-16](#fig-08-16)); forehand ventricularly oriented sutures and *«manœuvre magique»* at the anterior commissure ([Fig. 8-17](#fig-08-17)).
  * Overall needle trajectories: rectangular schema ([Fig. 8-18](#fig-08-18)).
  * Passing sutures through the sewing ring: proportional distribution (parallel on the anterior annulus, compressed/convergent on the posterior sewing ring to reduce dilated posterior annulus) ([Fig. 8-19](#fig-08-19), [Fig. 8-20](#fig-08-20), [Fig. 8-21](#fig-08-21)).

### Saline Testing & Evaluation Criteria
* **Intraoperative Pressurization:** Syringe bulb injection of 250 ml saline under aortic root venting to test competence before tying or releasing the ring ([Fig. 8-22](#fig-08-22)).
* **Triad of Successful Repair:**
  1. Symmetrical line of closure situated in the posterior third of the orifice ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23)).
  2. Coaptation height of at least 5 to 8 mm.
  3. Total absence of regurgitant leaks or residual prolapse/restriction ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), [Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont), [Fig. 8-25](#fig-08-25)).

### Leaflet Perforation
* Type I regurgitation from leaflet perforation (most commonly seen in bacterial endocarditis) is managed by patch reconstruction (extensively detailed in Chapter 28).

## ANNULAR PATHOLOGY AND THE REMODELING CONCEPT</summary><div class="details-content">

In the early years of valve surgery, annular dilatation was considered the primary cause of mitral valve regurgitation. With the exception of McGoon’s techniques of posterior leaflet plication, the usual repair techniques involved pledgetted sutures placed at the commissures or fabric bands supporting the annulus, in such a manner that the mitral valve orifice was constricted arbitrarily to 2 finger breadths and the posterior leaflet advanced toward the anterior leaflet ([Fig. 8-1](#fig-08-01)).

Recurrent mitral valve regurgitation or stenosis was frequent as a result of dysfunction of the leaflets, the persistent process of annular dilatation, and the fibrous transformation of the valve tissue around non-absorbable sutures.

<span id="fig-08-01"></span>![](_page_72_Figure_8-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-1**

#### The Concept of Annular Remodeling
To overcome these limitations, Carpentier introduced in 1968 the concept of "annular remodeling" using a prosthetic ring, which completely changed the philosophy of reconstructive valve surgery.

3 important characteristics differentiate the "annular remodeling concept" from "annular narrowing":
1. The prosthetic ring restores the normal systolic size and shape of the annulus without impairing leaflet motion.
2. Ring selection is based on precise measurements of the leaflet tissue.
3. The ring stabilizes the annulus and eliminates the risk of recurrent deformation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## THE 2 GOLDEN RELATIONSHIPS</summary><div class="details-content">

The mitral valve complex undergoes significant variations in size and shape during the cardiac cycle. Restoring the shape of the annulus implies restoring the normal systolic relationships between the transverse diameter and the anteroposterior diameter ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02)).

During systole, the shape of a normal annulus is ovoid with the transverse diameter, $T$, being greater than the anteroposterior diameter, $A$ ($T > A$), with a 4:3 ratio ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), a). In mitral valve regurgitation, this relationship is reversed, with $A$ being greater than $T$ ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), b). This deformation involves predominantly the posterior annulus and, to a lesser extent, the anterior annulus.

The anteroposterior diameter is approximately 3/4 of the transverse diameter ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), c).

<span id="fig-08-02"></span>![](_page_73_Figure_8-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-2**

#### The 2 Foundational Principles
The 2 golden relationships governing mitral valve reconstruction are:
1. **First Relationship:** The surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the surface area of the posterior leaflet ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), a).
2. **Second Relationship:** The surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the orifice area of the mitral valve in systole ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), b).

These relationships imply that the surface area of the anterior leaflet should be used as the reference to select the proper size of the prosthetic ring.

## REMODELING PROSTHETIC RINGS</summary><div class="details-content">

3 types of "disease-specific rings" have been developed to satisfy the anatomical requirements of the 3 most common valve diseases ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03), [Table 8-1](#table-08-01)).

<span id="fig-08-03"></span>![](_page_74_Figure_8-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-3**

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Characteristics of Disease-Specific Rings</summary><div class="details-content">

#### The Classic Ring
The Classic ring was primarily designed for rheumatic valvular disease. Its titanium core provides the optimal rigidity that is necessary to achieve the remodeling of a highly fibrotic annulus. The ring is open, however, to provide some flexibility and to allow adaptation to the saddle shape of the annulus. The remodeling effect takes place predominantly at the level of the posterior segment, which has a kidney-like shape, while the anterior segment remains straight to maintain the normal distance between the fibrous trigones ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03), a).

#### The Physio Ring
The Physio ring was designed for degenerative valve disease. It is a closed ring with selective flexibility. Its core is made of alternate layers of Elgiloy wire and Mylar plastic strips, providing transverse rigidity to preserve the intercommissural distance and anteroposterior flexibility to allow physiological variations of the anteroposterior diameter during the cardiac cycle. The 3D shape of the ring conforms to the normal saddle shape of the annulus in systole ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03), b).

#### The Ischemic Ring (IMR Ring)
The Ischemic ring (IMR) was designed for type IIIb ischemic mitral valve regurgitation. Its asymmetrical shape and reduced anteroposterior diameter are designed to compensate for the asymmetrical tethering of the leaflets, predominantly involving the P2 and P3 segments ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03), c).

Remodeling annuloplasty rings secure the annulus in a systolic position. This ensures an optimal surface of coaptation.

## RING SELECTION (SIZING)</summary><div class="details-content">

Regardless of the type of ring, ring selection is based on the measurement of the anterior leaflet, first its base and then its height ([Table 8-1](#table-08-01)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Measurement of the Anterior Leaflet</summary><div class="details-content">

The measurement of the base of the anterior leaflet requires placement of 2 landmark sutures at the commissures ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04)). The commissures (a) should not be confused with the trigones, which are larger and deeper structures not visible from an atrial view.

<span id="fig-08-04"></span>![](_page_75_Figure_8-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-4**

#### Maneuvers for Difficult Commissural Identification
Whenever the commissures are difficult to identify because of tissue thickening or jet lesions ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04), b), 1 of the following maneuvers may be helpful:
* Identify the characteristic fanlike commissural chordae that arise from the corresponding papillary muscle ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04), c).
* Visually extend the commissural edge of the anterior leaflet towards the annulus ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04), d).
* Exert traction on the main chordae of the anterior leaflet to create a furrow, which visualizes a commissural line ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04), e).

#### Intercommissural Sizing
Once the commissural sutures have been placed, traction is applied on them to slightly distend the base of the anterior leaflet. Then the distance between these 2 sutures (the intercommissural distance) is measured using specifically designed sizers ([Fig. 8-5](#fig-08-05)).

Each sizer presents 2 notches, which should be positioned to match the commissural sutures ([Fig. 8-5](#fig-08-05)). One sizer may look too small (a) or too large (b) or fit perfectly (c). In the first case, 1 should select a larger sizer; in the second case, a smaller sizer should be selected.

<span id="fig-08-05"></span>![](_page_75_Figure_8-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-5**

#### Anterior Leaflet Height Assessment
Many imperfect results following valve repair are due to improper sizing of the anterior leaflet and inadequate ring selection.

The measurement of the height of the anterior leaflet is then carried out. The leaflet is unfurled by vertical downward traction on the marginal chordae using either 2 nerve hooks ([Fig. 8-6](#fig-08-06)) or a right angle clamp ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07)).

<span id="fig-08-06"></span>![](_page_76_Figure_8-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-6**

The sizer selected from the measurement of the intercommissural distance is positioned to cover the entire surface area of the stretched anterior leaflet ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07)).

<span id="fig-08-07"></span>![](_page_76_Figure_8-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-7**

3 situations can be observed:
* In more than 90% of the cases, a good correlation is found between the intercommissural distance and the height of the anterior leaflet, as demonstrated by the free edge of the anterior leaflet not extending more than 2 mm beyond the inferior edge of the sizer ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07), a). In this case, the ring number is selected according to the sizer, and the type of ring according to the pathological process: a Classic ring when dealing with a rheumatic valvular disease, a Physio ring in degenerative valve disease, an IMR ring in ischemic valve disease. In the case of discrepancy between 2 sizes, 1 should choose the larger size, except in patients with type IIIb dysfunction.
* If the free edge of the anterior leaflet extends 2 to 4 mm beyond the inferior edge of the selected sizer ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07), b), a 1 size larger Physio ring is used to prevent the risk of systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior leaflet.
* In rare instances, the free edge of the anterior leaflet extends 5 mm or more beyond the inferior edge of the selected sizer ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07), c). In this case, a 1 size larger Classic ring should be selected, the vertical diameter of which is increased by bending its extremities to match the height of the anterior leaflet.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Measurement of the Posterior Leaflet</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Posterior Leaflet Height Assessment
Measurement of the posterior leaflet is not required to size the ring but is necessary to avoid SAM of the anterior leaflet.

The height of each posterior leaflet segment is measured after unfurling the leaflet tissue by traction on the marginal chordae ([Fig. 8-8](#fig-08-08)).

In the great majority of cases, the leaflet height is between 10 and 15 mm. If the height of any segment is up to or greater than 20 mm, the height of this segment should be reduced to avoid SAM.

Systematic measurement of the height of all posterior leaflet segments should always be performed to detect excess tissue.

**FIGURE 8-8**

## RING IMPLANTATION (SUTURE TECHNIQUES)</summary><div class="details-content">

Ring implantation is achieved by placing a series of 12 to 15 2-0 mattress sutures through the mitral annulus. The use of a 3/8-curvature needle is preferred. The 2 key factors for the success of this procedure are:
1. Proper placement of the sutures within the annulus fibrosus to avoid ring dehiscence.
2. Accurate placement of the sutures into the prosthetic ring to preserve ring geometry and avoid leaflet distortion.

Proper placement of the sutures first requires correct identification of the mitral annulus ([Fig. 8-9](#fig-08-09)). The annulus, which is not visible, is located at the atrio-valvular junction, corresponding to the hinge line of the leaflet.

<span id="fig-08-09"></span>![](_page_78_Figure_8-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-9**

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Placing Sutures within the Annulus</summary><div class="details-content">

Placing sutures within the anterior annulus should respect the following guidelines ([Fig. 8-10](#fig-08-10)):
* The needle should penetrate the atrio-valvular junction 1 mm outside the hinge with its tip oriented toward the ventricle to avoid the aortic leaflets and coronary vessels.
* The exit point should be 1 to 2 mm on the atrial side of the hinge line ([Fig. 8-10](#fig-08-10), a).
* Traction on the anterior leaflet should be applied perpendicularly to the plane of the annulus to display the hinge line.
* Deep bites into the fibrous skeleton risk catching an aortic cusp ([Fig. 8-10](#fig-08-10), b).
* The distance between the 2 entry points of the horizontal mattress suture should be approximately 3 to 4 mm.

<span id="fig-08-10"></span>![](_page_78_Figure_8-10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-10**

#### Anterior Annulus Sutures
During suture placement the position of the needle in the needle holder, the position of the surgeon’s arm, and the motion of the surgeon’s wrist vary according to the different segments of the annulus.

For sutures at the base of the anterior leaflet, a backhand position with a horizontally placed needle is preferred, beginning at the posterior commissure and proceeding towards the anterior commissure ([Fig. 8-12](#fig-08-12)). The arm is in abduction, the elbow extended, the wrist folded downward, and the hand in pronation. The needle holder should be held vertically and away from the surgeon in order for the needle to be properly oriented downwards towards the ventricle, thus avoiding injury to the aortic cusps.

<span id="fig-08-12"></span>![](_page_80_Figure_8-12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-12**

Approximately 4 to 5 horizontal mattress sutures are placed through the base of the anterior leaflet and adjacent commissural leaflets. The width of each mattress suture is guided by the curvature of the 3/8 needle. It must be no smaller than 8 mm to avoid pulling the suture through the annulus, and no larger than 1 cm to prevent distortion of the anterior leaflet ([Fig. 8-13](#fig-08-13), a). The interval between the mattress sutures should not exceed 2 mm. Care should be taken to avoid placing 2 adjacent sutures through the same hole as this may increase the size of the hole and create a periprosthetic leak ([Fig. 8-13](#fig-08-13), b).

<span id="fig-08-13"></span>![](_page_80_Figure_8-13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-13**

If a Classic ring is selected, the first 2 sutures should be placed on each side of the midline of the anterior leaflet attachment ([Fig. 8-14](#fig-08-14)).

<span id="fig-08-14"></span>![](_page_81_Figure_8-14.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-14**

#### Posterior Annulus Sutures
Placing sutures within the posterior annulus should follow the same recommendations just described, the only difference being that slightly deeper bites should be taken to reach the annulus ([Fig. 8-11](#fig-08-11)).

At this level, the needle should penetrate the atrio-valvular junction 2 mm outside the hinge and then go back towards the left atrium 2 mm outside the hinge ([Fig. 8-11](#fig-08-11), a). The angle between the needle and the leaflet hinge should remain 10° because a larger angle would increase the risk of catching secondary chordae ([Fig. 8-11](#fig-08-11), b). Proper orientation of the needle back towards the left atrium directs its tip 2 mm outside of the ring ([Fig. 8-11](#fig-08-11), c).

<span id="fig-08-11"></span>![](_page_79_Figure_8-11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-11**

For placement of mattress sutures around the posterior annular segment, the leaflet tissue is grasped transversally using a Rezzano forceps to expose the hinge ([Fig. 8-15](#fig-08-15)). A backhand position is preferred, progressing from the posteromedial to the anterolateral commissure. The elbow is folded, the wrist is in extension, and the hand is pronated. The needle holder is slightly oblique and close to the surgeon.

<span id="fig-08-15"></span>![](_page_81_Figure_8-15.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-15**

#### Commissural Sutures
For mattress sutures of the posterior commissural area, sutures are placed using a forehand position and oriented downwards ([Fig. 8-16](#fig-08-16)). In some cases, the placement of the suture near the posterior commissure is difficult because of the posteromedial trigone. In this case, it may be necessary to use a backhand position with the needle oriented upwards.

<span id="fig-08-16"></span>![](_page_82_Figure_8-16.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-16**

For sutures of the anterior commissural area, forehand sutures are also preferred ([Fig. 8-17](#fig-08-17), a). At this level, it is critical to respect the rule of orienting the needle towards the ventricular cavity so as to avoid the circumflex artery and the aortic leaflets ([Fig. 8-17](#fig-08-17), inset).

The anterolateral trigone often makes it difficult to place the sutures at this level. The *«manœuvre magique»* overcomes this difficulty by exerting traction on the adjacent suture of the posterior annulus so as to stretch the commissure and facilitate the exposure of the trigone ([Fig. 8-17](#fig-08-17), b).

<span id="fig-08-17"></span>![](_page_82_Figure_8-17.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-17**

[Fig. 8-18](#fig-08-18) summarizes the commonly used needle maneuvers. The annulus is schematically represented by a rectangle (inset):
* For the 2 horizontal sides, backhand sutures are used.
* Forehand sutures are used for the 2 vertical sides.

For left-handed surgeons the same rules apply, the difference being that the horizontal mattress sutures are placed from left to right.

<span id="fig-08-18"></span>![](_page_83_Figure_8-18.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-18**

### Placing Sutures within the Ring</summary><div class="details-content">

Before placing the sutures through the sewing band of the ring, it is essential to identify 4 landmarks: the midline of the anterior leaflet, the midline of the posterior leaflet, and the 2 commissures ([Fig. 8-19](#fig-08-19)).

<span id="fig-08-19"></span>![](_page_84_Figure_8-19.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-19**

When using the Classic ring, 2 mattress sutures should be placed on each side of the midline ([Fig. 8-19](#fig-08-19), inset).

When using a Physio ring, sutures should penetrate the sewing band inside the green suture line to avoid hitting or going through the metallic core of the ring ([Fig. 8-20](#fig-08-20), inset a). When using the Classic or Ischemic rings, the needle should be passed around the green circumferential suture that marks the sewing band ([Fig. 8-20](#fig-08-20), inset b).

<span id="fig-08-20"></span>![](_page_85_Figure_8-20.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-20**

The 2 commissural sutures are passed through the selected ring at the exact corresponding positions. This is an essential precaution to avoid a malposition of the ring and therefore a distortion of the annulus.

#### Proportional Suture Distribution
Since the intercommissural distance is identical on the annulus and the ring, the sutures are evenly and equally passed through the corresponding parts of the ring ([Fig. 8-21](#fig-08-21)). Therefore the sutures have a parallel disposition in the anterior segment of the annulus.

On the contrary, since the dilated posterior annulus must be reduced, the distance between sutures on the ring is smaller than the distance between sutures on the annulus ([Fig. 8-21](#fig-08-21)). Consequently, sutures in the posterior segment have a convergent disposition.

<span id="fig-08-21"></span>![](_page_86_Figure_8-21.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-21**

## SALINE TEST AND EVALUATION OF REPAIR</summary><div class="details-content">

The valve is tested before tying the sutures so that the ring can be pulled up should an additional correction prove to be necessary.

With the aorta still clamped and a light suction established through the aortic needle, a 250-ml bulb syringe filled with saline is used for a low-pressure injection through the mitral valve to fill the left ventricle and to remove the air from the aortic root ([Fig. 8-22](#fig-08-22)). Once air has been removed, the aortic suction line is clamped and another injection of saline into the ventricle is carried out with a slightly higher pressure to verify the morphology of the reconstructed valve.

<span id="fig-08-22"></span>![](_page_87_Figure_8-22.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-22**

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Criteria for an Optimal Result</summary><div class="details-content">

An optimal result is indicated by a symmetrical aspect and a posterior position of the closure line ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), a).

A closure line located more anteriorly with a ratio smaller than 3:1 ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), b) indicates excessive height of the posterior leaflet and/or anterior displacement of the papillary muscles, carrying a high risk of systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior leaflet.

Also unsatisfactory would be an asymmetrical line of closure, even in the absence of a leak, since it would indicate a persistent localized prolapse ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), c) or restricted leaflet motion ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), d).

<span id="fig-08-23"></span>![](_page_88_Figure_8-23.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-23**

#### Distinguishing Prolapse from Restriction
The distinction between these 2 mechanisms can be made by assessing the level of the free edge of the opposing leaflets:
* In the case of prolapse ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), c), the free edge of 1 leaflet is higher than that of the other (inset c′).
* In the case of restriction ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), d), the 2 free edges are at approximately the same level (inset d′).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Corrective Adjuncts for Suboptimal Results</summary><div class="details-content">

Once the anomaly has been identified, the ring may be pulled up if necessary to facilitate the additional repair ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24)):
* Excess tissue of the posterior leaflet is treated by reducing the height of this leaflet by ovoid resection ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), a).
* A tethered anterior leaflet is corrected either by cutting the most tethered secondary chordae or by extending the anterior leaflet patch ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), b).
* A residual limited prolapse is treated by resection of a small triangular area or chordal transfer ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), c).

<span id="fig-08-24"></span>![](_page_89_Figure_8-24.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-24**

Secondary leaks identified during testing are treated according to their cause ([Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont)):
* A local ring dehiscence with a periprosthetic leak is repaired by an additional mattress suture ([Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont), d).
* A periprosthetic leak located at a commissure is corrected by placing a figure-of-eight or mattress suture ([Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont), e).
* A leak due to an oversized ring is treated by changing the ring for a smaller size ([Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont), f).

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Concept of Remodeling Annuloplasty (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: How important was the remodeling ring annuloplasty concept with regards to the complementary techniques of leaflet reconstruction?
AC: An innovation can be called important when it opens new horizons and facilitates other discoveries. This was the case for the remodeling ring annuloplasty. Until the introduction of this technique, reconstructive valve surgery was viewed as unpredictable and difficult. The ring annuloplasty brought a solution to both problems. It was predictable because it systematically restored the normal size and shape of the orifice. It made the operation easier because it gave surgeons a stable orifice, which facilitated leaflet reconstruction. In other words, ring annuloplasty is the cornerstone of reconstructive valve surgery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Effects of Annular Dilatation on Contraction (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Does annular dilatation adversely affect annular contraction?
AC: This depends upon the degree of dilatation and the etiology. Moderate dilatation does not completely impair annular contraction, while severe dilatation leads to complete loss of contraction. In ischemic heart disease and cardiomyopathy, loss of contraction is usually seen early in the course of the disease. In degenerative valve disease, on the other hand, annular contraction is preserved for a long time.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Impact of Prosthetic Rings on Annular Dynamics (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Does a remodeling annuloplasty ring negatively affect annular contraction?
AC: Some people have said it does. However, for the reasons I just mentioned, the annulus is already akinetic or poorly kinetic in the vast majority of patients with severe mitral regurgitation. Therefore, a rigid or semirigid ring does not change the hemodynamics. Furthermore, experimental and clinical studies have shown that left ventricular function is not compromised by ring annuloplasty.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Involvement of the Anterior Annulus in Dilatation (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Is the anterior portion of the annulus involved in annular dilatation?
AC: It has always been recognized that the pathological process of annular dilatation involves predominantly the posterior annulus. This observation led some surgeons to believe that the anterior segment was never involved and that an annuloplasty should be limited to the posterior annulus. This was an oversimplification. Careful anatomical measurements have shown that the anterior annulus can dilate, particularly in patients with end-stage disease, massive left ventricular enlargement, or longstanding atrial fibrillation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Saddle Shape vs. Planar Ring Configuration (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Why does the Classic ring have a planar rather than a saddle shape configuration?
AC: The Classic ring was left open and flexible enough to adapt its shape to the contour of the annulus, including the saddle shape. The surgeon could facilitate this adaptation by slightly bending the 2 ends of the ring in the axial dimension.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Limitations of Deformable Rings and Bands (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What are the potential limitations of deformable rings or bands?
AC: Repairs with these devices have provided early good results. They are appealing at first glance because they are flexible and because bands do not require placement of sutures at the anterior annulus. In my opinion, they have 2 serious limitations. First, they do not remodel the annulus and do not provide an optimal shape and size, making it necessary to narrow the orifice in order to restore leaflet coaptation. Second, the mattress sutures used to secure these devices have the tendency to create multiple plications of the band, a potential cause of thrombus formation, fibrous proliferation, and even calcification.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Three Components of Ring Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Several times during Le Club Mitrale and the Heart Valve Club courses, you illustrated these concepts by metaphoric drawings (Fig. 8-26). Why did you take this approach?
AC: Napoléon used to say: “Un bon dessin vaut mieux qu’un long discours,”* meaning “A good drawing works better than a long speech.”

Like sailing boats, reconstructive valve surgery needs to be understood before being executed. I often illustrated these concepts by metaphoric drawings ([Fig. 8-26](#fig-08-26)). Why a boat sail? A boat sail is composed of 3 elements: the sail, the mast and boom, and the ropes (halyards, sheets). In reconstructive valve surgery, the leaflets are the sail, the annulus is the mast and boom, and the chordae are the ropes. If the mast bends or the boom moves out of place, the sail loses its shape and efficiency, regardless of how intact the fabric is. Restoring the mast and boom (the annulus) with a remodeling ring restores the mechanical foundation upon which the sail (leaflets) and ropes (chordae) can function effectively.

**FIGURE 8-26**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Development of Sizers and the Two Golden Relationships (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: How did you develop sizers in the beginning and how did you define “the two golden relationships” of the normal annulus?
AC: I accomplished both by numerous anatomical studies. I placed particular attention on the geometrical conditions that seemed to be necessary to ensure the competency of the valve during systole. In addition, I wanted to give surgeons a simple but precise measuring system to facilitate ring selection. As you know, 2 anatomical measurements were found to be fundamental in this regard: the intercommissural distance and the height of the anterior leaflet, in other words the surface area of the anterior leaflet. From these 2 simple measurements the appropriate ring size is selected, which has been calculated to correspond to the surface area of the anterior leaflet.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### C-Shaped Semirigid Rings and Anterior Sutures (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Does a C-shaped semirigid ring remodel the annulus while avoiding placement of sutures in the anterior segment?
AC: If you ask an architect to repair a building that has a damaged foundation, he will not repair only 3 walls and leave the fourth wall untouched. The anterior segment of the annulus provides the reference point for the entire repair. Leaving it unsupported ignores the fact that the intertrigonal distance can dilate and that the anterior leaflet needs a stable support to ensure long-term stability.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pitfalls in Ring Sizing (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: What is the most common pitfall in ring sizing?
AC: A common mistake is to measure the intertrigonal distance instead of the intercommissural distance. The trigones are deeper structures, and measuring between them will lead to undersizing the ring. Another error is to measure the posterior leaflet instead of the anterior leaflet. The posterior leaflet is often elongated or deformed by the disease process, making it an unreliable guide for ring sizing.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Suture Placement Errors in Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What are the most common suture placement errors in annuloplasty?
AC: One error is not placing the sutures large enough through the annulus, taking only superficial bites of the endocardium. This increases the risk of ring dehiscence. Another error is to place sutures too deep into the fibrous skeleton in the anterior segment, risking injury to the aortic valve cusps or the circumflex artery. Finally, placing 2 adjacent sutures through the same needle hole weakens the tissue and may cause a periprosthetic leak.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Landmarks and Ring Implantation Errors (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What is the most common error in ring implantation?
AC: The most common error is not respecting the correspondence between the anatomical landmarks of the annulus and the markers on the prosthetic ring. If the commissural sutures are not passed through the corresponding markers on the ring, the ring will be malrotated, creating annular distortion and leaflet asymmetry.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pledgetted Sutures in Ring Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Some surgeons use pledgetted mattress sutures to secure the ring by leaving the pledgets on the atrial side. What is your opinion?
AC: It may be a useful technique whenever there is an annular defect, severe tissue fragility, or in reoperations. However, for routine ring annuloplasty, nonpledgetted 2-0 mattress sutures provide excellent holding strength while avoiding the excess foreign material associated with pledgets.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Saline Testing Reliability (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: How reliable is saline testing to assess the efficacy of a valve reconstruction?
AC: Saline testing is optimal to recognize occasional technical errors, such as residual prolapse, restricted leaflet motion, or periprosthetic leaks. However, it is a static test performed in an arrested, non-pressurized heart. It does not replace postoperative transesophageal echocardiography, which evaluates the valve under dynamic, physiological loading conditions.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Beating Heart Testing vs. Cardioplegia (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Would it be even more reliable to test the valve on a beating heart?
AC: This method increases the risk of air embolism. In addition, the visual exposure is significantly impaired on a beating heart, making precise inspection of the closure line difficult. Testing on an arrested heart with aortic root venting provides the best compromise between safety and precision.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Repair of Ring Dehiscence (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: How do you repair a ring dehiscence?
AC: It depends upon the type and extent of the dehiscence. If the dehiscence is limited to a small area and the rest of the ring is well anchored, 1 or 2 figure-of-eight or mattress sutures with Teflon pledgets can be used to reattach the ring. If the dehiscence is extensive (more than 1/3 of the circumference) or associated with infection, the ring must be completely removed, the annulus debrided, and a new ring implanted.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Annular Dehiscence (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: What about annular dehiscence?
AC: Annular dehiscence can be observed after mitral valve reconstruction or replacement in the presence of extensive calcification or severe tissue fragility. The management of this challenging complication is discussed in detail in Chapter 9.

</div></details>
