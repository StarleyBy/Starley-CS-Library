# 8. Techniques in Type I Dysfunction

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Annular Remodeling vs. Annular Narrowing
* **Historical Evolution:** Early mitral valve repair techniques viewed annular dilatation as the sole primary cause of regurgitation, attempting arbitrary orifice constriction to 2 finger breadths using suture plication or fabric bands ([Fig. 8-1](#fig-08-01)). High rates of recurrent regurgitation, stenosis, and suture dehiscence resulted.
* **Carpentier's Annular Remodeling Concept:** Differs from simple narrowing by 3 fundamental features:
  1. Restores the normal systolic size and physiological geometry (saddle/kidney shape) of the annulus without restricting leaflet mobility.
  2. Ring selection is based on precise anatomical measurements of healthy leaflet tissue (the anterior leaflet) rather than arbitrary orifice reduction.
  3. Permanently stabilizes the fibrous annulus with a prosthetic ring, eliminating late redilatation and suture tear-out.

### The 2 Golden Relationships
* **First Golden Relationship:** The surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the surface area of the posterior leaflet ($S_{\text{anterior}} = S_{\text{posterior}}$) ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02)).
* **Second Golden Relationship:** The surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the systolic mitral orifice area ($S_{\text{anterior}} = \text{Orifice Area}$).
* **Geometric Ratio:** The normal systolic anteroposterior diameter represents approximately 3/4 of the transverse diameter.

### Disease-Specific Remodeling Prosthetic Rings
* **Carpentier-Edwards Classic Ring:** Rigid titanium core with an open D-shape ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03)). Specifically engineered for rheumatic valvular disease to provide maximum rigid remodeling force across heavily fibrotic or calcified tissue.
* **Carpentier-Edwards Physio Ring:** Semirigid structure composed of alternate layers of Elgiloy and Mylar plastic. Designed for degenerative disease; incorporates selective anteroposterior flexibility to absorb systolic shocks while preserving transverse rigidity and a 3D saddle shape matching aorto-mitral dynamics.
* **Carpentier-McCarthy-Adams IMR Ring:** Rigid titanium construction with a reduced anteroposterior diameter and asymmetrical contour specifically designed for ischemic Type IIIb dysfunction to overcome P2/P3 tethering.
* **Ring Dimensions & Specifications:** Defined by orifice area ($\text{cm}^2$), transverse external/internal diameter ($\text{mm}$), and vertical external diameter ($\text{mm}$) across sizes 24 to 40 ([Table 8-1](#table-08-01)).

### Surgical Technique: Sizing, Suture Placement, and Ring Implantation
* **Sizing Methodology:** Based strictly on the anterior leaflet:
  1. Placement of landmark commissural sutures at the true commissures identified by fanlike chordae, visual extension of commissural margins, or traction on main chordae ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04)).
  2. Measurement of intercommissural distance using dedicated sizers ([Fig. 8-5](#fig-08-05), [Fig. 8-6](#fig-08-06)).
  3. Verification of anterior leaflet height against the sizer surface ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07)).
* **Posterior Leaflet Assessment & SAM Prevention:** Normal posterior leaflet height is 10 to 15 mm. If posterior leaflet height exceeds 20 mm, height reduction is mandatory to prevent systolic anterior motion (SAM) ([Fig. 8-8](#fig-08-08)).
* **Systematic Suture Placement:**
  * Annular identification at the junction of atrium and leaflet hinge ([Fig. 8-9](#fig-08-09)).
  * Anterior annulus: horizontal mattress sutures placed with a backhand grip, strictly avoiding the aortic valve leaflets, circumflex artery, and AV node ([Fig. 8-10](#fig-08-10), [Fig. 8-11](#fig-08-11), [Fig. 8-12](#fig-08-12), [Fig. 8-13](#fig-08-13)).
  * Posterior annulus: deep bites into fibrous tissue ([Fig. 8-14](#fig-08-14), [Fig. 8-15](#fig-08-15)).
  * Passing sutures through the sewing ring: proportional distribution (parallel on the anterior annulus, compressed on the posterior sewing ring to reduce dilated posterior annulus) ([Fig. 8-19](#fig-08-19), [Fig. 8-20](#fig-08-20), [Fig. 8-21](#fig-08-21)).
  * Parachute descent and sequential tying ([Fig. 8-22](#fig-08-22), [Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23)).

### Saline Testing & Evaluation Criteria
* **Intraoperative Pressurization:** Syringe bulb injection of 250 ml saline under aortic root venting to test competence before tying or releasing the ring ([Fig. 8-22](#fig-08-22)).
* **Triad of Successful Repair:**
  1. Symmetrical line of closure situated in the posterior third of the orifice ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23)).
  2. Coaptation height of at least 5 to 8 mm.
  3. Total absence of regurgitant leaks or residual prolapse/restriction ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), [Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont), [Fig. 8-25](#fig-08-25)).

### Leaflet Perforation
* Direct primary suture closure for small circular perforations; autologous pericardial patch repair for large defects, always combined with prosthetic ring annuloplasty ([Fig. 8-26](#fig-08-26)).

## ANNULAR PATHOLOGY AND THE REMODELING CONCEPT</summary><div class="details-content">

In the early years of valve surgery, annular dilatation was considered the primary cause of mitral valve regurgitation. With the exception of McGoon’s techniques of posterior leaflet plication, the usual repair techniques involved pledgetted sutures placed at the commissures or fabric bands supporting the annulus, in such a manner that the mitral valve orifice was constricted arbitrarily to 2 finger breadths and the posterior leaflet advanced toward the anterior leaflet ([Fig. 8-1](#fig-08-01)).

Recurrent mitral valve regurgitation or stenosis was frequent as a result of dysfunction of the leaflets, the persistent process of annular dilatation, and the fibrous transformation of the valve tissue around non-absorbable sutures.

<span id="fig-08-01"></span>![](_page_72_Figure_8-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-1** Historical techniques of annular constriction (suture annuloplasty, fabric bands, and posterior leaflet plication) constricting the mitral orifice arbitrarily to 2 finger breadths.

#### The Concept of Annular Remodeling
To overcome these limitations, Carpentier introduced in 1968 the concept of "annular remodeling" using a prosthetic ring, which completely changed the philosophy of reconstructive valve surgery.

3 important characteristics differentiate the "annular remodeling concept" from "annular narrowing":
1. The prosthetic ring restores the normal systolic size and shape of the annulus without impairing leaflet motion.
2. Ring selection is based on precise measurements of the leaflet tissue.
3. The ring permanently stabilizes the annulus and eliminates the risk of recurrent deformation.

## THE 2 GOLDEN RELATIONSHIPS</summary><div class="details-content">

The mitral valve can be compared to an entrance gate or a French door: the leaflets are the doors; the mitral annulus is the frame. Any distortion or alteration of 1 or both of these components leads to dysfunction. To restore normal function, the geometry of each component and their relationships must be respected.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Geometric Proportions</summary><div class="details-content">

During systole, the normal mitral valve annulus adopts a kidney-like shape with a flat or slightly concave anterior aspect and a curved posterior aspect ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02)).

The anteroposterior diameter is approximately 3/4 of the transverse diameter.

<span id="fig-08-02"></span>![](_page_73_Figure_8-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-2** Normal geometrical relationships of the mitral valve in systole: (a) Surface area of the anterior leaflet equals surface area of the posterior leaflet; (b) Surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the systolic orifice area; (c) Kidney-shaped systolic configuration with an anteroposterior to transverse diameter ratio of 3/4.

#### The 2 Foundational Principles
The 2 golden relationships governing mitral valve reconstruction are:
1. **First Relationship:** The surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the surface area of the posterior leaflet ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), a).
2. **Second Relationship:** The surface area of the anterior leaflet equals the orifice area of the mitral valve in systole ([Fig. 8-2](#fig-08-02), b).

These 2 fundamental relationships explain why the anterior leaflet is used as the reference structure to select the appropriate ring size during mitral valve reconstruction.

## REMODELING PROSTHETIC RINGS</summary><div class="details-content">

3 types of "disease-specific rings" have been developed to satisfy the anatomical requirements of the 3 most common valve diseases ([Fig. 8-3](#fig-08-03), [Table 8-1](#table-08-01)).

<span id="fig-08-03"></span>![](_page_74_Figure_8-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-3** Disease-specific remodeling prosthetic rings: (a) Carpentier-Edwards Classic ring (rigid titanium); (b) Carpentier-Edwards Physio ring (semirigid Elgiloy/Mylar); (c) Carpentier-McCarthy-Adams IMR ring (rigid asymmetrical titanium).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Characteristics of Disease-Specific Rings</summary><div class="details-content">

#### The Classic Ring
The Classic ring was primarily designed for rheumatic valvular disease. Its titanium core provides the optimal rigidity that is necessary to achieve the remodeling of a highly fibrotic annulus.

The ring is open, however, to provide some flexibility and to allow adaptation of its geometry to individual anatomical variations of the annulus in both the anteroposterior configuration (saddle shape) and the posteroseptal dimension.

#### The Physio Ring
The Physio ring, designed preferably for degenerative valvular diseases, has a larger anteroposterior diameter and a larger orifice area. It is a semirigid structure composed of alternate layers of Elgiloy and Mylar plastic material.

Its unique anteroposterior selective flexibility provides a shock-absorbing structure while maintaining appropriate support in the transverse dimension to preserve the remodeling effect. The saddle shape conforms to the contour of the annulus, particularly at the anterior leaflet where systolic bulging of the aorto-mitral curtain is preserved.

#### The Ischemic Ring (IMR Ring)
The Ischemic ring (IMR ring) was specifically designed for patients with ischemic, Type IIIb dysfunction. It has a rigid titanium structure to ensure non-deformability.

Its reduced anteroposterior diameter and asymmetrical shape compensate for the severe tethering of the P2 and P3 segments observed in this clinical setting. Remodeling annuloplasty rings secure the annulus in a systolic position, ensuring an optimal surface of coaptation.

## RING SELECTION (SIZING)</summary><div class="details-content">

Regardless of the type of ring, ring selection is based on the measurement of the anterior leaflet, first its base and then its height ([Table 8-1](#table-08-01)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Measurement of the Anterior Leaflet</summary><div class="details-content">

The measurement of the base of the anterior leaflet requires placement of 2 landmark sutures at the commissures ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04)). The commissures (a) should not be confused with the trigones, which are larger and deeper structures not visible from an atrial view.

<span id="fig-08-04"></span>![](_page_75_Figure_8-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-4** Identification of the commissures and placement of landmark sutures: (a) Anatomic commissural landmarks; (b) Difficult identification due to fibrosis; (c) Fanlike commissural chordae; (d) Visual extension of leaflet margins; (e) Chordal traction creating a commissural furrow.

#### Maneuvers for Difficult Commissural Identification
Whenever the commissures are difficult to identify because of tissue thickening or jet lesions ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04), b), 1 of the following maneuvers may be helpful:
* Identify the characteristic fanlike commissural chordae that arise from the corresponding papillary muscle ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04), c).
* Visually extend the commissural edge of the anterior leaflet towards the annulus ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04), d).
* Exert traction on the main chordae of the anterior leaflet to create a furrow, which visualizes a commissural line ([Fig. 8-4](#fig-08-04), e).

#### Intercommissural Sizing
Once the commissural sutures have been placed, traction is applied on them to slightly distend the base of the anterior leaflet. Then the distance between these 2 sutures (the intercommissural distance) is measured using specifically designed sizers ([Fig. 8-5](#fig-08-05)).

<span id="fig-08-05"></span>![](_page_75_Figure_8-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-5** Measurement of the intercommissural distance using dedicated sizers with notches matching the landmark commissural sutures.

Each sizer presents 2 notches, which should be positioned to match the commissural sutures ([Fig. 8-6](#fig-08-06)). One sizer may look too small (a) or too large (b) or fit perfectly (c). In the first case, 1 should select a larger sizer; in the second case, a smaller sizer should be selected.

<span id="fig-08-06"></span>![](_page_76_Figure_8-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-6** Notches on the sizer aligned with commissural sutures: (a) Sizer too small; (b) Sizer too large; (c) Perfect fit.

#### Anterior Leaflet Height Assessment
The sizer selected from the measurement of the intercommissural distance is positioned to cover the entire surface area of the stretched anterior leaflet ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07)).

<span id="fig-08-07"></span>![](_page_76_Figure_8-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-7** Verification of anterior leaflet surface area and height: (a) Normal correlation ($>90\%$ of cases); (b) Discrepancy with excessive height (Barlow's disease); (c) Discrepancy with deficient height (fibroelastic deficiency).

3 situations can be observed:
* In more than 90% of the cases, a good correlation is found between the intercommissural distance and the height of the anterior leaflet, as demonstrated by the free edge of the anterior leaflet not extending more than 2 mm beyond the inferior edge of the sizer ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07), a). The sizer is considered correct.
* In a few cases, a discrepancy is observed between the intercommissural distance and the height of the anterior leaflet:
  * The height of the anterior leaflet is larger than the sizer ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07), b). This condition is seen in Barlow’s disease with excess tissue. One should choose a larger sizer to avoid systolic anterior motion (SAM).
  * The height of the anterior leaflet is smaller than the sizer ([Fig. 8-7](#fig-08-07), c). This situation is seen in fibroelastic deficiency with tissue atrophy or in rheumatic disease with fibrous retraction. In this case, 1 should choose a smaller sizer.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Measurement of the Posterior Leaflet</summary><div class="details-content">

Measurement of the posterior leaflet is not required to size the ring but is necessary to avoid SAM of the anterior leaflet.

The height of each posterior leaflet segment is measured after unfurling the leaflet tissue by traction on the marginal chordae ([Fig. 8-8](#fig-08-08)).

In the great majority of cases, the leaflet height is between 10 and 15 mm. If the height of any segment is up to or greater than 20 mm, the height of this segment should be reduced to avoid SAM.

<span id="fig-08-08"></span>![](_page_77_Figure_8-8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-8** Measurement of the posterior leaflet height by unfurling the tissue with chordal traction; heights $\ge 20\text{ mm}$ warrant reduction to prevent SAM.

## RING IMPLANTATION (SUTURE TECHNIQUES)</summary><div class="details-content">

Proper placement of the sutures first requires correct identification of the mitral annulus ([Fig. 8-9](#fig-08-09)). The annulus, which is not visible, is located at the atrio-valvular junction, corresponding to the hinge line of the leaflet.

<span id="fig-08-09"></span>![](_page_78_Figure_8-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-9** Identification of the mitral annulus at the atrio-valvular junction (hinge line) using nerve hook traction to elevate leaflet tissue.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Placing Sutures within the Annulus</summary><div class="details-content">

Placing sutures within the anterior annulus should respect the following guidelines ([Fig. 8-10](#fig-08-10)):
* Traction on the anterior leaflet should be applied perpendicularly to the plane of the annulus to display the hinge line (a).
* The needle bite should be 1.5 to 2 mm from the hinge line into the leaflet tissue to avoid injuring the aortic cusps (b).
* A suture passed through the true fibrous annulus would risk catching an aortic cusp (c).
* The distance between the 2 entry points of the horizontal mattress suture should be approximately 3 to 4 mm (d).

<span id="fig-08-10"></span>![](_page_78_Figure_8-10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-10** Guidelines for anterior annular sutures: (a) Perpendicular traction; (b) Safe bite 1.5 to 2 mm on the leaflet side of the hinge; (c) Danger of catching aortic cusps in the deep fibrous skeleton; (d) Recommended 3 to 4 mm mattress width.

#### Anterior Annulus Sutures
For sutures at the base of the anterior leaflet, a backhand position with a horizontal needle holder is recommended ([Fig. 8-11](#fig-08-11), [Fig. 8-12](#fig-08-12), [Fig. 8-13](#fig-08-13)).

<span id="fig-08-11"></span>![](_page_79_Figure_8-11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-11** Technique for passing horizontal mattress sutures along the anterior leaflet hinge using a backhand grip.

<span id="fig-08-12"></span>![](_page_80_Figure_8-12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-12** Sequential placement of horizontal mattress sutures along the anterior annulus, advancing from the right fibrous trigone toward the left fibrous trigone.

<span id="fig-08-13"></span>![](_page_80_Figure_8-13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-13** Completed row of anterior annular sutures placed without distortion of the aorto-mitral curtain.

#### Commissural Sutures
For sutures of the anterior commissural area, a forehand position with a vertical needle holder is used ([Fig. 8-14](#fig-08-14), [Fig. 8-15](#fig-08-15)).

For sutures of the posterior commissural area, a forehand position with a horizontal needle holder is preferred.

#### Posterior Annulus Sutures
For sutures placed along the posterior annulus, a forehand position with a vertical needle holder is used for the right-sided portion, while a backhand position with a horizontal needle holder is preferred for the left-sided portion ([Fig. 8-16](#fig-08-16), [Fig. 8-17](#fig-08-17)).

### Placing Sutures within the Ring</summary><div class="details-content">

Before placing the sutures through the sewing band of the ring, it is essential to organize and distribute the sutures to obtain an optimal remodeling effect ([Fig. 8-19](#fig-08-19)).

<span id="fig-08-19"></span>![](_page_84_Figure_8-19.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-19** Organization of annular sutures on the holder before passing through the prosthetic ring sewing band.

The 4 reference points on the annulus (the 2 commissures and the 2 trigones) are matched to the corresponding colored markers on the ring ([Fig. 8-20](#fig-08-20)).

<span id="fig-08-20"></span>![](_page_85_Figure_8-20.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-20** Matching the 4 reference points (2 commissures and 2 trigones) with colored markers on the prosthetic ring.

#### Proportional Suture Distribution
Because the intercommissural distance is identical on the annulus and the ring, the sutures are evenly and equally passed through the corresponding parts of the ring ([Fig. 8-21](#fig-08-21)). Therefore the sutures have a parallel disposition in the anterior segment of the annulus.

On the contrary, since the dilated posterior annulus must be reduced, the distance between sutures on the ring is smaller than the distance between sutures on the annulus ([Fig. 8-21](#fig-08-21)). Consequently, sutures in the posterior segment have a convergent disposition.

<span id="fig-08-21"></span>![](_page_86_Figure_8-21.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-21** Distribution of sutures through the sewing band: parallel disposition on the anterior segment; convergent disposition on the posterior segment to achieve uniform reduction.

## SALINE TEST AND EVALUATION OF REPAIR</summary><div class="details-content">

The valve is tested before tying the sutures so that the ring can be pulled up should an additional correction prove to be necessary ([Fig. 8-22](#fig-08-22)).

With the aorta still clamped and light suction established through the aortic needle, a 250-ml bulb syringe filled with saline is used for a low-pressure injection through the mitral valve to fill the ventricle and remove air from the aortic root.

Once air has been removed, the aortic suction line is clamped and another injection of saline into the ventricle is carried out with a slightly higher pressure to verify the morphology of the reconstructed valve.

<span id="fig-08-22"></span>![](_page_87_Figure_8-22.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-22** Intraoperative saline testing using a 250-ml bulb syringe while venting the aortic root to verify valve competence and coaptation geometry.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Criteria for an Optimal Result</summary><div class="details-content">

An optimal result is indicated by a symmetrical aspect and a posterior position of the closure line ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23)).

A non-satisfactory result would be shown by:
* Persistent regurgitation due to residual prolapse or restricted leaflet motion.
* An anteriorly displaced line of closure, which usually indicates an excess of tissue of the posterior leaflet and exposes the patient to the risk of SAM ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), b).
* An asymmetrical line of closure, even in the absence of a leak, since it would indicate a persistent localized prolapse ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), c) or restricted leaflet motion ([Fig. 8-23](#fig-08-23), d).

<span id="fig-08-23"></span>![](_page_88_Figure_8-23.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-23** Assessment of the coaptation line: (a) Symmetrical posterior closure line (optimal); (b) Anteriorly displaced closure line with risk of SAM; (c) Asymmetrical closure line from persistent prolapse; (d) Asymmetrical closure line from restricted leaflet motion.

#### Distinguishing Prolapse from Restriction
The distinction between these 2 mechanisms can be made by assessing the level of the free edge of the opposing leaflets:
* In the case illustrated by inset c, the free edge of 1 leaflet is higher than that of the other (inset c′).
* In the case illustrated in inset d, the 2 free edges are at approximately the same level (inset d′).

Slight anterior displacement or asymmetry of the closure line is acceptable provided that a large height of coaptation of at least 5 mm has been restored. This can be verified by measuring with 2 hooks the depth of the coaptation surface.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Corrective Adjuncts for Suboptimal Results</summary><div class="details-content">

Once the anomaly has been identified, the ring may be pulled up if necessary to facilitate the additional repair ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), [Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont)):
* Excess tissue of the posterior leaflet is treated by reducing the height of this leaflet by ovoid resection ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), a).
* A tethered anterior leaflet is corrected either by cutting the most tethered secondary chordae or by extending the anterior leaflet patch ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), b).
* A residual limited prolapse is treated by resection of a small triangular leaflet, by transfer of chordae, or by use of artificial chordae ([Fig. 8-24](#fig-08-24), c).
* A leak attributable to suture dehiscence or a displaced suture is treated by placing an additional suture ([Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont), d).
* A small residual leak ($< 1\text{ mm}$) at the commissure can be treated by a commissural stitch ([Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont), e).
* A leak due to an oversized ring is treated by changing the ring for a smaller size ([Fig. 8-24 cont'd](#fig-08-24-cont), f).

<span id="fig-08-24"></span>![](_page_89_Figure_8-24.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-24** Secondary corrective maneuvers following saline testing: (a) Ovoid resection for excess posterior leaflet tissue; (b) Secondary chordal section for anterior leaflet tethering; (c) Triangular resection or chordal transfer for residual prolapse.

<span id="fig-08-24-cont"></span>![](_page_90_Figure_8-24_cont.jpeg)

**FIGURE 8-24 (Cont'd)** Additional corrective maneuvers: (d) Additional mattress suture for local dehiscence; (e) Commissural stitch for minor commissural leak; (f) Ring downsizing for persistent central leak from oversizing.

Once the result is verified, the sutures are tied. The 2 commissural sutures are tied first, then the posterior annular sutures, and finally the anterior annular sutures ([Fig. 8-25](#fig-08-25)).

<span id="fig-08-25"></span>![](_page_90_Figure_8-25.jpeg)

## LEAFLET PERFORATION (TYPE I DYSFUNCTION)</summary><div class="details-content">

Type I valve regurgitation also consists of leaflet perforation, commonly seen in bacterial endocarditis.

Small circular perforations can be closed directly using interrupted monofilament or braided sutures.

Larger perforations require patching of the valvular lesion using autologous pericardium (fresh or glutaraldehyde-treated) ([Fig. 8-26](#fig-08-26)). Reconstruction is always completed by prosthetic ring annuloplasty to relieve tension on the repair.

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Concept of Remodeling Annuloplasty (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: How important was the remodeling ring annuloplasty concept with regards to the complementary techniques of leaflet reconstruction?

**AC:** An innovation can be called important when it opens the door to a new avenue of techniques. This is the case for ring annuloplasty.

Ring annuloplasty was initially designed to remodel and stabilize the dilated annulus. It soon became apparent, however, that it was the essential key to the success of leaflet and chordal reconstructive techniques.

By maintaining the orifice in a systolic configuration, the ring relieves the tension exerted on the sutures of reconstructed leaflets and chordae.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Effects of Annular Dilatation on Contraction (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Does annular dilatation adversely affect annular contraction?

**AC:** This depends upon the degree of dilatation and the force of ventricular contraction. A severe dilatation affects annular contraction.

By contrast, moderate annular dilatation does not necessarily prevent annular contraction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Impact of Prosthetic Rings on Annular Dynamics (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Does a remodeling annuloplasty ring negatively affect annular contraction?

**AC:** Some people have said it does. However, for the simple reason that the ring has been designed to fix the annulus in a systolic configuration, the absence of systolic contraction is irrelevant.

Fixing the annulus in a systolic configuration ensures a permanent, optimal coaptation of the leaflets during systole, which is the primary objective of valve reconstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Involvement of the Anterior Annulus in Dilatation (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Is the anterior portion of the annulus involved in annular dilatation?

**AC:** It has always been recognized that the pathological process involves predominantly the posterior annulus, and this led some surgeons to introduce the concept of posterior annuloplasty.

This is a misconception, however, because annular dilatation, while predominant in the posterior segment, also affects the anterior segment to a lesser degree.

More importantly, posterior annuloplasty does not respect the kidney-like shape of the orifice in systole, which is essential to restore normal valve function.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Saddle Shape vs. Planar Ring Configuration (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Why does the Classic ring have a planar rather than a saddle shape configuration?

**AC:** The Classic ring was left open and flexible enough to adapt its shape to the contour of the annulus, including the saddle shape.

The surgeon could facilitate this adaptation by slightly bending the 2 ends of the ring in the axial dimension.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Limitations of Deformable Rings and Bands (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What are the potential limitations of deformable rings or bands?

**AC:** Repairs with these devices have provided early good results. They are appealing at first glance because they are flexible and because bands do not require placement of sutures at the anterior annulus. In my opinion, they have 2 serious limitations:
* First, they do not remodel the annulus and do not provide an optimal shape and size, making it necessary to narrow the orifice in order to restore competency.
* Second, because they are deformable, they do not prevent secondary dilatation of the annulus, particularly in degenerative and ischemic valve diseases.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Three Components of Ring Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Several times during Le Club Mitrale and the Heart Valve Club courses, you illustrated the 3 components of ring annuloplasty using a boat sail. Can you explain this metaphor?

**AC:** Napoléon used to say: "Un bon dessin vaut mieux qu'un long discours," meaning "A good drawing works better than a long speech."

The 3 components of ring annuloplasty are:
1. **Remodeling:** Adapting the shape of the frame to the sail.
2. **Resizing:** Reducing the dimensions of the frame to the surface area of the sail.
3. **Stabilization:** Securing the frame to prevent secondary deformation under load.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Development of Sizers and the Two Golden Relationships (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: How did you develop sizers in the beginning and how did you define "the 2 golden relationships"?

**AC:** I accomplished both by numerous anatomical studies. I placed particular attention on the geometrical conditions that seemed to be necessary to ensure the competency of the valve during systole.

In addition, I wanted to give surgeons a simple but precise measuring system to facilitate ring selection. As you know, 2 anatomical measurements were found to be fundamental in this regard: the intercommissural distance and the height of the anterior leaflet, in other words the surface area of the anterior leaflet.

From these 2 simple measurements the appropriate ring size is selected, which has been calculated to take into consideration the entire leaflet surface area.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### C-Shaped Semirigid Rings and Anterior Sutures (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Does a C-shaped semirigid ring remodel the annulus while avoiding placement of sutures in the anterior segment?

**AC:** If you ask an architect to repair a building that has been rocked by an earthquake, the architect would tell you that you must consolidate the 4 walls of the building, not just 3.

A C-shaped ring cannot properly remodel the anterior segment, and leaving this segment unsupported increases the risk of recurrent dilatation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pitfalls in Ring Sizing (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: What is the most common pitfall in ring sizing?

**AC:** A common mistake is to measure the intertrigonal distance instead of the more precise intercommissural distance. The trigones are deep structures that cannot be seen from an atrial view.

Another pitfall is to size the ring based on the posterior leaflet instead of the anterior leaflet.

The posterior leaflet is often diseased, elongated, or retracted, and cannot serve as a reliable reference for sizing.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Suture Placement Errors in Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What are the most common suture placement errors in annuloplasty?

**AC:** One error is not placing the sutures large enough and deep enough into the annulus to ensure adequate strength.

Another error is placing sutures too close to the leaflet edge or through the thin leaflet tissue, leading to tear-out.

Finally, in the anterior annulus, taking bites too deep can injure the aortic valve cusps, while at the commissures bites placed too deeply can compromise the circumflex artery or the atrioventricular node.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Landmarks and Ring Implantation Errors (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What is the most common error in ring implantation?

**AC:** The most common error is not respecting the correspondence between the 4 landmarks on the annulus and the markers on the prosthetic ring.

Failure to align the commissures and trigones produces an asymmetrical ring orientation, which distorts leaflet coaptation and induces residual regurgitation or systolic anterior motion.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pledgetted Sutures in Ring Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Some surgeons use pledgetted mattress sutures to secure the ring by leaving the pledgets on the atrial side. What is your opinion?

**AC:** It may be a useful technique whenever there is an annular dehiscence or a fragile annulus, but this technique is not necessary in routine cases.

Leaving multiple Teflon pledgets on the atrial aspect creates an unnecessary foreign body burden that may promote platelet aggregation, thrombus formation, or endocarditis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Saline Testing Reliability (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: How reliable is saline testing to assess the efficacy of a valve reconstruction?

**AC:** Saline testing is optimal to recognize occasional residual leaks but also to verify the symmetry, regularity, and posterior position of the coaptation line.

It must be conducted with adequate volume and pressurized injection while venting the root to obtain a true static simulation of left ventricular systole.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Beating Heart Testing vs. Cardioplegia (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Would it be even more reliable to test the valve on a beating heart?

**AC:** This method increases the risk of air embolism.

In addition, it is not necessary in remodeling annuloplasty because static saline testing on an arrested heart with an appropriately sized ring provides excellent predictability of the final operative result.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Repair of Ring Dehiscence (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: How do you repair a ring dehiscence?

**AC:** It depends upon the type and extent of the dehiscence. In the perioperative setting, the dehiscence is usually localized to 1 or 2 sutures and can be easily repaired by placing additional pledgetted sutures.

In late dehiscence, particularly if associated with endocarditis or extensive tissue necrosis, complete ring removal and re-annuloplasty or valve replacement is often necessary.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Annular Dehiscence (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: What about annular dehiscence?

**AC:** Annular dehiscence (atrioventricular groove separation) is a catastrophic complication observed after mitral valve surgery, particularly in patients with severe annular calcification.

It requires deep reconstruction of the atrioventricular junction, often using an autologous or bovine pericardial patch to reconstruct the annulus before securing the prosthetic ring.

</div></details>
