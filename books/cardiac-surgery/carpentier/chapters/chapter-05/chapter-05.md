# 5. Surgical Anatomy and Physiology

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### The Functional Unit of the Mitral Valve
* **Multicomponent System:** The mitral valve apparatus functions as an indivisible integrated unit composed of five anatomically distinct but physiologically coupled structures:
  1. **The Left Atrial Wall and Atrio-Valvular Junction:** Serves as the muscular and fibrous hinge for the leaflets.
  2. **The Mitral Annulus:** A discontinuous, dynamic fibrocollagenous ring providing structural anchoring.
  3. **The Leaflets (Anterior and Posterior):** Flaps partitioned into functional scallops (A1, A2, A3; P1, P2, P3).
  4. **The Chordae Tendineae:** Fibrous cords (marginal, intermediary, and basal) distributing tensile load.
  5. **The Papillary Muscles and Left Ventricular Wall:** Dynamic muscular pillars maintaining subvalvular geometry and ventricular contractility.
* **Surgical Perspective:** Unlike conventional anatomical views based on longitudinal or transverse cardiac sections ([Fig. 5-1](#fig-05-01)), the surgeon visualizes the mitral valve through a left atriotomy from an atrial perspective ([Fig. 5-2](#fig-05-02)), where spatial relationships to surrounding conduction and vascular structures dictate operative safety.

### The Mitral Annulus and Surrounding Hazards
* **Anatomical Nature:** The annulus fibrosus is not a continuous rigid ring ([Fig. 5-3](#fig-05-03)). It exists primarily as a discontinuous fibrous band along the posterior leaflet attachment. At the anterior leaflet, the annulus is absent; the leaflet is continuous with the fibrous aorto-mitral curtain between the left and right fibrous trigones.
* **Dynamic Geometry:** During systole, the normal annulus undergoes a $26 \pm 3\%$ reduction in orifice area and a $13 \pm 3\%$ reduction in circumference ([Table 5-1](#table-05-01)), acting as an active sphincter that deepens into a 3D saddle shape ([Fig. 5-4](#fig-05-04), [Fig. 5-5](#fig-05-05)).
* **Critical Danger Zones ([Fig. 5-6](#fig-05-06)):**
  * *Circumflex Coronary Artery:* Runs within the left atrioventricular groove, closely approaching the annulus ($< 5\text{ mm}$) at the anterolateral commissure and the P1 scallop.
  * *Coronary Sinus:* Courses superiorly along the posterior atrioventricular groove (5 mm above the annulus), vulnerable during deep suture placement in P2–P3.
  * *Bundle of His and AV Node:* Situated in the central fibrous body adjacent to the posteromedial trigone and medial commissure; deeply placed sutures here risk complete heart block.
  * *Aortic Valve Noncoronary & Left Coronary Cusps:* Lie in direct fibrous continuity with the anterior leaflet; deep sutures through the aorto-mitral curtain risk cusp perforation or distortion.

### Leaflet Architecture and Sizing Principles
* **Leaflet Dimensions & Proportions ([Fig. 5-7](#fig-05-07), [Table 5-2](#table-05-02)):**
  * *Insertion Proportions:* The anterior leaflet occupies approximately one third of the annular circumference, whereas the posterior leaflet and commissural tissue occupy the remaining two thirds.
  * *Surface Areas:* Although differing markedly in shape, both leaflets share an equivalent surface area.
* **Anterior Leaflet Morphology ([Fig. 5-8](#fig-05-08)):** Trapezoidal shape with an insertion length of $32 \pm 1.3\text{ mm}$ and midline height of $23 \pm 0.9\text{ mm}$. Zonal architecture consists of a smooth basal translucent zone and a distal coaptation rough zone.
* **Scallop Segmentation ([Fig. 5-9](#fig-05-09)):**
  * *Posterior Leaflet:* Quadrate shape with 3 anatomical scallops separated by clefts/indentations: P1 (anterolateral), P2 (central, largest), and P3 (posteromedial). P2 height averages $14 \pm 0.9\text{ mm}$.
  * *Anterior Leaflet:* Subdivided conceptually into three segments (A1, A2, A3) mirroring the posterior scallops.
* **Commissural Areas & Coaptation Line ([Fig. 5-10](#fig-05-10), [Fig. 5-11](#fig-05-11)):**
  * *Commissural Anatomy:* Distinct 5 to 8 mm segments supported by fan-shaped chordae and dedicated papillary muscle tips.
  * *Coaptation Geometry:* Effective competence requires a broad coaptation height of $8 \pm 1.1\text{ mm}$ between the anterior leaflet and P2, acting as the "keystone of an arch" to relieve chordal tension.

### The Subvalvular Tension Apparatus
* **Papillary Muscles ([Fig. 5-12](#fig-05-12)):**
  * *Anterolateral:* Dual blood supply from LAD and circumflex marginal branches; usually a single stout trunk (Type I morphology) anchored to the anterior ventricular wall.
  * *Posteromedial:* Single, highly vulnerable blood supply (RCA or circumflex terminal); typically multi-headed (Type II morphology) anchored by small muscular digits to the posterior wall.
* **Chordae Tendineae Classification ([Fig. 5-14](#fig-05-14), [Fig. 5-15](#fig-05-15), [Table 5-3](#table-05-03)):**
  * *Marginal (Primary):* Insert on the free margin; prevent leaflet edge prolapse.
  * *Intermediary (Secondary):* Insert at the boundary of the rough zone; bear systolic tension and preserve ventricular geometry (Lillehei concept).
  * *Basal (Tertiary):* Arise from ventricular trabeculae to insert on the posterior leaflet base; anchor the annulus and prevent basal myocardial rupture.

### Surgical Physiology of the Cardiac Cycle
* **Diastole ([Fig. 5-16](#fig-05-16)):** Biphasic opening (early rapid filling followed by atrial contraction kick). Intraventricular asymmetrical vortex formation directs blood along the posterior and apical walls, creating a kinetic cushion that initiates passive leaflet pre-closure.
* **Systole ([Fig. 5-17](#fig-05-17)):** Coordinated annular sphincter contraction ($20\%\text{ to }30\%$ area reduction), saddle-shape deepening, papillary muscle contraction, and smooth leaflet coaptation create a funnel directing the ventricular stroke volume into the aortic vestibule.

## SURGICAL ANATOMY</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgeon's View of the Mitral Valve</summary><div class="details-content">

The anatomy of heart valves is traditionally described with respect to transverse or longitudinal anatomical sections of the heart, which provide optimal viewpoints of the heart’s different structures ([Fig. 5-1](#fig-05-01)). These classic anatomical views have limited practical value to cardiac surgeons because during an operation the mitral valve is observed from within the left atrium.

<span id="fig-05-01"></span>![](_page_27_Figure_5-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-1** Anatomical sections versus the surgeon's operative view of the cardiac valves: classic transverse anatomical section viewed from the base of the heart (left) compared with the surgeon's intraoperative perspective through a left atriotomy (right).

With the left atrium widely exposed through the interatrial groove, three distinct anatomical structures can be observed successively from the atrium toward the ventricle ([Fig. 5-2](#fig-05-02)): the atrio-valvular junction, the leaflets, and the subvalvular apparatus.

<span id="fig-05-02"></span>![](_page_28_Figure_5-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-2** Surgeon's operative view of the mitral valve: exposure through a left atriotomy demonstrating the atrio-valvular junction, the anterior and posterior leaflets, the commissures, and the underlying subvalvular apparatus.

The atrio-valvular junction corresponds to the hinge line of the leaflets. It is visualized as a distinct white line separating the pink, trabeculated atrial myocardium from the smooth, translucent leaflet tissue. The junction is located 1 to 2 mm from the fibrous core of the annulus and represents the primary surgical landmark for suture placement.

### The Mitral Valve Annulus</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Fibrous Architecture and Trigones</summary><div class="details-content">

The annulus fibrosus is not a continuous, rigid fibrous ring ([Fig. 5-3](#fig-05-03)). It is in reality a discontinuous, flexible band of connective tissue that exists primarily along the attachment of the posterior leaflet. Mixed circular and oblique fibrous bundles ensure continuity between the left atrium, the posterior leaflet hinge, and the muscular crest of the left ventricular free wall.

At the attachment of the anterior leaflet, a true fibrous annulus does not exist. The leaflet tissue is in direct continuity with the fibrous aorto-mitral curtain that extends inferiorly from the left and noncoronary cusps of the aortic valve.

<span id="fig-05-03"></span>![](_page_29_Figure_5-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-3** Architecture of the mitral annulus: (a) fibrous, muscular, and fibro-elastic components along the posterior atrioventricular junction; (b) fibrous aorto-mitral curtain extending between the anterolateral and posteromedial fibrous trigones.

The anterior attachment is bounded at each extremity by two dense fibrous structures: the anterolateral fibrous trigone and the posteromedial fibrous trigone. These trigones represent the strongest anchoring structures of the base of the heart and remain stable during the cardiac cycle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Dynamic Geometry and Saddle Shape</summary><div class="details-content">

During cardiac contraction, the mitral annulus undergoes dynamic conformational changes throughout the cardiac cycle ([Fig. 5-4](#fig-05-04)). In diastole, the annulus relaxes into a relatively planar, circular configuration to maximize diastolic inflow. In systole, active annular contraction reduces the orifice area by $26 \pm 3\%$ and circumference by $13 \pm 3\%$ ([Table 5-1](#table-05-01)), adopting a kidney-shaped configuration where the septolateral diameter is significantly smaller than the transverse diameter.

<span id="fig-05-04"></span>![](_page_29_Figure_5-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-4** Variations in annular shape during the cardiac cycle: (a) circular geometry during diastole; (b) kidney-shaped configuration during systole with significant reduction in the anteroposterior diameter.

Instead of having a planar configuration, the annulus exhibits a pronounced three-dimensional saddle-shaped configuration ([Fig. 5-5](#fig-05-05)). The two lowest points are located at the fibrous trigones, and the two highest points are located at the midpoints of the anterior and posterior annuli. The midpoint of the anterior annulus is higher than that of the posterior annulus, creating an angle of $120^\circ$ between the aortic and mitral valve planes. This dynamic 3D architecture markedly decreases mechanical stress on the leaflet tissue and chordae tendineae during peak systole.

### Surrounding Structures and Surgical Hazards</summary><div class="details-content">

Four vital anatomical structures surround the mitral annulus and represent significant operative hazards during annular dissection and prosthetic ring placement ([Fig. 5-6](#fig-05-06)):

1. **The Circumflex Coronary Artery:** Traverses the left atrioventricular groove. It runs within 3 to 4 mm of the annulus adjacent to the anterolateral commissure and the anterior portion of the posterior leaflet (P1 scallop). Deep suture placement in this sector can entrap, kink, or lacerate the artery, causing acute lateral myocardial infarction.
2. **The Coronary Sinus:** Runs along the epicardial aspect of the posterior atrioventricular groove approximately 5 mm superior to the annulus. Deeply placed sutures in the P2 and P3 sectors risk tearing the sinus, leading to severe concealed mediastinal hemorrhage.
3. **The Bundle of His:** Penetrates the central fibrous body adjacent to the posteromedial fibrous trigone and medial commissure. Sutures placed too deeply through the medial fibrous trigone or posterior commissural tissue risk injuring the conduction system and producing complete heart block.
4. **The Aortic Valve:** The noncoronary and left coronary aortic cusps are separated from the anterior mitral leaflet only by the thin aorto-mitral curtain. Suture bites placed deeply along the anterior annulus can pierce or distort the aortic cusps, creating acute postoperative aortic insufficiency. Although slight anatomical variations exist, the nadir of these cusps is 6 to 10 mm away from the anterior mitral annulus, which represents a safe zone provided the needle is properly oriented toward the ventricle.

Top inset: anatomical variations in the relationship of the aortic valve cusps to the anterior mitral annulus.

### The Leaflets</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Leaflet Dimensions and Annular Insertion Proportions</summary><div class="details-content">

The mitral valve comprises two distinct leaflets—anterior and posterior—separated by two commissures. These leaflets constitute the opening and closing units of the valve. Optimal closure requires precise fitting between the leaflet surface area and the mitral orifice area.

Although the anterior and posterior leaflets have different sizes and shapes ([Table 5-2](#table-05-02)), with the anterior leaflet more extended vertically and the posterior leaflet more extended transversally, they have a similar surface area.

The basal insertion of the anterior leaflet occupies approximately one third of the circumference of the mitral valve ([Fig. 5-7](#fig-05-07)). The remaining two thirds of the circumference attaches to the posterior leaflet and the commissural tissue. The anterior leaflet is primarily related to the left ventricular outflow tract via the aorto-mitral curtain, whereas the posterior leaflet is related to the muscular parietal base of the left ventricle.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Anterior Leaflet Morphology and Zonal Architecture</summary><div class="details-content">

The anterior leaflet, also called the aortic leaflet, has a characteristic trapezoidal shape ([Fig. 5-8](#fig-05-08)). Its base, which measures $32 \pm 1.3\text{ mm}$, is inserted on the aorto-mitral curtain and the adjacent fibrous trigones. The free edge presents with a slightly convex curvature. At the midline, the height of the anterior leaflet is $23 \pm 0.9\text{ mm}$.

From base to free margin, the anterior leaflet presents two distinct morphological zones: the atrial zone is smooth, pliable, and translucent, whereas the distal coaptation zone is thicker, rough, and corrugated, receiving the chordal insertions that support coaptation.

<span id="fig-05-08"></span>![](_page_32_Figure_5-8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-8** Morphology and zonal architecture of the anterior leaflet: smooth translucent atrial zone and thicker coaptation zone receiving chordal support; inset illustrates diastolic inflow and systolic outflow orientation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Posterior Leaflet Scallop Segmentation (Carpentier's Classification)</summary><div class="details-content">

The posterior leaflet is inserted on approximately two thirds of the annulus, along the crest of the left ventricular muscular wall. As a result of this configuration, maximum stress during systole is concentrated at the midline of the posterior leaflet.

The indentations of the posterior leaflet are normal anatomical structures that divide the leaflet into three scallops, designated P1 (anterolateral), P2 (middle), and P3 (posteromedial), to facilitate valve analysis ([Fig. 5-9](#fig-05-09)). By convention, the corresponding areas of the anterior leaflet are designated A1, A2, and A3, and the commissures are designated AC (anterolateral) and PC (posteromedial). P2 is normally the largest and highest scallop ($14 \pm 0.9\text{ mm}$).

<span id="fig-05-09"></span>![](_page_33_Figure_5-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-9** Carpentier's segmental classification of the mitral leaflets: posterior leaflet scallops P1 (anterolateral), P2 (middle), and P3 (posteromedial); corresponding anterior leaflet functional segments A1, A2, and A3; and commissures AC and PC.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Commissures and Line of Coaptation</summary><div class="details-content">

The commissures are functional entities consisting of two distinct structures: the commissural leaflet and the commissural chordae ([Fig. 5-10](#fig-05-10)). The commissural leaflet is a small, triangular segment of leaflet tissue attached to the annulus, with its free edge supported by one or two characteristic fan-like chordae.

<span id="fig-05-10"></span>![](_page_33_Figure_5-10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-10** Commissural anatomy: identification of the anterolateral and posteromedial commissures; inset demonstrates nerve hook retraction displaying the triangular commissural leaflet supported by characteristic fan-like chordae.

A commissure is a functional Y-shaped entity that provides continuous tissue transition between the anterior and posterior leaflets and allows them to coapt securely at their junction. During systole, the leaflets coapt along an extensive surface ($8 \pm 1.1\text{ mm}$ height) acting as the keystone of an arch. The line of coaptation lies parallel to the posterior annulus, approximately 15 mm from the anterior atrioventricular junction and 5 to 6 mm from the posterior annulus ([Fig. 5-11](#fig-05-11)). The line of coaptation is far from the left ventricular outflow tract and separates the orifice into anterior (75%) and posterior (25%) areas.

<span id="fig-05-11"></span>![](_page_34_Figure_5-11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-11** Morphological boundaries and coaptation relations of the leaflets: unfolded view displaying the anterior leaflet, commissural leaflet, and posterior leaflet with their respective atrial and coaptation zones.

</div></details>

### The Subvalvular Apparatus</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Papillary Muscles and Vascularization</summary><div class="details-content">

The papillary muscles insert on the inner muscular wall of the left ventricle and project into the ventricular cavity beneath the commissures ([Fig. 5-12](#fig-05-12)). They are arranged into two constant anatomical groups: the anterolateral and posteromedial papillary muscles.

Several distinct morphological types of papillary muscles are observed: Type I (bulky with a single head), Type II (bulky with multiple heads), Type III (slender with few chordae), Type IV (arch-shaped with dual muscular bases), and Type V (multiple small muscular trabeculae).

<span id="fig-05-12"></span>![](_page_35_Figure_5-12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-12** Morphological types of papillary muscles: (a) spatial orientation of the anterolateral and posteromedial papillary muscles; (b) anatomical variations: Type I (single head), Type II (multiple heads), Type III (slender), Type IV (arch-shaped); (c) apical and lateral wall implantation.

The vascularization of the papillary muscles is markedly asymmetric ([Fig. 5-13](#fig-05-13)). The anterolateral papillary muscle receives a robust, dual arterial supply from the left anterior descending (LAD) and circumflex marginal arteries.

Conversely, the posteromedial papillary muscle receives a single, terminal blood supply from either the right coronary artery (RCA) or the distal circumflex artery. This precarious solitary perfusion renders the posteromedial papillary muscle exceptionally vulnerable to acute ischemia, rupture, and fibrotic elongation.

<span id="fig-05-13"></span>![](_page_36_Figure_5-13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-13** Papillary muscle arterial vascularization: dual blood supply to the anterolateral papillary muscle (LAD + circumflex) versus single terminal supply to the posteromedial papillary muscle (RCA or circumflex).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Chordae Tendineae Classification</summary><div class="details-content">

The chordae tendineae extend from the fibrous tips of the papillary muscles to insert into the ventricular surfaces and free margins of the leaflets ([Fig. 5-14](#fig-05-14)). Three functional categories of chordae are distinguished based on their site of leaflet insertion:

<span id="fig-05-14"></span>![](_page_36_Figure_5-14.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-14** Three categories of chordae tendineae: (1) marginal (primary) chordae; (2) intermediary (secondary) chordae; (3) basal (tertiary) chordae.

* **Marginal (Primary) Chordae:** Insert directly on the free margin of the leaflets. Their primary mechanical function is to prevent marginal leaflet eversion and prolapse into the left atrium during systole.
* **Intermediary (Secondary) Chordae:** Insert at the junction between the clear zone and the rough zone. These thick, robust cords bear the bulk of systolic closing pressure and maintain optimal ventricular geometry.
* **Basal (Tertiary) Chordae:** Arise directly from ventricular trabeculae or the papillary muscle base to insert into the basal zone of the posterior leaflet. They anchor the annulus and prevent basal myocardial rupture ([Fig. 5-15](#fig-05-15)).

## SURGICAL PHYSIOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diastolic Phase and Fluid Dynamics</summary><div class="details-content">

During ventricular diastole, the mitral valve opens through a coordinated sequence driven by left ventricular relaxation, elastic chordal recoil, and transmitral pressure gradients ([Fig. 5-16](#fig-05-16)).

In early diastole, rapid ventricular untwisting and expansion generate an active suction effect, prompting rapid, wide separation of the leaflets. High-velocity inflow enters the left ventricle, separating into asymmetrical fluid streams.

<span id="fig-05-16"></span>![](_page_38_Figure_5-16.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-16** Diastolic transmitral fluid dynamics: (a) early rapid inflow and leaflet opening; (b) mid-diastolic vortex formation behind the anterior leaflet; (c) late diastolic atrial contraction kick.

As inflow encounters the apical myocardium, it curves along the posterior and lateral walls, generating an organized, energetic intraventricular vortex. This vortex circulates behind the anterior leaflet, pushing it gently toward the midline. This hydrodynamic cushioning pre-positions the leaflets in semi-apposition prior to the onset of ventricular systole.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Systolic Phase and Coaptation Biomechanics</summary><div class="details-content">

Ventricular systole initiates with isovolumetric contraction, driving immediate, secure coaptation along the rough zones of both leaflets ([Fig. 5-17](#fig-05-17)).

Simultaneously, active annular sphincter contraction reduces the orifice area by $20\%\text{ to }30\%$, while apical traction exerted by the contracting papillary muscles counters systolic intraventricular pressure and prevents leaflet billowing.

<span id="fig-05-17"></span>![](_page_38_Figure_5-17.jpeg)

**FIGURE 5-17** Systolic leaflet coaptation and ventricular ejection: stable rough-zone coaptation, annular reduction, and redirection of blood flow into the aortic vestibule.

The anterior leaflet serves a vital dual aerodynamic role: while its rough zone provides the coaptation barrier preventing regurgitation, its smooth basal body forms the posterior boundary of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), smoothly channeling the ejected stroke volume into the aortic root without turbulence.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Mitral Annulus: Concept, Contraction, and Saddle Geometry</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: What is the significance of the annulus in the context of the “atrio-valvular” junction?

**AC:** They are actually two different anatomical structures. One of them, the atrio-valvular junction, is visible. The other one, the annulus, is not.

I like to use the metaphor of a door: the leaflet is the door, the annulus is the invisible frame supporting the door, and the atrio-valvular junction is the hinge allowing the door to move. The precise anatomical relationship between the visible atrio-valvular junction and the nonvisible annulus allows the surgeon to identify the annulus, the structure through which she or he should pass the sutures used to secure the prosthetic ring.

#### FF: Is the mitral valve annulus real or virtual?

**AC:** An anatomist would probably answer this question by saying that the annulus does not exist. It is actually a junction between three anatomical structures: the atrium, the leaflet hinge, and the crest of the ventricle. This junction is filled with more or less dense connective tissue.

For the surgeon, the “reality” of the annulus is demonstrated by its capacity to hold sutures provided that the sutures are well positioned.

#### DA: What is the role of annular contraction in mitral valve function?

**AC:** Annular contraction is a consequence of ventricular contraction. Its role is to facilitate the competency of the mitral valve by reducing the orifice area by 20% to 30%, which in turn increases the surface area of leaflet coaptation.

For this reason, I gave a systolic configuration to the remodeling annuloplasty rings.

#### DA: How important is the saddle shape of the mitral annulus during cardiac contraction?

**AC:** The saddle shape results from complex morphological changes of the mitral valve components during the cardiac cycle and especially the bulging of the aorto-mitral curtain combined with the downward displacement of the trigones during systole.

The angle formed by the anterior part and the posterior part of the annulus is reduced during systole and contributes to the reduction of the orifice area, at least in normal hearts. In pathological hearts this angle has the tendency to flatten.

#### DA: Does a remodeling ring affect the saddle shape and function?

**AC:** Remodeling rings by design respect, instead of affect, the saddle shape. The original “Classic ring” was left open at the middle of its aortic segment and made flexible enough to conform to the bulging of the aorto-mitral curtain by a slight bending of the two extremities of the ring before lowering the ring into position.

The “Physio ring” and the “Ischemic ring” have a preestablished saddle shape configuration.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Leaflet Architecture, Coaptation, and Vascularization</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Is the shape of the anterior leaflet similar in different patients or are there significant variations, which should be taken into consideration during valve reconstruction?

**AC:** The shape of the anterior leaflet is quite consistent with little variations in the relationship between its height and its length of insertion. For this reason, the dimensions and the surface area of the anterior leaflet are used for ring selection.

Variations are possible, however, as a result of pathological processes. Reduction in height is seen in rheumatic valvular disease whereas increased height is observed in some degenerative valve disease or in obstructive cardiomyopathy. These variations have little practical consequences since sizing is based on actual measurements of the anterior leaflet in each individual patient.

#### FF: Can you elaborate further on the importance of the surface of coaptation?

**AC:** The surface of coaptation is important not only for determining the competency of the valve but also for reducing the stress on the chordae tendineae.

It plays the role of the keystone of an arch. The larger the keystone, the stronger the arch.

#### DA: Are the leaflets vascularized? Are there any drawbacks to performing temporary detachment?

**AC:** The base of the anterior leaflet presents a subendocardial network of tiny vessels arising from the branches of the coronary arteries.

This minute vascularization most probably does not play a significant role in the biophysiology of the mitral valve since I have never seen leaflet necrosis after leaflet detachment. No arterial network has been described at the level of the posterior leaflet.

#### FF: What is the role of the indentations separating the three scallops of the posterior leaflet?

**AC:** The indentations facilitate the opening motion of the posterior leaflet. In geometrical terms, the posterior leaflet looks like a cone, which would not easily fold without the presence of these indentations, much like a curtain pleat.

### Nomenclature: Diameters and Chordal Classification</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Why do you prefer the term “transverse diameter” instead of the term “commissure-to-commissure diameter,” which is often used in the medical literature?

**AC:** I prefer transverse first because it is a simpler and more straightforward expression and second because commissure-to-commissure better defines the intercommissural distance and not the largest diameter of the orifice.

#### DA: Two terminologies are also used to define the type of chordae attachment on the leaflets: basal, intermediary, and marginal, on the one hand, and primary, secondary, and tertiary, on the other hand. Why do you prefer the first terminology?

**AC:** Because I prefer the terms that speak for themselves. The terms basal or marginal immediately suggest a position at the base or at the margin of the leaflet. The terms primary or tertiary do not suggest anything.

The term “secondary chordae” is often used by others, and also by myself, to define intermediary chordae, particularly in the operative setting. Thus one can use equally the term “secondary chordae transposition” or “intermediary chordae transposition.”

### The Subvalvular Apparatus and Left Ventricular Function</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What is the role of the secondary chordae in left ventricular function?

**AC:** Walton Lillehei first postulated that not only secondary chordae but also the whole subvalvular apparatus could play an important role in ventricular function. This hypothesis was verified later by numerous experimental and clinical research studies.

Recent experimental studies by Messas and colleagues did show that cutting some of the chordae to enhance leaflet motion in ischemic valve disease had no deleterious effect on myocardial contractility. Today, it is routine practice to preserve some of the subvalvular apparatus in valve replacement in order to preserve ventricular-annular continuity and thus ventricular contractility. This also reduces the risk of midventricular rupture.

The important contribution of the subvalvular apparatus to ventricular function is best demonstrated by the superior results of reconstructive valve surgery.

#### FF: What is the role of the basal chordae?

**AC:** Basal chordae are important to preserve a normal relationship between the annulus and the papillary muscles and to reduce the stress on the annulus during systole.

They are also important for reducing the risk of myocardial rupture after valve replacement. This is the reason why they should be carefully preserved.

### Papillary Muscle Mechanics, Perfusion, and Morphology</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Can you elaborate on the vascularization of the papillary muscles?

**AC:** The vascularization is unequally developed in the two papillary muscles and this has important practical consequences. The posterior papillary muscle is usually vascularized by a single vascular network, which depends upon either the right coronary artery or the circumflex terminal branches. The anterior papillary muscle is vascularized by two rich networks, which arise from the marginal and the anterior descending arteries.

In brief, the vascularization of the posterior papillary muscle is less favorable than that of the anterior papillary muscle. Another vulnerability of the posterior papillary muscle is due to its attachment to the ventricular wall by multiple and often small muscular digits as opposed to the single and stronger attachment of the anterior papillary muscle to the junction between the septum and the anterior myocardial wall. These anatomical considerations explain why the posterior papillary muscle is more frequently and severely involved in myocardial ischemia.

#### FF: You have described several techniques involving the papillary muscles. Could you clarify how papillary muscle mobilization or suturing increases the risk of necrosis?

**AC:** One must take into consideration the type of papillary muscle and the mode of vascularization. The risk of ischemia or necrosis following papillary muscle mobilization is negligible in type I, II, and III papillary muscles.

The risk of ischemia or necrosis is more pronounced in type IV and V papillary muscles because the vascularization is assumed through the trabeculations extending from the muscular wall to the body of the papillary muscle. As a consequence, it is preferable to limit the mobilization of type IV and V papillary muscles to one third of their height.

Following papillary muscle shortening, the risk of ischemia or necrosis is negligible provided that one respects the central artery and works near the tip.

#### DA: How do you explain the difference in morphology between the anterior and posterior leaflets?

**AC:** The posterior leaflet has only one function: to participate in the systolic closure of the mitral orifice.

The anterior leaflet has two functions: one is also to participate in the closure of the mitral valve orifice, just like the posterior leaflet; however, the other is to direct the ventricular flow toward the aorta during systole.
