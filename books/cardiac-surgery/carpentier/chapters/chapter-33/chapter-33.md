# Chapter 33. Traumatic Valvular Injury

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Traumatic valvular injuries are rare but life-threatening lesions requiring prompt systematic recognition and repair:
1. **Etiological Mechanisms:** Valvular trauma encompasses penetrating injuries (stab/gunshot wounds producing direct laceration of leaflets, chordae, or papillary muscles), blunt deceleration trauma (motor vehicle collisions or falls producing acute hydraulic pressure spikes with leaflet rupture or annular avulsion), and iatrogenic trauma (complications of percutaneous balloon mitral dilatation, endomyocardial biopsy, or misplaced annuloplasty sutures).
2. **Topographical Distribution:** Blunt deceleration trauma most commonly tears the aortic valve (predominantly the non-coronary cusp), followed by chordal or papillary muscle rupture in the mitral and tricuspid valves.
3. **Reconstructive Philosophy:** In acute traumatic regurgitation, the underlying valvular tissue is typically healthy and pliable (except in rheumatic valves undergoing balloon dilatation). Valve reconstructive surgery is the primary treatment of choice, following the functional approach and the "one lesion one technique" principle, providing excellent long-term durability and avoiding anticoagulation in young patients.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiographic Profiling:** Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography are mandatory in all major trauma cases. Echocardiography precisely delineates the mechanism of dysfunction (Type II prolapse from chordal/papillary muscle rupture vs. leaflet tear or perforation), quantifies regurgitant severity, and identifies occult injuries.
* **Associated Traumatic Injuries:** The presence of valvular injury mandates a comprehensive trauma survey to identify associated myocardial contusion, hemopericardium, acute ventricular septal defect, aortic isthmus rupture, tracheobronchial tears, or pulmonary contusions.
* **Iatrogenic Post-Dilatation Patterns:** Following percutaneous balloon mitral dilatation, severe mitral regurgitation occurs in up to 10% of cases due to paracommissural tears (most frequent in posterior commissure), leaflet lacerations (A2 or P2), chordal rupture, or papillary muscle avulsion ([Table 33-1](#table-33-01)), often accompanied by an iatrogenic atrial septal defect.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Indications & Timing:** Emergent intervention is indicated for refractory acute pulmonary edema or cardiogenic shock. In hemodynamically stable patients with moderate-to-severe regurgitation after failed balloon dilatation, early surgery is strongly advocated before secondary fibrous changes compromise reconstructive feasibility.
* **Mitral Valve Repair Techniques:**
  * *Leaflet Tears:* Anterior leaflet lacerations are closed with an autologous glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial patch to maintain surface area and mobility; posterior leaflet tears are repaired by direct suture, quadrangular resection, or patch extension.
  * *Paracommissural Tears:* Repaired by direct primary suture approximation ([Fig. 33-1](#fig-33-01)).
  * *Subvalvular Disruption:* Papillary muscle rupture is addressed by primary reimplantation into a healthy muscular base; chordal ruptures are managed by secondary chordal transposition or artificial PTFE neo-chordae ([Fig. 33-2](#fig-33-02)).
  * *Adjunct Procedures:* Repair of iatrogenic atrial septal defects, standard commissurotomy for residual rheumatic stenosis, and remodeling annuloplasty sized to the reconstructed leaflet area.
* **Tricuspid Valve Repair Techniques:**
  * Blunt trauma and post-transplant endomyocardial biopsy injuries are treated by leaflet resection, patch augmentation, chordal replacement, and papillary muscle reimplantation ([Fig. 33-3](#fig-33-03), [Fig. 33-4](#fig-33-04)).
* **Aortic Valve Repair & Iatrogenic Entrapment:**
  * Traumatic tears are repaired with tailored autologous pericardial patches.
  * If acute aortic regurgitation occurs following mitral or tricuspid annuloplasty, immediate re-exploration is mandatory to remove the aberrantly placed suture catching the aortic cusp (usually non-coronary), with patch repair of any needle perforation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Delayed Diagnosis:** Blunt deceleration injuries may initially be hemodynamically compensated; high index of suspicion and routine echocardiography prevent catastrophic delayed heart failure.
* **Missed Iatrogenic ASD:** Balloon catheter septostomy can leave a large left-to-right interatrial shunt that must be systematically inspected and closed during post-dilatation repair.
* **Direct Suture of Fragile Aortic Perforations:** Small needle punctures in the aortic cusps from annuloplasty sutures should never be repaired under tension with direct sutures (which causes cusp retraction); patch closure is mandatory.
* **Annular Distortion:** Overzealous ring downsizing in mitral repairs can distort the adjacent aortic root, inducing secondary aortic insufficiency.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## PATHOLOGY AND CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Although rare, various types of trauma can be responsible for valvular lesions that may not be recognized unless a systematic search is conducted after major penetrating or blunt trauma or following accidents. Traumatic valve injury can also be manifested as iatrogenic lesions resulting from surgery or interventional cardiology.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Penetrating and Blunt Cardiac Trauma</summary><div class="details-content">

Traumatic injury to the cardiac valves occurs via two primary physical mechanisms:
* **Penetrating Cardiac Trauma:** Penetrating cardiac trauma can affect the right ventricle, the left ventricle, and the great vessels. Laceration of the cardiac valves with acute valvular regurgitation attributable to leaflet tear or chordae or papillary muscle rupture may also occur.
* **Nonpenetrating or Blunt Trauma:** Nonpenetrating or blunt trauma often occurs secondary to falls or motor vehicle accidents with deceleration injuries. Valve injury can occur in the aortic, mitral, or tricuspid position. In the aortic position, the most common lesion is aortic leaflet tear or detachment from the annulus, particularly the non-coronary leaflet. In the mitral and tricuspid positions, leaflet tear or leaflet prolapse secondary to papillary muscle or chordae rupture can be observed.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Hemodynamic Impact and Associated Traumatic Lesions</summary><div class="details-content">

In most instances, patients present with acute severe valvular regurgitation and hemodynamic deterioration requiring early diagnosis and surgical treatment.

Occasionally following blunt trauma, valvular regurgitation can be well tolerated initially and the diagnosis may be delayed. For this reason, the work-up should be comprehensive in the setting of trauma. Associated cardiac lesions should be carefully investigated (such as myocardial contusion, hemopericardium, or ventricular septal rupture) as well as thoracic injuries (such as lung injury), aortic isthmus rupture, or extrathoracic injuries.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## TRAUMATIC MITRAL REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Blunt Trauma Mechanisms and Reconstructive Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Traumatic mitral valve regurgitation can result from chordae rupture, leaflet tear or disruption, and papillary muscle rupture.

Reconstructive techniques are particularly indicated for traumatic mitral valve lesions in young patients because of the feasibility of valve reconstruction in the majority of cases. Excellent long-term results from reconstruction can be expected if the valvular tissue is otherwise normal. The techniques described in Section II can be used, guided by the functional approach and the "one lesion one technique" principle.

Chordae rupture and/or leaflet tear are the most frequent mechanisms of regurgitation. They can be corrected by using the techniques described in Chapters 10 and 11 of Section II. In the case of papillary muscle rupture, papillary muscle reimplantation is a viable option in selected patients.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Iatrogenic Injury Following Percutaneous Mitral Balloon Dilatation</summary><div class="details-content">

Percutaneous mitral dilatation is commonly used for the treatment of pure mitral stenosis without significant valvular calcification. The procedure can, however, be complicated by hemopericardium, left to right shunts, and the creation of a new mitral regurgitation.

Mitral valve regurgitation can be caused by several types of lesions ([Table 33-1](#table-33-01)). They include leaflet tear, paracommissural tear ([Fig. 33-1](#fig-33-01)), and papillary muscle or chordae rupture ([Fig. 33-2](#fig-33-02)). A combination of several types of lesions is a common finding. Transthoracic echocardiography allows classification and localization of the dysfunction, and may also identify lesions such as papillary muscle rupture.

### Surgical Techniques for Post-Dilatation Mitral Repair</summary><div class="details-content">

The surgical management of post-dilatation mitral regurgitation involves systematic timing and targeted repair:
* **Clinical Presentation & Urgency:** The immediate clinical presentation of this secondary mitral regurgitation is variable. Intractable pulmonary edema requiring emergent surgical correction may occur in some patients whereas in others the hemodynamic consequences are less dramatic and a conservative medical treatment can initially be established.
* **Surgical Timing & Feasibility:** Patients with moderate or severe mitral regurgitation should undergo valvular surgery soon after the percutaneous procedure since prolonged delay may compromise the likelihood of valve reconstruction. The feasibility of mitral valve reconstruction depends primarily on the extent of valvular lesions and the severity of calcifications. In our initial experience, valve reconstruction could be performed in more than 50% of the cases.
* **Repair of Traumatic Tears:** A careful analysis of the lesion produced by the trauma must be conducted. It may be useful to resect the edge of the lesions and to take advantage of a tear to resect secondary chordae. Anterior leaflet tears should be closed with an autologous pericardial patch in order to preserve optimal leaflet surface area and mobility. Posterior leaflet tears can be reconstructed by direct suturing, or by leaflet resection and reconstruction with annular plication or leaflet extension. A paracommissural tear should be repaired with direct suture.
* **Associated Lesions & Septal Repair:** Primary valve lesions are also addressed by the usual reconstructive techniques, such as standard commissurotomy, leaflet mobilization, prolapse correction, and remodeling annuloplasty. A septal inspection should also be performed to recognize a septal perforation commonly observed following percutaneous mitral dilation. Primary repair of this defect should be performed during the same procedure.

</div></details>

## TRAUMATIC TRICUSPID REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mechanisms: Blunt Chest Impact and Post-Transplant Biopsy Injury</summary><div class="details-content">

Traumatic tricuspid regurgitation attributable to chordae rupture, leaflet tear, or avulsion may be seen after blunt chest trauma ([Fig. 33-3](#fig-33-03), [Fig. 33-4](#fig-33-04)).

Following myocardial biopsy in cardiac transplant patients, tricuspid regurgitation is a well-known complication. Lesions are either leaflet laceration, chordae rupture, or partial papillary muscle rupture.

Whenever a severe tricuspid regurgitation makes a valve operation necessary, a valve reconstruction can frequently be performed. Leaflet resection with or without patching and annular plication are the most frequently used techniques. Papillary muscle reimplantation can also be done with good long-term results.

## TRAUMATIC AORTIC VALVE REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Blunt Deceleration Tears and Iatrogenic Surgical Entrapment</summary><div class="details-content">

In the aortic position, a leaflet tear or detachment can often be repaired as described in Chapter 22. It usually requires the use of a glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch tailored according to the geometry of the preserved leaflets.

If aortic valve regurgitation is noted after mitral or tricuspid valve surgery, immediate exploration of the aortic valve is mandatory. Usually, a suture has caught the base or belly of the non-coronary leaflet. The suture responsible for the lesion should be removed. Another annuloplasty suture is placed through the annulus and the ring.

Whenever a small perforation of the leaflet tissue is found, it should be treated by pericardial patch extension, not direct suture. In the setting of tricuspid surgery, an aberrantly placed suture can injure the right coronary sinus and the non-coronary sinus as well as the adjacent leaflets. The suture should be removed, the ring resecured, and the leaflets repaired.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Repair vs. Replacement in Post-Dilatation Rheumatic Mitral Valve (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** In the case of mitral valve regurgitation following percutaneous balloon dilatation in a 50-year-old patient with rheumatic valve disease, are you inclined to reconstruct the valve or to replace it because of the complexity of the lesions?

**AC:** It depends upon whether these lesions are easily repairable. The main considerations are the amount of valvular tissue and its flexibility. I always attempt to repair the valve as the primary option. I replace the valve only if the result does not appear satisfactory. Valve replacement is also preferred in cases of advanced rheumatic valve lesions in which the durability of the repair can be predicted to be less than the durability of a bioprosthesis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Impact of Anticoagulant Therapy (Coumadin) on Surgical Decision (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** The patient I mentioned in the preceding question is already taking Coumadin. How does this impact your decision of valve repair or replacement?

**AC:** Coumadin does not impact my decision to proceed to a valve reconstruction because an occasional atrial fibrillation can be reversible. It is in fact the severity of the valve lesions, the duration of the atrial fibrillation, and the likelihood of a durable result that are the primary considerations.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Interdisciplinary Dialogue Prior to Percutaneous Dilatation (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Do your cardiologists discuss cases with surgeons before attempting to dilate a mitral valve stenosis?

**AC:** The answer is no, mainly because the surgeon is not better positioned than the cardiologist to predict failure after valvular dilatation. Except in cases of visible calcifications, it is difficult to predict an iatrogenic complication, particularly paracommissural tears. Our cardiologists now have extensive experience, and we see very few complications at the present time as opposed to the past.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Timing Following Unsuccessful Percutaneous Dilatation (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What is your recommendation for the timing of surgery after a failing percutaneous procedure?

**AC:** Many cardiologists have the tendency to accept a 2+ or greater residual regurgitation and to refer the patient to the surgeon as late as possible. However, then the likelihood of repairing the valve is reduced because secondary lesions have occurred. After a suboptimal result of a percutaneous procedure, the patient should be sent to surgery if the initial lesions, analyzed by echocardiography, seem favorable to valve reconstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Post-Repair Aortic Regurgitation from Annular Distortion (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Following mitral valve repair, aortic valve regurgitation is occasionally found in the absence of visible aortic leaflet injury. What is the mechanism for this complication?

**AC:** It should be said that this situation is extremely rare in the absence of leaflet injury. The regurgitation in this case may be due to some distortion of the aortic root by the annuloplasty, particularly if the implanted ring is too small. This regurgitation is typically minimal and does not require further intervention. If the regurgitation is significant, it is preferable to proceed to a second look and eventually change or reposition the ring.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Reconstructive Feasibility in Nonpenetrating Valvular Trauma (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In your experience, what is the likelihood of a valvular reconstruction in patients with nonpenetrating leaflet injury?

**AC:** The majority of valves in this circumstance can be repaired with very good long-term results. Severe lesions of the subvalvular structures, in particular the papillary muscles, are more difficult to repair and may require a valve replacement. As far as the aortic valve is concerned, a valve replacement using a bioprosthesis may be a more reasonable option in elderly patients because of the fragility of the valvular tissues.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Concomitant Annuloplasty in Acute Trauma (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** What are the indications for an adjunct annuloplasty procedure following reconstruction in acute mitral or tricuspid valve injuries?

**AC:** I see only two circumstances: complex repairs involving the papillary muscles and cases in which the reconstruction has significantly reduced the normal leaflet surface area. In this latter scenario, the annuloplasty ring should be sized according to the residual leaflet surface area.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Biopsy-Induced Tricuspid Regurgitation in Cardiac Allografts (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In the presence of biopsy-induced severe tricuspid regurgitation in a patient with multiple episodes of allograft rejection undergoing frequent myocardial biopsies, would you recommend a primary valve replacement rather than a valve reconstruction?

**AC:** Not necessarily because future biopsies may have also a deleterious effect on either the native valve or the bioprosthetic valve. The indications for valve replacement should only be based on the severity of valvular lesions.

</div></details>
