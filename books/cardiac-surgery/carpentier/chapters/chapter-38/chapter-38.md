# Chapter 38. Medication-Induced Valvulopathy

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Medication-induced valvulopathy represents a distinct clinical and pathological entity characterized by proliferative, non-destructive fibrotic encasement of heart valves:
1. **Causative Agents:** In the 1990's, extensive clinical reports established an association between appetite-suppressant medications and valvular heart disease, predominantly causing aortic and mitral regurgitation. Implicated agents included fenfluramine and its D-isomer dexfenfluramine, prescribed either as monotherapy or in synergistic combination with phentermine ("fen-phen"). Ergot alkaloid derivatives (ergotamine therapy for migraine, as well as pergolide and cabergoline) produce identical valvular lesions.
2. **Serotonergic Vasoconstriction & Pulmonary Hypertension:** In addition to valvular lesions, secondary pulmonary hypertension is a common and severe finding in these patients. Serotonin-induced pulmonary vasoconstriction plays a primary role in the pathogenesis of this hypertensive pulmonary vascular state.
3. **Carpentier Type IIIa Dysfunction:** Echocardiography characteristically demonstrates Type IIIa valvular dysfunction with leaflet thickening, stiffening, and retraction, accompanied by chordal thickening and shortening in both the mitral and tricuspid positions.
4. **Carcinoid-Like Histopathology:** The microscopic and gross features closely mirror carcinoid heart disease. The underlying leaflet architecture remains intact, encased by an overlying proliferative plaque composed of myofibroblasts and an abundant acid-mucopolysaccharide-rich extracellular matrix.
5. **Stabilization After Drug Withdrawal:** Unlike progressive inflammatory, carcinoid, or radiation valvulopathies, cessation of the offending medication frequently leads to stabilization or partial regression of valvular lesions, significantly improving the biological feasibility of reconstructive surgery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiographic Evaluation:**
  * *Type IIIa Dysfunction:* Diffuse, symmetrical leaflet thickening and retraction with restricted systolic and diastolic excursion.
  * *Subvalvular Apparatus:* Thickening, shortening, and fusion of chordae tendineae and papillary muscles in mitral and tricuspid positions.
  * *Hemodynamics:* Multi-valve regurgitation (predominantly aortic and mitral, occasionally tricuspid) combined with varying degrees of valvular stenosis.
* **Pulmonary Hemodynamic Assessment:**
  * Doppler echocardiographic and invasive catheterization measurement of pulmonary arterial pressures to quantify the degree of serotonin-induced pulmonary hypertension.
* **Histopathological Characteristics:**
  * Intact trilaminar architecture of native leaflets without destructive elastolysis or necrosis.
  * Superimposed plaque formation containing proliferative myofibroblasts embedded in a rich collagenous and glycosaminoglycan extracellular matrix.
* **Clinical History:**
  * Documented history of current or remote exposure to anorexigens (fenfluramine, dexfenfluramine, phentermine) or ergot alkaloids.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Medical Discontinuation & Surveillance:**
  * Immediate and permanent cessation of the causative pharmacological agent.
  * Mild-to-moderate regurgitation should be managed conservatively with serial echocardiographic surveillance, as lesions frequently stabilize following drug withdrawal.
* **Indications for Surgical Intervention:**
  * Severe symptomatic regurgitation, progressive ventricular enlargement, or severe secondary pulmonary hypertension.
* **Aortic Valve Position:**
  * Valvular replacement is mandatory due to transmural cusp thickening, retraction, and loss of coaptation reserve.
* **Mitral and Tricuspid Positions:**
  * Valve reconstruction can be successfully considered in favorable cases with stabilized disease following drug cessation.
  * *Reconstructive Techniques:* Leaflet patch extension using glutaraldehyde-preserved autologous pericardium combined with a prosthetic remodeling annuloplasty ring restores leaflet mobility and provides an adequate coaptation surface (see Chapter 19).
* **Prognostic Advantage:**
  * Because the pathological stimulus ceases with drug withdrawal, long-term reconstructive stability may be superior to that observed in ongoing carcinoid or post-radiation disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Severe Pulmonary Hypertension:** Serotonin-induced pulmonary vasoconstriction can trigger acute right ventricular failure upon separation from cardiopulmonary bypass.
* **Premature Valve Replacement:** Assuming that all drug-induced lesions require replacement; favorable cases with stabilized pathology after drug withdrawal are excellent candidates for valve repair.
* **Continued Drug Exposure:** Failure to recognize and immediately discontinue ergot derivatives or anorexigens results in rapid disease progression and catastrophic failure of valve reconstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## ETIOLOGY AND PATHOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

In the 1990's several studies reported the association between appetite suppressant medications and valvular heart disease, particularly aortic and mitral regurgitation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Causative Agents, Serotonergic Mechanism, and Pulmonary Hypertension</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Anorexigenic Medications:** These medications included fenfluramine and its D-isomer dexfenfluramine used alone or in combination with phentermine. Although they were withdrawn from the market, patients with valvular involvement may be encountered and some may require surgical attention.
* **Clinical Severity and Course:** In most cases, the severity of valvular regurgitation is mild to moderate. Some studies have suggested the improvement of valvular disease after discontinuation of the medication. Occasionally, however, patients develop significant valvular regurgitation requiring surgical correction.
* **Pulmonary Hypertension:** In addition to valvular heart disease, pulmonary hypertension is a common finding in these patients. Serotonin-induced pulmonary vasoconstriction is believed to play a major role in the genesis of this condition.
* **Ergot Alkaloid Therapy:** Ergotamine therapy is another medication that may induce valvular heart disease. The rare cases of ergot alkaloid–induced valvular diseases involve the aortic and/or mitral position.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Histopathology and Carcinoid-Like Morphological Changes</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Carcinoid-Like Pathology:** The pathological findings are similar to those observed in carcinoid heart disease, with thickening of the valvular leaflets as well as the subvalvular apparatus.
* **Histological Features:** The architecture of the leaflets is intact, and histological examination shows proliferative myofibroblasts and a significant increase in the extracellular matrix.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Echocardiographic Assessment of Type IIIa Dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Type IIIa Dysfunction:** In most patients, echocardiography shows a Type IIIa valvular dysfunction with leaflet thickening and retraction and with chordae thickening and shortening in both the mitral and the tricuspid positions.
* **Combined Lesions:** Most patients present with valvular regurgitation combined with varying degrees of stenosis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Indications, Replacement, and Reconstructive Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Indications for Surgery:** In patients with severe symptomatic valvular disease, surgical therapy remains the only reliable option.
* **Aortic Valve Replacement:** In the aortic position, valve replacement is mandatory.
* **Mitral and Tricuspid Reconstruction:** In the mitral and tricuspid positions, a valve reconstruction could be considered in favorable cases using leaflet patch extension and remodeling annuloplasty.
* **Surgical Experience:** It should be noted that surgical experience is extremely limited in this setting with no long-term data currently available.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Relationship Between Causative Agent Discontinuation and Disease Stabilization (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In your experience, what relationship have you observed between the causative agent and the evolution of valvular disease?

**AC:** I have limited experience, but from what I read in the literature it seems that in many instances the discontinuation of the medication is associated with some stabilization of the valvular lesions. This particularity distinguishes this group of valvular diseases from inflammatory valvular diseases, radiation-induced diseases, and carcinoid heart diseases in which the valvular lesion evolves independently of the causative agent.

### Impact of Lesion Stabilization on Feasibility of Valve Reconstruction (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Do these different patterns influence your decision-making in the choice of surgical procedure?

**AC:** Some data from the literature suggest that patients with medication-induced valvulopathy may benefit from valve reconstruction more than patients with carcinoid diseases or radiation-induced valve diseases.
