# 9. Extensive Annular Calcification and Abscesses

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical Problem and Pathology
* **Etiological Context:** Mitral annular calcification (MAC) is most commonly encountered in long-standing degenerative valvular disease, but also occurs in rheumatic valve disease and endocarditis. Massive calcification or infectious abscesses can deform the annulus, leading to Type I dysfunction or compromising leaflet motion.
* **Surgical Challenge:** Simple placement of annuloplasty sutures through heavily calcified or infected tissue leads to suture tear-out, severe paravalvular leaks, or circumflex artery injury.
* **The Two-Step Strategy:** Reconstruction proceeds systematically in 2 distinct steps:
  1. Annular decalcification en bloc or radical abscess debridement.
  2. Annular reconstruction to restore atrioventricular continuity and establish a stable base for ring implantation.

### Annular Decalcification and Debridement
* **En Bloc Decalcification:** Incision of the endocardium along the atrial and ventricular borders of the calcium bar using a sharp knife ([Fig. 9-1](#fig-09-01), [Fig. 9-2](#fig-09-02)). Preserving the thin fibrous capsule around the bar enables en bloc mobilization without fragmentation, avoiding the use of rongeurs.
* **Abscess Debridement:** All infected and necrotic tissue is radically excised using scissors (not curettes!) until healthy bleeding tissue is reached ([Fig. 9-3](#fig-09-03)). Multiple tissue specimens are harvested for microbiological and histological analysis.

### Reconstructive Techniques
* **Conventional Technique:** Indicated for moderate-depth decalcification or debridement beds ([Fig. 9-4a](#fig-09-04a), [Fig. 9-4b](#fig-09-04b), [Fig. 9-4c](#fig-09-04c), [Fig. 9-4d](#fig-09-04d)). Atrioventricular continuity is restored using vertical, nonpledgetted figure-of-eight mattress 2-0 braided sutures that approximate the atrial wall to the ventricular myocardium, followed by leaflet reattachment and ring annuloplasty.
* **Sliding Atrium Technique:** Preferred when deep resection leaves extensive bare ventricular myocardium ([Fig. 9-5](#fig-09-05)). The left atrial wall is extensively mobilized from the epicardium and advanced into the defect, covering the ventricular muscle and creating a resilient new fibrous ledge.
* **Pericardial Patch Reconstruction:** Indicated for large anterior annular abscesses or extensive fibrous trigone destruction where local tissues cannot bridge the defect ([Fig. 9-6](#fig-09-06)).

### Complications and Quality Gate
* **Atrioventricular Disjunction:** The most feared complication of radical decalcification. Prevented by avoiding excessive depth into the atrioventricular groove and systematically placing an additional running monofilament suture to reinforce atrioventricular continuity before ring fixation.
* **Circumflex Artery Protection:** Ventricular suture bites must be placed shallow and parallel to the groove, taking advantage of fibrous remnants rather than deep myocardial bites.

## ANNULAR DECALCIFICATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Besides annular dilatation, the mitral valve annulus can be severely affected by calcification or abscesses. This pathology requires specific techniques of reconstruction, the surgical management of which proceeds in 2 steps: annular decalcification (or debridement) and annular reconstruction.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Types of Annular Calcification</summary><div class="details-content">

Annular calcification is commonly seen in elderly patients with degenerative valvular disease ([Fig. 9-1](#fig-09-01)).

In most patients it is limited to the annulus or the base of the posterior leaflet ([Fig. 9-1](#fig-09-01), a). In more extensive forms, calcification involves the entire posterior annulus or forms a circumferential ring ([Fig. 9-1](#fig-09-01), b).

The calcium bar may extend deeply into the ventricular myocardium, creating a high risk of atrioventricular groove perforation or circumflex artery injury during debridement.

<span id="fig-09-01"></span>![](_page_94_Figure_9-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-1** Types of annular calcification: (a) Localized bar of calcium involving the posterior annulus; (b) Circumferential calcium bar.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### En Bloc Resection Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

Decalcification is initiated by incising the endocardium at the atrial and ventricular limits of the calcium formation using a sharp blade knife ([Fig. 9-2](#fig-09-02), a).

The tip of the blade should be oriented so that the surrounding tissue is separated from the calcium bar ([Fig. 9-2](#fig-09-02), inset). Preserving the thin fibrous sheath encapsulating the calcium bar allows an en bloc resection without calcium fragmentation, thus avoiding the use of rongeurs ([Fig. 9-2](#fig-09-02), b).

The dissection is performed by developing a circumferential plane from 1 extremity of the calcium bar towards the other end, ensuring the entire calcium bloc is removed. The removal of calcium facilitates the exposure of the atrioventricular connective tissue and fat, which contain the circumflex artery and coronary sinus.

<span id="fig-09-02"></span>![](_page_95_Figure_9-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-2** Technique of annular decalcification: (a) Incision of the endocardium along the atrial and ventricular borders; (b) En bloc mobilization and removal of the calcium bar preserving the capsular sheath.

</div></details>

## DEBRIDEMENT OF ANNULAR ABSCESSES</summary><div class="details-content">

Several types of annular abscesses can be observed in bacterial endocarditis. The abscess may present as a localized cavity within the annulus or as an extensive destruction of the atrioventricular junction.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Debridement Methodology</summary><div class="details-content">

As soon as the lesions are exposed, multiple specimens should be harvested and sent to bacteriology and pathology for analysis.

Debridement consists of excising with scissors all infected, necrotic, or fragmented tissues until healthy bleeding tissue is exposed ([Fig. 9-3](#fig-09-03), a, b).

Curettes should be used with extreme caution because they may tear the fragile ventricular wall or injure adjacent coronary vessels.

<span id="fig-09-03"></span>![](_page_96_Figure_9-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-3** Surgical debridement of annular abscess: (a) Exposure of the abscess cavity within the annulus; (b) Sharp excision of necrotic and infected tissue with scissors until healthy margins are reached.

</div></details>

## ANNULAR RECONSTRUCTION</summary><div class="details-content">

Depending upon the extension of the annular decalcification or debridement, 2 techniques are used to restore the integrity of the annulus: the conventional technique and the sliding atrium technique.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Conventional Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

The standard technique is indicated whenever the ventricular extension of the decalcification or debridement is moderate and fibrous tissue usually covers both the atrial and ventricular edges ([Fig. 9-4a](#fig-09-04a)).

The atrioventricular continuity is restored by taking advantage of any fibrous tissue or papillary muscle remnants identified at the ventricular edge.

Nonpledgetted, vertical figure-of-eight mattress 2-0 braided sutures are placed to approximate the atrial wall to the ventricular myocardium.

<span id="fig-09-04a"></span>![](_page_96_Figure_9-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-4** Conventional annular reconstruction: (a) Bed of resection after en bloc decalcification; (b) Exposure of the atrial edge and ventricular myocardium; (c) Initial placement of vertical figure-of-eight mattress sutures.

#### Suturing and Leaflet Reattachment
Each suture is first passed into the ventricular edge and then into the atrial edge ([Fig. 9-4b](#fig-09-04b), d).

Once all sutures have been placed, they are tied to restore atrioventricular continuity ([Fig. 9-4b](#fig-09-04b), e).

The detached leaflet is then reattached to the newly reconstructed annulus using a continuous or interrupted suture technique ([Fig. 9-4c](#fig-09-04c), f, g, h).

<span id="fig-09-04b"></span>![](_page_97_Figure_9-4_cont1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-4 (Cont'd 1)** Restoration of atrioventricular continuity: (d) Passage of figure-of-eight sutures through ventricular and atrial edges; (e) Tying of sutures to approximate tissues without tension.

<span id="fig-09-04c"></span>![](_page_98_Figure_9-4_cont2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-4 (Cont'd 2)** Leaflet reattachment: (f) Positioning the detached leaflet over the reconstructed annulus; (g, h) Suture fixation of the leaflet margin to the newly formed fibrous ridge.

#### Ring Fixation and Stabilization
The reconstruction is completed by prosthetic ring annuloplasty ([Fig. 9-4d](#fig-09-04d), i, j).

The prosthetic ring relieves all tension from the reconstructed annulus and the reattached leaflet, ensuring durable long-term competence.

<span id="fig-09-04d"></span>![](_page_99_Figure_9-4_cont3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-4 (Cont'd 3)** Completion of reconstruction: (i) Passage of annuloplasty ring sutures through the reconstructed annulus; (j) Final seated prosthetic ring stabilizing the repair.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Sliding Atrium Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

The sliding atrium technique is indicated when decalcification or debridement leaves a deep defect with bare ventricular myocardium without fibrous tissue coverage ([Fig. 9-5](#fig-09-05), a).

Using living tissue for annular reconstruction is always preferable to synthetic materials.

The atrial edge is dissected from surrounding tissue to mobilize an atrial flap that can be advanced into the ventricular defect ([Fig. 9-5](#fig-09-05), b).

<span id="fig-09-05"></span>![](_page_100_Figure_9-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-5** The sliding atrium technique: (a) Deep myocardial defect without fibrous coverage; (b) Extensive mobilization of the left atrial wall; (c) Advancing the atrial flap into the ventricular groove; (d) Suture fixation of the atrial flap to the ventricular myocardium; (e) Leaflet reattachment and ring implantation.

#### Atrial Flap Fixation
Figure-of-eight mattress 2-0 braided sutures are placed between the deep ventricular myocardium and the mobilized atrial flap ([Fig. 9-5](#fig-09-05), c, d).

During this maneuver of atrial sliding, 1 should avoid pulling up the ventricular myocardium, which could distort the circumflex artery.

The leaflet is then reattached to the advancing edge of the atrial flap, and a remodeling annuloplasty ring is implanted to secure the reconstruction ([Fig. 9-5](#fig-09-05), e).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Annular Reconstruction Using Pericardial Tissue</summary><div class="details-content">

Although we prefer to use the sliding atrium technique in large annular debridements, pericardial tissue is a valuable alternative, especially in an annular abscess involving the anterior portion of the annulus ([Fig. 9-6](#fig-09-06)).

After complete excision of the infected focus, a large defect may remain across the anterior annulus or intervalvular fibrosa.

An autologous pericardial patch (treated with glutaraldehyde) is trimmed to fit the defect and secured to the ventricular muscle and atrial wall with continuous or interrupted sutures.

<span id="fig-09-06"></span>![](_page_101_Figure_9-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 9-6** Annular reconstruction using autologous pericardium: (a) Extensive defect of the anterior annulus; (b) Tailoring and suture anchoring of the pericardial patch; (c) Leaflet reattachment and prosthetic ring fixation.

#### Complex Aortic Root Involvement
Associated aortic valve involvement requires a combined technique of valve replacement and annular reconstruction.

The pericardial patch is used to reconstruct the aorto-mitral curtain, securing both the mitral annulus and the aortic annulus.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Etiology and Clinical Setting of Annular Calcification (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: In what setting is annular calcification observed?

**AC:** The most common setting is long-lasting degenerative valvular diseases. Annular calcification can also be observed in rheumatic disease, endocarditis, and chronic renal failure with secondary hyperparathyroidism.

In degenerative disease, calcification begins as focal deposits in the posterior annulus that progressively coalesce into a rigid bar of calcium.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pure Annular Calcification Causing Regurgitation (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Can mitral annular calcification cause regurgitation in the absence of other valvular lesions?

**AC:** In this particular situation, annular calcification may produce a Type I dysfunction attributable to deformation and flattening of the annulus, which prevents normal coaptation.

In addition, the rigid calcium bar may elevate the base of the posterior leaflet, displacing the coaptation line toward the left atrium and creating significant regurgitation despite normal leaflet tissue.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Contraindications to Extensive Annular Decalcification (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Is there any contraindication to perform extensive annular decalcification?

**AC:** 2 factors influence the decision:
* One factor is patient age beyond 80 years. In elderly, frail patients, extensive decalcification carries an excessively high operative risk.
* The other factor is the extension of the calcium bar deep into the left ventricular myocardium. When calcification invades deeply between the papillary muscles and the epicardium, radical excision risks catastrophic ventricular rupture.

In these high-risk circumstances, partial decalcification or conservative valve replacement is preferable.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complete vs. Partial Decalcification in Endocarditis (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: In patients with mitral valve endocarditis and annular calcification, should a complete annular decalcification be performed?

**AC:** It is preferable to proceed to a complete decalcification because the true limit of the infectious process cannot be determined in the presence of calcium formations.

Infected calcium acts as a permanent bacterial nidus that inevitably leads to recurrent endocarditis or early prosthetic dehiscence if left in place.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Technical Precautions to Prevent Circumflex Artery Injury (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: There are concerns that the ventricular bites of the figure-of-eight mattress sutures may compromise the circumflex artery. How do you prevent this complication?

**AC:** Some important technical precautions practically eliminate this risk:
* First, the ventricular bites must be large in width but superficial in depth, taking advantage of any fibrous tissue remnants rather than driving deeply into myocardium.
* Second, sutures should be oriented parallel rather than perpendicular to the atrioventricular groove.
* Third, during tying, 1 must avoid excessive upward traction that could kinking or compress the circumflex artery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy in Circumferential Calcification (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Do you recommend any specific technique in patients with circumferential calcification?

**AC:** Usually the calcium bar is thinner at the anterior segment of the annulus and does not require annular decalcification. It is sufficient to decalcify the posterior annulus.

If the anterior segment is severely calcified, decalcification must be performed with extreme care to avoid entering the aortic root or injuring the non-coronary and left coronary aortic valve cusps.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Incidence and Mechanism of Atrioventricular Disjunction (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Have you ever observed an atrioventricular disjunction following the use of your technique of annular reconstruction?

**AC:** I observed 2 instances of atrioventricular disjunction in my first series of 50 patients with extensive calcification operated on between 1970 and 1975.

Both cases occurred when deep, aggressive debridement breached the epicardial fat in the atrioventricular groove, and the reconstruction sutures tore through fragile, non-reinforced myocardium under ventricular systolic pressure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Prevention of Atrioventricular Disjunction (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What is the best way to prevent this difficult situation?

**AC:** Following these 2 cases of atrioventricular disjunction, I systematically used an additional running suture of 3-0 or 4-0 monofilament to reinforce the atrioventricular groove before placing the ring sutures.

In addition, mobilizing the left atrium (the sliding atrium technique) to cover the bare ventricular myocardium provides living, vascularized tissue that reliably prevents dehiscence.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Long-Term Tissue Evolution of the Reconstructed Annulus (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: In patients reoperated for progressive valve deterioration without annular disjunction, how does the reconstructed annulus look?

**AC:** In a patient in whom the valve had to be replaced several years after the operation, I observed a strong fibrous tissue consolidation in the decalcified area.

The mobilized atrial flap and the ventricular myocardium had fused into a dense, solid fibrous ridge that was indistinguishable from a normal native annulus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Role of Partial Annular Decalcification (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What do you think about partial annular decalcification?

**AC:** Partial annular decalcification is a reasonable alternative for surgeons with limited experience or whenever extensive decalcification is considered too dangerous.

In this approach, only the superficial, protruding portion of the calcium bar is shaved down with a scalpel or ultrasonic aspirator, leaving the deep base untouched to prevent atrioventricular groove disruption.

</div></details>
