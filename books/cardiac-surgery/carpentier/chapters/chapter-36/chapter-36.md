# Chapter 36. Carcinoid Valvular Disease

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Carcinoid heart disease is a unique manifestation of neuroendocrine tumor metastasis characterized by extensive endocardial fibrous plaque deposition:
1. **Epidemiology and Origin:** Carcinoid tumors are rare neuroendocrine neoplasms with an annual incidence of approximately 4 per 100,000 in the general population. They originate from enterochromaffin cells, situated in the gastrointestinal tract in 70% of cases and less frequently in the bronchopulmonary system.
2. **Pathophysiology of Carcinoid Heart Disease:** Primary tumors are typically clinically silent until hepatic metastases develop. Hepatic metastases release large quantities of vasoactive substances—primarily serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), 5-hydroxytryptophan, histamine, bradykinin, tachykinins, and prostaglandins—directly into the systemic venous circulation, escaping hepatic metabolic degradation and bathing the right-sided cardiac chambers.
3. **Valvular Topography:** Right-sided valves are predominantly affected, resulting in severe tricuspid regurgitation and/or stenosis ([Fig. 36-1](#fig-36-01)) and pulmonary regurgitation and/or stenosis ([Fig. 36-2](#fig-36-02)). Left-sided valve involvement is less common (mitral > aortic, [Fig. 36-3](#fig-36-03)) and occurs almost exclusively in patients with a patent foramen ovale (PFO), bronchial carcinoid, or exceptionally high circulating serotonin levels.
4. **Carpentier Type IIIa Dysfunction:** Deposition of pearly-white fibrocellular plaques on the endocardium leads to diffuse thickening, stiffening, retraction, and chordal shortening, producing combined stenosis and regurgitation with restricted leaflet excursion in both systole and diastole ([Fig. 36-5](#fig-36-05)).
5. **Surgical Realities:** Right-sided lesions are almost universally too advanced for reconstructive procedures and require valve replacement. Conversely, left-sided lesions are exposed to lower concentrations of vasoactive amines and are frequently amenable to reconstructive repair.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Clinical Hallmarks:** Carcinoid syndrome characterized by episodic cutaneous flushing, diaphoresis, secretory diarrhea, bronchospasm, and facial telangiectasia, accompanied by progressive signs of right ventricular failure (peripheral edema, hepatomegaly, ascites) and labile blood pressure.
* **Biochemical Markers:**
  * *Urinary 5-HIAA:* Elevated 24-hour urinary excretion of 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA) is the primary diagnostic and monitoring gold standard for tumor secretory activity.
  * *Circulating Serology:* Marked elevation of blood serotonin, chromogranin A, and atrial natriuretic factor (ANF).
* **Echocardiographic Features:**
  * *Type IIIa Dysfunction:* Severe diffuse leaflet thickening, leaflet retraction, and subvalvular chordal fusion/shortening without commissural fusion.
  * *Hemodynamic Lesion:* Rigid, non-coapting leaflets resulting in severe "free-travel" regurgitation coupled with valvular stenosis.
  * *Calcification:* Notably absent or minimal, distinguishing carcinoid from rheumatic or degenerative disease.
* **Histopathological Characteristics:** Fibrocellular plaques composed of proliferating myofibroblasts and smooth muscle cells embedded in an abundant acid-mucopolysaccharide and collagen-rich stroma ([Fig. 36-4](#fig-36-04)), superimposed upon intact underlying valve architecture.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Indications for Intervention:** Symptomatic right heart failure, progressive right ventricular dilatation/dysfunction, or severe left-sided valvular dysfunction in patients with medically stabilized tumor burden.
* **Perioperative Carcinoid Crisis Prevention:**
  1. *Somatostatin Analog Prophylaxis:* Continuous intravenous infusion of octreotide initiated at the induction of anesthesia, maintained throughout the operation, and continued for at least 24 hours postoperatively.
  2. *Adjunctive Medication:* Preoperative administration of antihistamines to prevent mediator-induced bronchospasm and flushing.
* **Right-Sided Valvular Management:**
  1. *Tricuspid Valve:* Advanced retraction and subvalvular fusion preclude reconstruction; tricuspid valve replacement (TVR) is performed.
  2. *Pulmonary Valve:* Valve replacement (PVR) or patch enlargement/cusp excision is necessary when severe stenosis or regurgitation coexists.
  3. *Prosthesis Selection:* Biological tissue valves (bioprostheses) are strongly preferred over mechanical prostheses due to hepatic dysfunction, coagulopathy, and the frequent need for subsequent abdominal resections. Mechanical valves are considered only in select patients with high tumor activity at risk of accelerated bioprosthetic plaque encapsulation.
* **Left-Sided Valvular Management:**
  * In patients with moderate left-sided involvement, reconstructive valve surgery is preferable to prosthetic replacement because lesions are generally less severe.
* **Post-Cardiopulmonary Bypass Weaning Protocol:**
  1. *Rhythm Optimization:* Restoration of normal rhythm at 85 to 90 beats per minute.
  2. *Preload Optimization:* Targeted volume loading to maintain a mean arterial pressure of 75 to 80 mm Hg.
  3. *Vasoactive Support:* Judicious administration of epinephrine and/or phenylephrine alongside continuous octreotide; intra-aortic balloon counterpulsation for associated left ventricular failure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Intraoperative Carcinoid Crisis:** Massive release of vasoactive substances triggered by tumor manipulation, anesthesia, or hypothermia, manifesting as refractory hypotension, bronchospasm, and flushing; managed by high-dose intravenous octreotide boluses.
* **Acute Right Ventricular Failure:** The primary cause of early postoperative mortality, stemming from combined systolic overload and restrictive diastolic impairment secondary to myocardial and endocardial plaque deposition.
* **Hemorrhagic Diathesis:** Liver metastases impair hepatic synthesis of coagulation factors, compounded by venous hypertension from tricuspid regurgitation.
* **Plaque Recurrence on Bioprostheses:** Ongoing uncontrolled secretion of vasoactive amines can induce late carcinoid plaque deposition on bioprosthetic valve leaflets, leading to recurrent structural deterioration.

</div></details>

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND MORPHOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

Carcinoid tumors are rare neuroendocrine neoplasms with an incidence of about 4 per 100,000 in the general population per year. They originate from enterochromaffin cells, which are localized in 70% of cases in the gastrointestinal system and more rarely in the bronchopulmonary system.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Secretory Products, Hepatic Metastasis, and Carcinoid Syndrome</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Endocrine Secretion:** Carcinoid tumors are characterized by excess secretion of serotonin and other vasoactive substances.
* **Tumor Latency:** The primary tumor is often asymptomatic until hepatic metastases take place, which then produce the carcinoid syndrome.
* **Clinical Syndrome:** This syndrome is characterized by cutaneous flushing, sweating, diarrhea, bronchoconstriction, and facial telangiectasia.
* **Vasoactive Amines:** Valvular manifestations occur almost always in patients with secondary hepatic metastases, which allow vasoactive amines to reach the right-sided valves without being metabolized by the liver. These vasoactive substances include 5-hydroxytryptamine, 5-hydroxytryptophan, histamine, bradykinins, tachykinins, and prostaglandins.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Topography of Valvular Lesions and Histopathology</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Right-Sided Predominance:** Carcinoid heart disease occurs following hepatic metastasis and involves primarily the right-sided valves displaying tricuspid regurgitation and/or stenosis ([Fig. 36-1](#fig-36-01)) or pulmonary valve regurgitation and/or stenosis ([Fig. 36-2](#fig-36-02)).

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

The carcinoid syndrome is present in most patients with carcinoid heart disease.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clinical Symptoms and Biological Markers</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Heart Failure and Blood Pressure:** Clinical signs of right-sided heart failure are common findings because of the involvement of the tricuspid and/or pulmonary valves. Labile blood pressure is another characteristic feature, depending on the concentration of the vasoactive substances in the circulation.
* **Diagnostic Biomarkers:** The diagnosis is confirmed by elevation in the levels of biological products derived from serotonin metabolism, such as 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA).
* **Laboratory Assessment:** Urinary levels of 5-HIAA have been classically used as a reliable biological marker for the assessment of tumor activity. Furthermore, levels of other biological markers such as serotonin, chromogranin A, and atrial natriuretic factor are typically elevated in the blood.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Echocardiographic Assessment of Type IIIa Dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiographic Findings:** Echocardiography shows a Type IIIa dysfunction in the tricuspid and pulmonic positions ([Fig. 36-5](#fig-36-05)).
* **Leaflet and Chordal Pathology:** The leaflets are thickened and retracted and the chordae are fused and shortened, resulting in a combined valvular stenosis and regurgitation.
* **Absence of Calcification:** Calcification is rarely observed in these patients.

## SURGICAL INDICATIONS AND RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

Progress in medical therapy has resulted in better control of the carcinoid syndrome and an increasing interest in the surgical management of the valvular involvement.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Indications and Operative Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Right-Sided Lesions:** Right-sided valvular lesions are often too advanced and not amenable to reconstructive surgery. Most of these patients require valvular replacement in the tricuspid and pulmonic positions.
* **Prosthesis Choice:** Limited available data suggest that bioprosthetic valve replacement is preferable to mechanical valves in these patients who often present with abnormal liver profiles and associated coagulopathies. Severe involvement of the pulmonary valve also requires a valve replacement.
* **Left-Sided Reconstructive Strategy:** The optimal surgical procedure for left-sided valvular disease also remains debatable. Our experience suggests that left-sided valvular lesions may be less advanced compared to those on the right side because of the lower concentration of vasoactive substances in the arterial circulation. In patients with moderate left-sided valvular lesions, reconstructive surgery is therefore preferable.
* **Perioperative Management:** The perioperative management should include the administration of a somatostatin analog to decrease the risk of hypotension. Antihistamines are also used to prevent bronchospasm and flushing.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Results and Clinical Outcomes</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Clinical Series:** Our experience comprises 10 patients with metastatic carcinoid tumors who underwent multivalve surgery (tricuspid = 10, pulmonic = 9, mitral = 3, and aortic = 1) between January 2001 and October 2006.
* **Procedures Performed:** Surgical procedures included isolated tricuspid valve replacement in 1 patient, tricuspid and pulmonic valve replacement in 6, triple valve surgery in 2, and quadruple valve surgery in 1 patient.
* **Operative Mortality and Morbidity:** There were 2 operative deaths secondary to right ventricular failure and subsequent multiorgan failure. No major morbidities or carcinoid crisis occurred in the remaining patients.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Role of Valve Reconstruction in Carcinoid Valve Disease (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Do you believe there is a role for valve reconstruction in patients with carcinoid valve disease?

**AC:** We should make a distinction between the right-sided and left-sided valves. On the right side, the tricuspid or pulmonary lesions are generally too advanced to be amenable to valve reconstruction. On the left side, the valvular lesions are often less pronounced and valve reconstruction can be envisioned in the most favorable cases.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Prosthetic Valve Selection in Right-Sided Carcinoid Valve Disease (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** What type of prosthetic valve do you favor in patients with right-sided carcinoid valve disease?

**AC:** The choice of the prosthetic valve remains controversial in these patients. It is, however, important to point out that these patients frequently require abdominal surgery during their lifetime. They also commonly present with an abnormal liver profile and associated coagulopathy attributable to liver metastasis. For all these reasons, I think that a bioprosthesis is recommended. One exception, however, should be mentioned: patients with high tumoral activity in whom early structural valve deterioration has been reported. These patients may have a better outcome with a mechanical valve implantation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Prevention and Incidence of Perioperative Carcinoid Crisis (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** How often have you encountered carcinoid crisis perioperatively?

**AC:** In the past, this complication was a serious consideration, particularly if patients required intraoperative vasoactive medications. Today, the intravenous administration of the somatostatin analog octreotide has significantly reduced the incidence and severity of the carcinoid symptoms. I want to emphasize that an intravenous somatostatin analog should be initiated at the very beginning of the surgery and prolonged at least 24 hours postoperatively. Afterwards the oral treatment is reinstituted.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Right Ventricular Dysfunction During Weaning from Bypass (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Moderate to severe right ventricular dysfunction is common in patients with carcinoid valve disease. What protocol do you recommend to wean these patients from cardiopulmonary bypass?

**AC:** Right ventricular failure is the most serious complication in these patients. The diastolic component of ventricular failure is due to the endocardial infiltration by the carcinoid plaques. The systolic component is the consequence of long-lasting tricuspid and pulmonic valve dysfunctions. In practice, the following measures should be applied: first, restoration of a normal cardiac rhythm with a rate of 85 to 90 beats per minute; second, optimization of preload to obtain a mean arterial pressure of 75 to 80 mm Hg; third, continuous intravenous administration of octreotide to avoid any episode of hypotension. If despite these measures right ventricular function and cardiac output remain marginal, vasoactive agents such as epinephrine and/or phenylephrine can be added without major concern for an increased risk of carcinoid crisis. An intraaortic balloon pump is useful in patients with left-sided involvement.

</div></details>
