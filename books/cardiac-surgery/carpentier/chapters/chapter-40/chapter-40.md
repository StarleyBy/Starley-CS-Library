# Chapter 40. Reconstructive Valve Surgery Made Simple

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

The concluding chapter synthesizes the philosophy, educational paradigm, and organizational framework required to master and institutionalize reconstructive valve surgery:
1. **The Fallacy of Preemptive Replacement:** Reluctance to perform valve reconstruction often stems from perceived technical complexity or fear of surgical failure, leading surgeons to invoke a misapplied "precautionary principle" (*principe de précaution*) and default to replacement. Comprehensive clinical follow-up over 25 years has unequivocally demonstrated that successful valve reconstruction offers superior long-term survival, reduced thromboembolic and hemorrhagic hazards, and improved quality of life compared to prosthetic replacement.
2. **Rise of Nonthrombogenic Valve Surgery:** Modern cardiology increasingly emphasizes nonthrombogenic solutions—reconstructive valve repair and glutaraldehyde-processed bioprostheses—to liberate patients from lifelong anticoagulation ([Fig. 40-1](#fig-40-01)).
3. **The Multidisciplinary Valve Team:** Reconstructive surgery is fundamentally a team effort uniting the clinical cardiologist, intraoperative echocardiographer, cardiac anesthesiologist, and operating surgeon. The availability of real-time intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) in the operating room is indispensable.
4. **The Cartesian Educational Paradigm:** Learning reconstructive techniques relies on Cartesian principles: dividing complex pathologies into simpler components (the Pathological Triad and Functional Classification) and proceeding step-by-step from simple lesions (Type II P2 prolapse) to complex multivalent disease (Barlow's disease, endocarditis, rheumatic valve disease).
5. **The Two Golden Rules of Repair:**
   * *First Golden Rule:* "Try to repair before deciding to replace." Every valve deserves at least 10 minutes of disciplined intraoperative analysis before committing to replacement.
   * *Second Golden Rule:* "No residual leak over 1+ should be left without proceeding to a second look."

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Institutional Repair Benchmarks:** Leading centers achieve mitral repair rates exceeding 90% for degenerative disease; lower volume centers exhibit substantially lower repair rates, underscoring that institutional experience, not patient lesion complexity, determines the likelihood of repair.
* **Intraoperative Echocardiographic Requirements:**
  * Categorization into one of the 4 functional groups.
  * Precise segmental localization of excess or restricted leaflet tissue.
  * Direct measurement of annular dimensions to guide annuloplasty ring sizing.
  * Immediate post-cardiopulmonary bypass assessment to detect residual regurgitation or systolic anterior motion (SAM).
* **The 10-Minute Analytical Window:** Allocating 10 minutes under cold cardioplegic arrest to systematically inspect leaflet segments, chordal tension, and annular geometry before electing valve replacement.
* **Residual Regurgitation Threshold:** Any residual regurgitation grade > 1+ on post-bypass TEE is unacceptable and mandates immediate reinstitution of bypass for surgical correction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Step-by-Step Surgical Progression:**
  1. *Entry-Level Repair:* Posterior leaflet P2 prolapse due to chordal rupture. Select chordal transfer (normal tissue volume), triangular resection (moderate excess), or quadrangular resection with sliding leaflet plasty (extensive tissue excess).
  2. *Intermediate Repair:* Anterior leaflet prolapse due to chordal rupture or elongation, utilizing limited triangular resection, chordal transfer, or PTFE artificial neo-chordae.
  3. *Advanced Repair:* Multisegment degenerative disease (Barlow's disease), severe active or healed infective endocarditis, rheumatic leaflet retraction requiring autologous pericardial patch augmentation, and extensive annular calcification.
* **Knowledge Acquisition Pipeline:**
  1. *Wet Lab Simulation:* Rigorous dissection and reconstructive practice in the autopsy room or wet lab animal tissue facilities prior to human operations.
  2. *Surgical Mentorship:* Direct hands-on apprenticeship (*compagnonage*) assisting senior colleagues "elbow-to-elbow" (*au coude à coude*).
  3. *Interactive Video Demonstration:* Participation in interactive case broadcasts and surgical forums (e.g., Le Club Mitrale).
* **Democratization of Expertise:** Reconstructive expertise must be acquired by cardiac surgeons across all centers rather than restricted to a small cadre of "super-specialists," ensuring universal patient access to durable valve repair.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **The Pretext of the "Precautionary Principle":** Replacing a reparable valve out of surgical hesitation or lack of training deprives the patient of the substantial survival advantages of repair.
* **Neglecting Intraoperative TEE:** Failing to perform comprehensive post-bypass TEE allows significant residual leaks or unrecognized SAM to leave the operating room, triggering early repair failure.
* **Skipping Step-by-Step Training:** Attempting complex multisegmental or calcified repairs without prior mastery of isolated segmental techniques in wet labs leads to poor operative outcomes.
* **The "One-Size-Fits-All" Fallacy:** Applying rigid resective techniques to tissue-deficient valves or omitting annuloplasty rings leads to repair breakdown.

## A TEAM EFFORT</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgeons who prefer valve replacement over valve reconstruction often claim that they are uncertain about the long-term benefits of valve reconstruction. Another assertion is that failure following valve repair is generally attributed to the surgeon, whereas a complication after valve replacement can more easily be blamed on the valve itself.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Multidisciplinary Valve Team and Nonthrombogenic Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Overcoming Surgical Reluctance:** Surgeons who are doubtful about their expertise in performing a successful valve repair procedure prefer replacing the valve, citing the fashionable "precautionary principle" (*principe de précaution*) as the basis for their decision, which actually does not always benefit the patient. These arguments have been ruled out by numerous publications showing the excellent results of reconstructive valve surgery in patients followed up to 25 years.
* **Institutional Variation:** Guidelines emphasize that valve repair is superior to replacement provided it is performed by an expert surgeon. However, the chance of receiving an efficient repair varies significantly; some centers boast a 90% valve repair rate while others perform almost exclusively valve replacements.
* **Nonthrombogenic Valve Surgery:** Cardiologists are now deeply involved in decision-making and give growing importance to "nonthrombogenic valve surgery," which includes valve repair and tissue bioprostheses ([Fig. 40-1](#fig-40-01)). These techniques eliminate mandatory anticoagulation therapy and ensure a superior quality of life.

### Interdisciplinary Roles: Cardiologist, Echocardiographer, and Anesthesiologist</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Role of the Cardiologist:** Obtains a comprehensive history and accurate evaluation of symptoms to assess dysfunction and timing. A major evolution has been the referral of asymptomatic patients for early valve reconstruction.
* **Role of the Echocardiographer:** Completes a comprehensive pre-bypass analysis of etiology, lesions, and functional groups. Enables localization of the predominant dysfunction, sizing of the annuloplasty ring, and prediction of the required repair technique. Post-bypass TEE is critical to detect residual dysfunction and recommend an immediate second look if the result is not fully satisfactory.
* **Role of the Anesthesiologist:** Uses TEE to assess ventricular function, optimize ventricular filling, verify de-airing, and evaluate the efficacy of inotropes or intra-aortic balloon counterpulsation.
* **Role of the Surgeon:** Must be psychologically and technically prepared to perform repair rather than predetermined replacement, recognizing personal limitations and seeking mentorship when necessary.

## TRANSFER OF KNOWLEDGE</summary><div class="details-content">

As stated by Hippocrates in his oath, the transfer of knowledge is part of the sacred mission of the medical profession.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mentorship, Wet Lab Training, and the Cartesian Method</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Mentorship and Hands-on Training:** The most efficient means to achieve knowledge transfer is the master-pupil relationship (*compagnonage*), in which the young surgeon assists "elbow-to-elbow" (*au coude à coude*) with the senior surgeon. Wet lab training in autopsy facilities or animal tissues must precede operating on humans.
* **The Cartesian Method:** Reconstructive surgery benefits directly from two fundamental Cartesian principles:
  1. *Decomposition:* Separating any complex problem into its simpler components (accomplished via the Pathological Triad and Functional Classification).
  2. *Step-by-Step Progression:* Proceeding systematically from the simplest towards more complex situations.
* **Clinical Progression Example:**
  * *Step 1:* Type II P2 prolapse due to chordal rupture (chordal transfer, triangular resection, or quadrangular resection).
  * *Step 2:* Anterior leaflet prolapse (limited triangular resection, chordal transfer, or artificial chordae).
  * *Step 3:* Complex multi-segment pathology (Barlow's disease, endocarditis, rheumatic valve disease, annular calcification).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Two Golden Rules of Reconstructive Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

In any complex clinical situation, two golden rules govern reconstructive surgery:
* **The First Golden Rule:** *"Try to repair before deciding to replace."* Every mitral valve deserves 10 minutes of the surgeon's time under direct vision to analyze lesions and determine reconstructive feasibility.
* **The Second Golden Rule:** *"No residual leak over 1+ should be left without proceeding to a second look."*

## A NEW COMMUNICATION AGE</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Informed Patients, Decentralization of Expertise, and Lifelong Durability</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Transformation of the Patient-Physician Relationship:** The Internet has revolutionized medical communication, transforming the traditional private consultation (*colloque singulier*). Informed patients understand the differences between valve replacement, palliative repair, and reconstructive surgery.
* **Democratizing Expertise:** Reconstructive surgery must be made accessible to all patients by establishing surgical expertise in all cardiac centers, rather than restricting complex cases to an exclusive circle of "super subspecialists."
* **The Ultimate Surgical Mission:** *"The duty of any valvular surgeon today is no longer to correct a mitral valve regurgitation, but to correct a mitral valve regurgitation for the rest of the patient’s life."*
