# 3. Perioperative Management

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Preoperative Diagnostic Protocol
* **Standard Workup:** Comprehensive clinical examination, chest X-ray, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), routine laboratory profiling, and dental evaluation under antibiotic coverage.
* **Coronary Angiography Thresholds:** Mandatory in patients aged 45–50 years and older, and in younger individuals presenting with atherosclerotic risk factors (smoking, obesity, diabetes, hypercholesterolemia), impaired ventricular function, or congenital anomalies.
* **Advanced Cross-Sectional Imaging:** Computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is indicated for aortic root pathologies. Head and abdominal CT scans are imperative in infective endocarditis to detect clinically silent embolic foci and mycotic aneurysms. Preoperative chest CT is essential in reoperations to delineate retrosternal clearance between the ascending aorta, right ventricle, and sternal plate.

### Preoperative Medication Management
* **Maintenance Therapy:** Beta-blockers, antiarrhythmic agents, and diuretics should be continued until the morning of surgery.
* **Discontinuation Schedules:**
  * **ACE Inhibitors:** Discontinue 48 hours prior to operation to eliminate the risk of refractory vasoplegic intraoperative hypotension.
  * **Clopidogrel:** Discontinue 5–7 days preoperatively whenever clinically feasible.
  * **Aspirin:** May be safely continued in coronary patients without excessive bleeding risk, though a brief pause is optional.
  * **Warfarin:** Withheld 2–3 days preoperatively; high-risk patients (mechanical prostheses, severe LV dysfunction, atrial fibrillation) require bridge therapy with unfractionated or low-molecular-weight heparin.

### Hemodynamic Optimization and Mechanical Support
* **Preoperative Decompensation:** Decompensated mitral regurgitation requires aggressive diuretic diuresis and inotropic therapy for low cardiac output states.
* **Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP) Indications:** Preoperative balloon counterpulsation is mandatory in patients presenting with cardiogenic shock or acute pulmonary edema (e.g., acute papillary muscle rupture), and strongly recommended in patients with left ventricular ejection fraction < 15%.
* **The "Pop-Off" Myth Refuted:** The historical notion of preserving mild residual mitral regurgitation to unload the left ventricle is obsolete. Modern pharmacological afterload reduction and temporary mechanical circulatory support effectively manage post-repair hemodynamics.

### Postoperative Care and Intensive Care Management
* **Early Extubation:** Patients with preserved ventricular function, adequate urinary output, and normal neurological status should be extubated within hours postoperatively.
* **Hemodynamic and Afterload Management:** Weaning from inotropes is coupled with early initiation of ACE inhibitors once minimal inotropic support is reached and systolic blood pressure is $\ge 110\text{ mmHg}$. Pulmonary hypertension is managed with inhaled nitric oxide, nesiritide, or phosphodiesterase inhibitors transitioning to oral sildenafil.
* **Tight Glycemic Control:** Continuous intravenous insulin infusion maintaining target blood glucose at 80–110 mg/dL is standard for all patients (diabetic and non-diabetic alike) for at least 48 hours.

### Anticoagulation and Rhythm Strategies
* **Post-Reconstruction Warfarin:** Coumadin is administered for 2 months targeting an INR of 2.0–2.5 to cover the period of prosthetic ring endothelization and prevent thromboembolism during the peak incidence of postoperative atrial fibrillation (seen in ~ 20% of patients).
* **Pediatric Exception:** Aspirin alone replaces warfarin in children with preserved ventricular function and minimal left atrial enlargement.
* **Cardioversion Protocol:** Postoperative atrial fibrillation is managed initially with rate control (beta-blockers, amiodarone) and anticoagulation. Electrical cardioversion is scheduled after several weeks of amiodarone (400 mg/day for 1 week) following transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) to rule out atrial thrombi. Long-standing chronic atrial fibrillation (up to 5 years) can successfully convert if the atrial myocardium is non-fibrotic.
* **Operative Mortality Benchmarks:** Reconstructive surgery carries an operative mortality of 0.5%–2% in degenerative and rheumatic disease, 4%–6% in ischemic and dilated cardiomyopathy, and < 0.5% in elective low-risk patients.

## PREOPERATIVE INVESTIGATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

Sound perioperative management of the patient undergoing a valve operation requires careful attention to details to ensure an optimal outcome.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Standard Diagnostic Workup</summary><div class="details-content">

All patients undergoing valvular surgery should have a complete workup including clinical examination, chest X-ray, transthoracic echocardiography, and anesthesia consultation. In addition, laboratory tests are used to detect any biological or bacteriological anomalies.

In diabetic patients, it is important to assess the effectiveness of blood glucose level control by measurement of hemoglobin A1c. Associated organ dysfunctions such as respiratory insufficiency, gastrointestinal disorders, cerebrovascular disease, or renal failure require targeted supplementary testing.

Finally, with the exception of emergency cases, a complete dental clearance should be obtained. Extensive dental extraction, commonly advised in the past, is no longer required provided that all decayed teeth are treated conservatively under proper antibiotic coverage.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Advanced Imaging and Coronary Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with aortic root disease require computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Coronary angiography is recommended in patients beyond the age of 45–50 years or in younger patients with congenital malformations, depressed ventricular function, and/or atherosclerotic risk factors. Right and left heart catheterization should be additional tests ordered for patients with depressed ventricular function and/or congenital malformations.

Because of the high risk of systemic embolization, patients with endocarditis should undergo head and abdominal computed tomography to detect asymptomatic foci of embolization or mycotic aneurysms.

In the reoperative setting, chest computed tomography is essential to assess the anatomical relationship between the ascending aorta, the right ventricle, and the posterior table of the sternum.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Risk Stratification and Patient Information</summary><div class="details-content">

Preoperative investigations allow determination of the risk factors for morbidity and mortality in each individual patient using standard scoring systems (such as EuroSCORE).

The patient should always be carefully informed about his or her disease, the proposed operation in light of the surgeon’s experience, and the risk factors linked to the patient’s condition and this operation.

</div></details>

## PREOPERATIVE CARE</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Chronic Medications</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients undergoing valvular surgery should continue their medications, including beta-blockers, diuretics, and drugs for associated diseases, until the night before the operation.

There are a few important exceptions:
* **ACE Inhibitors:** It is advisable to discontinue angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors 48 hours before surgery to avoid refractory intraoperative hypotension.
* **Antiplatelet Agents:** A significant number of patients with previous percutaneous transluminal coronary angioplasty and stenting are referred for surgery while taking clopidogrel; in those patients it is advisable to discontinue clopidogrel 5–7 days before surgery whenever possible. In contrast, patients taking aspirin for coronary artery disease can undergo surgery without a significant increase of postoperative bleeding, although it is still preferable to discontinue this drug for a few days before surgery.
* **Oral Anticoagulants:** Patients taking warfarin generally discontinue this medication 2–3 days before surgery. Patients at high risk for thromboembolic complications (such as low ejection fraction, atrial fibrillation, or previously implanted mechanical valves) should be protected by an alternative anticoagulant strategy such as intravenous unfractionated heparin or subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Hemodynamic Optimization and Mechanical Support</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with mitral regurgitation and severe heart failure require hemodynamic optimization before surgery. This includes increased amounts of diuretics to address volume overload.

In patients with low cardiac output or low mixed venous oxygen saturation, inotropic support may be necessary.

In patients with severe acute decompensation from acute mitral regurgitation secondary to conditions such as papillary muscle rupture, intra-aortic balloon counterpulsation (IABP) is essential to stabilize hemodynamic conditions prior to surgical induction.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

Based on years of experience, most patients benefiting from valve reconstruction have a very low operative mortality (< 1%). However, appropriate postoperative care is critical to ensure the optimal success of the operation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### ICU Care and Hemodynamic Weaning</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with preserved ventricular function, good urine output, optimal arterial blood gases, and normal neurological status are extubated within hours following the completion of the cardiac procedure.

Patients with ventricular dysfunction should be weaned progressively from inotropic support. Afterload reduction with ACE inhibitors should be initiated when the patient is receiving minimal inotropic support with a systolic blood pressure $\ge 110\text{ mmHg}$. Most patients also require intravenous and then oral diuretics at least for a few weeks.

Patients with persistent pulmonary artery hypertension can benefit from the administration of inhaled nitric oxide, nesiritide, and phosphodiesterase inhibitors in the intensive care unit followed by oral sildenafil citrate.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Strict Glycemic Control</summary><div class="details-content">

Strict glycemic control is important for all patients. Intensive insulin therapy with a target blood glucose level of 80–110 mg/dL should be instituted for both diabetic and non-diabetic patients.

An intravenous continuous insulin infusion is started intraoperatively and continued postoperatively.

The intravenous insulin therapy is transitioned to subcutaneous therapy or oral antidiabetic therapy, or it is discontinued after 48 hours.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Anticoagulation Protocol</summary><div class="details-content">

Anticoagulation is an important adjunct to valve surgery. Following valve reconstruction, patients are systematically anticoagulated with warfarin (Coumadin) with a goal International Normalized Ratio (INR) of 2.0–2.5 for approximately 2 months, which represents the time necessary for the prosthetic ring and suture lines to be fully covered by host tissue.

Atrial fibrillation is present in about 20% of patients after reconstructive valve surgery. Beta-blockers should be administered postoperatively to decrease the rate of new-onset atrial fibrillation.

Most episodes of atrial fibrillation can be treated with a rate control strategy using beta-blockers or amiodarone and oral anticoagulation. In patients with atrial fibrillation, the use of intravenous heparin remains controversial because of the risk-benefit ratio between mediastinal bleeding and thromboembolism.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Atrial Fibrillation and Cardioversion</summary><div class="details-content">

In patients with atrial fibrillation persisting longer than 2 months, cardioversion should be attempted after the patients have received a full dose of amiodarone (400 mg/day for 1 week) and after transesophageal echocardiography has demonstrated the absence of atrial thrombi.

In chronic atrial fibrillation, cardioversion is generally considered unsuccessful. Our experience, however, has shown that some patients with atrial fibrillation that has persisted up to 5 years can be converted into sinus rhythm by electrical shock. The chance of success is increased in younger patients with a moderately enlarged left atrium and a preserved thickness of the left atrial wall with no fibrotic transformation.

In patients having had a successful concomitant Maze procedure, warfarin should be continued for several months until the durability of sinus rhythm is firmly demonstrated.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Predischarge Evaluation and Operative Outcomes</summary><div class="details-content">

Predischarge echocardiography is recommended to assess both valvular and ventricular function and to serve as an objective reference for long-term follow-up.

Valve reconstructive surgery today can be performed with a very low operative mortality and low incidence of complications provided that normal valve function has been restored and rigorous perioperative management has been carried out.

The operative mortality in patients with rheumatic or degenerative valvular disease is 0.5%–2%. In patients with ischemic or dilated cardiomyopathy, operative mortality ranges between 4%–6%. In selected elective cases, operative mortality should be less than 0.5%, which justifies considering early operation in totally asymptomatic patients with severe mitral valve regurgitation.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION AND SURGICAL DIALOGUE</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Standard Diagnostic Workup and Coronary Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

**Dr. David H. Adams (DA):** What is the standard workup for an otherwise healthy 45-year-old patient undergoing an operation for degenerative valve disease?

**Prof. Alain Carpentier (AC):** The standard workup should be very simple. It comprises clinical examination, chest X-ray, transthoracic echocardiography, dental workup, routine laboratory tests, and anesthesia consultation. Heart catheterization or CT angiography is not necessary in the absence of risk factors such as smoking, obesity, hypercholesterolemia, and diabetes. Beyond the age of 50 years, however, a coronary imaging study is recommended.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Role of Preoperative vs. Intraoperative TEE</summary><div class="details-content">

**Dr. Farzan Filsoufi (FF):** Do you request a preoperative transesophageal echocardiography?

**AC:** Usually not before the operation, but always intraoperatively. In the operating room, pre-bypass transesophageal echocardiography confirms the valve dysfunction recognized by transthoracic echo. It allows precise localization of the dysfunctioning segments where the surgeon should concentrate attention to make a full inventory of the lesions. It also provides important information with regard to the size of the annulus, the size of the anterior leaflet, and the degree and extent of prolapse.

Post-bypass transesophageal echocardiography assesses the quality of valve reconstruction, ventricular function, and the completeness of de-airing. At discharge, transthoracic echocardiography serves as a reference document.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Role of Ventriculography in Valvular Assessment</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Does ventriculography have any role in assessing mitral valve regurgitation?

**AC:** It would be an unnecessary expense because it is not a relevant method to assess mitral valve function. In addition, it carries a potential risk of renal failure caused by the toxicity of radiographic contrast in selected patients.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The "Pop-Off" Concept and Afterload Management</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Tell us the history of the "pop-off" concept of mitral valve regurgitation in the setting of severe mitral valve dysfunction.

**AC:** Years ago, concerns were expressed about possible adverse consequences of increasing afterload following the correction of severe mitral regurgitation, and many surgeons believed that leaving a certain degree of valve regurgitation could be beneficial. Today, it seems more an excuse to accept a suboptimal result than a true concern for afterload. Modern pharmacological therapy and, in select cases, balloon counterpulsation make the "pop-off" concept completely obsolete.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Hybrid Procedures and Revascularization</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In patients with coronary artery disease undergoing a valve operation, what is the role of hybrid procedures?

**AC:** In primary valve operations I do not see any role for hybrid procedures. Since a valve operation is necessary, a complete surgical revascularization should be performed during the same procedure with the additional advantage of using the internal mammary arteries. Hybrid procedures are useful, however, in patients with previous mediastinitis or previous coronary artery bypass grafts, particularly in the presence of a critical stenosis of a marginal branch.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Preoperative Intra-Aortic Balloon Counterpulsation</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** When do you recommend the preoperative insertion of an intra-aortic balloon pump in patients with severely depressed ventricular function undergoing mitral valve surgery?

**AC:** It is a necessary practice for patients in cardiogenic shock or with pulmonary edema, and it is advisable for patients with an ejection fraction less than 15%.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Rationale for Two Months of Warfarin Anticoagulation</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Do you recommend anticoagulation with warfarin following valve reconstruction?

**AC:** Yes; warfarin may be administered for a period of 2 months unless there are serious contraindications such as increased bleeding risk from advanced age, gastrointestinal diseases, or cerebral aneurysm. I recommend this for two reasons: first, 2 months are necessary for the suture lines and the ring to be fully covered by host tissues; second, there is a risk of atrial fibrillation within the weeks following surgery. Anticoagulation can be discontinued 2 months after the operation in the absence of arrhythmia, left atrial thrombus, or recent thromboembolism.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Aspirin Alternative: Indications and Pediatric Exception</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Several centers recommend aspirin therapy alone following valve reconstruction in patients with no history of atrial fibrillation. What is your opinion?

**AC:** They may be right, but I am not aware of any randomized trial supporting this policy. Knowing that even in patients with no previous history of atrial fibrillation postoperative episodes of arrhythmia may occur, I recommend using warfarin for 2 months at reasonable doses to achieve a 2.0–2.5 INR. The only circumstance in which I use aspirin instead of warfarin is in children with preserved heart function and moderately enlarged left atria because the possibility of postoperative atrial fibrillation is rare in children.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Optimal Timing of Cardioversion</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** What is the optimal timing of cardioversion in patients with new-onset or recurrent atrial fibrillation following mitral valve repair?

**AC:** Before considering a cardioversion, one should attempt to determine the cause of arrhythmia: examples include valve dysfunction, coronary artery disease, systemic hypertension, heart failure, electrolyte imbalance, or discontinuation of antiarrhythmic medications. These causes should be vigorously addressed before proceeding with cardioversion.

If no obvious cause has been identified, cardioversion may be attempted after several weeks of medical treatment with amiodarone or a beta-blocker. If an initial attempt is unsuccessful, a repeat cardioversion should be performed a few weeks later. If multiple attempts have been unsuccessful, there is no reason to persist. Whenever atrial fibrillation has significant hemodynamic consequences, an earlier cardioversion under intravenous pharmacological treatment may be attempted.

</div></details>
