# Chapter 7. Valve Exposure, Intraoperative Valve Analysis, and Reconstruction

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Mitral Valve Exposure
* **Interatrial Approach:** The standard, preferred approach in most instances. Provides excellent exposure of the mitral valve with significantly less myocardial trauma than Dubost’s biatrial transseptal approach. Dissection proceeds along the interatrial groove to the fossa ovalis; the left atrial roof is incised after cardioplegic arrest and extended 1 cm from the superior vena cava superiorly and midway between the right inferior pulmonary vein and the inferior vena cava inferiorly ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01)).
* **Sternal Retraction System:** Maximal left atrial exposure is achieved using a specialized self-retaining sternal retractor with coordinated upper, lower, and lateral blades ([Fig. 7-2](#fig-07-02)).
* **Maneuvers for Difficult Exposure:** Complete pericardial adhesiolysis up to the apex, dissection of the vena cavae with cephalad traction on caval tapes, extension of the atriotomy, and a 1-cm vertical right atrial and transverse septal extension up to the fossa ovalis ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01), insets).
* **Transseptal Approach:** Indicated in reoperative cases after prior aortic valve replacement, small left atrium, dense right atrial-pericardial adhesions, or giant left atrium where incision placement in a high-velocity flow area reduces thromboembolic risk ([Fig. 7-3](#fig-07-03)).

### Systematic Intraoperative Valve Analysis ("Fil d'Ariane")
* **Sequential Clockwise Functional Analysis:** Systematic inspection begins in the left atrium (endocardial thickening, calcification, jet lesions) and annulus, followed by tactile interrogation of all leaflet segments using 2 nerve hooks ([Fig. 7-4](#fig-07-04)).
* **The Reference Point Method:** Segment P1 serves as the primary reference point in most cases because it is rarely affected by abnormal leaflet motion. The free edge of P1 is pulled upward; its normal motion is confirmed when it does not override the plane of the annulus and is not tethered. All other segments are sequentially compared against P1.
* **Managing Complex Reference Scenarios:** If P1 is diseased, an alternate normal segment is selected. In diffuse multisegment prolapse or Barlow’s disease, 1 posterior leaflet segment is repaired first (placing its free edge 5 to 8 mm below the annular plane) to serve as the reference point for the remaining segments.
* **Subvalvular Apparatus Exposure:** When reconstruction involves the papillary muscles, traction sutures on anterior leaflet chordae, atraumatic leaflet retractors, Rezzano forceps, and lateral placement of a papillary muscle clamp provide safe visualization ([Fig. 7-5](#fig-07-05)).

### Fundamental Principles of Valve Reconstruction
* **"One Lesion, One Technique":** Reconstruction addresses individual anatomical lesions rather than the disease as a whole.
* **The 3 Reconstructive Dogmas:**
  1. Preserving or restoring normal leaflet motion across all valve segments.
  2. Creating a large surface of leaflet coaptation.
  3. Remodeling and stabilizing the mitral annulus with ring annuloplasty ([Fig. 7-6](#fig-07-06)).
* **Surgical Precedence:** Although prosthetic ring annuloplasty is the final step executed during mitral reconstruction, its foundational role in stabilizing geometry justifies studying it first.

## MITRAL VALVE EXPOSURE</summary><div class="details-content">

After extracorporeal circulation has been instituted, adequate exposure of the mitral valve is mandatory to perform a safe and effective operation. Pericardial adhesions, if present, are released up to the apex.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Interatrial Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

The interatrial approach is used in most instances ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01)). It provides better access to the mitral valve than the classic direct atrial approach and less damage than Dubost’s biatrial transseptal approach.

The interatrial groove is dissected and the 2 atria are divided up to the fossa ovalis (a). This dissection exposes the roof of the left atrium, which is incised after the heart has been arrested by cardioplegia (b).

The incision is extended superiorly to 1 cm from the superior vena cava, and inferiorly to midway between the right inferior pulmonary vein and the inferior vena cava (c, d). If the right atrium is opened inadvertently, it is closed later when left atrial closure is performed.

<span id="fig-07-01"></span>![](_page_64_Figure_7-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7-1**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Sternal Retraction and Exposure Maneuvers</summary><div class="details-content">

Maximal exposure of the left atrium is obtained using the self-retaining sternal retractor ([Fig. 7-2](#fig-07-02)). The upper blade is positioned first: its curvature is pushed upwards with the right thumb while the left hand hooks the blade to the sternal retractor.

The lower blade is then positioned by pushing its curvature upwards with the left thumb while its straight portion is hooked using the right hand. A lateral blade is available to improve the exposure of the posterior aspect of the mitral valve if necessary.

<span id="fig-07-02"></span>![](_page_65_Figure_7-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7-2**

#### Adjunct Maneuvers for Suboptimal Exposure
If exposure of the mitral valve remains suboptimal, several additional maneuvers may be useful:
* All pericardial adhesions are released up to the apex.
* The vena cavae are dissected from the pericardial tissue and upwards traction is placed on the caval tapes.
* The left atrial incision is extended between the inferior vena cava and the right inferior pulmonary vein.
* A 1-cm incision perpendicular to the atrial incision is added involving the right atrium vertically and the septum transversally up to the fossa ovalis ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01), e, insets). This adjunct technique replaces the classic Dubost biatrial incision.

Once the atrium is fully opened and the mitral valve exposed, a left atrial vent is placed to drain the pulmonary venous return.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Transseptal Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

To obtain optimal exposure, a transseptal approach may be indicated in cases with previous aortic valve replacement, a small left atrium, or the presence of severe adhesions between the right atrium and the pericardium ([Fig. 7-3](#fig-07-03)).

This approach is also favored in patients with a giant left atrium to obtain easier access to the mitral valve and to position the incision in an area of high-velocity blood flow, thus minimizing the risk of clot formation.

After a craniocaudal (longitudinal) right atriotomy (a), the septum is incised through the fossa ovalis toward the space between the coronary sinus and the orifice of the inferior vena cava (b). Inverted mattress sutures are placed to hold the septal edge (c), which is then retracted with blades (d).

## VALVE ANALYSIS</summary><div class="details-content">

The aim of mitral valve analysis is to confirm or modify the preoperative echocardiographic findings, the functional type, and the segmental localization. Then, a full inventory of the lesions is undertaken.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Systematic Left Atrial and Annular Inspection</summary><div class="details-content">

The left atrium is carefully inspected. Endocardial thickening, thrombus formation, calcification, or jet lesions are noted.

Jet lesions are important as they indicate a leaflet prolapse opposite to the jet or a restricted leaflet motion on the side of the jet ([Fig. 7-3](#fig-07-03), d).

The mitral annulus is examined first to evaluate the degree of annular dilatation and its symmetrical or asymmetrical shape, and to recognize occasional calcifications or fibrin deposits.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Segmental Functional Analysis ("Fil d'Ariane")</summary><div class="details-content">

The leaflets are examined with 2 nerve hooks to proceed to a "functional analysis" of all the leaflet segments in an organized manner ([Fig. 7-4](#fig-07-04)). Tissue pliability and leaflet motion are analyzed, beginning at the P1 segment and proceeding clockwise.

The free edge of P1 typically serves as "the reference point," since P1 is more rarely affected by abnormal leaflet motion than other leaflet segments.

The normal leaflet motion of P1 should be confirmed, however, by pulling its free edge upwards with a nerve hook. The free edge should not override the plane of the mitral orifice nor be tethered by short chordae or a displaced papillary muscle.

<span id="fig-07-04"></span>![](_page_67_Figure_7-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7-4**

#### Sequential Segment Comparison
Using another hook, the remaining valvular segments are examined in a sequential manner and compared to P1 to determine whether they are prolapsed or restricted.

The "segmental functional analysis" is the "Fil d'Ariane" (guiding thread) of mitral valve reconstructive surgery. It allows recognition and localization of the dysfunctions, analysis of the lesions, and selection of the appropriate reconstructive techniques.

Whenever P1 motion is abnormal, another segment should be identified to serve as the reference point. Whenever a prolapse involves all the leaflet segments, 1 segment should be corrected first with the annulus serving as the reference point, and the remaining segments should be corrected using the corrected segment as the reference point.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Exposure of the Subvalvular Apparatus</summary><div class="details-content">

Proper exposure of the subvalvular apparatus is necessary whenever valvular reconstruction involves 1 or 2 papillary muscles. Traction sutures are first placed around the main chordae of the anterior leaflet ([Fig. 7-5](#fig-07-05)).

The posterior leaflet is gently retracted using the atraumatic retractor. In most instances, the anterior papillary muscle is easily visualized.

Exposure of the posterior papillary muscle is more difficult and requires placement of an atraumatic retractor in the P3 region. The head of the posterior papillary muscle is grasped and pulled upwards using Rezzano forceps. This maneuver exposes muscular and fibrous bands that attach the base of the papillary muscle to the ventricular wall.

<span id="fig-07-05"></span>![](_page_68_Figure_7-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7-5**

#### Papillary Muscle Clamping
A papillary muscle clamp is used to further expose the papillary muscle. The clamp is placed laterally to the papillary muscle across the fibrous bands, carefully avoiding its base ([Fig. 7-5](#fig-07-05), inset).

</div></details>

## FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES OF VALVE RECONSTRUCTION</summary><div class="details-content">

Guided by functional valve analysis, the surgeon should identify all the lesions, whether primary or secondary, and then proceed to valve reconstruction according to the "one lesion one technique principle."

The ultimate aim of valve reconstruction is to restore durable normal valve function. 3 basic conditions are required to achieve this goal ([Fig. 7-6](#fig-07-06)):
1. Preserving or restoring normal leaflet motion of all valve segments.
2. Creating a large surface of leaflet coaptation.
3. Remodeling the mitral valve orifice.

<span id="fig-07-06"></span>![](_page_69_Figure_7-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7-6**

#### Implementation Strategy
Restoring normal valve function may imply the resection of pathological tissues (such as calcification) or excess tissue whenever necessary.

In the following chapters of this section, the techniques of valve reconstruction are described according to the "functional approach" and the "one lesion one technique principle."

Although among these techniques ring annuloplasty is the last step of a mitral valve reconstruction, its key importance justifies studying it first.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Approach in Minimally Invasive Surgery (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: In patients undergoing less invasive mitral valve surgery using the lower median ministernotomy approach, do you recommend an interatrial or a transseptal approach?

**AC:** Either 1 is applicable but I prefer the interatrial approach because the atrial edge is stronger and easier to retract than a septal edge. In addition, it is a less traumatic incision.

#### FF: Under which circumstances do you convert a less invasive approach into a more invasive approach?

**AC:** Whenever the conditions for visualization of the mitral valve or for access to the subvalvular apparatus are suboptimal, thus compromising the quality of valve reconstruction, a more open surgical approach should be used. One should never proceed to a valve reconstruction without a perfect view of all valvular structures.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mitral Valve Exposure via Aortotomy (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Can we precisely analyze and treat the mitral valve through the aortic root?

**AC:** Through the aortic root, the surgeon can only see the ventricular aspect of the anterior leaflet. Therefore a comprehensive valve analysis is not possible with regard to treatment. A left atrial exposure is always preferable when dealing with an associated mitral valve regurgitation.

On the contrary, an aortotomy should be associated with a left atrial approach in patients with mitral valve endocarditis involving the anterior leaflet and in patients with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy and mitral valve regurgitation.

The only procedure that can be performed through the aortic root is the resection of calcium formations or thickened or tethered secondary chordae of the anterior leaflet.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mitral Valve Intervention via Left Ventriculotomy (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: When do you consider a valve repair through a left ventriculotomy?

**AC:** A palliative procedure such as edge-to-edge valve repair or reduction annuloplasty or even a valve replacement in patients requiring a concomitant resection of a large ventricular aneurysm or plaque is possible through a left ventriculotomy.

It is most preferable, however, to approach the valve through a left atriotomy, which allows a precise valve analysis and a true valve reconstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Atrial Retraction During Cardioplegia Delivery (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Should the blades retracting the left atrium be removed during antegrade cardioplegia delivery?

**AC:** It is preferable to release the traction on the retracting blades during antegrade cardioplegia delivery to avoid potential compression of the right coronary artery and prevent possible aortic valve distorsion and subsequent regurgitation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Timing of Valve Analysis: Fibrillating Heart vs. Cardioplegic Arrest (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: For many years, you performed valve analysis before cardioplegia. What was your rationale for this approach?

**AC:** I used to analyze the valve on a fibrillating heart because cardioplegia makes the heart flaccid, which may produce a false leaflet prolapse.

This is no longer advisable because accurate valve analysis is obtained today by echocardiography and intraoperative valve examination using the "reference point method."

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Evolution of Valve Analysis: Intraoperative Tactile Assessment vs. Echocardiography (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Before the introduction of echocardiography, was the intraoperative assessment of leaflet pliability and motion reliable enough to guide valve reconstruction?

**AC:** It was to a certain extent, but echocardiography brought more precision and an improved reliability.

Echocardiography proved to be particularly useful in type IIIb dysfunction. I remember mistakes I made while treating this condition because the traction with nerve hooks on a leaflet supported by a thin, infarcted ventricular wall produced false leaflet prolapse, which was caused by upward displacement of the papillary muscle under traction.

Correcting this prolapse was the opposite of what I should have done. Today, echocardiography easily identifies a type IIIb regurgitation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Resolving Discrepancies Between Echocardiography and Surgical Analysis (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Whenever there is a discrepancy between preoperative echocardiographic findings and intraoperative valve analysis, which results do you consider more reliable?

**AC:** A discrepancy in valve analysis requires that the surgeon repeats valve analysis, which therefore makes it more reliable than echocardiographic findings. For example, a commissural prolapse is not very well visualized by echocardiography whereas a surgical valve analysis can easily identify this dysfunction.

Another example is a discrete prolapse of the anterior leaflet in rheumatic valvular disease, which should be recognized and corrected.

With the exception of type IIIb mitral regurgitation for the reason I just described, the surgical analysis should prevail with the surgeon having "le dernier mot" (the final say).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Establishing the Reference Point in Complex Barlow's Disease (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: How do you define the reference point in diffuse Barlow’s disease with bileaflet prolapse?

**AC:** In severe Barlow’s disease, P1 cannot be used as a reference point because it may also be prolapsed like the other segments. It may also display restricted leaflet motion when fibrous retraction or calcification of the tip of the anterior papillary muscle is present.

In these circumstances, the surgeon should explore the other segments of the valve and select as the reference point a segment with the free edge located at least 4 to 5 mm below the plane of the orifice when traction is exerted with a nerve hook.

If no segment exists that meets this condition, the surgeon should first correct the prolapse of any 1 segment of the posterior leaflet, repositioning the free edge 5 to 8 mm below the plane of the annulus. The free edge of this corrected segment is then used as a reference point to reconstruct the other segments.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Subvalvular Mesh and Abnormal Chordal Network in Barlow's Disease (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Does the abnormal chordae network in Barlow’s disease influence valve analysis?

**AC:** The abnormal chordae network does not influence the functional valve analysis, but it makes valve reconstruction more complex; this is attributable to the mesh underneath the leaflets, formed by abnormal transverse chordae, that delineates pockets of leaflet tissue and restricts leaflet pliability.

Since a quadrangular resection of a segment of the posterior leaflet is usually performed, it provides extensive access to the subvalvular apparatus.

The transverse chordae of the adjacent segments can then be easily visualized and resected to ensure leaflet mobility and eliminate the pockets of tissue, which may be the site of platelet aggregates and source of embolization.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Assessment of Leaflet Free Edge in Type IIIb Dysfunction (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: In type IIIb dysfunction, how do you assess the position of the free edge of the anterior leaflet?

**AC:** Whenever type IIIb dysfunction is asymmetrical and predominant in the P3 segment, such as in ischemic mitral valve regurgitation, the position of the free edge of the anterior leaflet should be assessed first by echo and then intraoperatively by comparing A1, the usually non-tethered segment, to A3, the most tethered segment.

In symmetrical type IIIb dysfunction such as seen in dilated cardiomyopathy, the free edges of all leaflet segments are compared to the level of the annulus.

</div></details>
