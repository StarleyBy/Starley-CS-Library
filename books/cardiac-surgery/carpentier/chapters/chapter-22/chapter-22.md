# Chapter 22. Reconstructive Techniques

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Fundamentals of Aortic Valve Reconstruction
Several reconstructive techniques have been developed to correct aortic valve dysfunction. However, unlike mitral valve reconstruction, relatively few have achieved gold-standard status due to historical unpredictability in long-term durability. This limitation arises directly from the diminutive volume of tissue and narrow surface of coaptation in aortic leaflets compared with the atrioventricular valves. Successful aortic reconstruction adheres strictly to three fundamental principles:
1. Restore or preserve full leaflet mobility;
2. Provide a large, stable surface of leaflet coaptation;
3. Remodel and stabilize the functional aortic annulus.

### Surgical Approach and Exposure
Access is achieved via a transverse aortotomy placed 5 mm above the sinotubular junction, extendable into the non-coronary sinus of Valsalva if exposure is constrained. Two atraumatic retractors apply gentle suspension on the right and non-coronary sinus rims to present the aortic root and valve apparatus for systematic analysis.

### Technique Spectrum by Functional Dysfunction
- **Type I (Normal Leaflet Motion):**
  - *Annular Dilatation:* Corrected with a double-row circular annuloplasty using vertical 2-0 braided mattress sutures passed downward and upward through the annulus to achieve homogeneous circular narrowing without leaflet distortion.
  - *Leaflet Perforation:* Reconstructed using glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patches tailored with a 2 mm seam allowance and anchored with interrupted 5-0 monofilament sutures tied on the aortic aspect.
  - *Infective Vegetations:* Managed by sharp scissor excision followed by topical chemical sterilization with 0.6% glutaraldehyde for 2 to 3 minutes and thorough saline irrigation.
  - *Traumatic Tears or Avulsions:* Repaired by direct primary suture or pericardial patch interposition.
- **Type II (Leaflet Prolapse):**
  - Restored via calibrated central triangular resection with curved, convex margins (adding 2 mm per side for closure), re-approximating leaflet continuity with interrupted 5-0 monofilament sutures tied strictly on the aortic aspect to prevent hemolysis and abrasion.
- **Type III (Restricted Leaflet Motion):**
  - *Type IIIa:* Mobilized by targeted commissurotomy with wedge resection of fused fibrous tissue (avoiding tissue shaving or decalcification). Retracted leaflets are augmented via pericardial cusp extension (transverse basal incision with an ovoid patch, or free-edge augmentation with a 6 to 8 mm high rectangular patch).
  - *Type IIIb:* Addressed via valve-sparing aortic root operations (David reimplantation or Yacoub remodeling) or supracoronary tube graft replacement to restore the physiologic ratio between the sinotubular junction and annulus.

### Multivalvular Sequencing and Clinical Outcomes
In multivalve interventions, the surgical sequence follows rigid hemodynamic logic: mitral first, aortic second, and tricuspid third (mitral always first, tricuspid always last). Long-term clinical experience from Hôpital Broussais in 95 young rheumatic patients demonstrated 3.3% operative mortality and durable reconstruction in 87% of patients at 11 years. A subsequent series of 89 young patients (mean age 16 years) undergoing standardized triple aortic cusp extension achieved 2.2% operative mortality and 90% freedom from valve-related complications at 7 years.

## SURGICAL APPROACH AND FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLES</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Exposure of the Aortic Valve
Several repair techniques have been described to correct aortic valve dysfunction. In contrast to mitral valve repair techniques, few of them have become the gold standard because of the unpredictability of their results. This is primarily attributable to the small amount of tissue and consequent small surface of coaptation present in the aortic leaflets, as compared to the large amount of tissue found in the mitral and tricuspid valves. Nonetheless, certain aortic valve lesions are amenable to reconstructive valvular techniques, particularly in young patients.

The aortic valve is approached through a transverse aortotomy placed 5 mm above the sinotubular junction ([Figure 22-1](#fig-22-01), a). Whenever the surgical exposure is suboptimal, the aortotomy can be extended obliquely downward towards the non-coronary sinus of Valsalva.

<span id="fig-22-01"></span>![](images/_page_221_Figure_22-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-1**

Two atraumatic aortic retractors are used to apply gentle traction on the edge of the right coronary sinus and the non-coronary sinus ([Figure 22-1](#fig-22-01), b). The surgeon then proceeds to a careful analysis of both the aortic root and the leaflets to recognize the specific lesions responsible for the leaflet dysfunction identified by preoperative echocardiography.

#### Principles of Aortic Reconstruction
The surgeon's intraoperative findings guide the choice of the reconstructive technique according to the fundamental "One lesion, one technique" principle. Similar to mitral valve reconstruction, three basic principles of valve reconstruction should be respected to achieve an optimal and stable long-term result:
1. Restore or preserve full leaflet mobility;
2. Provide an optimal, large surface of leaflet coaptation;
3. Remodel and stabilize the annulus.

## TECHNIQUES IN TYPE I DYSFUNCTION: NORMAL LEAFLET MOTION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Isolated Aortic Annular Dilatation
Aortic valve regurgitation with normal leaflet motion is rare and often amenable to valve reconstruction. The surgical techniques vary depending upon the underlying anatomical lesions.

Annular dilatation without involvement of the sinotubular junction is rare ([Figure 22-2](#fig-22-02)). However, it may occur in dilated cardiomyopathy and in chronic rheumatic valvular disease, causing mild to moderate regurgitation in association with concomitant mitral valve dysfunction.

<span id="fig-22-02"></span>![](images/_page_222_Figure_22-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-2**

If the leaflets display minimal thickening and/or retraction, a circular annuloplasty is carried out to ensure adequate leaflet coaptation. A double-row circular annuloplasty provides a homogeneous, circular narrowing of the dilated annulus. This technique employs a continuous vertical braided 2-0 mattress suture passed through the annulus, successively downward from the aorta toward the ventricle and then upward from the ventricle toward the aorta ([Figure 22-2](#fig-22-02), a).

Optimal remodeling of the orifice is obtained when the leaflets coapt without excessive folding of their free edges ([Figure 22-2](#fig-22-02), b). A second row of sutures is then added that opposes the mattress loops so as to obtain a continuous line of suture ([Figure 22-2](#fig-22-02), c).

#### Leaflet Perforation
In the setting of chronic or healed bacterial endocarditis, leaflet perforation may involve only the belly of the leaflet, while the free edge is completely preserved ([Figure 22-3](#fig-22-03)). The defect is usually limited and displays a healed fibrotic edge ([Figure 22-3](#fig-22-03), a).

<span id="fig-22-03"></span>![](images/_page_222_Figure_22-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-3**

This condition is favorable for valve reconstruction by patching using glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium ([Figure 22-3](#fig-22-03), b). The patch is tailored according to the shape and size of the defect, adding a 2 mm margin for suturing.

The pericardial patch is sutured to the edge of the defect using interrupted 5-0 monofilament sutures tied on the aortic side of the leaflet. In the presence of a larger defect, a continuous suture can be utilized provided that narrow or locked bites are employed to avoid an unwanted purse-string effect.

#### Infective Vegetations
Small and pedicled vegetations can usually be sharply excised with scissors without producing a leaflet perforation ([Figure 22-4](#fig-22-04), a). Once the excision has been completed, local application of 0.6% glutaraldehyde for 2 to 3 minutes followed by generous rinsing with saline achieves local sterilization ([Figure 22-4](#fig-22-04), b).

<span id="fig-22-04"></span>![](images/_page_223_Figure_22-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-4**

Larger vegetations must be resected and the resulting valve defect repaired by pericardial patching. In all instances, the ventricular aspect of the anterior mitral leaflet should be carefully inspected to detect potential "kissing lesions." An associated annular dilatation producing a lack of coaptation between leaflets requires an additional circular annuloplasty.

#### Leaflet Tear or Avulsion
A leaflet tear or avulsion from the annulus resulting from blunt chest trauma can also be reconstructed in many instances ([Figure 22-5](#fig-22-05)). The most favorable lesion is a tear of the belly of the leaflet that leaves the commissural areas intact ([Figure 22-5](#fig-22-05), a).

<span id="fig-22-05"></span>![](images/_page_223_Figure_22-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-5**

This type of lesion can occasionally be treated by direct primary suture. If a significant lack of tissue is present, an autologous pericardial patch is secured to the edges of the tear ([Figure 22-5](#fig-22-05), c). Traumatic leaflet avulsion from the annulus is best treated by pericardial patching.

</div></details>

## TECHNIQUES IN TYPE II DYSFUNCTION: LEAFLET PROLAPSE</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Triangular Leaflet Resection
Type II aortic valve regurgitation may be corrected by reconstructive techniques, particularly when the prolapse is limited and involves only one leaflet, as encountered in some congenital malformations or rheumatic valve diseases.

Since the underlying mechanism of prolapse is usually distension of the free edge of the leaflet, appropriate tension and coaptation can be restored by a central triangular resection ([Figure 22-6](#fig-22-06), a). This technique implies that the commissures are preserved and functional.

<span id="fig-22-06"></span>![](images/_page_223_Figure_22-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-6**

The amount of triangular resection is calculated by measuring the length of the free margin of the corresponding halves of adjacent reference leaflets and adding 2 mm on each side of the triangle for suturing ([Figure 22-6](#fig-22-06), b). The two sides of the resected triangle should be tailored with a convex curvature to restore optimal bulging of the belly of the leaflet.

It is particularly important to remember that a triangular leaflet resection tends to be too extensive, leading to restricted leaflet motion. Leaflet continuity is restored using interrupted 5-0 monofilament sutures tied on the aortic side ([Figure 22-6](#fig-22-06), c), since knots positioned on the ventricular side may lead to mechanical abrasion of adjacent leaflets and/or hemolysis.

</div></details>

## TECHNIQUES IN TYPE III DYSFUNCTION: RESTRICTED MOTION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Type IIIa: Commissurotomy
Type IIIa aortic regurgitation may result from rheumatic valvular disease, senile valve sclerosis, or bicuspid valve malformation with commissural fusion. Whenever the amount of leaflet tissue is adequate with preserved pliability, leaflet mobilization by commissurotomy can be attempted.

<span id="fig-22-07"></span>![](images/_page_224_Figure_22-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-7**

The commissurotomy should ideally remove a wedge of fibrous tissue ([Figure 22-7](#fig-22-07), a, b). Leaflet shaving, which was used in the past, is no longer recommended because of secondary fibrous leaflet retraction. Leaflet decalcification should also be avoided for the same reason.

#### Type IIIa: Leaflet Extension
A retracted leaflet can be extended by patching using glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium ([Figure 22-8](#fig-22-08)).

<span id="fig-22-08"></span>![](images/_page_224_Figure_22-8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-8**

##### Patch Tailoring and Sizing Principles

Whenever the free edge of the retracted leaflet is well preserved, a transverse incision is performed at its base. An ovoid patch extension is then added using a locked continuous suture to avoid a purse-string effect ([Figure 22-8](#fig-22-08), a).

Whenever the free edge of the leaflet is retracted, a 6 to 8 mm high rectangular patch is tailored according to the shape and dimensions of the other leaflets ([Figure 22-8](#fig-22-08), b). A generous amount of extra tissue is necessary to compensate for the suturing and to slightly increase the surface of coaptation.

This technique has been extended to all three leaflets in young adults and children with reasonably good midterm results that help these patients avoid prosthetic valve replacement. The length of the patches is adjusted in relation to the intercommissural distances. In practice, the length of the patch is 3 to 4 mm greater than 1/3 of the aortic circumference at the sinotubular junction.

#### Type IIIb: Valve-Sparing Root Procedures
In patients with isolated sinotubular junction dilatation and well-preserved leaflets, a supracoronary tube graft interposition is often sufficient to correct aortic valve regurgitation. The goal of the procedure is to restore the normal ratio between the circumference of the sinotubular junction and the aortic annulus. In patients with extended aortic root dilatation, aortic valve-sparing procedures such as those described by Yacoub (remodeling) and David (reimplantation) represent an excellent indication.

## MULTIPLE VALVE OPERATIONS AND PROCEDURAL SEQUENCES</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Sequencing Principles in Complex Operations
In multiple valve operations, valve reconstruction is usually preferable in children and young adults to avoid double or triple valve replacement. However, valve reconstruction may be combined with valve replacement whenever severe, irreversible lesions make it necessary. The main technical question raised by a multiple valve operation is the precise operative sequence to be used.

In a combined mitral-aortic valve operation with aortic valve stenosis, the mitral valve should be treated first to benefit from multiple antegrade cardioplegia delivery through the aortic root and to facilitate the saline test after mitral valve reconstruction. If the aortic valve is regurgitant, the aorta should be opened for direct antegrade cardioplegia delivery through the coronary ostia, but the mitral valve is still treated first because the bulging and rigidity of the aortic root, whenever a prosthetic valve is used, would make it difficult to access the mitral annulus.

#### Specific Operative Sequences
The optimal sequences across multivalvular scenarios are as follows:
- **Mitral-Tricuspid Surgery:** Mitral first, tricuspid second, and closure of both atria third.
- **Aortic-Tricuspid Surgery:** Aortic valve first, followed by closure of the aorta to deliver antegrade cardioplegia through the aortic root, and tricuspid second.
- **Triple Valve Surgery:** Mitral first, aortic second, and tricuspid third.

In the setting of multiple valve endocarditis, radical debridement of all involved valves must be the first step before proceeding to surgical reconstruction or replacement.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Broussais Experience in Rheumatic Valve Reconstruction
In general, the results of aortic valve reconstruction have not been as gratifying as those of mitral or tricuspid valve reconstruction. In 1983 we reported a series of 95 young patients with rheumatic valvular disease who underwent surgery in our institution between January 1971 and December 1982. Aortic valve repair using the techniques described in this chapter was performed for all 95 patients.

Fifteen of these patients (16%) had isolated aortic valve involvement, whereas 80 patients (84%) had multiple valve diseases mostly treated by valve reconstruction. Hospital mortality was 3.3% for the entire group. Reoperation for severe residual aortic valve regurgitation was necessary in 8 patients (13%). Residual moderate aortic regurgitation persisted in 9 patients (15%), but reoperation was not necessary within the 11-year time frame of the study. From these results, we concluded that aortic valve reconstruction should only be used for favorable lesions in young adults and children.

#### Triple Cusp Extension Outcomes
More recently, our group reported a series of 89 patients with a mean age of 16 years who underwent triple aortic leaflet extension between 1992 and 2000 (Grinda et al.). All patients had rheumatic valvular disease with Type IIIa dysfunction.

<span id="fig-22-09"></span>![](images/_page_225_Figure_22-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 22-9**

The technique used was standardized and simple enough to be easily reproducible by less experienced surgeons. It consisted of tailoring three rectangular 8 mm wide strips of glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium. The strips were then carefully trimmed and sutured to the free edge of the leaflet tissue and the aorta above the commissures. No annuloplasty was deemed necessary because of the excess pericardial tissue.

Hospital mortality was 2.2%. At 7 years of follow-up, 76 patients (90%) were free from valve-related complications, and only 10% of them presented persistent or recurrent grade 2 aortic regurgitation ([Figure 22-9](#fig-22-09)).

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### When do you decide to reconstruct a ruptured commissure in the setting of acute aortic dissection? (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** In aortic dissection with limited involvement of the sinus of Valsalva, the commissures often remain attached to a sufficient amount of aortic tissue to be repaired. They can be resuspended so as to effectively correct the leaflet prolapse. If the involvement of the sinus of Valsalva is extensive, a Bentall procedure or a valve-sparing operation is recommended.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### What is the potential role of aortic wrapping? (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** Aortic wrapping with a tube graft is a palliative procedure that does not remove pathological tissue. Its primary indication is moderate aortic dilatation in elderly patients to limit the process of dilatation and minimize the risk of aortic rupture.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Remodeling ring annuloplasty is a key fundamental in valve reconstruction. Could you explain why it has not been used for repair of aortic annular dilatation? (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** A few years after the introduction of the remodeling annuloplasty concept, I tried to apply it to the aortic valve by developing a series of scalloped rings inserted in the subannular position. The results were not satisfactory because of the difficulty of constructing a large surface of coaptation between leaflets. In the interim, the durability of valvular bioprostheses was improved and they became a more reliable alternative.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### How do you handle moderate aortic regurgitation in patients undergoing mitral valve surgery? (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** After mitral valve reconstruction, the aortic valve should be explored. When the mechanism of aortic regurgitation is clearly identifiable, a limited prolapse can be corrected by a small triangular resection, and a mild degree of annular dilatation can be repaired by an annuloplasty or a commissuroplasty. A moderate aortic regurgitation should not be treated by valve replacement surgery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### What is the potential risk of injury to the bundle of His during circular annuloplasty? (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** We have observed that the risk is similar to that of aortic valve replacement. Knowing that the area at risk involves the commissure separating the right from the non-coronary leaflets, sutures into this area should not be placed too deep in the annulus and excess narrowing should be avoided.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### What is your experience in aortic valve reconstruction in Marfan patients? (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** The indication of valve reconstruction surgery is based on two considerations: first, the functional type of regurgitation; and second, the improved durability of bioprostheses achieved in the last decade.

In Type I aortic regurgitation with a normal configuration of the leaflets, a valve-sparing operation with annular stabilization is a valuable option. In Type II aortic regurgitation, the repair of a leaflet prolapse is possible but hazardous considering the unusual fragility of the leaflet tissue. Therefore, I would prefer using the Bentall procedure with a bioprosthetic valve replacement. This is also the case for Type III regurgitation; however, this opinion is not shared by the proponents of valve-sparing operations.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### What degree of residual regurgitation do you accept following aortic valve reconstruction? (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** The evaluation is currently made by post-bypass transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) with semiquantitative measurement. It is important to emphasize that the severity of residual regurgitation is the strongest predictor of late valve repair failure. Therefore, if the residual regurgitation is greater than mild, an aortic valve replacement should be strongly considered. In children, moderate residual regurgitation is accepted provided that a careful follow-up is undertaken.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### What approach do you recommend in a patient undergoing cardiac surgery who has small and mobile leaflet calcification of Lambl's excrescences at the aortic leaflet margin that were incidentally found by TEE? (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** These excrescences are usually small, filiform, and mobile masses that occasionally may become calcified. If the patient has never presented with embolic events, the chance of embolization of this lesion is negligible.

However, it is preferable to explore the valve. If the calcification process is also present in the leaflet tissue, it should be left intact. If the lesion is larger than 5 mm or if any episodes of embolization have been documented, the protruding lesion should be excised.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### In a multiple valve operation, do you always use the sequences you described in this chapter? (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** The order of the sequences may be modified, correcting the tricuspid first in case of a bulging and fragile aortic segment of the tricuspid annulus. As a practical reminder, you can keep in mind that the mitral valve is always the first valve repaired and the tricuspid valve is usually the last.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### In aortic leaflet repair, why do you recommend tying the sutures on the aortic side and not on the ventricular side? (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** I have seen 2 cases of hemolysis following a repair in which sutures were placed on the ventricular side. I postulated that the protruding knots could be the cause of hemolysis. At the reoperation, 3 and 4 weeks after the initial procedure, the leaflets appeared perfectly healed. Therefore, I simply removed the sutures and there was complete resolution of the hemolysis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### In patients with double aortic and mitral valve disease, does a mechanical aortic valve influence your decision of replacing or reconstructing the mitral valve? (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** The answer is no, because in any multivalve scenario a mitral valve reconstruction is always preferable. A double valve replacement carries a higher rate of valve-related complications compared to a single valve replacement.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### What is your opinion about the use of fresh autologous pericardium in aortic leaflet extension? (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**AC:** This technique was widely used in the early days of cardiac surgery. It has been abandoned because of shrinkage or distension of the pericardial tissue in the long-term. These complications led to my proposal of fixing the tissue in glutaraldehyde. I found that 10 minutes of fixation resulted in 80% collagen cross-linkage and sufficient tissue stabilization.

</div></details>
