# Chapter 30. Dilated Cardiomyopathy

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Pathophysiological Paradigm and Valve Remodeling
Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) refers to a progressive cardiac remodeling process characterized by massive, global dilatation of the ventricular chambers accompanied by profound systolic impairment, in the absence of primary organic coronary or valvular disease:
1. **Mechanism of Mitral Incompetence:** Unlike ischemic mitral regurgitation, which is typically regional and asymmetrical, dilated cardiomyopathy produces a **symmetrical Type IIIb dysfunction**. Global, spherical ventricular enlargement displaces both anterolateral and posteromedial papillary muscles apically and laterally away from the annulus, imposing severe, symmetric systolic tethering on both the anterior and posterior mitral leaflets ([Fig. 30-1](#fig-30-01)).
2. **Annular Deformity:** Symmetrical annular dilatation invariably accompanies the ventricular remodeling, abolishing the normal systolic anteroposterior-to-transverse ratio and producing a large central coaptation defect.
3. **Biventricular Involvement & Tricuspid Regurgitation:** The disease process frequently involves both ventricles. Secondary tricuspid regurgitation is common, driven by severe annular dilatation and tethering predominantly affecting the anterior and septal tricuspid leaflets.

### Clinical Spectrum and Etiology
While designated as "idiopathic" when no secondary cause is identified, DCM encompasses a heterogeneous spectrum of risk factors and systemic triggers ([Table 30-1](#table-30-01)), including post-viral myocarditis, toxic exposures (alcohol, cocaine, anthracyclines), volume overload, metabolic derangements, and collagen vascular diseases. A familial pattern is identified in 25% of patients, generally transmitted in an autosomal dominant manner.

### Surgical Indications and Preoperative Evaluation
Severe secondary mitral regurgitation significantly worsens long-term survival in patients with end-stage DCM. Valve reconstructive surgery is indicated in symptomatic patients (NYHA functional class III or IV) with severe mitral regurgitation to improve functional capacity, alleviate symptoms, and potentially alter long-term survival. Comprehensive preoperative assessment mandates:
* **Left Ventricular Dimensions & Thresholds:** Precise echocardiographic quantification of ventricular diameters and volumes. Patients with extreme ventricular dilatation (left ventricular end-diastolic diameter > 75 mm or end-diastolic volume > 250 ml/m²) exhibit a markedly diminished capacity for reverse remodeling and a high rate of repair failure.
* **Hemodynamic Profiling:** Measurement of pulmonary artery pressures to guide aggressive preoperative optimization in patients with moderate-to-severe pulmonary hypertension.
* **Right Ventricular & Tricuspid Evaluation:** Rigorous assessment of right ventricular contractility; significant tricuspid regurgitation must be addressed concomitantly.

### Surgical Techniques and Long-Term Durability
* **Myocardial Protection & Cannulation:** Meticulous myocardial protection is paramount in these fragile, failing hearts. In selected patients with an ejection fraction < 15%, a beating heart approach on cardiopulmonary bypass may be considered.
* **Undersized Remodeling Annuloplasty:** The cornerstone of repair is an undersized remodeling annuloplasty downsized by one to two sizes (using a rigid or semi-rigid Carpentier-Edwards Physio or Classic ring) sized strictly to the anterior leaflet tissue. Undersizing shortens the anteroposterior diameter and draws the posterior annulus anteriorly to restore an adequate surface of coaptation. Arbitrary, excessive undersizing must be avoided to prevent ring dehiscence.
* **Suture Technique & Tricuspid Repair:** Due to severe tissue fragility and continuous wall tension, annular sutures along the posterior circumference should overlap or be placed in a double row. Concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty is performed in the majority of patients.
* **Adjunct Ventricular Restraint:** To counteract progressive late ventricular dilatation and promote reverse remodeling, adjunct myocardioplasty or external cardiac support devices (e.g., the Acorn CorCap device) provide valuable structural restraint.
* **Prosthetic Ring Durability:** Landmark long-term studies demonstrate that rigid or semi-rigid remodeling rings provide markedly superior durability compared to completely flexible rings or posterior bands, which suffer from a significantly higher incidence of recurrent regurgitation (9.5% vs. 2.5%, $p < 0.05$). Operative mortality in specialized centers is < 5% (2% in the Mount Sinai series), with 3-year survival exceeding 80%.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND MECHANISMS</summary><div class="details-content">

The World Health Organization restricts the use of the term "dilated cardiomyopathy" to diseases of unknown cause that primarily involve the myocardium. The term "idiopathic cardiomyopathy" would be more accurate to define a myocardial disease with no discernible cause using the current state of knowledge, while the term "dilated cardiomyopathy" refers to a cardiac remodeling process ultimately increasing the volume of the ventricular chambers with significant systolic dysfunction.

The left ventricle is most frequently involved with usual extension to the right ventricle. Rarely is the right ventricle initially involved, as in right ventricular dysplasia. The presence of atrioventricular valve dysfunction is a common finding during the late stage of the disease.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mechanism of Mitral and Tricuspid Valve Dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

The mechanism of valve dysfunction is type IIIb with restricted leaflet motion of both anterior and posterior mitral leaflets caused by global ventricular enlargement ([Fig. 30-1](#fig-30-01)). In most cases, the restricted leaflet motion and the associated annular dilatation are symmetrical, in contrast to ischemic mitral regurgitation.

**FIGURE 30-1**

In the tricuspid position, severe tricuspid regurgitation originates from restricted leaflet motion involving predominantly the anterior and septal leaflets. Severe annular dilatation is always associated.

### Etiology and Risk Factors</summary><div class="details-content">

As defined, dilated cardiomyopathies form the largest group of heart diseases responsible for heart failure both in adults and in children. In the adult, the etiology in most patients is undetermined; however, knowledge of its risk factors is important so that proper care of the patient can be undertaken ([Table 30-1](#table-30-01)).

The familial form of the disease is present in 25% of patients. Genetic disorders are generally transmitted in an autosomal dominant manner.

## SURGICAL INDICATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Valvular Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

Previous studies have shown that in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy, the long-term prognosis is significantly worse in the presence of severe mitral regurgitation. Therefore, in patients with severe mitral regurgitation and functional class III or IV, surgical intervention may be justified to improve patients’ symptomatology and potentially long-term survival.

Although mitral valve reconstructive surgery has been performed with greater frequency in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy during the last decade, no definite data have yet been published to demonstrate a survival benefit following this procedure. Randomized studies in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy and severe mitral regurgitation will be necessary to assess the potential benefit of mitral valve reconstructive surgery and to define its indications.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Preoperative Diagnostic Evaluations</summary><div class="details-content">

Preoperative investigations should provide precise information that could impact the outcome of the procedure and should include the following evaluations:
* **Left Ventricular Assessment:** Left ventricular size and function should be evaluated carefully. Pulmonary artery pressures should be measured. In patients with moderate to severe pulmonary hypertension, preoperative hemodynamic optimization may be warranted.
* **Right Ventricular & Tricuspid Assessment:** Right ventricular size and function and tricuspid valve function should also be assessed carefully because most patients present with biventricular dilatation. Concomitant tricuspid valve regurgitation should be addressed during the same procedure.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Myocardial Protection</summary><div class="details-content">

Intraoperatively, adequate myocardial protection is of critical importance in these patients with severely impaired ventricular function. Selected patients with ejection fraction below 15% may benefit from a beating heart procedure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Undersized Remodeling Annuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

An undersized mitral remodeling annuloplasty is necessary to correct mitral regurgitation. The degree of undersizing should take into consideration the severity of leaflet tethering and annular dilatation. Typically, the prosthetic ring is undersized one or two sizes.

Considering that in most patients type IIIb valvular dysfunction and annular dilatation are symmetrical, the implantation of an undersized Physio ring or Classic ring is recommended. Annular sutures should overlap or double-row sutures should be placed around the posterior segment of the annulus to decrease the tension on the sutures and to minimize the risk of ring dehiscence. A concomitant tricuspid annuloplasty should be performed in most instances as the disease process often involves both ventricles.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Adjunct Myocardioplasty and Ventricular Support Devices</summary><div class="details-content">

To prevent further ventricular dilatation or to promote reverse ventricular remodeling by decreasing ventricular volumes and optimizing ventricular geometry, the additional techniques of cardiomyoplasty or use of a support device have shown to be beneficial.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Weaning from Cardiopulmonary Bypass and Perioperative Support</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients should be weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass progressively with inotropic support. Inhibitors of phosphodiesterase are of great interest in these patients with chronic heart failure and down-regulation in beta-receptors. The insertion of an intraaortic balloon pump is preferable to the administration of escalating doses of inotropic agents.

In patients with ejection fraction less than 15%, a pretransplantation investigation should be carried out before the valvular procedure in the event that transplantation becomes necessary.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Operative Mortality and Institutional Outcomes</summary><div class="details-content">

Although most patients present with severely depressed left ventricular function, ejection fraction is not a predictor of hospital mortality in this group of patients. With modern techniques of myocardial protection and improved perioperative management, mitral and tricuspid valve annuloplasty can be performed with an operative mortality of less than 5%.

The current studies conducted at Mount Sinai Hospital include a consecutive series of 45 patients who underwent mitral annuloplasty alone or in conjunction with tricuspid annuloplasty for idiopathic cardiomyopathy or dilated cardiomyopathy; the study period ranged from 2002 to 2006. There was only 1 operative death in the group (2%). Following the procedure, most patients presented with improved symptomatology and were in functional class I or II. Our experience indicates a 3-year survival of more than 80%, similar to Bolling et al. who reported a 5-year survival of approximately 70%.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Prosthetic Ring Selection and Repair Durability</summary><div class="details-content">

A recent report from the same group comparing flexible versus nonflexible rings concluded that patients having a flexible ring have a higher likelihood of developing recurrent mitral regurgitation requiring reoperation (9.5% versus 2.5%, $p < 0.05$).

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Patient Selection and Limits of Benefit (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: In your opinion, is there any group of patients with dilated cardiomyopathy and severe mitral regurgitation who may not benefit from an undersized remodeling annuloplasty?

AC: Patients with extremely dilated left ventricles and extremely depressed ventricular function may survive the surgery for some time but their quality of life is typically not improved significantly and durably.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Left Ventricular End-Diastolic Diameter Cut-Off (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Is there a cut-off value of left ventricular end-diastolic diameter above which you do not recommend a mitral remodelling annuloplasty?

AC: Knowing that the absence of reverse ventricular remodeling, in patients with severe left ventricular enlargement, is associated with a high failure rate of remodeling annuloplasty in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy. I am reluctant to operate on patients with a left ventricular end-diastolic diameter greater than 75 mm or volume greater than 250 ml/m2. Other criteria should be taken into account, in particular right ventricular involvement and co-morbidities.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clinical Contraindications and Co-Morbidities (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: In addition to the ventricular size, do you use any other clinical criteria to proscribe a remodeling annuloplasty in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy?

AC: Advanced age is a determinant factor particularly in patients with additional co-morbidities such as history of stroke, renal failure, and peripheral vascular disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Preoperative Intraaortic Balloon Pump Insertion (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Do you use a "prophylactic" preoperative intraaortic balloon pump in patients with end-stage dilated cardiomyopathy undergoing a mitral valve annuloplasty?

AC: In cases of extremely depressed ventricular function with an ejection fraction of less than 15% and in the biventricular forms, the insertion of an intraaortic balloon pump is recommended in my opinion.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Principle of Undersizing vs. Arbitrary Ring Choice (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: The concept of undersized annuloplasty in patients with type IIIb dysfunction was introduced by Bolling. What do you think about the idea of inserting the smallest ring possible in these patients without any specific measurements?

AC: Although I agree with the concept of undersized annuloplasty, I believe that the size of the selected ring should take into consideration the amount of leaflet tissue and the degree of valve tethering. In most patients, a two-size undersizing is sufficient to effectively increase the surface of coaptation. Using a ring that is too small increases the risk of dehiscence.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Remodeling Annuloplasty vs. Flexible Bands (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Can you elaborate on the concept of remodeling annuloplasty versus reduction annuloplasty with totally flexible rings or bands in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy as the latter is associated with a high incidence of recurrent mitral regurgitation?

AC: A remodeling annuloplasty restores the natural shape of the annulus in systolic position and distributes in a harmonious manner the traction forces on the annulus. The other techniques do not remodel the annulus, the shape of which changes over time.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Role of Adjunct Ventricular Support Devices (Acorn) (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: The Acorn clinical trial has shown the additional benefit of a cardiac support device to the undersized remodeling annuloplasty in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy. Do you use this type of device routinely in your practice?

AC: In dilated cardiomyopathy, an adjunct procedure has to be performed to limit the process of ventricular dilatation. I do use the Acorn device.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Scientific Evidence and Skepticism in Valvular Surgery for DCM (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: The medical community is still largely skeptical regarding the potential benefits of correcting mitral regurgitation in patients with dilated cardiomyopathy. Can you comment on this attitude?

AC: This skepticism is mostly based on the fact that current clinical studies are retrospective, include a small number of patients, and do not provide long-term outcome data. There is a pressing need for randomized clinical trials.

</div></details>
