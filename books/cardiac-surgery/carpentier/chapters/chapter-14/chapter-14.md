# Chapter 14. Techniques in Type IIIb: Systolic Restricted Leaflet Motion

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical Spectrum and Pathophysiology of Type IIIb Dysfunction
* **Functional Hallmark:** Pure systolic restricted leaflet motion with completely preserved leaflet pliability and tissue architecture (in contrast to the diastolic restriction and organic stiffening of Type IIIa).
* **Primary Etiologies:**
  * Ischemic heart disease with localized myocardial infarction and segmental ventricular remodeling.
  * Idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy with global left ventricular spherical dilatation.
  * End-stage cardiomyopathy of diverse etiologies.
* **Common Pathophysiological Trait ("Trait Commun"):** Valvular leaflet tethering and apical tenting during ventricular systole caused by papillary muscle displacement relative to the mitral annulus.

### Segmental Ventricular Dilatation and Asymmetrical Tethering
* **Pathological Anatomy:** Posteromedial papillary muscle involvement produces predominant tethering of the P2, P3, and posteromedial commissural (PC) segments, frequently extending to the A3 segment.
* **Annular Geometry:** Asymmetrical annular dilatation predominantly affecting the posterior commissure, P3, and P2 annular segments.
* **Secondary Chordal Tension:** Differential traction on secondary (strut) chordae distorts the anterior leaflet belly, generating the characteristic "effet de mouette" (seagull sign) on echocardiography.
* **Reconstructive Solution:** Implantation of an asymmetric remodeling ring (Carpentier-McCarthy-Adams IMR ETlogix ring) matched to the anterior leaflet surface area without downsizing, or symmetric ring (Classic / Physio) undersized by 1 or 2 sizes.

### Global Ventricular Dilatation and Surgical Strategy
* **Pathological Anatomy:** Symmetrical apical and lateral displacement of both papillary muscles causing symmetrical tethering of both leaflets and global annular dilatation.
* **Reconstructive Solution:** Aggressive symmetrical annular remodeling with a rigid Classic or semi-rigid Physio ring downsized two sizes below anterior leaflet measurement.
* **Coaptation Targets:** Minimal coaptation depth of 3 mm positioned below the annular plane due to persistent ventricular tethering.
* **Adjunct Procedures:** Selective cutting of tethered anterior secondary chordae, posterior leaflet pericardial augmentation, and papillary muscle reimplantation during ventricular aneurysmectomy (Jatene-Dor procedure) preserving left ventricular volume >= 60 ml/m2 BSA.

## SEGMENTAL VENTRICULAR DILATATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Pathophysiological Mechanisms and Experimental Findings

The most important functional characteristic of type IIIb dysfunction, compared to type IIIa, is a pure systolic restricted leaflet motion with preserved leaflet pliability. The most common causes of this dysfunction are ischemic myocardial disease, idiopathic dilated cardiomyopathy, and end-stage heart disease from other causes.

The functional "trait commun" of these different etiologies is the tethering of the mitral valve leaflets during systole ([Fig. 14-1](#fig-14-01)) caused either by segmental ventricular dilatation with asymmetrical valve tethering (a) or by global ventricular dilatation with symmetrical valve tethering (b).

#### Evolution of Concepts in Ischemic Regurgitation

In the past, the fundamental mechanism of type IIIb dysfunction seen in ischemic valve disease was thought to be a papillary muscle dysfunction. Experimental studies in our laboratory in 1980 showed that papillary muscle dysfunction produced by injecting formaldehyde in the posterior papillary muscle alone did not cause significant mitral valve regurgitation.

To obtain regurgitation, it was necessary to inject formaldehyde also in the myocardial wall supporting this papillary muscle.

#### Anatomical Substrates of Posteromedial Tethering

Segmental ventricular dilatation is commonly seen in ischemic heart disease, resulting in asymmetrical apical and lateral displacement of one papillary muscle and tethering of the corresponding leaflet segments.

The posteromedial papillary muscle is most frequently involved in ischemic mitral valve regurgitation. It produces a systolic restricted leaflet motion of the corresponding P2, P3, and PC segments and to some extent the A3 segment.

#### Asymmetrical Annular Dilatation and the Effet de Mouette

In addition to asymmetrical leaflet tethering, the annulus fibrosus is asymmetrically dilated, involving predominantly the P2, P3, and PC regions ([Fig. 14-2](#fig-14-02), a).

Leaflet tethering may also create a differential tension on the secondary chordae of the anterior leaflet that may distort the belly of this leaflet, producing what we described as an "effet de mouette" deformity often seen on echocardiography (b). The leaflet tissue usually has a normal appearance in type IIIb mitral regurgitation; however, it may present secondary lesions as a result of long-lasting valve dysfunction.

## ANNULAR REMODELING AND RING SELECTION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Surgical Angle of Attack and Exposure

Several techniques have been developed to correct type IIIb regurgitation in ischemic mitral valve disease, the most important being annular remodeling using ring annuloplasty. Although the left atrium may be small in type IIIb mitral regurgitation, adequate exposure of the mitral valve can be obtained as described in Chapter 7.

The most practical "angle d'attaque" to treat type IIIb dysfunction is at the valvular level where the main anomalies are annular dilatation and valve tethering.

#### Principles of Overcorrection and Ring Sizing

An undersized remodeling ring annuloplasty can restore leaflet coaptation without the risk of stenosis because the leaflet tissue is pliable. Since leaflet tethering limits functional valvular tissue, an overcorrection of the annulus dimensions is necessary to ensure an adequate surface of coaptation.

The selection of the ring depends upon whether the annular dilatation is symmetrical or asymmetrical. In all circumstances, however, the method of sizing is identical and based on the measurement of the surface area of the anterior leaflet ([Fig. 14-3](#fig-14-03)).

<span id="fig-14-03"></span>![](_page_158_Figure_14-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 14-3**

#### Asymmetric Remodeling with the IMR Ring

Since the annular deformation is usually asymmetrical, the use of an IMR ring, specially designed for this condition, is preferable. This ring has an asymmetrical design (a < b) to compensate for the leaflet tethering commonly seen in the P3 segment and the adjacent P2 and PC segments.

The IMR ring does not need to be downsized because its dimensions have been altered to ensure an optimal surface of coaptation on the basis of the measurement of the anterior leaflet. The size of the selected ring is therefore the size indicated by the sizer covering the surface area of the anterior leaflet.

#### Symmetric Ring Alternatives and Downsizing Rules

As an alternative, a Classic ring or a Physio ring can be used, undersizing one or two sizes depending upon the degree of leaflet tethering. For example, if a 28-mm sizer fits the anterior leaflet, a 28-mm IMR ring size is implanted. Alternatively, a size 24-mm Physio or Classic ring could be used.

Aggressive downsizing restores robust coaptation by displacing the posterior leaflet anteriorly to meet the tethered anterior leaflet edge.

#### Technical Details of Ring Implantation

The technique of ring implantation ([Fig. 14-4](#fig-14-04)) is described in Chapter 8. However, important recommendations should be made because an aggressive downsizing must be achieved to avoid recurrent regurgitation:

First, spacing of the sutures along the posterior segment of the annulus (b) should be closer than that of the anterior leaflet (a). This allows the surgeon to compress the elongated posterior annulus onto the downsized prosthetic ring.

Second, the middle of the anterior segment of the ring corresponds to the middle of the anterior leaflet (c), while the middle of the posterior segment of the ring corresponds to the middle of the P2 segment (c, d).

## GLOBAL VENTRICULAR DILATATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Symmetrical Pathology in Dilated Cardiomyopathy

Global ventricular dilatation, seen in dilated cardiomyopathy, produces symmetrical displacement of the papillary muscles, tethering of all leaflet segments, and symmetrical dilatation of the annulus. The dilatation involves predominantly the posterior annulus but intercommissural distance is also increased.

In this condition, symmetrical leaflet tethering displaces the coaptation line deeply into the left ventricular cavity.

#### Symmetrical Annular Remodeling and Downsizing

Global annular dilatation can be treated at the annular level by a complete remodeling of the annulus ([Fig. 14-5](#fig-14-05)). Since the annular dilatation is usually symmetrical, a Classic ring or a Physio ring should be used.

Aggressive downsizing is necessary to restore leaflet coaptation. A ring size two sizes smaller than that indicated by the anterior leaflet measurement is selected. For example, if a 28-mm sizer fits the anterior leaflet, a 24-mm ring size is selected. If a 30-mm sizer fits, a 26-mm ring size is selected.

<span id="fig-14-05"></span>![](_page_160_Figure_14-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 14-5**

#### Morphology of the Reconstructed Valve

After injecting saline in the ventricular cavity, the morphology of the reconstructed valve looks different than in other types of corrected mitral regurgitation ([Fig. 14-6](#fig-14-06)). The reason is the persistent dilatation of the left ventricle and therefore the persistent leaflet tethering.

As a consequence, the surface of coaptation is positioned well below the plane of the annulus. Depending upon whether the tethering is predominant on one leaflet or both leaflets and whether the papillary muscle displacement is predominant on one papillary muscle or both, the line of coaptation may be symmetrical (a) or asymmetrical (b).

## ADJUNCT RECONSTRUCTIVE TECHNIQUES</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Overview of Adjunct Procedures

Adjunct techniques may be useful in some settings after or prior to remodeling annuloplasty ([Fig. 14-7](#fig-14-07)). They comprise leaflet extension or a variety of techniques aiming at repositioning the papillary muscles.

These adjunct techniques address the subvalvular and ventricular components of tethering when annular remodeling alone may be insufficient.

#### Posterior Leaflet Pericardial Extension

Leaflet extension using a glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch (a) has been proposed to enlarge the posterior leaflet and thereby compensate for posterior leaflet tethering. In our experience, posterior leaflet extension is time-consuming and, more importantly, a properly downsized remodeling annuloplasty ring alone is almost always sufficient.

However, in extreme tethering where downsizing creates excessive annular tension, augmentation provides supplementary surface area.

#### Secondary Chordae Cutting

Cutting of the most tethered secondary chordae (b) of the anterior leaflet responsible for the "effet de mouette" often seen on echocardiography may enhance anterior leaflet mobility and potentially improve leaflet coaptation.

This maneuver selectively releases the strut chordal tension that bows the body of the anterior leaflet into the ventricle. Care is taken to preserve all primary marginal chordae along the free edge to avoid producing unmasked leaflet prolapse.

<span id="fig-14-07"></span>![](_page_161_Figure_14-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 14-7**

#### Ventricular Resection and Papillary Muscle Reimplantation

In large aneurysms or dyskinetic plaque, the displaced papillary muscle can be reimplanted after resection of the fibrous tissue ([Fig. 14-8](#fig-14-08)).

The identification of the limit of the resection is facilitated by venting the left ventricle after cardiopulmonary bypass has been instituted. The negative pressure in the ventricle depresses the ventricular wall and helps to identify the fibrotic ventricular segment.

After ventriculotomy in this area, the origin of the papillary muscle and the limit of the scar tissue are easily identified by examining the left ventricular cavity.

#### Surgical Suture and Cavity Volume Targets

The papillary muscle is detached and the fibrous end attaching the chordae is trimmed, leaving a small fibrotic cuff (a). The fibrotic plaque is resected and the ventricle is reconstructed by suturing the two edges of the resection using 2-0 mattress sutures reinforced with Teflon bands (b).

Care should be taken to preserve a ventricular volume of at least 60 ml/m2 body surface area. The papillary remnant is reimplanted within the suture line at a level corresponding to the unaffected papillary muscle (b). Additional running 4-0 suture is used to secure the closure of the ventriculotomy. The procedure is completed with a remodeling annuloplasty (c).

#### Evaluation of Papillary Muscle Relocation and Restraint

A variety of techniques aiming at repositioning the papillary muscle by either intraventricular approach or external compression have been described. These techniques are still under evaluation.

A global external compression by a restraining device has been proposed. This technique seems more useful for limiting further dilation rather than repositioning papillary muscles.

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surface of Coaptation and Suture Technique (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** What is an acceptable surface of coaptation following repair of a type IIIb dysfunction?
**AC:** The minimal length of leaflet coaptation is 3 mm; with this length the competency of the valve is ensured even though the edges of the two opposing leaflet segments are displaced into the ventricle.

**DA:** Do you ever use pledgetted sutures to secure the ring in ischemic mitral regurgitation?
**AC:** Everting pledgetted mattress sutures would further reduce the mobility and surface area of the posterior leaflet. Simple sutures, closely spaced, are preferred.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for the IMR Ring and Anterior Infarction (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Is the IMR ring indicated in anteroseptal myocardial infarction with anterior papillary muscle displacement?
**AC:** The Ischemic regurgitation ring was designed for asymmetrical posterior leaflet tethering. In the presence of anterior papillary muscle displacement, a Classic or Physio ring downsized one or two sizes is preferred.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Role of Leaflet Extension and Residual Regurgitation (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Is there a role for posterior leaflet extension in type IIIb reconstruction?
**AC:** This technique is appealing because it creates an improved leaflet curvature, which compensates for the posterior displacement of the coaptation line. However, an undersized remodeling annuloplasty ring almost always restores an adequate surface of coaptation. Consequently, leaflet extension is rarely indicated.

**FF:** Following reconstruction with the IMR ring, how would you handle residual commissural regurgitation?
**AC:** A residual commissural regurgitation may occur if the ring has not been properly selected or positioned. In these cases, it should be reselected and reimplanted. In another setting, the regurgitation is due to excess traction on one or two commissural chordae. This can be improved by resecting these chordae. This maneuver does not necessarily produce a leaflet prolapse. If it does, a magic suture, with or without chordae transfer, corrects the prolapse.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Algorithm for Recurrent Regurgitation and Dor Procedure (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What is your operation algorithm for recurrent regurgitation after type IIIb reconstruction?
**AC:** Recurrent regurgitation requires a careful analysis of the mechanisms involved. Adequate undersizing and proper ring orientation should be confirmed or otherwise corrected. Ring dehiscence should be corrected by ring reimplantation, and chordae rupture should be rectified by chordae transfer or replacement. The most frequent mechanism, however, is the progression of the dilatation of the ventricle. In selected cases, a Jatene-Dor operation with reimplantation of the displaced papillary muscle may be indicated. In most instances, particularly in dilated cardiomyopathy, a chordae-sparing valve replacement is a more reasonable alternative.

**DA:** How do you examine and then reconstruct a mitral valve in a patient undergoing a Jatene-Dor procedure?
**AC:** In most patients requiring a Jatene-Dor procedure, the mitral valve is not regurgitant. In the presence of significant mitral regurgitation, a remodeling ring annuloplasty should be performed. Whenever the posterior papillary muscle is involved within the aneurysm, a papillary muscle repositioning can be carried out together with the Jatene-Dor procedure. I have carried out this operation successfully using the technique described in this chapter.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Ventricular Annuloplasty and Relocation Procedures (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Can you perform a reliable annuloplasty from a ventricular approach during a Jatene-Dor procedure?
**AC:** In this setting, the annular dilatation is usually too severe and extensive to obtain an optimal result by a narrowing annuloplasty or a plication of the commissure. Therefore in most cases, I recommend a direct left atrial approach to the valve and a remodeling annuloplasty, which provides a more reliable result. Some cases, however, that present with localized and moderate dilatation can be corrected by using two or three large compression sutures. This requires resection of the secondary chordae to facilitate access to the annulus.

**DA:** What is your opinion about papillary muscle repositioning procedures in type IIIb dysfunction?
**AC:** Several techniques have been described to relocate the displaced papillary muscle. Results beyond those that are short term are lacking at this time. In my opinion, external compression forcing papillary muscle repositioning holds the most promise.
