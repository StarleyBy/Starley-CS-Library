# 18. Pathophysiology, Valve Analysis, and Surgical Indications

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### The Pathophysiological Triad in Tricuspid Disease
The pathophysiological triad (etiology, lesions, dysfunctions) establishes a rigorous foundation for tricuspid valve analysis. Tricuspid diseases are fundamentally divided into functional and organic groups. Functional tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is characterized by the absence of primary valvular or myocardial organic lesions and is potentially reversible early in its course, whereas organic TR features irreversible structural damage.

### Mechanisms of Annular Dilatation and Leaflet Dysfunction
Annular dilatation is the primary cause of functional TR. Dilatation is asymmetrical, predominantly expanding the posterior segment (up to 80% increase) and anterior/aortic segments (up to 40%), while the septal segment remains relatively spared due to its attachment to the central fibrous skeleton. Transthoracic echocardiographic cut-off diameters of 40 mm (or > 21 mm/m²) in the four-chamber view and 70 mm in the transgastric view indicate severe annular dilatation.

### Carpentier Functional Classification and Surgical Strategy
Under Carpentier's functional classification, TR is categorized based on leaflet motion:
- **Type I:** Normal leaflet motion (annular dilatation or leaflet perforation).
- **Type II:** Excess leaflet motion / leaflet prolapse (chordal elongation/rupture, trauma, endocarditis).
- **Type IIIa:** Diastolic restricted leaflet motion (rheumatic, carcinoid disease, commissural fusion).
- **Type IIIb:** Systolic restricted leaflet motion (ventricular dilatation, papillary muscle displacement, pulmonary hypertension, pacing leads).

Even moderate TR negatively impacts long-term survival. During left-sided surgery, tricuspid remodeling annuloplasty is indicated for moderate-to-severe TR, or when annular dilatation exceeds leaflet surface area (intraoperative measurement), or in the presence of pulmonary hypertension or right atrial enlargement.

#### Classification of Etiological Groups
Tricuspid valve diseases are primarily divided into two etiological groups: functional and organic. The term "functional" tricuspid regurgitation (TR) is used with many different nosologic interpretations that require clarification ([Table 18-2](#table-18-02)).

Since the qualifier "functional" is the opposite of organic, the most logical definition would be to use "functional tricuspid regurgitation" in the absence of well-identified valvular or myocardial lesions. The advantage of this nosologic distinction is emphasized by the fact that organic tricuspid regurgitation is irreversible whereas functional regurgitation is potentially reversible.

#### Pathophysiology of Functional Regurgitation
"Functional" tricuspid valve regurgitation (i.e., tricuspid regurgitation without organic valvular or myocardial lesions) is by far the most frequent cause of tricuspid valve dysfunction. This condition is commonly observed in patients with mitral valve diseases. In one of our early studies of 150 patients presenting with mitro-tricuspid valve dysfunction, functional tricuspid regurgitation was present in 70% of the patients. Functional tricuspid regurgitation can also be observed in a variety of diseases producing pulmonary hypertension and/or right ventricular dysfunction.

The high frequency of functional tricuspid regurgitation is explained by the peculiar sensitivity to loading conditions of the thin wall of the right ventricle. Volume overload (preload), right ventricular dysfunction, and increased afterload can all contribute to the onset of functional tricuspid regurgitation, a dysfunction that can be reversible if normal loading conditions and ventricular function are restored.

The most important characteristic of functional tricuspid regurgitation is its variability in a given patient, depending upon cardiac output, ventricular contractility, blood volume, and medical treatment. This variability makes difficult the assessment of the severity of the regurgitation and therefore the decision to repair the regurgitation. A long-lasting evolution of functional tricuspid regurgitation creates the conditions of irreversible dysfunction and secondary organic lesions, a general observation applied to many functional disturbances as highlighted by René Leriche.

#### Spectrum of Organic Tricuspid Valve Diseases
Organic tricuspid valve regurgitation mainly results from primary involvement of the tricuspid valve by a variety of diseases ([Table 18-3](#table-18-03)). Rheumatic valvular disease is prevalent in developing countries whereas degenerative valvular diseases and bacterial endocarditis are more common in Western countries ([Fig. 18-1](#fig-18-01)).

*\*Bacterial endocarditis may be a primary valve disease or may complicate the course of a pre-existing valve disease.*

Organic tricuspid valve regurgitation may also result from primary myocardial diseases such as myocardial infarction, dilated cardiomyopathy, or endomyocardial fibrosis. Severe adhesions between the right ventricle and the sternum after cardiac surgery have been reported as responsible for rare cases of tricuspid regurgitation attributable to the deformation of the annulus. The release of these adhesions corrects the regurgitation.

#### Clinical and Diagnostic Assessment
The determination of the etiology of tricuspid valve regurgitation is based on several considerations such as age, medical history, geographical considerations, socioeconomic conditions, and clinical presentation. In many cases, however, the etiology remains uncertain until echocardiography is performed or surgery is undertaken, both of which permit assessment of the characteristic gross morphology of the valve.

Although often difficult, the etiological diagnosis is important because it determines the complexity of the operation and the long-term prognosis. The clinical and pathological characteristics of these different valvular diseases are extensively studied in Section 5.

#### Topographical Distribution of Valvular Lesions

#### Segmental Vulnerability and Asymmetric Distension
The dilatation of the tricuspid annulus does not affect its four segments to the same degree. The difference depends upon the surrounding anatomical structure and the etiology of the regurgitation ([Fig. 18-3](#fig-18-03)).

<span id="fig-18-03"></span>![](_page_186_Figure_18-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 18-3**

*[Figure 18-3](#fig-18-03). Segmental dimensions in mm are reported for normal (dotted line) and pathologic conditions. (From Deloche A. et al.)*

In functional tricuspid regurgitation, the most severely dilated segment is the posterior segment of the annulus, which may increase its normal length by up to 80% (a). The anterior and aortic segments are affected to a lesser degree, up to a 40% increase, followed by the septal segment, which displays moderate changes because of its tight relationship with the interventricular septum.

Distension also affects the commissures, mainly the anteroposterior and posteroseptal commissures (up to 30%) and to a lesser degree the anteroseptal commissure.

#### Annular Dilatation in Rheumatic Pathology
In rheumatic valvular disease, the degree of annular dilatation depends upon whether the regurgitation is isolated (b) or associated with a certain degree of stenosis (c). In the latter case, the degree of annular dilatation is not limited by the commissural retraction. In all circumstances, the regurgitation is worsened by lesions involving other components of the valve such as leaflets, chordae, papillary muscles and the ventricular wall.

Echocardiography provides important information on the type and degree of dysfunction resulting from the lesions.

## DYSFUNCTIONS: THE FUNCTIONAL CLASSIFICATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Principles of Functional Valve Assessment
The classic separation between valve regurgitation, valve stenosis, and combined valve regurgitation and stenosis is insufficient to assess the feasibility of tricuspid valve reconstruction. Echocardiography and a functional approach assessing leaflet mobility provide more accurate information to the surgeon, who actually determines during the surgery whether or not valve reconstruction is feasible ([Table 18-5](#table-18-05)).

#### Tricuspid Valve Regurgitation Categories
Based on leaflet motion, three types of valve dysfunction are described depending on whether leaflet motion is normal (type I), excessive (type II), or restricted (type III) ([Fig. 18-4](#fig-18-04)).

<span id="fig-18-04"></span>![](_page_187_Figure_18-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 18-4**

*[Figure 18-4](#fig-18-04). Carpentier's functional classification of tricuspid valve dysfunctions: Type I (normal leaflet motion with annular dilatation or perforation), Type II (excess leaflet motion / prolapse), Type IIIa (diastolic restriction / rheumatic stenosis), Type IIIb (systolic restriction / tethering in right ventricular dilatation).*

#### Type I Dysfunction: Normal Leaflet Motion
In type I tricuspid valve dysfunction, the course of the leaflets between systole and diastole has normal amplitude. The regurgitation usually results from annular dilatation, the major cause of "functional tricuspid regurgitation." Another cause is leaflet perforation.

#### Type II Dysfunction: Excess Leaflet Motion
In type II tricuspid regurgitation, the motion of one or more leaflets is increased, with the free edge of the leaflet(s) overriding the plane of the orifice during systole. Leaflet prolapse may be a result either of chordae rupture or elongation or of papillary muscle rupture or elongation.

The most common causes of type II tricuspid regurgitation are bacterial endocarditis or trauma. In most cases of type II regurgitation, a secondary annular dilatation is present as an associated secondary lesion. Leaflet prolapse is a valve dysfunction, not a valve disease.

#### Type III Dysfunction: Restricted Leaflet Motion
In type III tricuspid regurgitation, leaflet motion is limited either during diastole (type IIIa) or during systole (type IIIb).

- **Type IIIa tricuspid regurgitation** is typically seen in rheumatic valve disease, often leading to some degree of associated valve stenosis. The valve motion is limited not only during diastole but also during systole because of commissure fusion, chordae thickening, and fusion.
- **Type IIIb tricuspid regurgitation** displays a leaflet tethering usually attributable to ventricular dilatation and papillary muscle displacement. Localized ventricular wall dyskinesia (as seen in ischemic cardiomyopathy), global dilatation of the ventricle (as seen in end-stage cardiomyopathy), or acute volume overload or pulmonary hypertension can all lead to type IIIb dysfunction. Leaflet tethering caused by trans-tricuspid electrical leads is also seen with increasing frequency. In most cases of type III regurgitation, annular dilatation is associated as a secondary lesion.

#### Tricuspid Valve Stenosis
Tricuspid valve stenosis results from severe restricted leaflet motion due to commissural fusion, leaflet thickening, chordae fusion, and, more rarely, associated calcification. The severity of involvement of these different structures should be assessed first preoperatively and then intraoperatively to determine the feasibility of valve reconstruction which depends upon leaflet pliability and subvalvular structure involvement ([Table 18-6](#table-18-06)).

## HEMODYNAMIC CONSEQUENCES AND SURGICAL INDICATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Echocardiographic Grading of Regurgitation
The precise evaluation of the hemodynamic consequences of tricuspid regurgitation is problematic because of the variability of preload, afterload, and ventricular function, which often renders this evaluation difficult. Quantitative measurements of tricuspid regurgitation by echocardiography are performed using the vena contracta width, proximal isovelocity surface area (PISA), jet area, and flow reversal in the hepatic veins ([Table 18-8](#table-18-08)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### <span id="table-18-08"></span>TABLE 18-8: Grading of Tricuspid Valve Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **RV / RA / IVC size** | Normal | Normal or dilated | Dilated |
| **Jet area - central jets (cm²)** | < 5 | 5–10 | > 10 |
| **VC width (cm)** | Not defined | Not defined, but < 0.7 | > 0.7 |
| **PISA radius (cm)** | ≤ 0.5 | 0.6–0.9 | > 0.9 |

#### Long-Term Prognosis and Impact of Regurgitation
Even moderate tricuspid regurgitation negatively impacts survival.

#### Surgical Indications in Organic Regurgitation
With the exception of endocarditis, most patients with organic tricuspid regurgitation present with concomitant severe mitral and/or aortic valvular disease. In those patients, the presence of a dilated right atrium even in the absence of a documented regurgitation should lead to systematic exploration of the tricuspid valve and to repair of lesions during the left-sided valve operation ([Table 18-9](#table-18-09)).

Organic lesions, whatever the degree of resulting dysfunction, require correction to prevent or minimize an evolutive process. Isolated severe tricuspid regurgitation requires surgical intervention in symptomatic patients with right-sided heart failure or peripheral venous congestion. In asymptomatic patients, surgery is indicated before the occurrence of right ventricular enlargement or dysfunction, supraventricular arrhythmia, or clinical signs of peripheral congestion.

#### Surgical Indications in Functional Regurgitation
In the past, functional tricuspid valve regurgitation was often neglected in patients undergoing surgical correction of left-sided valvular disease; it was assumed that the regurgitation would decrease after correction of the mitral valve dysfunction. However, the major issue is that the regression of tricuspid regurgitation is unpredictable after surgical treatment of mitral and/or aortic valvular diseases. The persistence or the worsening of this condition correlates with an increased morbidity and mortality mainly attributable to congestive heart failure.

Furthermore, it has been shown that even moderate tricuspid regurgitation negatively impacts survival regardless of left ventricular function or pulmonary artery pressures.

#### Intraoperative Exploration and Sizing Criteria
Mild or intermittent tricuspid regurgitation or a dilated right atrium requires surgical exploration of the tricuspid valve during a mitral or aortic valve operation.

Moderate to severe functional tricuspid regurgitation should therefore be corrected using remodeling annuloplasty. Mild functional tricuspid regurgitation or a dilated right atrium should lead to exploration of the tricuspid valve by a right atriotomy during a left-sided operation, particularly in the presence of increased pulmonary artery pressures, right ventricular dysfunction, and left ventricular dysfunction ([Table 18-9](#table-18-09)).

The amount of valvular tissue is assessed by measuring the anteroposterior leaflet surface area, which is then compared to the orifice area to determine whether annuloplasty is recommended (see Fig. 19-5).

#### Surgical Indications in Tricuspid Stenosis
Tricuspid valve stenosis, usually accompanied by involvement of the left-sided valve, also requires valvular surgery when associated with a severely dilated right atrium, supraventricular arrhythmia, and/or clinical signs of peripheral congestion.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Diagnostic Reliability of Echocardiography
> **FF:** In your daily practice, how reliable is echocardiography for determining the mechanism of tricuspid regurgitation?
>
> **AC:** Compared to the mitral valve, echocardiography of the tricuspid valve does not provide information that is as accurate and detailed. However, significant valvular dysfunctions can be easily identified.

#### Pathological Features of Barlow's Disease
> **DA:** What are the characteristics of Barlow's syndrome affecting the tricuspid valve?
>
> **AC:** The tricuspid valve in this condition presents the same characteristic features as seen in the mitral valve: excess tissue, leaflet billowing, and severe dilatation of the annulus. However, compared to the mitral valve, myxoid degeneration is less pronounced and valve regurgitation is by far less frequent because of the lower ventricular pressure.

#### Concept of Reversibility in Functional Disease
> **FF:** Why have you been reluctant to use the term "functional tricuspid regurgitation" in patients with annular dilatation in the presence of mitral valve disease?
>
> **AC:** I am not reluctant to use the term "functional regurgitation" in patients presenting with annular dilatation provided that there are good reasons to believe that the regurgitation is reversible after mitral valve reconstruction. The problem is that, in many instances, the reversibility is difficult to predict; this unpredictability is due to the fact that, as pointed out by René Leriche, long-lasting functional disturbances can lead to organic lesions. It would be particularly important to predict potential reversibility of a tricuspid regurgitation, but this is not possible today with enough reliability. In the near future, we may be able to identify predictors of reversibility or irreversibility of regurgitation. This distinction will have important practical consequences than functional versus organic.

#### Risk Factors for Regurgitation Progression
> **FF:** In your experience, have you been able to identify risk factors for the progression of tricuspid valve regurgitation despite the correction of mitral regurgitation?
>
> **AC:** Left or right ventricular dysfunction and dilatation, persistent pulmonary hypertension, long-lasting atrial fibrillation, and a severely dilated left atrium can be considered risk factors for progression of tricuspid regurgitation, but again unpredictability is the main characteristic of the progression of functional tricuspid valve regurgitation.

#### Post-Transplantation Tricuspid Regurgitation
> **DA:** Elaborate on the mechanisms of tricuspid regurgitation following cardiac transplantation.
>
> **AC:** Several factors can be responsible for tricuspid regurgitation in this setting, including the following: pulmonary hypertension, donor-recipient atrial dysynchrony, standard versus bicaval technique of transplantation, atrial enlargement, and biopsy-induced chordae rupture.

#### Management in Elderly Patients
> **FF:** Mild to moderate degrees of tricuspid regurgitation are common findings in elderly patients with no pulmonary hypertension who are referred for aortic or coronary bypass surgery. Does this valvular dysfunction need to be addressed at the time of surgery?
>
> **AC:** In elderly patients with reasonably good right ventricular function and no dilatation of the right cardiac chambers, a mild to moderate tricuspid regurgitation can be left untreated in the absence of organic lesions.

#### Indications for Direct Intraoperative Inspection
> **DA:** In the absence of documented tricuspid valve regurgitation, or significant annular dilatation as assessed by echo, would you systematically inspect the valve at the time of mitral valve surgery?
>
> **AC:** I systematically inspect this valve in patients with pulmonary hypertension or a dilated right atrium. I believe this is necessary because echocardiography performed on an anesthetized patient is not always reliable.

#### Thresholds for Concomitant Annuloplasty
> **FF:** In patients with left-sided valvular disease and functional tricuspid regurgitation, what are your criteria to perform an associated tricuspid annuloplasty?
>
> **AC:** Patients with greater than mild functional tricuspid regurgitation should undergo systematic remodeling annuloplasty. For those with less than mild regurgitation or intermittent regurgitation, several elements should be taken into consideration such as left ventricular dysfunction, pulmonary hypertension, and right ventricular enlargement, which are predictors of progression of tricuspid regurgitation despite the correction of left-sided valve disease. In the absence of these risk factors, the size of the annulus is also relevant. Patients with an annular dilatation beyond one size greater than the amount of valvular tissue should be considered for remodeling annuloplasty.
