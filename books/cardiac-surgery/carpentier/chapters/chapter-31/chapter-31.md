# Chapter 31. Hypertrophic Obstructive Cardiomyopathy

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is a primary disease of the left and/or right ventricle characterized by myocardial hypertrophy predominantly involving the interventricular septum, occurring in the absence of extrinsic volume or pressure overload:
1. **Genetic Basis:** In the majority of instances, the disease is genetically transmitted in an autosomal dominant manner with variable penetrance.
2. **Triad of Dynamic Obstruction:** Severe obstruction of the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) results from a dynamic interaction between asymmetrical septal hypertrophy, anterior displacement of the papillary muscles and coaptation line, and systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the elongated anterior mitral leaflet with resulting late mitral regurgitation ([Fig. 31-1](#fig-31-01), [Fig. 31-2](#fig-31-02)).
3. **Flow Dynamics:** Septal bulging and an abnormally narrow aorto-mitral angle redirect systolic blood flow toward the septum and inflow chamber. The posterior leaflet closes early, pushing the elongated anterior leaflet into the outflow tract during midsystole, causing dynamic muscular obstruction and late mitral incompetence.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Septal Thickness:** Echocardiography typically demonstrates asymmetrical septal hypertrophy ranging between 15 and 25 mm, with maximal thickness located at the level of the anterior leaflet free edge in diastole.
* **Hemodynamic Gradients:** Cardiac catheterization and Doppler echocardiography demonstrate a resting or exercise-induced left ventricle-to-aortic peak gradient exceeding 50 mm Hg in severe obstructive forms.
* **Dynamic Variability:** The outflow gradient is highly dynamic and modulated by three physiological determinants: increased contractility increases the gradient, whereas decreased preload or afterload exacerbates obstruction.
* **Associated Pathologies:** Structural abnormalities include anterior positioning of papillary muscles, excessive posterior leaflet height (> 20 mm), an elongated anterior leaflet ("langue de bœuf"), and elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure with secondary pulmonary hypertension.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Indications:** Surgical intervention is indicated in symptomatic patients with severe resting or provocable LVOT obstruction refractory to medical therapy (beta-blockers) or interventional cardiology procedures (ethanol septal ablation, pacing). Early intervention is indicated in mildly symptomatic patients when SAM is associated with moderate-to-severe mitral regurgitation amenable to reconstruction.
* **Standard Technique:** Extended transaortic septal myomectomy (Morrow procedure) remains the primary surgical procedure of choice, provided myomectomy is extended deeply between the two papillary muscles ([Fig. 31-4](#fig-31-04)).
* **Carpentier Reconstructive Technique for Complex Disease:** In patients with severe septal hypertrophy, an excessively small hyperkinetic cavity, and an abnormal elongated anterior leaflet:
  1. *Transaortic Myotomy:* Initial transaortic transverse myocardial incision 5 mm below the right coronary cusp, extended to the nadir of the left coronary cusp and 1 to 2 cm downward (8 to 10 mm depth).
  2. *Transatrial Exposure & Leaflet Incision:* Left atriotomy; transverse or vertical incision of the anterior mitral leaflet to gain direct, wide visualization of the deep ventricular septum ([Fig. 31-5](#fig-31-05)).
  3. *Extended Low Myomectomy:* The detached septum is grasped with Rezzano forceps and myomectomy is completed low between the papillary muscles under direct vision.
  4. *Leaflet Patch Reconstruction:* Reconstruction of the incised anterior leaflet using an ovoid patch of glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium sutured with 5-0 monofilament locked running sutures ([Fig. 31-6](#fig-31-06)).
  5. *Posterior Leaflet Reduction:* Resection of posterior leaflet tissue if height exceeds 20 mm.
  6. *Remodeling Annuloplasty:* Implantation of a rigid Classic ring (typically size 32 or 34 mm) sized to the surface area of the reconstructed anterior leaflet to prevent hyperkinetic systolic sphincter constriction ([Fig. 31-7](#fig-31-07)).
* **Right Ventricular Involvement:** Trans-tricuspid resection of hypertrophied muscular bands to relieve right ventricular outflow obstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Residual Outflow Gradient:** Most commonly caused by an inadequate distal myomectomy leaving a residual muscular shelf at the level of the papillary muscles, or unrecognized anterior leaflet elongation. A residual gradient > 30 mm Hg warrants a second pump run.
* **Septal Perforation & Conduction Blocks:** Excessive myomectomy near the membranous septum risks complete heart block; thinning of the ventricular septum risks ventricular septal defect (digital palpation through a small right atriotomy provides reliable thickness control in complex cases).
* **Flange Turbulence from Plication:** Isolated horizontal plication of an elongated anterior leaflet creates a rigid, protruding tissue flange that induces turbulence and late fibrous retraction; patch reconstruction is strongly preferred.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## ANATOMY AND PATHOPHYSIOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is a disease of the left and/or right ventricle characterized by the development of myocardial hypertrophy predominant at the interventricular septum. There is no direct cause, such as volume or pressure overload. In most instances, the disease is genetically transmitted in an autosomal dominant manner.

Ventricular hypertrophy is often asymmetrical and its localization and severity influence clinical manifestations. In the majority of cases, ventricular hypertrophy is mild to moderate with no obstruction. These patients often present with no symptoms or minimal symptoms, especially during exercise, and can be treated medically with beta-blockers. The obstructive form of the disease is characterized by a variable and dynamic obstruction of the left ventricular outflow tract that results in the manifestation of clinical symptoms with eventual progression to congestive heart failure.

Following surgical myotomy, introduced in the early 1960-s, surgical myomectomy, developed by Morrow in 1968, became the conventional surgical treatment and is still in use today. More recently, two interventional cardiology techniques have been added as alternatives to reduce outflow tract obstruction. The first is the use of a dual pacemaker, the efficacy of which is still controversial. The second is percutaneous transluminal septal myocardial ablation using ethanol injection into septal branches of the left anterior descending artery. This latter procedure is used more and more frequently as an alternative to the surgical septal myomectomy, which remains the most currently used treatment. However, patients with more severe forms of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy with mitral valve involvement may require a more complex reconstructive operation, as described later in this chapter.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Septal Bulging and Mitral Geometry</summary><div class="details-content">

In hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy, the left ventricular cavity is small and the hypertrophy is asymmetrical, predominant at the anterolateral wall ([Fig. 31-1](#fig-31-01)). The septum is extremely hypertrophic with maximal thickening at the level of the free edge of the anterior leaflet in diastolic position (a).

Although the principal cause of obstruction is the bulging of the interventricular septum, systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve is another feature that results in obstruction (b). Echocardiography has been a key imaging study for elucidating the characteristic features of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy and the mechanism of obstruction.

**FIGURE 31-1**

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mechanism of Obstruction and SAM</summary><div class="details-content">

The characteristic features ([Table 31-1](#table-31-01)) include the anterior position of the papillary muscles contributing to the anterior displacement of the line of coaptation toward the left ventricular outflow tract. Additional features are narrow aorto-mitral angle, excess tissue of the posterior leaflet, and abnormal morphology of the anterior leaflet, which is elongated to such an extent that it mimics a "langue de bœuf" (see Glossary).

The mechanism of obstruction is illustrated in [Figure 31-2](#fig-31-02). As a result of the septal bulging, and the narrow aorto-mitral angle, the blood flow normally directed towards the apex is now directed towards the septum and the inflow chamber (a). The posterior leaflet closes first and limits the full development of the anterior leaflet, which is pushed towards the left outflow tract during midsystole, causing a dynamic obstruction (b) and late mitral regurgitation (c).

**FIGURE 31-2**

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Symptoms and Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinical manifestations of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy include angina, fatigue, dyspnea on exertion, and syncope. Most patients present with a variety of symptoms that may appear at any age during the evolution of the disease.

There is no direct correlation between the severity of symptoms and the severity of obstruction of the left ventricular outflow gradient. Supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmia may also occur and potentially result in sudden death.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diastolic and Systolic Ventricular Function</summary><div class="details-content">

Left ventricular diastolic dysfunction is a common finding in patients with hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. This results from an impairment of ventricular relaxation and leads to increased diastolic filling pressures.

Systolic function is preserved during the early phase of the disease. Impaired ventricular contractility may, however, appear at the late stage because of the development of myocardial fibrosis, explaining the symptoms of severe congestive heart failure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Echocardiographic and Hemodynamic Evaluation</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography is the key element in the diagnosis of hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. It allows analysis of the location and severity of ventricular and septal hypertrophy and the degree of obstruction of the left ventricular outflow tract. The thickness of the septum ranges between 15 and 25 mm. Echocardiography also detects structural abnormalities such as anterior position of the papillary muscles or abnormal morphology of the mitral valve, as well as the presence of SAM that may be associated with mitral regurgitation ([Fig. 31-3](#fig-31-03)). Echocardiography is also used to evaluate systolic and diastolic ventricular function.

<span id="fig-31-03"></span>![](images/_page_311_Figure_31-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 31-3**

Cardiac catheterization shows commonly an elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure and a left ventricle/aortic peak gradient greater than 50 mm Hg in severe obstructive forms. However, this is a dynamic parameter and left ventricular outflow gradients may change over time. The three main factors that influence this variable are ventricular contractility, preload and afterload.

Increased ventricular contractility and decreased preload and/or afterload lead to increased outflow gradients. Finally, pulmonary artery pressures may also be elevated in patients with long-lasting disease and in those with SAM associated with mitral regurgitation.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical intervention is mostly indicated in symptomatic patients with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction at rest or during exercise that is refractory to medical therapy or repeated interventional cardiology procedures.

We also recommend an early surgical intervention in patients with mild symptoms whenever SAM is associated with moderate to severe mitral regurgitation, provided that the patient can benefit from reconstructive surgery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Conventional Techniques and Morrow Procedure</summary><div class="details-content">

Historically, initial surgical attempts consisted of performing a myotomy, as proposed in the mid-1960-s by Brock, Cleland, and Bigelow. The goal of the myotomy was to disrupt the contractile function of the sphincter-like muscular ring surrounding the outflow tract, thus relieving obstruction. However, this procedure was associated with a high incidence of residual gradient and abandoned.

Later, Morrow introduced the concept of septal myomectomy to increase the diameter of the left ventricular outflow tract and subsequently minimize the obstruction ([Fig. 31-4](#fig-31-04)). The only limitation of this technique is that it may leave residual septal bulging (inset) because of the difficulty of achieving optimal orientation of the knife.

<span id="fig-31-04"></span>![](images/_page_312_Figure_31-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 31-4**

Provided that the myomectomy is extended far into the ventricle between the two papillary muscles to avoid residual septal bulging, the results of this surgery are excellent. It is therefore the technique of choice in the majority of cases. However, anomalies of the mitral valve may lead to suboptimal results.

In these circumstances, we favor a reconstructive technique developed in the 1970-s that addresses all the features of the disease. This surgical procedure includes the following components:
1. A vertical or transverse incision of the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve, which provides direct access to the septum;
2. An extensive septal myomectomy;
3. A reduction of the height of the posterior leaflet if it is greater than 20 mm;
4. A remodeling annuloplasty using a Classic (rigid) ring to limit excess narrowing of the mitral valve orifice during hyperkinetic contraction.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## RECONSTRUCTIVE TECHNIQUES</summary><div class="details-content">

Using standard cardiopulmonary bypass and careful myocardial preservation, the lesions are approached by both aortotomy and left atriotomy or by left atriotomy alone for surgeons having extensive experience with the procedure.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Combined Transaortic and Transatrial Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

Through a transverse aortotomy, the myomectomy is initiated by incising the myocardium transversally 5 mm below the right coronary leaflet attachment, carefully preserving the membranous septum. As the incision approaches the subcommissural angle separating the right from the left coronary leaflets, it can be closer to the aortic annulus 2 mm away from the leaflet hinge. The incision is extended to the nadir of the left coronary leaflet, carefully preserving the commissures of the mitral valve. This myotomy is extended 1 to 2 cm downward by mobilizing an 8- to 10-mm thickness of myocardial tissue.

At this stage, the operation is continued through a left atriotomy ([Fig. 31-5](#fig-31-05)).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anterior Leaflet Incision and Extended Myomectomy</summary><div class="details-content">

The anterior leaflet is incised either transversally 5 mm below the annulus, whenever its height is smaller than its width (a), or vertically, whenever the width is significantly smaller than the height (b). The incision is extended from the subcommissural triangle separating the left and non-coronary leaflets of the aortic valve to the edge of the anterior leaflet. This edge is preferably preserved but may be incised 2 to 3 mm vertically between 2 to 3 paramedial chordae whenever it is thickened and everted.

Taking advantage of the large vertical or horizontal incision, the upper edge of the detached septum is grasped using Rezzano forceps and brought downward to complete the myomectomy low between the two papillary muscles. In complex forms, it may be useful during this excision to introduce a finger into the right ventricle through a small right atriotomy to control the thickness of the resection.

<span id="fig-31-05"></span>![](images/_page_313_Figure_31-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 31-5**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Leaflet Patch Reconstruction and Remodeling Annuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

The anterior leaflet is reconstructed using an ovoid patch of glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium sutured to the edges of the leaflet incision with 5-0 monofilament locked running sutures to avoid a purse-string effect ([Fig. 31-6](#fig-31-06)).

<span id="fig-31-06"></span>![](images/_page_313_Figure_31-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 31-6**

Whenever the posterior leaflet height is greater than 20 mm, it should be reduced to less than 20 mm by an ovoid resection.

Finally, in severe forms with an excessively small annulus and a hyperkinetic ventricle, a rigid annuloplasty ring (Classic) is implanted; the size of the ring is determined by measurement of the surface area of the reconstructed anterior leaflet ([Fig. 31-7](#fig-31-07)). In cases in which there is an abnormal anterior leaflet, the shape of the ring can be adjusted by slightly bending its extremities and/or reducing its transverse dimension.

<span id="fig-31-07"></span>![](images/_page_314_Figure_31-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 31-7**

Following closure of the aorta and the left atrium, cardiopulmonary bypass is discontinued after careful de-airing under transesophageal echocardiography control.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Right Ventricular Obstructive Cardiomyopathy</summary><div class="details-content">

Severe right ventricular obstructive cardiomyopathy can occur alone or be associated with left ventricular involvement.

With the approach through the tricuspid valve, the hypertrophied muscular bands can be easily resected to release the obstruction and enlarge the right ventricular cavity while carefully preserving the tricuspid valve.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Operative Mortality and Long-Term Hemodynamics</summary><div class="details-content">

The results of conventional myomectomy have improved in recent decades with 70% of patients displaying significant improvement for 5 or more years.

Operative mortality in elderly patients has been dramatically reduced from approximately 15-17% to less than 2%. However, many patients with the most severe forms of this disease still require high doses of beta-blockers that may present side effects.

The results in 15 patients undergoing surgery using Carpentier’s technique have been particularly gratifying, showing minimal residual gradient and residual regurgitation and decreased need for beta-blockers.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Role of Venturi Effect in SAM Genesis (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** The pioneering work of your group has been critical in the better understanding of the mechanism of SAM in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy. Some authors, however, still suggest that the "Venturi effect" (that is, high-velocity blood flow displaces the protruding edge of the anterior leaflet toward the aortic annulus and left ventricular outflow tract) plays a major role in the genesis of SAM. What is your opinion of this theory?

**AC:** It is a hypothesis, and like all hypotheses, it should be proven. Recent studies have somewhat ruled out this mechanism. I personally believe that the main factors are an abnormal configuration of the mitral valve, whether congenital or acquired, concomitant with an abnormal configuration of the ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Morphological Variants of Anterior Leaflet and Papillary Muscles (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What are the different morphologies of the anterior leaflet and the anterior papillary muscle in this disease, and what is their surgical relevance?

**AC:** The anterior leaflet may present excess tissue formation at its base, or a narrow intercommissural distance. The papillary muscles may be displaced anteriorly. The most frequent valvular anomalies are an elongated anterior leaflet and/or P2 segment. I do not know if these anomalies are primary or secondary lesions, I personally believe that the modified morphology of the anterior leaflet is a secondary lesion due to the traction on the valvular apparatus exerted by the small hyperkinetic ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Acceptable Residual Outflow Gradient and Indications for Second Pump Run (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Following the repair procedure, what kind of residual outflow gradient do you accept as medically manageable without further intervention? In which circumstances would you recommend a second pump run?

**AC:** After a Morrow procedure which is the technique of choice, I rarely observe a residual gradient greater than 10 to 20 mm Hg. I believe that gradients greater than 30 mm Hg justify a second pump run using the technique I have described.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mechanisms and Management of Residual LVOT Gradients (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** What are the possible mechanisms for a residual gradient and how you would address them?

**AC:** The most frequent cause is an inadequate myomectomy that leaves a circumferential band of muscular tissue at the level of the papillary muscles. In this case, it is recommended that the residual lesion be approached through a left atriotomy with retraction or temporary incision of the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve. Another cause is an anomaly of the anterior leaflet, which has not been recognized.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Sizing and Systematic Selection of Annuloplasty Rings (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Do you systematically use a prosthetic ring during the reconstructive procedure? What are the most common sizes of ring implanted in these patients?

**AC:** The reconstructive procedure presented in this chapter is used only in extreme forms of the disease with severe hypertrophy, small hyperkinetic ventricle, and abnormal morphology of the anterior leaflet. I like to implant a ring in most of these cases to adjust the size of the annulus to that of the reconstructed anterior leaflet. A Classic ring is preferred with slight enlargement of the anteroposterior diameter. The most common ring sizes are 32 and 34 mm. The role of the ring in this particular technique is to enlarge the mitral valve orifice to displace posteriorly the posterior leaflet and to oppose the hyperkinetic contraction of the annulus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anterior Leaflet Horizontal Plication versus Patch Augmentation (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** It has been suggested that anterior leaflet horizontal plication is a sufficient procedure for the resolution of SAM and outflow tract gradient. What is your opinion about this rather simple procedure?

**AC:** Anterior leaflet horizontal plication may be efficient in an elongated anterior leaflet but it produces a protruding flange, a cause of turbulence and fibrous retraction. I personally prefer to proceed to a horizontal leaflet resection or a vertical patch augmentation, depending upon the morphology of the anterior leaflet.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Role of Mitral Valve Replacement in HOCM (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Is there any role for mitral valve replacement in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and mitral regurgitation with SAM?

**AC:** I have not yet encountered a case in which a reconstructive operation could not be performed successfully.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Trans-Tricuspid Approach to the Right Interventricular Septum (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What do you think about the technique of approaching the right side of the septum through the tricuspid valve to proceed to a myomectomy?

**AC:** I have not tried the interesting technique proposed by Borisov. In severe form of obstructive cardiomyopathy, I like to open the right atrium to palpate the septum while working on the left ventricle side and I may occasionally remove some muscular bands within the right ventricle particularly if there is a gradient at this level.

</div></details>
