# 11. Techniques in Type II Posterior Leaflet Prolapse

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical Spectrum and Pathological Anatomy
* **Frequency and Etiology:** Type II posterior leaflet dysfunction is the most common lesion encountered in degenerative mitral regurgitation (fibroelastic deficiency and Barlow's disease). It may also result from bacterial endocarditis and rheumatic disease in young children.
* **Anatomical Distribution:** Prolapse predominantly involves the P2 segment (central scallop), but may extend to P3 and more rarely P1.
* **Underlying Mechanism:** Primarily caused by chordae rupture, elongation, or a combination of both.
* **Secondary Lesions and Laplace's Law:** Distension and tissue proliferation cause the prolapsing segment to expand to two to three times its normal size. In accordance with Laplace's law (tension is directly proportional to radius: $T \propto P \cdot r$), leaflet distension dramatically increases mechanical stress on remaining chordae. Resection of excess diseased tissue normalizes curvature radius and reduces chordal tension.

### Surgical Strategy According to Extent of Prolapse
* **Limited Prolapse (<1/3 of scallop margin):**
  * *Triangular Resection:* Resection of <10% surface area with height greater than base ($H > B$) and convex edges. Interrupted 4-0 or 5-0 sutures with knots placed on the ventricular side.
  * *Alternative Techniques:* Chordae transposition (secondary or basal chordae) or chordae replacement (ePTFE) if excess tissue is minimal; chordae shortening for isolated chordal elongation.
* **Extensive Prolapse (>1/3 of scallop margin or entire segment):**
  * *Quadrangular Resection with Annular Plication:* Trapezoidal resection preserving indentations. Indicated when residual gap is <20 mm and remnant height is <20 mm. Annular plication performed using 2-0 mattress and figure-of-eight sutures.
  * *Quadrangular Resection with Leaflet Height Adjustment:* If remnant height exceeds 20 mm, height is reduced to 10 to 15 mm via base horizontal triangular resection to prevent systolic anterior motion (SAM).
  * *Sliding Leaflet Technique:* Indicated when the gap is >=20 mm or in Barlow's disease with excess height (>20 mm). Remnants detached along annulus for half the gap length, secondary chordae resected, annular circumference reduced by 3 to 4 compression sutures without plication, height adjusted, and remnants reattached using continuous 4-0 or interrupted sutures.
  * *Composite Technique:* Indicated when prolapse involves >40% of the posterior leaflet. Maximum 40% resection combined with chordae transposition or artificial chordae to preserve mobile leaflet tissue and natural indentations.
  * *Multiple Segment Prolapse (e.g., P2 + P3):* Resection of the most diseased segment (P2) combined with detachment and sliding plasty of the adjacent segment (P3) +/- chordal repair.

### Critical Surgical Rules
* **40% Resection Rule:** Resection of the posterior leaflet should never exceed 40% of its total surface area.
* **20-mm Height Rule:** Posterior leaflet remnant height must not exceed 20 mm (ideal height 10 to 15 mm) to avoid pushing the coaptation line forward into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT), thereby triggering SAM.
* **Mandatory Remodeling Ring:** A remodeling prosthetic annuloplasty ring is mandatory in all repairs to stabilize the annulus, restore normal orifice geometry, and ensure long-term durability.

## INTRODUCTION AND GENERAL PRINCIPLES</summary><div class="details-content">

Type II posterior leaflet dysfunction is the most frequent dysfunction in mitral valve regurgitation caused by degenerative valvular disease. It can also be encountered in other etiologies such as bacterial endocarditis, and even rheumatic valvular disease in young children.

The prolapse usually affects P2 but can be extended to P3 and more rarely P1. It is generally due to chordae rupture and/or elongation.

#### Pathophysiological Mechanisms and Laplace's Law

A special feature of this dysfunction is the important secondary lesions affecting the prolapsed area. Over time the leaflet tissue becomes distended with proliferation of pathological tissue: the P2 segment may be two to three times the normal size.

The consequence is that some portion of the excess tissue should be resected to restore a more normal valve geometry, to reduce the tension on the remaining chordae, and to stop progression of the process.

The tension is reduced as a consequence of Laplace's law, which states that the larger the radius of a portion of a sphere (i.e., the prolapsed leaflet) the greater the tension on its surface, and therefore on the chordae. As shown by very long-term results, this approach effectively prevents recurrent regurgitation in most cases. An additional advantage is that it is an easy and reliable technique, which does not require surgical correction at the subvalvular level.

#### Technical Hierarchy and Clinical Taxonomy

Resection of a portion of excess leaflet tissue is preferable in posterior leaflet prolapse to reduce the tension on the remaining leaflet tissue and chordae.

Several techniques of reconstruction can be used depending upon the extent and mechanism of the leaflet prolapse ([Table 11-1](#table-11-01)).

## LIMITED PROLAPSE</summary><div class="details-content">

A limited prolapse is a prolapse involving less than one third of the free margin of a posterior leaflet segment.

Whenever the prolapsed segment displays only a small amount of excess tissue and discrete secondary lesions, chordae transposition or chordae replacement are elegant solutions.

Most of the time, however, excess tissue or pathological tissue makes a limited triangular resection preferable, which has the advantage of operating at the valvular level and reducing the tension on the remaining leaflet and chordae. The degree of resection depends upon the extent of the prolapse and amount of grossly abnormal tissue.

#### Triangular Leaflet Resection

This technique evolved from the historical McGoon's plication technique (inset), which was efficient but had the single disadvantage of leaving a segment of pathological tissue, which could later cause fibrous proliferation or leaflet retraction.

A triangular leaflet resection ([Fig. 11-1](#fig-11-01)) avoids this drawback. It is indicated whenever the length (B) of the free margin of the prolapsed segment is limited to less than one third of the total length (A) of the leaflet segment involved (B <= A/3) (b).

<span id="fig-11-01"></span>![](_page_124_Figure_11-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11-1** Triangular leaflet resection: historical McGoon's plication (a, inset); anatomical assessment of prolapse margin $B \le A/3$ (b); placement of 2-0 stay sutures around normal boundary chordae and definition of triangle height $H > B$ (c); convex resection of diseased leaflet segment (d); restoration of leaflet continuity with interrupted sutures (e, f); placement of inverted sutures with ventricular knots (g); nerve hook testing for residual defects (h); closure of adjacent opened indentations (i).

#### Surgical Technique of Triangular Resection

To perform a limited triangular resection, two 2-0 stay sutures are passed around the non-elongated chordae at the limits of the prolapsed portion of the leaflet (c). The height (H) of the triangular resection should be slightly longer than its base (H > B) (c).

Its sides are cut slightly convex so as to preserve the curvature of the reconstructed leaflet (d). Leaflet continuity is restored using 4-0 or 5-0 interrupted sutures (e, f).

Depending upon the thickness of the tissue, these sutures can be inverted, with the advantage that the knots are positioned on the ventricular side of the leaflet (g). A continuous suture technique is not recommended because it impairs leaflet pliability and has the tendency to produce a purse-string effect.

#### Verification and Closure of Indentations

Once the sutures have been completed, a nerve hook is used to detect any residual defect, which would be corrected by an additional everted suture (h).

Once the leaflet continuity has been restored, the adjacent indentations are carefully examined to assess whether their edges have been separated, a potential source of leak. In this case the open indentation must be closed by two or three interrupted sutures (i).

<span id="fig-11-02"></span>![](_page_125_Figure_11-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11-2** Extensive prolapse of posterior leaflet: extensive prolapse involving $>1/3$ of free margin (a); adverse consequence of large triangular resection producing excessive free edge tension, restricted motion, and separation of adjacent indentations (b); quadrangular resection with annular plication restoring normal tension and alignment (c); symmetrical and asymmetrical quadrangular resection patterns (d).

#### Chordal Transposition, Replacement, and Shortening

When the prolapsed leaflet segment does not present a significant amount of excess pathological tissue, transposition of secondary or basal chordae to the free edge of the leaflet or chordae replacement are valuable alternatives. These techniques are described in Chapter 10.

When the prolapse is due to chordae elongation in the absence of significant excess tissue, one of the techniques of chordae shortening illustrated in Figure 10-12 can be used.

## EXTENSIVE PROLAPSE</summary><div class="details-content">

Extensive prolapse of the posterior leaflet involves more than one third of the length of the free edge of the corresponding segment ([Fig. 11-2](#fig-11-02), a).

In this case, chordae transposition or replacement is not advisable because it would leave excess tissue and therefore excess tension on the reconstructed leaflet and chordae.

A large triangular resection is not recommended either because it would produce excess tension on the free edge of the leaflet, resulting in restricted leaflet motion (curtain effect) and separation of one or two adjacent indentations, a source of new sites of regurgitation (b).

#### Principles of Quadrangular Resection

These drawbacks are prevented by performing a leaflet quadrangular resection and annular plication, which reduces the tension on the leaflet remnants (c).

The quadrangular resection can be symmetrical or asymmetrical (d), depending upon the location of the prolapse. This does not influence the technique.

A large triangular resection of the free edge of a leaflet may cause tenting of the free edge and separation of the indentations.

#### Quadrangular Resection with Annular Plication

This technique ([Fig. 11-3](#fig-11-03)) is the most frequently used technique in posterior leaflet prolapse. It has the advantage of removing most pathological tissue, restoring a more normal geometry of the leaflet, and reducing the tension on the suture line of the reconstructed leaflet.

The technique is simple and efficient and does not require surgery at the subvalvular level. The resection should be quadrangular with a slight trapezoidal shape and should be completed by a plication of the annulus to approximate the leaflet remnants.

Quadrangular resection of the prolapsed area with annular plication is used only in extensive posterior leaflet prolapse.

<span id="fig-11-03"></span>![](_page_126_Figure_11-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11-3** Quadrangular resection with annular plication: identification of boundary normal chordae and trapezoidal resection keeping functional indentations (a); resection of restrictive secondary chordae near edges (b); measurement of remnant height (<20 mm) and gap (<20 mm) (c); annular plication with 2-0 mattress suture (d, e); placement of figure-of-eight plicating suture (f); approximation inspection and additional annular bite if required (g); restoration of leaflet continuity with 5-0 sutures (h); nerve hook inspection and correction of residual gaps (i, inset).

#### Step-by-Step Surgical Execution

The normal chordae at the limits of the prolapse area are identified (a). Approximately 2 to 3 mm from these normal chordae, a line of resection is projected from the free margin down to the annulus so that a trapezoid or a rectangle is formed.

Whenever possible it is preferable to keep one or two indentations to facilitate the opening motion of the posterior leaflet. The leaflet tissue delineated by the trapezoid is resected (a).

The secondary chordae, close to the edges of the resection, are cut to facilitate leaflet mobilization (b). The height of the posterior leaflet remnants is then measured. Whenever the height of the remnants is <20 mm and the residual gap is <20 mm (c), a plication of the annulus is used (d).

#### Annular Suture Placement and Reconstruction

An interrupted 2-0 mattress suture is placed through the annulus at the limits of the resected area (e). Exerting traction on this suture facilitates placement of another interrupted figure-of-eight suture to close the plicated annular segment (f).

Pledgets are not necessary since the prosthetic ring that will be implanted ensures adequate repartition of the stress. The sutures are tied, making sure that the two leaflet remnants are well approximated. If this is not the case, an additional suture is used, taking a larger bite on the annulus (g).

The leaflet continuity is then restored (h) using everted or inverted 5-0 sutures. The suture line is examined to identify occasional residual defects (i), which should be repaired by additional everted stitches (inset). The repair is completed by the implantation of a prosthetic ring.

#### Quadrangular Resection with Leaflet Height Adjustment

When the height of one leaflet remnant ([Fig. 11-4](#fig-11-04), a) or both remnants (b) measures more than 20 mm, a small horizontal triangular resection at the base of the leaflet remnant reduces the height to 15 mm.

The edges of the leaflet remnants must be adjusted to the same length using continuous 4-0 sutures.

Each detached remnant is reattached to the annulus while exerting gentle traction on the leaflet tissue ("sliding maneuver") to reduce the gap left by the leaflet resection. The remainder of the procedure is unchanged from the procedure for quadrangular resection with annular plication, including annuloplasty.

## SLIDING LEAFLET TECHNIQUE</summary><div class="details-content">

This technique ([Fig. 11-5](#fig-11-05)) is primarily indicated in extensive leaflet prolapse. Following the resection of the prolapsing segment, if the gap is equal to or greater than 20 mm, the "sliding leaflet technique" is indicated to avoid an annular plication that is too extensive (a).

In addition, in patients with Barlow's disease and excess leaflet tissue, following the resection of a prolapsing segment of the posterior leaflet, this technique is useful to reduce the height of the remnants and minimize the risk of systolic anterior motion (SAM).

The sliding leaflet technique is rarely used only in very extensive posterior leaflet prolapse, with excess tissue.

<span id="fig-11-05"></span>![](_page_128_Figure_11-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11-5** Sliding leaflet technique (detachment and compression sutures): broad resection gap $\ge 20$ mm (a); bilateral leaflet detachment along annulus equal to half gap length (b, c); resection of basal and secondary chordae near edges to mobilize remnants (d); placement of 2-0 braided compression sutures passing through annulus to ventricular side (e); return bite from ventricular side to atrium (f).

#### Detachment of Leaflet Remnants and Chordal Release

On each side of the gap left by the resection, the adjacent leaflet tissue is detached from the annulus over a distance equal to one half the length of the gap (b, c).

To facilitate the "sliding," the secondary chordae close to the edge of the detached leaflet remnants are resected (d).

"Compression sutures" are then used to reduce the size of the annulus: 2-0 braided sutures are passed successively through the annulus towards the ventricle, then through a slight thickness of the annulus on its ventricular side (e), and then through the annulus from the ventricular side towards the atrium (f).

#### Annular Compression Sutures and Ring Preparation

At the two extremities of the resected areas, the compression sutures must take larger bites beyond the resected area to initiate the sliding. Approximately three to four compression sutures are usually necessary (g).

Pulling (h) and/or tying (i) these sutures effectively reduces the size of the annulus by compression without plication. In most instances only two to three compression sutures are tied whereas the others are pulled upwards and used subsequently to secure the ring (i).

Taking advantage of the large exposure of the mitral annulus, additional mattress sutures are then placed, which will be used later to secure the ring (i).

<span id="fig-11-05-cont"></span>![](_page_129_Figure_11-5_cont.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11-5 (cont'd)** Sliding leaflet technique (compression and ring sutures): placement of 3 to 4 compression sutures with larger bites beyond resection gap (g); pulling sutures to compress annulus without plication (h); tying compression sutures and placing additional ring mattress sutures (i).

#### Leaflet Remnant Height Reduction

Leaflet reconstruction is carried out after the height of each leaflet remnant has been measured and, if necessary, reduced ([Fig. 11-6](#fig-11-06)).

For those remnants measuring more than 20 mm (inset a), a limited horizontal triangular resection is carried out at the base to adjust their height to avoid systolic anterior motion.

The height of each remnant may not be identical, so the resection should be adapted to restore an optimal height for each remnant (inset b).

<span id="fig-11-06"></span>![](_page_129_Figure_11-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11-6** Leaflet remnant height reduction: measurement of remnant height exceeding 20 mm (inset a); basal horizontal triangular resection tailored to restore optimal remnant height of 10 to 15 mm to prevent systolic anterior motion (inset b).

#### Remnant Fixation to the Annulus

After resection, the leaflet remnants are secured to the annulus using a continuous 4-0 suture ([Fig. 11-7](#fig-11-07), a). Each bite is passed first downward through the annulus and then upward through the base of the leaflet remnant (inset).

If the distance on the annulus remains slightly larger than the base of the leaflet remnants, the discrepancy should be corrected, first by starting with a large plicating horizontal mattress suture in the annulus, and then by travelling more on the annulus than on the leaflet.

#### Suture Placement and Suture Line Verification

A sliding leaflet technique is indicated to cover a large gap after leaflet resection. It facilitates a reduction of the height of the remaining posterior leaflet segments whenever necessary.

While passing the sutures through the leaflet remnants, traction is exerted to facilitate the sliding of the leaflets.

The suture line is checked with a nerve hook to detect any residual defect. Leaflet continuity is restored and a ring annuloplasty is performed (b-d).

<span id="fig-11-07"></span>![](_page_130_Figure_11-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 11-7** Remnant fixation to the annulus: securing leaflet remnants to annulus with continuous 4-0 suture passing through annulus and remnant base (a, inset); exerting sliding traction to advance tissue toward the gap, restoring continuity, and completing ring annuloplasty (b, c, d).

#### Alternative Annular Fixation Technique

An alternative technique for leaflet remnant fixation on the annulus that is more rarely employed and that avoids triangular leaflet resection is to pass the annular mattress sutures through the base of the leaflet remnants and then through the sewing band of the ring ([Fig. 11-8](#fig-11-08)).

This modality has the advantage of further reducing the height of the posterior leaflet whenever necessary and positioning the ring above the suture line (inset).

## COMPOSITE TECHNIQUE AND MULTIPLE SEGMENT PROLAPSE</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Composite Reconstruction Framework

Leaflet resection should be limited to 40% of the total surface area of the posterior leaflet.

If the prolapse is more extensive, a partial quadrangular resection is performed, removing the most diseased part of the prolapsed area.

The remaining prolapsed area is corrected by chordae transposition or replacement using artificial chordae (Fig. 10-8). If chordae are elongated and not ruptured, papillary muscle sliding plasty is indicated (Fig. 10-10).

#### Preservation of Native Indentations

This composite technique is also recommended to preserve at least one posterior leaflet indentation to facilitate free motion of the leaflet.

Preserving functional indentations ensures harmonious opening of the valve during diastole and reduces leaflet stress.

#### Multi-Segment Prolapse Anatomy

Although in most patients, a type II posterior leaflet dysfunction affects the P2 segment, it is not unusual to observe a prolapse involving multiple leaflet segments (e.g., P2 and P3).

In this scenario, it is impossible to proceed with an extensive leaflet resection. The most diseased segment, usually P2, is resected with the preservation of strong secondary chordae.

#### Surgical Management of Multi-Scallop Lesions

The other prolapsing segment (e.g., P3) is detached from the annulus and a sliding leaflet plasty is performed. This maneuver increases the tension on the marginal chordae of this segment and may be sufficient for the correction of the leaflet prolapse.

If the prolapse persists, it should be treated by an additional chordae transposition or replacement.

## SUMMARY</summary><div class="details-content">

Posterior leaflet prolapse, the most frequent valvular dysfunction, displays many different pathological features that may be addressed by different techniques, from the most simple to the more complex.

Although their respective indications may seem complicated, the different scenarios are based on two simple considerations: the extent of the prolapse and the amount of residual leaflet tissue ([Fig. 11-9](#fig-11-09)).

A tailored surgical strategy ensures maximal restoration of valve competence, preservation of natural leaflet mobility, and prevention of systolic anterior motion.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Technical and Conceptual Evolution

**FF: Do you see any evolution in the use of the different techniques of valve reconstruction during the last decade?**
> **AC:** Two aspects should be considered: the evolution of the techniques and the evolution of the indications. As far as the techniques are concerned, the basic principles of valve reconstruction have been validated by most surgeons. Some think they could "simplify" this surgery by not resecting pathological tissues, by not remodelling the annulus, and/or by artefacts limiting the motion of the leaflets. These palliative techniques may be appealing for a surgeon, but they are not as reliable as reconstructive techniques because they don't respect the three basic principles of valve reconstruction.
> 
> By contrast, a true technical advance has been the introduction of mini invasive reconstructive valve surgery. Even if they don't use the optimal port access techniques, many surgeons today limit the skin incision and sternal retraction to half the distances used in the past. This has been associated with reduced pain, reduced bleeding, and reduced sternal and respiratory complications.

#### Evolution of Surgical Indications

**FF: What about the evolution in the indications?**
> **AC:** We should rather call it a revolution in which both the surgeons and cardiologists have participated; the surgeon by adopting true reconstructive techniques and reporting excellent long-term results for up to 25 years, and the cardiologists by dramatically changing their surgical indications to the point of referring asymptomatic patients.
> 
> One or two decades ago, most of the patients referred to surgery were in NYHA functional class III or IV. Due to a long-lasting evolution, the advanced lesions required complex techniques and large leaflet resection.
> 
> Today, patients are referred much earlier with less evolving lesions so that a great proportion of the patients with fibroelastic deficiency and posterior leaflet prolapse can be treated by more limited leaflet resection or even chordae transposition with no resection whenever there is no significant excess tissue.

#### Management of Leaflet Height and SAM Prevention

**DA: Does correction of excess height of the posterior leaflet always require a sliding leaflet technique?**
> **AC:** Many people think it does. But this depends on the amount of excess leaflet tissue and the geometry of the leaflet segments involved.
> 
> Whenever only the height of a leaflet segment is increased, height reduction can be achieved by the simple technique of ovoid transverse resection at the base of the leaflet segment.
> 
> Whenever a large amount of excess tissue is present and requires a large quadrangular resection, a sliding leaflet technique is mandatory with reduction to 20 mm of the height of the leaflet remnants.

#### Management of Resection Gaps and Remnant Alignment

**DA: After quadrangular leaflet resection, some surgeons advise rotation of the free edge of the remnants to cover the gap created by the resection. What is your opinion?**
> **AC:** You can do it for a small gap, but when dealing with a larger gap this technique distorts the geometry of the leaflet remnants, leading to excess tension on the leaflet free edge and enlargement of the adjacent indentations.
> 
> An asymmetrical or symmetrical sliding leaflet plasty is preferable because it avoids these drawbacks.

#### Biomechanical Effects of Leaflet Sliding

**FF: Does the sliding of a leaflet segment--P1 for example--correct a moderate degree of prolapse of this particular segment?**
> **AC:** The sliding displaces chordae laterally. As a result of their new oblique orientation more tension is applied on the leaflet edge. The sliding may therefore correct a moderate degree of prolapse.

#### Annular Remodeling and Echocardiographic Leaflet Motion

**FF: After remodeling annuloplasty, a restricted posterior leaflet motion is frequently observed on echo with the posterior leaflet poorly moving, only serving as a buttress against which the anterior leaflet coapts. Can you comment on that?**
> **AC:** Two mechanisms can be evoked to explain this frequently observed finding. The first is the pronounced reverse remodeling of the base of the heart due to the systolic remodeling of the annulus by the ring. This increases the distance between the leaflet-free edge and the papillary muscle, leading to a certain degree of leaflet tethering. This tethering often disappears after a few months once the ventricular remodeling has taken place.
> 
> The second mechanism is the absence of functional posterior leaflet indentations because either they are fused or have been resected or sutured. For this reason, I insist on limiting the extent of leaflet resection, preserving one or two indentations of the posterior leaflet as they contribute to wider valve opening.
> 
> Finally, I like to emphasize that this is an echocardiographic finding without significant clinical relevance as shown by our 92% freedom from reoperation at 20 years in degenerative valvular disease.

#### "One Lesion, One Technique" vs. Universal Approaches

**DA: The concept of "one lesion one technique" you developed during the past three decades has been challenged recently by the concept of "all lesions one technique," namely the edge-to-edge suture technique or Gore-Tex chordae without true annuloplasty.**
> **AC:** It is a natural tendency of man to look for panacea, I mean, to search for a universal solution to address several problems. Another trend is to favour simple techniques because they are less demanding.
> 
> I like simple solutions too, and I frequently use limited leaflet resection, chordae transfer, and no ring whenever this is possible. But, given the diversity and the complexity of many valvular lesions, it is just not realistic to think that they can all be treated durably with a simple technique, particularly if you want to broaden your indications and to give the benefit of valve reconstruction to more complex cases.

#### Evaluation of the Edge-to-Edge Technique

**DA: What do you think about the edge-to-edge suture technique?**
> **AC:** This technique was originally described and used in the early time of valve surgery by Lillehei and Nichols in 1960. It was abandoned some years later because of unreliable and suboptimal results. I refer to it as a palliative and not a reconstructive technique because it does not respect the fundamental principle of preserving or restoring full leaflet mobility.
> 
> In addition, it does not make sense to me to increase the load on a non-prolapsed leaflet, the substance of which is affected by the same degenerative process. When your mule is tired, you don't put an additional sack of wheat on its back. Do you?

#### Long-Term Risks of Edge-to-Edge Repair

**FF: What else?**
> **AC:** Another potential disadvantage of the edge-to-edge suture technique, in my opinion, is that, more often than said, it is a "no-return technique." Whenever the result is not satisfactory, it makes it difficult, and often impossible after some time, to carry on a genuine redo reconstructive valve operation because the edge-to-edge suture produces tight adhesions between the two leaflets, which are difficult to separate.
> 
> In addition, flow turbulence produced by the narrower orifices is a likely trigger of leaflet fibrosis and calcification as we learned from the palliative techniques of commissure plication used in the past.
> 
> The natural history of rheumatic valvular disease with fused commissures teaches the same lesson. These experiences warn us that the most probable long-term fate of an edge-to-edge repair is valve replacement.

#### Artificial Chordae vs. Leaflet Resection

**FF: What do you think about the use of artificial chordae without leaflet resection to treat posterior leaflet prolapse?**
> **AC:** Your question raises two different issues: first, leaflet resection; second, the use of artificial chordae. Correction of leaflet prolapse without a resection is a valuable technique in the early phase of mitral valve regurgitation before the prolapse has generated leaflet tissue proliferation and secondary lesions. At a later stage when significant excess tissue has developed, a partial resection is a preferable technique.
> 
> The rationale is based on the Laplace's law, which states that the larger a spheric surface the greater the surface tension. Finite element studies have validated this in the setting of excess mitral valvular tissue. Leaving a large amount of excess leaflet tissue may have a negative impact that results from increased tension on the leaflets, the chordae, and the annulus.
> 
> Removal of excess tissue by limited triangular or quadrangular leaflet resections is such a simple, logical, and well-proven durable procedure that I am surprised that some surgeons are reluctant to use it. Now, with regards to the use of artificial chordae to correct the prolapse, I use it from time to time, but the alternative technique of chordae transposition whenever feasible is simpler, less hazardous, and more physiological. It has been validated by many surgeons with very long-term follow-up.

#### Clinical Impact of Retained Excess Tissue

**FF: When excess tissue is left in place, does it impair valvular function in the long term?**
> **AC:** This depends on the amount of excess pathological tissue kept in place. The more pathological tissue, the higher the risk of further leaflet distension or fibrous retraction.

#### Multi-Segment Prolapse Strategy

**FF: What is your strategy whenever the prolapse involves multiple segments of the posterior leaflet?**
> **AC:** The most severe lesions should be resected. Segments with acceptable mobility should be preserved and corrected by chordae shortening, chordae transposition or artificial chordae in order to save as much tissue as possible.

#### Evaluation Criteria for Emerging Techniques

**FF: Generally speaking, what place should be given to new reconstructive techniques and new rings that are seen to proliferate today?**
> **AC:** The place they deserve. I see three conditions. First, they should address an unsolved problem; second, they should be scientifically tested by studies with patient consent; third they should prove to be equal to or superior to current reconstructive techniques.
> 
> Remember that the gold standard in symptomatic patients with degenerative valvular disease is a 92% freedom from reoperation at 20 years. Any new technique should challenge these results.
