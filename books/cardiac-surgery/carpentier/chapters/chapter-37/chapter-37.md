# Chapter 37. Radiation-Induced Valvular Lesions

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Mediastinal irradiation (classically administered for Hodgkin's disease or breast cancer) produces a complex, progressive spectrum of pan-cardiac and extracardiac radiation-induced injuries:
1. **Radiation-Induced Heart Disease (RIHD):** Pathological manifestations include constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy with severe diastolic dysfunction, premature ostial coronary artery disease, conduction system block, and progressive valvular disease. Associated non-cardiac conditions (radiation pulmonary fibrosis, severe esophagitis, chest wall necrosis) are frequent.
2. **Latency and Natural History:** There is a characteristically prolonged latency period of 10 to 20 or more years between thoracic radiation and the onset of overt cardiac manifestations. The prevalence and severity of valvular lesions increase markedly in patients surviving beyond 20 years post-irradiation.
3. **Valvular Pathology & Carpentier Type IIIa Dysfunction:** Radiation induces extensive leaflet fibrosis, thickening, retraction, and variable calcification, resulting in Type IIIa dysfunction (restricted motion in systole and diastole). Aortic, mitral, and tricuspid valves are commonly involved, with multi-valve involvement occurring in a substantial proportion of patients.
4. **Unique Calcification Pattern:** Unlike degenerative disease, radiation-induced calcification typically spares the commissures and coronary ostial ridges but preferentially involves the aortic root, the aorto-mitral curtain, and the base of the anterior mitral leaflet.
5. **High-Risk Surgical Population:** Surgical management carries high operative mortality and morbidity due to biventricular restrictive physiology, sternal poor wound healing, conduit failure, and porcelain aorta.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiographic Features:**
  * *Type IIIa Dysfunction:* Diffuse leaflet thickening and retraction with restricted systolic and diastolic excursion ([Fig. 37-1](#fig-37-01)).
  * *Subvalvular Apparatus:* Thickening, shortening, and fusion of chordae tendineae and papillary muscles in the mitral and tricuspid positions.
  * *Hemodynamics:* Typically combined valvular regurgitation with varying degrees of stenosis.
* **Hemodynamic and Ventricular Assessment:**
  * *Restrictive Cardiomyopathy:* Biventricular diastolic dysfunction with elevated filling pressures despite preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). A low cardiac index attributable to diastolic restriction is the strongest independent predictor of adverse operative outcome.
  * *Concomitant Coronary Disease:* Mandatory selective coronary angiography with specific visualization of the coronary ostia and verification of internal mammary artery (IMA) patency.
* **Computed Tomography (CT) Imaging:** Non-contrast thoracic CT to quantify ascending aortic and root calcification ("porcelain aorta"), chest wall vascularity, and pulmonary parenchymal fibrosis.
* **Histopathology:** Dense avascular acellular fibrosis with loss of elastin architecture, collagen hyalinization, and dystrophic calcification.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Single-Stage Comprehensive Repair:** All significant cardiac lesions (valvular, coronary, pericardial) amenable to surgical correction must be addressed during the index operation. Reoperative surgery in an irradiated field carries prohibitive operative risk. Concomitant aortic valve replacement is indicated even for moderate aortic stenosis when performing coronary bypass or mitral surgery.
* **Surgical Access Selection:** Due to the elevated risk of radiation-induced sternal devascularization and osteoradionecrosis, a thoracotomy approach should be considered whenever feasible.
* **Prosthesis Selection:** Mechanical prostheses are favored over biological tissue valves, particularly in young patients, to prevent late structural valve degeneration and avoid the perils of reoperation.
* **Valve Reconstruction Techniques:**
  * Mitral and tricuspid valve reconstruction can be performed if leaflet substance is not excessively fibrotic.
  * Leaflet patch extension using glutaraldehyde-preserved autologous or bovine pericardium combined with a prosthetic remodeling annuloplasty ring restores mobility and coaptation depth ([Fig. 37-2](#fig-37-02)), provided subvalvular fibrosis is not prohibitive.
* **Aortic Cannulation & "Porcelain Aorta" Management:**
  * Extensive aortic calcification prohibits safe cross-clamping and direct cannulation. Right axillary artery cannulation should be utilized routinely.
  * Severe ascending aortic calcification requires either deep hypothermic circulatory arrest with replacement of the ascending aorta and valve, an apical-aortic conduit, or a transcatheter (TAVI) approach.
* **Post-Bypass Management of Diastolic Failure:**
  1. *Myocardial Protection:* Meticulous, cold blood cardioplegic delivery.
  2. *Rhythm & Preload:* Active restoration of sinus rhythm and atrial kick; volume loading to optimize ventricular filling.
  3. *Inotrope Restriction:* Minimizing pure inotropes due to preserved systolic function; liberal early use of intra-aortic balloon counterpulsation (IABP) for low cardiac output syndrome.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Postoperative Low Cardiac Output Syndrome (LCOS):** Driven by severe diastolic dysfunction and low stroke volume rather than systolic ejection failure; refractory to inotropic overstimulation.
* **Sternal Dehiscence and Osteoradionecrosis:** High doses of radiation cause microvascular endarteritis obliterans of internal mammary collateral beds, leading to fatal mediastinitis.
* **Embolization from Porcelain Aorta:** Manipulation or cross-clamping of a calcified aorta causes devastating cerebral atheroembolism; axillary arterial inflow and hypothermic circulatory arrest mitigate this risk.
* **Internal Mammary Graft Failure:** Unrecognized radiation-induced intimal hyperplasia or occlusion of internal mammary arteries leads to acute perioperative myocardial infarction.
* **Unpredictable Fibrotic Progression:** Ongoing post-radiation fibrogenesis may cause late failure of conservative valve repairs.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## PATHOLOGY AND CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Mediastinal irradiation, such as that used in the treatment of Hodgkin’s disease or breast cancer, may induce cardiac lesions namely constrictive pericarditis, restrictive cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease, conduction disturbances, and/or valvular disease. The involvement of other thoracic organs and the presence of associated diseases such as pulmonary fibrosis and esophagitis are also typical in this scenario.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Etiology, Latency, and Valve Topography</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Dosage and Latency:** The severity of the lesions depends upon the duration and the dosage of the radiation received. The evolution is progressive with almost always a latency period between the radiation and the cardiac manifestations.
* **Valvular Distribution:** Radiation may affect the aortic, mitral, or tricuspid valves. A significant number of patients present with involvement of several valves.
* **Long-Term Prevalence:** The prevalence of valvular disease increases significantly in patients who have survived for more than 20 years following mediastinal irradiation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Echocardiographic Features and Histopathology</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiographic Appearance:** On echocardiography, most patients present with Type IIIa dysfunction with leaflet thickening and retraction ([Fig. 37-1](#fig-37-01)). Subvalvular involvement is a common finding in the mitral and tricuspid positions. Valvular regurgitation with varying degrees of stenosis is present.

* **Histological Characteristics:** Results of histological examination show leaflet fibrosis with or without calcification in most instances.
* **Anatomical Distribution of Calcifications:** Calcifications are present at the level of the aortic valve and root, the aorto-mitral curtain, and the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve.
* **Clinical Monitoring:** Patients with mild to moderate valvular disease should be followed closely to assess the progression of the disease and its potential impact on ventricular function. Patients with severe symptomatic valvular disease should be offered surgical therapy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

The surgical strategy should take into consideration several characteristics specific to this population:

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Preoperative Work-up and Restrictive Ventricular Dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Comprehensive Work-up:** A comprehensive cardiac work-up should be performed to identify all the valvular, cardiac, and extracardiac lesions.
* **Diastolic Function Assessment:** The evaluation of myocardial function is critical because most patients present with associated restrictive cardiomyopathy and diastolic dysfunction involving both the right and the left ventricles.
* **Prognostic Impact:** Decreased cardiac output attributable to diastolic dysfunction despite a preserved ventricular ejection fraction is a strong negative predictor of outcome.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Access, Prosthesis Choice, and Valve Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Surgical Approach:** Because of the increased risk of postoperative sternal necrosis that is directly correlated to the total dosage of radiation received, a surgical approach by thoracotomy should be preferred whenever feasible.
* **Single-Stage Repair Principle:** All significant cardiac lesions amenable to surgical correction should be addressed during the same operation because a reoperative procedure is very challenging and carries a prohibitive risk. For example, patients with moderate aortic stenosis referred for mitral valve surgery or myocardial revascularization should undergo concomitant aortic valve replacement to avoid the risk of a second operation.
* **Prosthesis Selection:** A mechanical valve should be preferred to a valvular bioprosthesis, particularly in young patients.
* **Reconstructive Feasibility:** Mitral and tricuspid valve reconstruction can be performed if the leaflet tissue is not severely fibrotic, taking into consideration that the progression of fibrotic lesions is not predictable ([Fig. 37-2](#fig-37-02)).

### Revascularization and Aortic Cannulation Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Internal Mammary Artery Assessment:** In patients requiring myocardial revascularization, the patency of the internal mammary arteries should be verified during cardiac catheterization.
* **Axillary Cannulation for Calcified Aorta:** Calcification of the aorta should be anticipated and may complicate aortic cannulation and aortic valve access. In this setting the right axillary artery should be used for cannulation.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Feasibility and Role of Mitral Valve Reconstruction (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** It has been suggested that the progression of valvular disease can compromise the results of valve reconstruction. Therefore should mitral valve reconstruction be attempted in patients with radiation-induced valvulopathy?

**AC:** Leaflet extension with glutaraldehyde-preserved pericardium, either autologous or heterologous, offers a new opportunity to preserve the valve in patients with radiation-induced valvulopathy provided that the subvalvular apparatus is not too severely fibrotic and retracted. However, no long-term study with a sufficient number of patients is available.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Prophylactic Pericardiectomy (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Do you perform a “prophylactic” pericardiectomy in patients with extensive radiation undergoing valvular surgery?

**AC:** The answer is no if the pericardium looks normal and the radiation was carried out more than 5 years earlier. However, I have no data to support this strategy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Reoperation versus Heart or Heart-Lung Transplantation (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** A second reoperation is extremely difficult in these patients, and some have advocated heart or heart-lung transplantation in this group of patients. What is your opinion?

**AC:** Considering the fact that any transplantation requires immunosuppressive treatment with its potential associated risks, a reoperation is preferable.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Severe Postoperative Diastolic Dysfunction (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Postoperative diastolic dysfunction with low cardiac output is a common and challenging problem in these patients. What are your recommendations to best address this complication?

**AC:** Intraoperatively, these patients should benefit from especially careful myocardial protection. When weaning from bypass, it is important to restore the atrial contraction and to optimize the filling pressures. The use of inotropic agents should be minimal because these patients have a preserved systolic function. If despite these measures low cardiac output syndrome persists, intraaortic balloon counterpulsation should be used.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Options in Severe Ascending Aortic Calcification (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In patients with aortic valve stenosis and severe calcification of the ascending aorta, what is your recommendation?

**AC:** Up to recently, I used axillary cannulation and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest to replace the valve and the ascending aorta. Two other alternatives should be discussed today: the first is an apical valve conduit and the second is a transfemoral or transapical aortic valve replacement.

</div></details>
