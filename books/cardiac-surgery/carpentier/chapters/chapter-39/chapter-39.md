# Chapter 39. Reoperations

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Reoperations following reconstructive valve surgery are uncommon when fundamental principles of repair are strictly respected:
1. **Temporal Classification of Repair Failures:**
   * *Immediate Failures (Intraoperative):* Identified immediately by transesophageal echocardiography prior to chest closure. Primarily caused by technical shortcomings (residual regurgitation, systolic anterior motion [SAM], or residual stenosis).
   * *Early Failures (Within the First 2 Years):* Predominantly related to technical factors, most frequently the omission of a remodeling annuloplasty ring during the index operation.
   * *Late Failures (Beyond 2 Years):* Primarily driven by the natural progression of the underlying etiology (recurrent prolapse in degenerative disease, progressive fibrosis and calcification in rheumatic disease, or adverse ventricular remodeling in dilated or ischemic cardiomyopathy).
2. **The "Zero Residual Defect" Golden Rule:** No patient should leave the operating suite with residual mitral regurgitation or significant residual stenosis (mean gradient ≥ 8 mm Hg). Residual dysfunction is the single most powerful independent predictor of late repair failure and reoperation.
3. **Mandatory Prosthetic Ring Remodeling:** Omission of an annuloplasty ring carries an unacceptable long-term failure rate. In our longitudinal experience, patients repaired without a ring experienced a linearized reoperation rate of 3.3% per patient-year (pt/yr) compared to 1% per pt/yr in patients receiving a remodeling ring.
4. **Feasibility of Re-Repair:** A failed previous repair does not mandate valve replacement. Re-repair is feasible in more than 50% of contemporary reoperations for degenerative disease, utilizing techniques performed either through an in situ prosthetic ring or following temporary partial ring detachment ([Fig. 39-1](#fig-39-01)).
5. **Tricuspid Reoperations:** Suture annuloplasty techniques (such as De Vega semicircular annuloplasty) carry an excessive 10-year reoperation rate of 6.4%, compared to only 0.6% when a prosthetic remodeling ring is used. Severe functional tethering (Type IIIb) requires aggressive annular undersizing by 2 to 3 sizes and construction of a coaptation surface up to 10 mm.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Intraoperative Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE):**
  * *Residual Regurgitation:* Any residual jet greater than trivial/mild requires immediate re-exploration.
  * *Systolic Anterior Motion (SAM):* Dynamic left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction accompanied by severe eccentric posterior jet, caused by excessive posterior leaflet height or a disproportionately small ring.
  * *Residual Stenosis:* A mean diastolic transmitral gradient ≥ 8 mm Hg warrants immediate re-exploration.
* **Etiology-Specific Failure Patterns:**
  * *Degenerative Disease:* New chordal elongation or rupture leading to leaflet prolapse (actuarial freedom from reoperation at 20 years: 97% for posterior prolapse, 86% for anterior prolapse, and 83% for bileaflet prolapse; [Table 39-4](#table-39-04)).
  * *Rheumatic Disease:* Progressive leaflet stiffening and subvalvular retraction (freedom from reoperation: 82% at 10 years and 55% at 20 years).
  * *Ischemic & Dilated Cardiomyopathy:* Left ventricular end-diastolic dimension > 65 mm and severe tenting/tethering predict recurrent ischemic mitral regurgitation.
* **Right Ventricular Dysfunction:** Progressive right ventricular enlargement and declining TAPSE in recurrent tricuspid regurgitation indicate urgent reoperation before irreversible right heart failure occurs.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Immediate Failure Management (Intraoperative):**
  * *Residual Regurgitation:* Repair through indentation closure, suture dehiscence repair, artificial chordae, or leaflet patch extension ([Table 39-1](#table-39-01)).
  * *SAM-Induced Failure:* Ovoid resection of excess posterior leaflet height; replacement of an undersized ring with a larger prosthetic ring (e.g., Physio II). Avoid isolated ring removal, which precipitates severe recurrent regurgitation.
  * *Residual Stenosis:* Replacement of an improperly oriented/sized ring; convert to prosthetic valve replacement if irreversible Type III restriction is present.
* **Early Failure Management (≤ 2 Years):**
  * *Absence of Ring:* Immediate implantation of a remodeling prosthetic ring ([Table 39-2](#table-39-02)).
  * *Ring Dehiscence:* Refixation of the ring to the fibrous annulus with reinforced sutures.
  * *Chordal Failure:* Implantation of PTFE artificial chordae or triangular leaflet resection.
* **Late Failure Management (> 2 Years):**
  * *Degenerative Disease:* Perform re-repair through the intact ring if ring size is ≥ 34 mm. For smaller rings, temporarily detach one-third to one-half of the ring circumference ([Fig. 39-1](#fig-39-01)) to provide subvalvular exposure for artificial chordae or papillary muscle sliding plasty.
  * *Rheumatic Disease:* Re-repair (repeat commissurotomy, leaflet augmentation) is reasonable only for slow, late recurrences (> 10 years) with pliable leaflets; otherwise, valve replacement is mandatory ([Table 39-3](#table-39-03)).
  * *Ischemic/Dilated Cardiomyopathy:* Rigid or semirigid remodeling rings significantly outperform flexible bands (reoperation rates: 2.5% vs. 9.5%, p < 0.05).
* **Tricuspid Valve Reoperation:**
  * Convert failed suture annuloplasties to a rigid or semirigid prosthetic ring.
  * In Type IIIb functional disease, aggressively undersize by 2 to 3 sizes to achieve up to 10 mm of leaflet coaptation; consider adjunct anterior leaflet extension if tethering is profound.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Leaving Residual Regurgitation:** Accepting "mild-to-moderate" residual regurgitation at the index operation leads to early repair breakdown.
* **Edge-to-Edge "Rescue" Trap:** Using an Alfieri edge-to-edge stitch as a quick rescue for residual prolapse converts a Type II lesion into a restrictive Type III lesion prone to progressive calcification and stenosis ("Charybdis to Scylla").
* **Ring Removal for SAM:** Excising the annuloplasty ring to treat SAM cures the outflow gradient but causes immediate, severe annular dilatation and recurrent regurgitation.
* **Chordal Abrasion in Trenches:** Suture placement in close contact with buried chordae during papillary muscle trench shortening causes late chordal rupture.
* **Delayed Tricuspid Reoperation:** Waiting for severe symptomatic right ventricular failure in recurrent tricuspid regurgitation leads to prohibitive operative mortality and irreversible cirrhosis.

</div></details>

## MITRAL VALVE REOPERATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

Reoperations are uncommon following a successful valvular reconstruction. When they become necessary, the circumstances of the recurrent dysfunction, the mechanisms involved, and the surgical management are important considerations since accurate knowledge of occasional failures is a condition of technical progress.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Perioperative Management and Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Reoperative Conditions:** Reoperation for a residual or recurrent mitral stenosis or regurgitation requires specific conditions and approaches, which have been extensively described in Chapters 3 and 4.
* **Classification by Timing:** Failures after mitral valve reconstruction can be classified into:
  1. *Immediate Failures:* Intraoperative failures recognized by transesophageal echocardiography before chest closure.
  2. *Early Failures:* Failures occurring within the first 2 years following an initially satisfactory result.
  3. *Late Failures:* Failures occurring beyond 2 years.
* **Etiological Mechanisms:** The causes of immediate and early failures are often technique-related, whereas late failures are primarily due to the progression of the disease.
* **Historical Distribution:** In our first 15-year experience, among 72 reoperations, 28% were due to early failures and 72% to late failures.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Immediate Failures and Re-Repair Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Intraoperative Detection:** Immediate failures resulting in residual regurgitation or stenosis or abnormal leaflet motion are revealed by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography. The mechanisms identified by the echocardiographer are recognized by the surgeon during a second valve exploration ([Table 39-1](#table-39-01)).
* **Corrective Procedures:** Most residual regurgitations or systolic anterior motion (SAM) can easily be corrected by one of the reconstructive procedures listed in Table 39-1. By contrast, a residual rheumatic stenosis with a mean gradient ≥ 8 mm Hg usually requires valve replacement.
* **The Golden Rule:** Our golden rule that *"no patient should leave the operating room with residual mitral regurgitation or stenosis"* is based on the fact that a residual valve dysfunction is the strongest predictor of late failure and reoperation.
* **Incidence Reduction:** The incidence of immediate failure, which accounted for approximately 10% of the reconstructive valve operations in our early series, has been minimized with increasing experience and rigorous respect of fundamental principles: preserving or restoring optimal leaflet motion, creating a large surface of coaptation, and remodeling the annulus.

### Early Failures and Annuloplasty Omission</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Definition of Early Failure:** Early failure is defined as the recurrence of mitral regurgitation or stenosis within the first 2 years following an initially satisfactory result.
* **Omission of Ring Annuloplasty:** Among the numerous causes of early failure ([Table 39-2](#table-39-02)), annular dilatation is the most frequent in patients without ring annuloplasty. A ring had not been implanted either because the annulus was minimally dilated or because of the reluctancy to use a ring in children.
* **Linearized Reoperation Rates:** In our experience from 1975 to 1997, among 120 patients who underwent a mitral valve reconstruction without an annuloplasty ring, 20% required reoperation at a mean interval of 2 years. The linearized rate of reoperation of 3.3% per patient year (pt/yr) contrasts with the 1% per pt/yr reoperation rate found in another series of patients who systematically had a remodeling annuloplasty.
* **Surgical Re-Repair Through In Situ Ring:** An early failure can often be corrected during a reoperation ([Table 39-2](#table-39-02)). Whenever a ring implanted during the first operation appears well-positioned and inserted, it may be possible to leave it in place and to choose a technique at the leaflet level, for example, a limited triangular resection or an edge repositioning by chordae transfer ([Fig. 39-1, a](#fig-39-01)).
* **Temporary Partial Ring Detachment:** Whenever the ring is small or extensive access to the subvalvular level is necessary to proceed to a papillary muscle sliding plasty or the placement of artificial chordae, the ring can be partially detached and mobilized in the area of prolapse ([Fig. 39-1, b](#fig-39-01)). The upward traction on the detached segment combined with lateral traction of the corresponding segment of the annulus provides access to the ventricular cavity sufficient enough to proceed to the selected maneuver ([Fig. 39-1](#fig-39-01)).

### Late Failures and Disease-Specific Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Late Failure Mechanisms:** Late failures, beyond 2 years after the operation, are often related to the progression of the disease. The underlying mechanism is a new prolapse in patients with degenerative valve disease, progression of the fibrotic process in patients with rheumatic disease, or continuing ventricular remodeling in dilated or ischemic cardiomyopathies ([Table 39-3](#table-39-03)).
* **Degenerative Valve Disease:** Degenerative valve diseases have the lowest incidence of reoperation. In our early series of 153 patients followed up to 25 years, the linearized rate of reoperation was 0.4% per pt/yr. Most reoperations were due to late failures, occurring 3 to 12 years following the initial procedure. Anterior leaflet prolapse and bileaflet prolapse were more prone to reoperation than posterior leaflet prolapse ([Table 39-4](#table-39-04)). In our early experience, nearly 15% of the patients benefited from another valve reconstruction whereas this proportion today is greater than 50%.
* **Rheumatic Valvular Disease:** Rheumatic valvular diseases have a less favorable prognosis than degenerative valve diseases. In patients with mitral valve stenosis, freedom from recurrent stenosis following open mitral commissurotomy averages 90% at 10 years. In slow progressive recurrent stenosis (beyond 10 years), a repeat commissurotomy can be performed. In patients with mitral regurgitation attributable to rheumatic valvular disease, freedom from reoperation in our early experience of 951 patients was 82% and 55% at 10 and 20 years, respectively (overall rate 2% per pt/yr; Type II: 1.9% per pt/yr; Type III: 2.7% per pt/yr).
* **Ischemic Mitral Regurgitation:** Recurrent mitral regurgitation can develop as a result of recurrent myocardial infarction and/or a persistent process of ventricular dilatation, particularly in patients with an end-diastolic ventricular diameter greater than 65 mm and/or severe leaflet tethering. Rigid or semirigid rings provide significantly better annular stability than flexible rings or bands.
* **Dilated Cardiomyopathy:** A significantly undersized annuloplasty providing a large coaptation surface delays recurrent regurgitation. Superior durability is achieved with rigid or semirigid rings compared to flexible rings or bands (reoperation rates: 2.5% vs. 9.5%, p < 0.05).

## TRICUSPID VALVE REOPERATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Annuloplasty Techniques, Ring Superiority, and Reoperation Timing</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Annuloplasty Technique and Durability:** The most common cause of recurrent regurgitation after tricuspid valve reconstruction is related to the type of annuloplasty employed. In a series of 1345 tricuspid valve repairs in combination with mitral and aortic valve surgery, a ring annuloplasty was used, while in another series of 135 patients a De Vega type semicircular suture technique or band was employed.
* **Reoperation Rates at 10 Years:** The incidence of reoperation at 10 years was 0.6% in the prosthetic ring group and 6.4% in the semicircular annuloplasty group. Since the most frequent lesion in the latter group was recurrent annular deformation or dilatation attributable to suture dehiscence, the regurgitation could be corrected in most cases by a remodeling annuloplasty ring.
* **Timing of Reoperation:** Finally, it is important to stress that to prevent severe progressive impairment of right ventricular function, a reoperation for recurrent tricuspid regurgitation should not be delayed.

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Timing and Feasibility of Repeat Rheumatic Commissurotomy (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** When do you think it is reasonable to attempt a new mitral valve commissurotomy or valve reconstruction rather than a valve replacement in patients with rheumatic valvular disease?

**AC:** Whenever feasible, a new conservative procedure is reasonable if the recurrent valve dysfunction has slowly developed beyond 10 years after the first operation. Valve replacement is preferable in earlier recurrent valve dysfunction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Immediate SAM: Ring Removal versus Leaflet Height Reduction (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In immediate failure attributable to SAM following mitral valve reconstruction, it has been reported that removal of the ring alone led to resolution of SAM. What is your opinion about this approach?

**AC:** The removal of the ring does lead to the resolution of SAM but also to early recurrence of regurgitation. It is preferable to carefully identify the cause of SAM. The height of the posterior leaflet should be reduced by ovoid resection. If excess leaflet is present and the ring is too small in relation to the height of the anterior leaflet, it should be replaced with a larger ring. The use of a Physio II ring model is particularly indicated in such circumstances.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Edge-to-Edge Rescue Technique for Residual Prolapse (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In immediate failure attributable to residual prolapse, some authors have reported successful use of an edge-to-edge technique as a rescue procedure. What are your thoughts about this approach?

**AC:** A sound rescue procedure should not lead you from "Charybdis to Scylla"; that is, it should not lead you from excess leaflet motion to restricted leaflet motion, which carries the risk of fibrotic retraction and long-term calcification. True reconstructive techniques are available to deal with the problem of residual prolapse.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Posterior Leaflet Indentation Closure (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** In what circumstances during a reconstructive procedure do you recommend systematic closure of an indentation to prevent residual leak?

**AC:** Well-functioning indentations are critical for optimal function of the posterior leaflet. Only when an indentation is leaking or fibrotic should it be closed by one or two 5-0 sutures. In this case, I like to resect the marginal chordae attached to the edges of the indentation and the secondary chordae attached to the adjacent segments to facilitate leaflet motion.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Papillary Muscle Trench Chordal Shortening and Failure Mechanism (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In some studies, chordae shortening has been identified as a predictor of failure following valve reconstruction. What has been your experience with this technique that you have described and used extensively in the past?

**AC:** This technique consisted of burying the extra length of the chordae within a longitudinal trench created in the supporting papillary muscle. Some studies have reported a higher incidence of failure with this technique than when using chordae transposition or artificial chordae. Since this had not been our experience, we tried to understand what could have been the reason for this difference. The cause was most probably chordae abrasion by the sutures used to close the trench when they were placed in close contact with the shortened chordae. I can make this statement because this was the mechanism in the few cases I have encountered myself. Technical recommendations are given in this book to avoid this complication. I still use this technique from time to time.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Long-Term Durability and Freedom From Continuous Regurgitation (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Some have reported a continuous rate of recurrent regurgitation following mitral valve repair. What is your opinion about this finding?

**AC:** This has not been our experience. On the contrary, we have demonstrated that reconstructive techniques are durable. More importantly, the same durability has been confirmed by many other groups when they respected the fundamental principles of valve reconstruction. The main risk factor for this recurrence is the absence of ring annuloplasty during the first operation or the use of deformable rings or bands. Another possible mechanism is a suboptimal surface of coaptation. A valve with only a 2- to 3-mm surface of coaptation does not leak for many months or years but may begin to leak when a prolapse of one leaflet develops progressively. Yet recurrent valve regurgitation is possible after valve reconstruction, but the actuarial rate of reoperation is only 0.4% pt/yr in degenerative valve disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Reconstructive Surgical Technique with In Situ Prosthetic Ring (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Can you elaborate further on your technique of proceeding to a valve reconstruction with the ring in place?

**AC:** If the ring is large enough (that is, 34 mm or larger), it is usually easy to access the subvalvular apparatus and then proceed to a papillary muscle sliding plasty or placement of artificial chordae. If the ring is small, one third to one half of its circumference should be detached temporarily. Taking advantage of the ring’s flexibility, the insertion of a traction suture on the detached segment and retraction of the corresponding annulus provide optimal access to the subvalvular apparatus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Reoperation Risk in Rheumatic Disease and Atrial Fibrillation Management (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Knowing that the incidence of reoperation is significantly higher in patients with rheumatic valvular disease than in those with degenerative valvular disease, would it be preferable to systematically proceed to valve replacement, particularly in patients who have atrial fibrillation?

**AC:** We have shown that the durability of a mitral valve reconstruction in patients with rheumatic disease is not as bad as it has been described in the past, provided that large remodeling rings and leaflet extension have been used. The incidence of reoperation was only 2% per pt/yr in our experience. This should be put into perspective by comparison with the 2 to 3% rate of thromboembolism and 2 to 3% rate of hemorrhage observed after mechanical valve replacement. In patients with atrial fibrillation, valve reconstruction should still be presented as a valuable alternative to valve replacement because in many patients sinus rhythm can be restored after a Cox maze or Haissaguerre procedure. In those patients with persistent arrhythmia, the incidence of thromboembolism and hemorrhagic complications is less with a reconstructive operation than with a valve replacement; however, the higher reoperative rate found in these patients should be taken into account.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Residual Tricuspid Regurgitation and Annular Undersizing in Type IIIb (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** It has been reported that tricuspid annuloplasty with a remodeling ring, although superior to other techniques, is associated with 15% residual or recurrent regurgitation. What is the potential reason?

**AC:** Our experience of more than 1300 tricuspid ring annuloplasties, which have been reported in the literature, did not show such a high incidence. The high incidence you have described could be the result of suboptimal ring undersizing in patients with type IIIb regurgitation. In these patients, the anterior papillary muscle is implanted far into the right ventricle and the ventricular wall is thin and distends easily, resulting in severe leaflet tethering. Taking this into consideration, the surgeon should undersize the tricuspid annulus much more than the mitral annulus, in fact, two to three sizes rather than one or two. The construction of a larger surface of coaptation, up to 10 mm, is recommended. If it seems difficult to achieve this goal, Gilles Dreyfus proposed to systematically proceed to an additional anterior leaflet extension.

</div></details>
