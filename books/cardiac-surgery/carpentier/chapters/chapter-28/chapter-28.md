# Chapter 28. Infective Endocarditis

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical Paradigm and Diagnostic Criteria
Infective endocarditis remains a virulent, destructive pathology defined by Sir William Osler as a "malignant disease." The contemporary surgical management of bacterial endocarditis rests on two transformative paradigms:
1. **Early Surgical Intervention:** Operating early once an organism is identified and appropriate antimicrobial therapy is initiated avoids progressive, extensive tissue destruction associated with prolonged, futile attempts at medical sterilization.
2. **Reconstructive Feasibility:** Native valve reconstruction is feasible in the vast majority of mitral and tricuspid cases, offering superior resilience against persistent or recurrent infection compared to artificial prostheses.

Diagnosis in patients with fever and a cardiac murmur is confirmed using the simplified Duke criteria ([Table 28-1](#table-28-01)), requiring 2 major criteria (typical positive blood cultures, new valvular regurgitation, or characteristic echocardiographic lesions such as vegetations/abscesses) or 1 major and 3 minor criteria (recent involvement of another valve, high-risk epidemiological context, or worsening of a preexisting murmur).

### Functional Classification and Pathological Spectrum
Regardless of acute or healed status, valve reconstruction is guided by Carpentier's functional and segmental analysis ([Table 28-2](#table-28-02)):
* **Type I (Normal Leaflet Motion):** Destruction with preserved motion, characterized by vegetations, focal leaflet perforations, or localized tissue abscesses.
* **Type II (Leaflet Prolapse):** Valvular incompetence resulting from partial or complete chordal rupture, most commonly affecting the posterior leaflet or commissural scallops.
* **Type III (Restricted Leaflet Motion):** Observed predominantly in healed endocarditis or mixed etiologies, characterized by fibrous leaflet thickening, calcifications, and leaflet scarring/adhesions to the ventricular wall or papillary muscles.
* **Imaging Hallmarks:** Echocardiography ([Fig. 28-1](#fig-28-01)) visualizes dynamic vegetations and subannular abscesses (a) or flail leaflet segments from ruptured chordae (b). Intraoperative pathology ([Fig. 28-2](#fig-28-02)) confirms vegetations with perforation (a), invasive annular abscesses (b), and severe chordal disruption (c).
* **Double-Valve & "Kissing" Lesions:** Infection spreads across the aorto-mitral curtain via direct abscess extension or via a regurgitant aortic jet striking the anterior mitral leaflet, creating an infected "kissing lesion" in 10%–15% of aortic endocarditis cases.

### Surgical Indications: Three Classical Pillars
Surgical intervention is mandated across three distinct clinical categories ([Table 28-3](#table-28-03)):
1. **Bacteriological:** Infections caused by highly virulent or resistant organisms (fungi, MRSA), persistent uncontrolled septicemia despite targeted antibiotics, or persistent fever lasting >10 days without an extracardiac source.
2. **Hemodynamic:** Cardiogenic shock or acute refractory pulmonary edema from severe regurgitation warrants immediate emergency surgery; severe regurgitation even if compensated mandates early intervention to forestall irreversible ventricular dysfunction.
3. **Pathological:** Large vegetations (>10 mm) with high mobility, progressive vegetation growth on therapy, prior systemic embolization, or invasive annular abscess. In hemorrhagic stroke, surgery is ideally delayed 14 days if hemodynamically stable; in ischemic stroke, intervention is safe within 48–72 hours if no hemorrhage is present.

### Reconstructive Surgical Principles and Long-Term Results
* **Radical Debridement:** Complete, sharp excision of all macroscopically infected and necrotic tissue with a 2-mm healthy margin, without preemptive concern regarding repair feasibility. Curettage is strictly avoided to prevent bacterial dissemination and disruption of circumscribing fibrous barriers.
* **Tissue Reconstruction:** Small leaflet defects are closed primarily or with fresh autologous/bovine pericardial patches. Anterior leaflet defects of any size can be reconstructed with pericardial patches, provided the free edge and supporting marginal chordae are spared. Abscess cavities are sharply debrided and left open to the bloodstream to enhance antibiotic penetration, or reconstructed using pericardium or the sliding atrium flap.
* **Annular Remodeling & Durability:** A prosthetic remodeling ring is safely implanted whenever the annulus is dilated or in preexisting degenerative/rheumatic disease; rings are not avoided out of fear of infection. In Carpentier's landmark surgical series, perioperative mortality was 2.5%, late mortality was 2.5%, and over a mean follow-up of 30 months, there were zero recurrences of endocarditis and zero reoperations for recurrent regurgitation.

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Diagnostic Challenges and Historical Context

Despite all the progress made in antimicrobial therapy, bacterial endocarditis remains a "malignant disease," as defined by Sir William Osler in the pre-antibiotic era. This is attributable to the difficulty of establishing its diagnosis as a result of its late presentation, the emergence of antibiotic-resistant organisms, and the increasing co-morbidity of affected patients. The term "endocarditis" suggests an infection of the endocardium. Although this condition can be observed in scattered cases, the lesions generally extend the entire thickness of valvular tissue.

All cardiac valves can be involved, with in order of decreasing frequency aortic, mitral, tricuspid, and pulmonary valves. The infectious lesions affecting these valves may be destructive or proliferative. Destructive lesions include leaflet perforation, aorto-mitral separation, or chordae rupture. Proliferative lesions include vegetations and abscesses of the annulus or the leaflet tissue.

#### Advances in Surgical Timing and Native Tissue Preservation

In recent years, the surgical treatment of bacterial endocarditis has benefited from two major advances: first, the recognition that early surgical intervention is preferable to prolonged preoperative antibiotic therapy, a strategy that actually promotes the extension of valvular lesions; and second, the development of reconstructive techniques that have proven to be feasible and effective in the majority of mitral and tricuspid cases with the advantage of preserving native tissues resilient to ongoing or recurrent infection.

Bacterial endocarditis presents with either acute or chronic manifestations. Acute manifestations include sepsis, heart failure, or systemic embolization. Sepsis is manifested by the abrupt onset of fever, chills, neurological disorders, and general signs of systemic toxicity, which may lead to death. The classic subacute form is more indolent with a prolonged history of low-grade fever, arthralgia, myalgia, and splenomegaly. Blood cultures are often negative when the patient has received nonspecific antibiotic therapy.

#### Physical Examination and Clinical Suspicion

In this setting, the association of preexisting valvular disease and an unexplained fever suggests the diagnosis of endocarditis, particularly in the context of a potential source of bacterial contamination. Modifications of a preexisting murmur or the appearance of a new murmur involving another valve makes the diagnosis even more probable. In the absence of preexisting valve pathology, the discovery of valve regurgitation is also highly suggestive of bacterial endocarditis in an infectious context.

The diagnosis of endocarditis is more difficult in the setting of valve regurgitation without any signs of infection. A careful history, however, often finds episodic acute fever following dental care or subsequent to other infections treated by an incomplete course of antibiotic therapy. Whatever the clinical setting, the diagnosis of bacterial endocarditis in patients with the association of a cardiac murmur and acute or chronic fever can be established by two major or by one major and three minor simplified "Duke criteria" ([Table 28-1](#table-28-01)).

## BACTERIOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Microbial Spectrum and Predisposing Factors

In patients without preexisting valve pathology but who have risk factors such as insulin-dependent diabetes or intravenous drug abuse, the most frequent causative organism is *Staphylococcus aureus*. During the last decade, however, *Staphylococcus epidermidis* has assumed an increasing role in patients with underlying degenerative mitral valve disease.

In the subacute forms, which typically result from hematogenous seeding of a previously damaged mitral valve, the source of contamination is mostly oral with the various strains of *Streptococcus viridans*. Enterococci are a possible cause of endocarditis, particularly in patients with colorectal pathology or urinary tract infection.

#### Rare Pathogens and Culture-Negative Endocarditis

*Candida* or gram-negative organisms, particularly the HACEK group (*Haemophilus*, *Actinobacillus*, *Cardiobacterium*, *Eikenella*, and *Kingella*), are also potential, albeit rare, causes of endocarditis.

When routine blood cultures remain negative, bacterial investigations should be pursued using special media and prolonged incubation periods.

## PATHOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

### Aortic Valve Endocarditis

#### Acute Destructive and Chronic Cicatricial Lesions

The usual lesions in the acute phase of aortic valve endocarditis include leaflet vegetations, leaflet destruction, and annular abscess. Vegetations, consisting of bacteria, altered polynuclear cells, and fibrin deposits, are indicators of an acute infectious process. They may be adherent or mobile with a sessile or pedicled base of insertion.

Vegetations vary in size and rate of growth. Highly virulent organisms such as HACEK, MRSA (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*), and fungus cause particularly large vegetations that are at high risk for embolization. In the chronic phase, lesions include leaflet perforation, retraction and/or calcification.

### Isolated Mitral Valve Endocarditis

#### Morphological Variations and Abscess Formation

Several types of lesions can be observed in mitral valve endocarditis ([Fig. 28-2](#fig-28-02)), such as vegetations and leaflet perforation (a), leaflet and annular abscess (b), and chordae rupture (c). Vegetations are commonly localized on the atrial side of the leaflets.

<span id="fig-28-02"></span>![](images/_page_287_Figure_28-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 28-2**

Valvular abscesses are typically seen within the body of a leaflet, most often the anterior leaflet. A leaflet abscess may form a true aneurysm, which is followed by leaflet perforation as the final stage in its evolution. A valvular abscess can also extend to the annulus and/or the aorto-mitral curtain, leading to anterior leaflet partial detachment. Annular abscess is the most deleterious lesion in mitral valve bacterial endocarditis since it can cause a false aneurysm of the left atrio-ventricular junction.

### Associated Mitral and Aortic Endocarditis

#### Mechanisms of Spread and "Kissing Lesions"

Associated mitral and aortic endocarditis can occur because of the close anatomical relationship between these two valves. The infectious process can start from either the aortic or the mitral valve and extend towards the other. The extension from the aortic valve to the mitral valve may occur from two mechanisms:
1. An aortic annular abscess can extend to the aorto-mitral curtain and anterior leaflet, eventually causing partial or complete detachment from the annulus.
2. The diastolic jet of aortic regurgitation can produce an endothelial lesion on the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve, which then becomes secondarily infected. This so-called "kissing lesion" may occur in up to 10% to 15% of patients with aortic valve endocarditis.

Extension of endocarditis from the mitral valve to the aortic valve may also occur. This may result from direct extension of a mitral annular abscess or present as an isolated satellite lesion. The lesion is usually a small vegetation on the ventricular side of a leaflet that does not result in aortic valve regurgitation. Therefore a systematic analysis of the aortic valve is mandatory in mitral valve endocarditis.

### Isolated Tricuspid Valve Endocarditis

#### Right-Sided Endocarditis Subsets

Isolated tricuspid valve endocarditis usually occurs in patients who are intravenous drug users or in patients who have catheters or pacemaker leads that extend from the right atrium across the tricuspid valve.

### Bacteriological Indications

#### Uncontrolled Sepsis and Persistent Pyrexia

There are three primary bacteriological indications for early surgical intervention: first, the confirmation of a drug-resistant or particularly virulent organism (e.g., fungus or *Staphylococcus aureus*); second, uncontrolled septicemia despite appropriate antibiotic therapy. These two situations should trigger urgent surgical intervention after the patient has started receiving an appropriate antibiotic treatment.

The third indication is a fever persisting longer than 10 days despite appropriate antibiotic treatment in the absence of other organ infection; in this case, a valve operation should also be recommended. The strategy of prolonging preoperative antibiotic therapy to "sterilize" the valve is no longer recommended since it may promote extension of local lesions.

### Hemodynamic Indications

#### Acute Heart Failure and Valve Destruction

Severe valve regurgitation is an indication for mitral valve surgery in the setting of endocarditis. Patients with cardiogenic shock or pulmonary edema should undergo surgical intervention immediately regardless of the bacterial organism.

In patients tolerating severe valve regurgitation, in the absence of bacteriological indications for urgent intervention, the operation can be delayed to obtain negative blood cultures, but early surgical intervention is still mandatory to prevent ventricular impairment or further valve destruction.

### Pathological Indications

#### Embolic Risk and Vegetation Dynamics

Pathological indications for surgical intervention in valve endocarditis center around leaflet vegetations and annular abscess. Vegetations, which present a higher risk of embolization because of their size, number, or mobility, warrant surgical intervention.

The growth rate of the vegetations under antibiotic treatment should also be closely monitored. Whenever vegetations, regardless of their size, have caused systemic embolization, surgery should be undertaken without delay. One exception, however, is embolization to the central nervous system, causing hemorrhage. In this setting, the surgical procedure should be delayed a few days unless severe hemodynamic conditions justify an emergent operation.

Annular abscess warrants early surgical intervention to prevent extension to or destruction of adjacent structures.

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Core Principles of Repair in Acute Infection

In most instances of bacterial endocarditis involving the mitral or the tricuspid valves, a reconstructive valve operation is possible even in the context of acute infection. The following two principles must be respected:
1. **Prompt Timing:** The operation should be carried out immediately after the organism has been identified and antibiotic treatment has been instituted.
2. **Radical Debridement:** All macroscopically involved tissues are excised with a 2-mm margin of noninfected tissue, without any concern about the possibility of repair. Once the debridement is performed, the various techniques of valve reconstruction extensively described under Sections II through IV are applied.

### Vegetations

#### Excision and Local Chemical Sterilization

Pedicled vegetations may often be removed without creating a leaflet defect. They must be excised and the area of implantation sterilized by local application of Betadine or glutaraldehyde.

If a perforation occurs, in most circumstances an aortic valve replacement is necessary but mitral or tricuspid valve reconstruction is possible.

### Leaflet Defects

#### Primary and Patch Reconstruction

Leaflet defects can also be treated by excision of the infected lesion. Occasionally a patch closure of an aortic valve defect is possible.

In the mitral or tricuspid position, patch reconstruction can extend to the whole belly of the anterior leaflet. Posterior leaflet perforation is usually handled by resection and annular plication or sliding plasty.

### Annular Abscess

#### Radical Excision and Cavity Exposure

Annular abscess requires a complete sharp excision of the lesions. Debridement of the lesions with a curette should be avoided as this technique does not completely eradicate the infected tissue and may disrupt the organized tissue circumscribing the infection.

Whenever possible the abscess cavity should be left open in the bloodstream to facilitate antibiotic penetration. An aortic valve annular abscess is best treated by valve or root replacement. Whenever the atrio-valvular or atrio-ventricular junctions have been disrupted, a reconstruction should be carried out using fresh pericardium or the "sliding atrium" technique (Chapter 9). The use of pledgets or Teflon felt should be avoided as they may facilitate recurrent infection.

### Chordae Rupture

#### Native Chordal Transposition and Repair Techniques

Anterior leaflet chordae rupture can be treated by chordae transposition from the anterior or the posterior leaflet provided that the selected chordae are intact and a few millimeters distant from the resected infected area. Native chordae, as opposed to artificial chordae, should be utilized as often as possible because living tissues are more resistant to infection.

Chordae rupture of the posterior leaflet or the commissures is treated by leaflet triangular or quadrangular resection. Leaflet continuity is restored either by annular plication or by the sliding leaflet technique depending upon the extent of the resection. In rare occasions, extensive chordae rupture may require the use of artificial chordae to avoid valve replacement (see Chapters 10 and 11).

### Associated Aortic or Tricuspid Valve Lesions

#### Combined Valve Reconstruction Strategies

Severe associated aortic valve lesions require an aortic valve replacement preferably using a homograft valve. The anterior leaflet of the homograft valve may be preserved to reconstruct the aorto-mitral curtain, the anterior atrio-valvular junction, and a part of the anterior leaflet.

Limited cusp lesions, such as a small perforation, can be treated by patching. Small vegetations attached to the ventricular side of the cusp can be excised if in the absence of perforation their implantation site can be sterilized by glutaraldehyde 0.6%.

### Postoperative Care

#### Prolonged Antimicrobial Regimen

Antibiotic treatment should be continued for a minimum of 4 to 6 weeks after surgery.

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Shift from Delayed to Early Surgical Policy

For a long time, acute bacterial endocarditis with severe valvular involvement was initially treated by antibiotic administration for a period of several weeks to "cool" the infective process. In 1990, however, our group published the results of a strikingly different policy of operating at a much earlier stage, as soon as the organisms had been identified and the antibiotic treatment initiated. The aim was to avoid the continuous deleterious effect of local infection on the valvular tissue.

This early experience included 40 patients with severe mitral regurgitation and positive blood cultures. Indications for operation included heart failure (30 patients), severe valvular regurgitation (4), septic emboli (3), and uncontrolled sepsis (2). Criteria to perform valve repair were adequate antibiotic therapy for at least 1 week and large excision of all macroscopically involved tissues.

#### Clinical Outcomes and Annuloplasty Ring Safety

In all cases, reconstructive techniques were used. A ring annuloplasty was found necessary in 47% of the patients, mainly those patients who had a previous underlying pathology. Perioperative mortality was 2.5%. Reoperation was necessary in one patient. Late mortality was 2.5% (one patient). After a mean follow-up of 30 months, no recurrence of endocarditis and no reoperation for valvular regurgitation were observed.

Since this publication, our more extensive experience and other publications have confirmed that valve reconstruction in acute or chronic mitral endocarditis is possible and effective in most instances with excellent long-term results.

### Routine Head CT Scan in Endocarditis (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Should a head CT scan be systematically performed in all patients with endocarditis?

**AC:** A CT scan is mandatory in the presence of clinical neurological manifestations. Because of the existence of silent stroke, I also order a CT scan in patients presenting with vegetations.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Timing of Surgery Following Neurological Events (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What is the optimal timing of surgery following a neurological event?

**AC:** Three main factors have to be considered: (1) the type of neurological event—ischemic versus hemorrhagic; (2) the hemodynamic conditions; (3) the size and mobility of the vegetations. If there is an emergent indication for surgery (such as hemodynamic compromise resistant to medical therapy), the surgery should not be postponed. If the patient remains stable, the surgery should be postponed for at least 14 days in the setting of hemorrhagic stroke and 7 days in the presence of ischemic stroke. In the latter situation, recent data suggest that surgery can be performed safely after 48 hours. Finally, if the vegetation responsible is large and mobile, increasing the risk of recurrent embolization, early intervention is recommended.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Decision-Making Based on Vegetation Characteristics (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** How important are the size, the shape, and the mobility of vegetations in your decision-making process to intervene early?

**AC:** It is generally accepted that vegetations larger than 10 mm and mobile with prior episodes of embolization should be considered for early intervention. On the other hand, in the absence of episodes of embolization, the risk becomes minimal beyond 2 or 3 weeks, and medical treatment can be prolonged with the expectation that the vegetation will resolve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Endocarditis in Moderate Mitral Stenosis (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Would you consider operating on an asymptomatic patient with moderate rheumatic mitral valve stenosis and endocarditis that has been effectively treated medically?

**AC:** If the mitral valve stenosis is not severe enough to require surgical repair, I would treat the patient medically for at least 3 months. In the case of recurrence, valve surgery should be indicated even in moderate stenosis.

### Repair vs. Replacement in Cured Endocarditis (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Would you consider valve replacement or valve reconstruction in the previous patient?

**AC:** Valve reconstruction is preferable because it is more resistant to infection than a valvular prosthesis.

### Definition and Clinical Significance of "Kissing Lesions" (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Can you precisely define a "kissing lesion"?

**AC:** A kissing lesion is an infective process of the mitral valve that usually involves the anterior leaflet secondary to a perforation or a vegetation of the nearby aortic valve. More rarely a lesion of the belly of the anterior mitral leaflet contaminates the aortic leaflets. Any infective lesion of one of these valves should lead the surgeon to systematically explore the other valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Exploration of Aortic Valve in Mitral Endocarditis (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** With the availability of transesophageal echocardiography, should the aortic valve be surgically inspected systematically in the setting of mitral endocarditis?

**AC:** Whenever there are extensive lesions of bacterial endocarditis of the mitral valve, it is preferable to inspect the aortic valve even in the absence of any lesion or aortic regurgitation. On several occasions, I have found tiny vegetations on the ventricular aspect of one or two aortic leaflets that I could easily remove by shaving and then sterilizing the site of implantation by local application of Betadine or glutaraldehyde.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy in Double-Valve Endocarditis (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** In the setting of double valve endocarditis, if the aortic valve is replaced should the surgeon try to replace the mitral valve?

**AC:** Long-term survival is superior with single valve replacement as compared to double valve replacement. Therefore the mitral valve should be reconstructed, not replaced, whenever feasible.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Late Endocarditis with Previous Ring Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In a patient with a previous ring annuloplasty who develops a late endocarditis limited to the leaflet tissue while the prosthetic ring appears well endothelialized, what is your attitude regarding the ring?

**AC:** The ring should be systematically removed and replaced. The endocardial covering should be carefully excised, the valve lesions should be corrected, and a new ring should be implanted, placing sutures slightly outside of the groove left by the previous ring.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Limits of Leaflet Tissue Resection (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Is there a limitation in the amount of leaflet tissue you can remove without compromising a valvular reconstruction in patients with mitral valve bacterial endocarditis?

**AC:** There is very little limitation in the amount of anterior leaflet tissue you can remove and replace by pericardial patching. On several occasions I had the opportunity to replace almost the entire anterior leaflet, taking advantage that the free edge of the leaflet and the supporting marginal chordae could be spared intact. As far as the mural leaflet is concerned, a resection of more than 40% of the total surface area would compromise the result.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Remodeling Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Should a remodeling annuloplasty be performed in every patient?

**AC:** A remodeling annuloplasty should be performed only whenever necessary—that is, whenever the annulus is dilated and deformed. In the case of quadrangular resection and annular plication, one may have the temptation of not using a ring because the annulus has resumed a normal size. This is an acceptable attitude in bacterial endocarditis if the annulus is not affected by the disease process nor severely dilated. In contrast, in bacterial endocarditis complicating the course of degenerative or rheumatic valvular diseases, the original pathological process also involves the annulus and makes reinforcement of the annulus by a remodeling annuloplasty preferable.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pericardial vs. Prosthetic Ring Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Is there a role for pericardial annuloplasty?

**AC:** It has been said that a pericardial annuloplasty is more resistant to recurrent infection than a prosthetic ring. However, in our experience the use of a prosthetic ring has been associated with a minimal risk of recurrent infection provided that all infected tissues have been carefully excised. Thus I believe there is no reason for forgoing the use of a remodeling annuloplasty ring that can provide optimal reconstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Safety of Prosthetic Rings in Acute Endocarditis (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Some surgeons hesitate to use prosthetic ring annuloplasty because of concerns about recurrent infection. What is your impression of this practice?

**AC:** If the infection process does not involve the annulus, a prosthetic ring can be used safely when indicated. If the infection extends to the annulus, all infected lesions must be excised. The excised tissue is replaced by pericardium, and a prosthetic ring is inserted if necessary. In a series of more than 100 patients with acute endocarditis treated conservatively using a ring, I have seen only 2 cases of recurrent infection, which occurred several years after the initial surgery.

</div></details>

**FF:** You mention that whenever localized lesion can be excised without creating leaflet perforation, you sterilize the valve by local application of glutaraldehyde.

**AC:** This technique allows to preserve the valve. I have not seen a recurrent infection due to this technique.
