# Chapter 27. Calcified Annular Disease

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical and Pathological Entity
Extensive calcification of the mitral valve annulus (MAC) is a complex clinicopathological condition that may occur in isolation or complicate preexisting primary mitral valve disease. Characteristically forming a rigid, semilunar bar within the posterior annulus with limited extension to leaflet tissue, it differs fundamentally from rheumatic calcification, which primarily involves the commissures and leaflet free edges before spreading to the annulus. Two distinct patient subsets are recognized:
* **Primary (Senile/Metabolic) MAC:** Encountered predominantly in elderly patients and those with systemic hypertension, end-stage renal disease, diabetes mellitus, or altered calcium-phosphate metabolism (e.g., hyperparathyroidism). These patients are frequently asymptomatic or manifest mild functional stenosis, with diagnosis established incidentally via chest radiography, echocardiography ([Fig. 27-1](#fig-27-01), a), or cardiac catheterization/fluoroscopy ([Fig. 27-1](#fig-27-01), b).
* **Secondary (Degenerative/Syndromic) MAC:** Occurs in younger or middle-aged patients as a sequela of longstanding mechanical stress in Barlow's disease, fibroelastic deficiency, Marfan syndrome, or Hurler's syndrome.

### Pathogenesis and Laplace's Law
The pathogenesis of mitral annular calcification is rooted in excessive mechanical tension at the atrioventricular junction interacting with underlying connective tissue fragility ([Table 27-1](#table-27-01)):
* **Mechanical Factor (Laplace's Law, $T = P \times r$):** Elevated intraventricular systolic pressure ($P$) and increased leaflet radius of curvature ($r$) generate severe wall tension ($T$) concentrated at the leaflet hinge.
* **Structural Alterations:** Disorganization of collagen bundles and proteoglycan degradation weaken the annular hinge, producing radial fissures and cracks at the base of billowing leaflets ([Fig. 27-2](#fig-27-02), a).
* **Mineralization Cascade:** These micro-dehiscences become saturated with circulating blood components—lipids, plasma proteins, and platelet aggregates—initiating dystrophic mineralization and punctate calcium crystallization within a distinct fibrous capsule.
* **Progression to Regurgitation:** Annular rigidity and inward bar protrusion distort the subvalvular geometry, increasing chordal traction and precipitating chordal elongation, chordal rupture, and severe mitral regurgitation.

### Reconstructive Technique: En Bloc Decalcification and Annular Reconstruction
Surgical intervention is indicated exclusively for severe mitral regurgitation or severe functional stenosis. Direct suturing into or through the calcium bar invites catastrophic complications, including calcium fragmentation, annular dehiscence, circumflex artery laceration, perivalvular leaks, and fatal atrioventricular groove rupture. The Carpentier reconstructive technique adheres to two core surgical tenets:
1. **En Bloc Resection within the Fibrous Sheath:** The posterior leaflet is detached and retracted. Sharp, meticulous circumferential dissection is carried out along the endocardium around the atrial and ventricular borders of the calcium bar ([Fig. 27-3](#fig-27-03)). Maintaining the integrity of the fibrous encapsulating sheath permits complete, en bloc removal of the rigid bar without fragmentation, safely exposing the underlying atrioventricular connective tissue and epicardial fat harboring the circumflex coronary artery and coronary sinus.
2. **Annular Reconstruction Using Living Tissue:** The atrioventricular junction is reconstructed using deep 2-0 braided figure-of-eight mattress sutures placed vertically along the longitudinal axis of the ventricular myocardium (spanning one third of myocardial thickness and 1 cm in width, avoiding ischemic pledgets) and through the atrial cuff. Controlled traction on these sutures and downward displacement of the atrial edge close the atrioventricular groove and displace coronary vessels away from the neo-annulus. An additional running 4-0 polyester suture seals the junction against blood dissection, restoring a viable, pliable living annulus.

### The Sliding Atrium Flap and Subvalvular Management
* **The "Sliding Atrium Technique":** When calcium penetrates deeply into the ventricular myocardium, bar excision leaves a broad bare myocardial defect. Dissection and mobilization of the left atrial wall create a full-thickness atrial flap that is advanced downward and secured over the exposed myocardium with figure-of-eight and running 4-0 sutures, avoiding epicardial dehiscence.
* **Subvalvular Involvement:** Calcification extending into the papillary muscle heads is addressed by reallocating intact chordae to adjacent non-calcified heads, chordal transfer, or artificial chordal replacement. Extensive, transmural calcification involving the papillary muscles and entire subvalvular apparatus mandates prosthetic valve replacement.
* **Leaflet Reconstruction & Ring Remodeling:** Following annular reconstruction, leaflet prolapse is addressed by triangular or quadrangular resection, or sliding plasty. Annuloplasty is completed using a properly sized Carpentier-Edwards remodeling ring anchored by the preplaced figure-of-eight mattress sutures and interrupted horizontal sutures.

### Surgical Outcomes and Clinical Guidance
* **Clinical Series (67 Patients):** In a consecutive series of 67 patients with extensive, complex MAC undergoing en bloc decalcification and reconstruction, operative (in-hospital) mortality was 3.3%, with zero instances of atrioventricular rupture.
* **Durability:** Freedom from reoperation at 9 years was 87%, and freedom from all valve-related complications was 85% (linearized rate of 3.5% per patient-year).
* **Clinical Decision-Making in Concomitant Disease:** In elderly patients undergoing aortic valve replacement with concomitant severe MAC and moderate mitral regurgitation, addressing the aortic stenosis alone frequently reduces mitral regurgitation, warranting conservative avoidance of annular decalcification. When mitral regurgitation is severe, localized decalcification with ring remodeling or adapted valve replacement is performed.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Clinical Presentation and Etiological Dichotomy

Extensive calcification of the mitral valve annulus is a pathological entity that may or may not be associated with other mitral valve diseases. In its most characteristic configuration, it forms a semilunar deposit of calcium within the posterior annulus with limited extension to the leaflet tissue. This process differs from calcification in rheumatic valve disease, which usually involves the commissures and the leaflet tissue with only late extension to the annulus.

Mitral annular calcification can be observed in two different circumstances depending upon the presence or absence of a preexisting primary valve pathology. Annular calcification without preexisting mitral disease is seen most frequently in the elderly and in patients with systemic hypertension or metabolic disorders (e.g., diabetes, hyperparathyroidism, chronic renal failure). These patients are usually asymptomatic with the exception of rare cases of significant mitral valve stenosis. In most instances the diagnosis is made by x-ray, echocardiography ([Fig. 27-1](#fig-27-01), a), or cardiac catheterization ([Fig. 27-1](#fig-27-01), b) during a systematic clinical examination.

#### Extent and Fibrous Sheath Encapsulation

Annular calcification may also develop in situations of a preexisting mitral valve disease such as fibroelastic deficiency in older patients or Barlow's, Marfan, or Hurler's syndrome in younger patients.

The calcification process typically involves the posterior annulus with variable degrees of extension ([Fig. 27-2](#fig-27-02)). The calcification process is limited to the annulus in most patients (a) but may extend to the leaflet tissue, the ventricular myocardium, and/or the papillary muscles (b). Calcium formation is encapsulated into a fibrous sheath distinct from the surrounding tissues except in areas of myocardial infiltration. In most instances the annular calcium formation can be removed en bloc by dissecting the fibrous sheath from the surrounding tissues.

#### Biomechanical Mechanisms and Annular Fissures

The pathogenesis of mitral annular calcification remains unknown. We believe that mechanical constraints on the atrio-valvular junction play a major role in this process ([Table 27-1](#table-27-01)). In our experience, we have consistently noticed that the calcification correlates with the presence of annular fissures and cracks that are found at the base of leaflets with excess tissue ([Fig. 27-2](#fig-27-02), a). These radial fissures or dehiscences likely result from excess tension at the atrio-valvular junction, a consequence of Laplace's law.

These defects are saturated with lipoid substances, protein deposits, and platelet aggregates that initiate the process of calcification as demonstrated by punctate lesions of early mineralization. Besides mechanical factors, biochemical factors also play a role in the process. Histochemical studies have shown that proteoglycans and collagen are severely altered in degenerative valvular disease. The resulting disorientation of the collagen fibers and the increased tissue weakness initiate a vicious cycle that leads to annular dehiscence and the different stages of calcification.

#### Progression to Mitral Regurgitation

Mitral annular calcification may contribute to the onset of mitral valve regurgitation as a result of increasing tension on the subvalvular apparatus leading to chordae elongation and/or rupture. Only when the regurgitation becomes severe should surgery be undertaken.

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Surgical Challenges and En Bloc Principle

The management of annular calcification is a surgical challenge. Several surgical techniques of valve replacement have been published, with mixed results. Passing sutures through the calcium bar to secure the prosthesis is difficult, and although it is usually possible it carries a risk of calcium fragmentation, annular dehiscence, atrio-ventricular dissociation, or perivalvular leak.

The technique of en bloc excision of the calcium bar and subsequent valve reconstruction was developed to minimize these potential complications. The surgical management has been extensively described in Chapter 9. It is based on the following principles: annular decalcification by en bloc resection rather than calcium fragmentation, and reconstruction of the annulus using living tissue.

### Annular Decalcification

#### Sharp Dissection of the Encapsulated Sheath

In the typical setting of calcification of the posterior annulus, the base of the posterior leaflet is detached from the calcium block and retracted. This provides extensive exposure of the calcified annulus. Decalcification is initiated by incising the endocardium around the atrial and ventricular borders of the calcified bar using sharp dissection.

The tip of the blade should be oriented such that the surrounding tissue is separated from the calcium bar. Preserving the fibrous sheath encapsulating the calcium bar allows an en bloc resection without calcium fragmentation ([Fig. 27-3](#fig-27-03)). The dissection is performed by developing a circumferential plane from one extremity of the calcium bar towards the other until the entire length of the block is removed.

<span id="fig-27-03"></span>![](images/_page_281_Figure_27-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 27-3**

#### Protection of Circumflex Artery and Coronary Sinus

The removal of the bar exposes the atrio-ventricular connective tissue and fat, which contain and protect the circumflex artery and the coronary sinus.

### Annular Reconstruction

#### Figure-of-Eight Mattress Sutures

In most instances, the calcification process is limited to the annulus, and the en bloc decalcification leaves two edges of fibrous sheath delineating the atrium superiorly and the ventricle inferiorly. The atrio-valvular junction is reconstructed by a series of "2/0-figure-of-eight-mattress" braided sutures placed through the atrial and ventricular edges extensively described in Fig. 9-4.

The ventricular bites are oriented along the longitudinal axis of the ventricle to preserve myocardial vascularization. These sutures should involve one third of the thickness of the myocardial wall and be 1 cm wide, taking advantage of any fibrous tissue identified at the surface of the endocardium. To prevent myocardial ischemia caused by possible compression of the myocardium by the pledgets, use of pledgetted mattress sutures should be avoided.

#### Closure of the Atrioventricular Groove and Continuous Suture

Exerting traction on the "figure-of-eight-mattress" sutures, while displacing downward the atrial edge with forceps, reduces the size of the annulus and closes the atrio-ventricular groove, displacing the connective vessels and surrounding fat away from the reconstructed annulus. The two ends of the "figure-of-eight mattress" sutures are set aside to secure the ring at a later stage.

A continuous 4-0 polyester suture is used to further approximate the atrioventricular junction and to prevent its dissection by infiltration of blood. Using this technique, the atrio-valvular junction is restored as a living structure.

### Valve Reconstruction

#### Restoration of Leaflet Continuity and Ring Implantation

In circumstances where annular calcification is the only lesion, the posterior leaflet can be reattached to the reconstructed annulus following the previously described procedure. When the posterior leaflet is prolapsed, standard techniques are used, including triangular or quadrangular resection. In the case of anterior leaflet and/or commissural prolapse, which requires a technique involving the papillary muscle, the repair should precede posterior leaflet reconstruction.

After the leaflet reconstruction is completed, simple horizontal sutures are placed. A properly sized prosthetic ring is then inserted using these sutures and the "figure-of-eight-mattress" sutures. The latter sutures must be tied with gentle traction on both ends to set appropriate tension on the intermediate loops.

### The "Sliding Atrium Technique"

#### Mobilization of the Atrial Flap

Whenever calcium extends to the ventricular myocardium the en bloc removal of the calcium bar leaves a large area of myocardium without fibrous tissue coverage (see Fig. 9-5). The atrial edge is dissected to mobilize an atrial flap, which is used to cover the exposed area of myocardium. The fat and connective tissue surrounding the circumflex artery and coronary sinus are left attached to the ventricular side.

"Figure-of-eight-mattress" 2-0 braided sutures are used as described previously, with large vertical ventricular bites that take advantage of any fibrous tissue covering the ventricular endocardium. While gentle traction is applied to these sutures, the atrial flap is displaced downward with forceps to cover the area of exposed myocardium. An additional 4-0 running suture is used to secure the fixation of the atrial flap edge.

### Management of Calcified Papillary Muscle

#### Subvalvular Calcification and Papillary Muscle Heads

The heads of one or several papillary muscles may be calcified, in addition to the annulus. The calcification may or may not extend to the chordae. Intact chordae can be separated and reattached to an adjacent noncalcified papillary muscle head.

Whenever several marginal chordae require resection, chordae transposition or artificial chordae may be used.

#### Indications for Valve Replacement in Extensive Subvalvular Calcification

In cases of extensive calcification of a papillary muscle and adjacent chordae, valve replacement is indicated.

The technique of en bloc decalcification and annular reconstruction can also be used in cases with complex annular calcification that require valve replacement.

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Morbidity and Operative Mortality

Perhaps no other mitral valve lesion presents such a high potential for technical complications. For this reason, many authors recommend that the surgeon leave the calcium formation in place. However, the technique of annular decalcification and valve reconstruction described here has been associated with a low incidence of mortality and morbidity in our experience.

In a series of 67 patients, there was no incidence of atrio-ventricular rupture. The in-hospital mortality was 3.3% and the freedom from reoperation at 9 years was 87%.

#### Long-Term Freedom from Reoperation and Complications

Four patients required reoperation within 17 and 62 months for recurrent mitral valve prolapse caused by chordae rupture ($n = 2$), bacterial endocarditis ($n = 1$), and hemolysis attributable to prosthetic ring dehiscence ($n = 1$).

The results are quite remarkable in the setting of such a complex and high-risk surgical disease; they should prompt systematic use of annular decalcification in cases of calcified annulus whether a valve repair is possible or a valve replacement necessary. Freedom from all valve-related complications at 9 years was 85% for a linearized rate of 3.5% per patient/year.

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathological Differences from Calcific Aortic Stenosis (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Why does the annular calcification process in Barlow's valve remain limited to the annulus as opposed to what is seen in degenerative aortic valve stenosis?

**AC:** The reason may be the significant difference in the pathology and the stress applied to these two different valves. In Barlow's valve, the calcification process results from the weakness of the leaflet hinge and the increased tension caused by excess leaflet tissue. The calcium formation tends to concentrate at this point and turbulence plays a minor role. On the contrary, in aortic valve stenosis the primary lesion is valve sclerosis or a bicuspid valve malformation. Turbulence also plays a major role and the stress is predominantly located on the leaflet tissue.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Primary Calcification vs. Calcification in Barlow's Disease (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Is the mechanism involved in primary mitral valve annular calcification similar to that of annular calcification seen in Barlow's valve?

**AC:** Both conditions have in common the fact that the calcification process complicates the course of a degenerative annular disease. The difference is that in primary mitral valve annular calcification advanced age contributes to the degenerative process whereas in Barlow's valve the primary cause is excess tissue and annular fissures.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Low Incidence of Intertrigonal Annular Calcification (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What is your hypothesis to explain the rarity of mitral calcification in the intertrigonal portion of the annulus?

**AC:** It is not rare but less frequent. I can only make some postulations. During systole the blood ejected towards the aorta progresses without significant obstruction along the anterior leaflet and the intertrigonal area. As a result, there is little stress in this area. By contrast, at the level of the posterior annulus the jet of blood, directed toward the posterior leaflet, is constrained by this leaflet, which forms a natural obstacle. In this cul de sac, maximum stress is applied on the posterior annulus, explaining the greater tendency to calcify. But again, this is pure speculation on my part.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Critique of Alternative Surgical Techniques (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Other alternatives than your technique of annular decalcification and reconstruction have been proposed. Can you comment on these alternatives?

**AC:** These alternatives include partial decalcification and annular plication, or calcium debridement and annular reconstruction with pericardial or Teflon felt bands. Partial decalcification may be carried out in the area of leaflet resection but the corresponding plication, which is necessary to restore leaflet continuity, is rendered difficult by the rigidity of the adjacent annulus. As far as annular decalcification is concerned, an en bloc resection is preferable to fragmentation using rongeurs. Finally, annular reconstruction techniques using Teflon felt or pericardial bands are not easier to perform than the technique I described. My technique also has the advantages of using vascularized tissues and providing optimal healing conditions.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pledgetted Ventricular Sutures in Limited Calcification (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** In selected cases of limited calcification, I place pledgetted sutures around the calcium bar from the ventricular side and select a slightly large annuloplasty ring. Can you comment on this technique?

**AC:** It is an interesting alternative in very elderly patients. Care must be taken not to catch secondary chordae with the sutures.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Management of Annular Fissures at Leaflet Hinges (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** If there are fissures at the attachment of the leaflets, do you recommend resection?

**AC:** Fissures are the first stage of a progressive process leading to annular calcification, so it is preferable to resect the corresponding area of the annulus and adjacent leaflet tissue. These lesions usually occur in Barlow's disease with excess posterior leaflet tissue requiring detachment of the leaflet anyway.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Deep Ventricular Infiltration and Reconstruction Strategy (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Do you ever reject performing decalcification when calcium formation extends to the ventricular wall?

**AC:** This is a difficult situation. If the extension is limited as in most instances, one can decalcify the annulus and cover the exposed myocardium using an atrial sliding plasty, which is my preferred technique. If the calcification is more extensive or deeply penetrates the myocardial wall, it is preferable to leave the calcium formation intact or to cover it with a patch of pericardium in case of calcium fragmentation by the sutures. Valve replacement may be necessary. Prosthetic sewing extension may be necessary.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Conservative Alternatives for Non-Aggressive Surgeons (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Do you propose other surgical alternatives, even if less optimal, to surgeons who do not feel comfortable performing annular decalcification?

**AC:** An alternative I have used in elderly patients is a large leaflet extension without annuloplasty.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Giant Aseptic Annular Abscess with Toothpaste-Like Calcification (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** How do you manage a giant aseptic annular abscess with toothpaste-like calcium?

**AC:** These giant aseptic abscesses are usually in the center of the calcium formation and encapsulated by fibrous tissue. After having carefully suctioned and removed the liquefied debris, you may be left with two options: (1) en bloc excision of the remaining calcium formation can be carried out as described previously or (2) the cavity can be closed with a large pericardial patch sutured to the fibrous edge of the cavity. Then valve replacement is performed using large bites placed through the fibrous edge and the pericardial tissue.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Avoiding Pledgets in Figure-of-Eight Mattress Sutures (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Could you explain why you do not recommend the use of pledgets when using figure-of-eight mattress sutures?

**AC:** This is a matter of personal preference and surgical experience. I have observed that pledgets may produce ischemia of the myocardium as a result of their orientation parallel to the annulus and perpendicular to the vessels vascularizing the subannular region. By contrast, the orientation of the figure-of-eight sutures is parallel to these vessels and perpendicular to the annulus. The sutures are therefore nonischemic and more resistant than pledgets.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Concomitant Severe Calcific Aortic Stenosis and Moderate MR (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** A significant number of elderly patients with severe calcified aortic stenosis also present with extensive mitral annular calcification and a moderate degree of regurgitation. During an aortic valve replacement, when do you intervene on the mitral valve and what procedure do you employ?

**AC:** In this particular situation not all associated mitral regurgitation should be corrected. The relief of the aortic valve stenosis by valve replacement often leads to a significant reduction of mitral valve regurgitation. For this reason and because of the difficulty in handling these lesions, it is preferable not to address the mitral valve in most instances.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Severe Mitral Regurgitation in Concomitant Calcific Aortic Stenosis (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** For the same situation as described in the preceding question, how would you address patients with severe mitral regurgitation?

**AC:** This is such a challenging condition that a pragmatic attitude should be adopted. I usually decalcify the posterior annulus and select a Classic ring of appropriate size, the shape of which is modified to comply with the geometry of the calcified aorto-mitral curtain. Two very large bites are passed through the calcium formation on each side of the midline of the attachment of the anterior leaflet. These two large bites are enough to secure the two bended extremities of the ring.

</div></details>
