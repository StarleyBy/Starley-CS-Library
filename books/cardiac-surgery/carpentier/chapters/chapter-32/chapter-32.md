# Chapter 32. Endomyocardial Fibrosis

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Endomyocardial fibrosis is a progressive, debilitating disorder characterized by massive fibrous thickening of the ventricular endocardium resulting in restrictive cardiomyopathy:
1. **Epidemiological Distribution:** The disease exhibits high prevalence in tropical and subtropical regions where parasitic infestations are common. In temperate regions, it is encountered in association with hypereosinophilia as part of Löffler’s fibroplastic endocarditis.
2. **Topographical Distribution:** The pathological process involves the left ventricle in 30%, the right ventricle in 10%, and both ventricles (biventricular involvement) in 60% of cases.
3. **Centrifugal Progression:** The dense fibrotic process with scattered adherent mural thrombi characteristically originates at the ventricular apex and progresses toward the inflow tract, outflow tract, and atrioventricular valves. It encases first the papillary muscles and chordae tendineae, subsequently incorporating the leaflets and annulus.
4. **Hemodynamic Manifestations:** The two cardinal pathophysiological consequences are ventricular obliteration leading to restrictive diastolic filling ("absence of diastole") and atrioventricular valve dysfunction (predominantly Type IIIa restricted leaflet motion, and occasionally Type II leaflet prolapse from chordal elongation).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiography:** Demonstrates severe biatrial enlargement, apical ventricular obliteration, and marked endocardial thickening. Atrioventricular valve incompetence presents with diastolic leaflet tethering (Type IIIa) incorporating the posterior mitral leaflet or septal tricuspid leaflet, chordal fusion, and secondary annular dilatation.
* **Cardiac Catheterization:** Records a typical restrictive diastolic filling pattern with a "dip-and-plateau" (square root) contour in the right atrium, right ventricle, and pulmonary artery. Left-sided disease produces elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure, postcapillary pulmonary hypertension, and apical amputation on left ventriculography.
* **Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** Reveals intense, diffuse late gadolinium hyperenhancement of the ventricular endocardium, delineating the extent of endocardial encasement and its anatomical relationship to the subvalvular apparatus without requiring endomyocardial biopsy.
* **Histopathology:** Dense, fibrous endocardial thickening with collagenous infiltration into adjacent myocardial layers, dystrophic calcification, and overlying mural thrombosis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Historical Milestone:** First successfully operated in 1971 by Charles Dubost; the objective is to excise the fibrous peel, restore ventricular cavity compliance, and preserve or reconstruct atrioventricular valve competence.
* **Timing of Intervention:** Given the evolutive nature of endocardial fibrosis, surgical decortication is indicated as early as possible—as soon as diagnosis and apical obliteration are established—before extensive irreversible valvular destruction occurs.
* **Surgical Scenarios & Techniques:**
  1. *Limited Valvular Extension:* Transvalvular ventricular endocardiectomy. A surgical cleavage plane is developed between the fibrotic peel and myocardium; the endocardium is peeled toward the apex, with extreme care to avoid myocardial perforation. Decortication is carried retrograde up to the annulus and outflow tract ([Fig. 32-1](#fig-32-01)).
  2. *Extensive Valvular Involvement:* Transatrial exposure facilitated by temporary detachment of the posterior leaflet from the annulus. The subvalvular apparatus is dissected free from all fibrotic encasement. Leaflet continuity is restored directly or with an autologous glutaraldehyde-preserved pericardial patch (reconstructing a posterior leaflet height of approximately 20 mm).
  3. *Anterior Leaflet Management:* Chordal elongation producing Type II prolapse is repaired with secondary chordae transposition or artificial polytetrafluoroethylene neo-chordae.
  4. *Remodeling Annuloplasty:* Systematic implantation of a prosthetic remodeling ring in all patients to support the fragile, dilated annulus and prevent suture dehiscence.
  5. *Right Ventricular Specifics:* A protective thin layer of fibrous tissue must be deliberately preserved over the bundle of His and conduction system to prevent postoperative complete atrioventricular block. Systematic inspection of the right ventricle is mandatory in all cases, even when disease appears confined to the left ventricle. Tricuspid ring sizing is calibrated against the anteroposterior leaflet surface area (see Chapter 19, Fig. 19-5).
* **Postoperative Medical Regimen:** 6 months of anticoagulation followed by long-term antiplatelet therapy; aggressive medical treatment of hypereosinophilia and parasitic infections to prevent disease recurrence.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Ventricular Perforation at the Apex:** Adhesion between the fibrotic peel and myocardium is greatest at the apex, where the ventricular wall is pathologically thinned. Excessive traction can invaginate the apex and lead to full-thickness ventricular rupture.
* **Complete Heart Block:** Aggressive endocardiectomy in the right ventricle near the membranous septum risks transecting the conduction system; decortication must be avoided in the region of the atrioventricular node and bundle of His.
* **Incomplete Resection & Recurrence:** Recurrent fibrosis is exceptionally rare following complete endocardectomy; residual endocardium and uncontrolled persistent hypereosinophilia represent the two main risk factors for recurrence.
* **Contraindication to Ventriculotomy:** Direct apical left ventriculotomy is strictly contraindicated due to severe wall thinning and extreme risk of fatal postoperative rupture.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND PATHOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

Endomyocardial fibrosis is characterized by progressive fibrosis and thickening of the ventricular endocardium that result in restrictive cardiomyopathy. The prevalence of this disease is greater in tropical and subtropical countries where parasitosis is frequently encountered. Endomyocardial fibrosis can also be encountered in temperate countries and is then associated with hypereosinophilia as part of Löffler’s syndrome.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Etiology and Ventricular Distribution</summary><div class="details-content">

The disease process involves the left ventricle (30%), the right ventricle (10%), or both ventricles (60%).

A thick fibrotic layer with scattered thrombi severely adherent to the ventricular endocardium typically begins at the apical level and then extends towards the outflow tracts and atrioventricular valves, involving first the papillary muscles and chordae and later the leaflets and annulus.

The two most common consequences of this disease process are ventricular obliteration with restrictive diastolic filling and atrioventricular valve dysfunction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clinical Picture and Hemodynamic Consequences</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinical picture is that of the absence of diastole, with progressively severe symptoms of pulmonary edema, peripheral edema, and hepatomegaly.

Either mitral or tricuspid regurgitation, or both, is frequently present at a later stage.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Modalities: Echocardiography, Catheterization, and MRI</summary><div class="details-content">

Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation combines multiple imaging modalities:
* **Echocardiographic Features:** On echocardiography, the most striking anomaly is the huge dilatation of the left or right atrium, or both atria, and the most characteristic finding is the apical obliteration and endocardial thickening of the ventricles.
* **Valvular Pathology:** The most common mechanism of atrioventricular valve regurgitation is diastolic restricted leaflet motion (type IIIa). The posterior leaflet of the mitral valve or the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve is commonly thickened and incorporated in the fibrotic process. Subvalvular involvement with chordae fusion and thickening and papillary muscle fibrosis may also be present. Occasionally anterior leaflet prolapse (type II) resulting from chordae elongation can also be observed. Finally, significant annular dilatation is a common secondary lesion in these patients.
* **Cardiac Catheterization:** Cardiac catheterization shows a restrictive diastolic filling pattern with a dip-plateau recording aspect in the right atrium, right ventricle, and pulmonary artery in patients with extensive right-sided disease. In patients with left-side involvement, elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure and pulmonary hypertension are common findings. Left ventricular angiography commonly shows an apical defect.
* **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** On magnetic resonance imaging, endomyocardial fibrosis is characterized by diffuse and intense hyperenhancement of the ventricular endocardium.
* **Histopathological Findings:** Histological findings show significant fibrotic thickening of the ventricular endocardium with infiltration of fibrous tissue in the underlying myocardium. During the evolution of the disease, calcification of the fibrotic process is a common observation. In addition, thrombus formation covering the surface of the fibrotic layer may be observed.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

In 1971 Dubost first surgically treated this condition. The goal of the surgical procedure was to increase the size and the compliance of the ventricular cavity and to preserve or restore atrioventricular valve competence if necessary. Depending upon the extension of the lesions, the following two scenarios are possible.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Endocardial Fibrosis with Limited Extension to the Valvular Apparatus</summary><div class="details-content">

In this situation, ventricular endocardiectomy is used to correct the fibrosis. Through the valve orifice, the fibrotic process obstructing the apex and the inflow chamber can be easily identified ([Fig. 32-1](#fig-32-01)). Thrombus formation and even calcification are often present (a). The fibrous process reaches the base or the body of the papillary muscles but spares the chordae and the leaflet tissue.

With an approach through the valvular orifice, the dissection is initiated with the goal of creating a plane of cleavage between the fibrosis and the myocardium (b). The thickened endocardium is peeled towards the ventricular apex where the adhesion to the myocardium is greatest. Particular care must be taken at this level to resect as little normal myocardial tissue as possible, considering that dissection in the wrong plane would cause ventricular perforation.

After having removed the fibrotic process, the surgeon should continue with removal of the remaining endocardium up to the annulus and the outflow tract to prevent extension of the fibrosis (c).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Endocardial Fibrosis with Involvement of the Valvular Apparatus</summary><div class="details-content">

Even in this setting the valve can often be preserved if the leaflet tissue is still intact. Since the fibrotic process usually extends up to the annulus, the decortication is facilitated by temporary detachment of the posterior leaflet from the annulus. This provides direct access to the fibrotic process and the subvalvular apparatus, which can be dissected free from all fibrotic tissue.

The continuity between the posterior leaflet and the annulus is restored either by directly attaching the leaflet or by using leaflet extension with a glutaraldehyde-preserved autologous pericardial patch. The height of the reconstructed posterior leaflet should be approximately 20 mm.

Anterior leaflet dysfunction, if present, is best treated by secondary chordae transposition or artificial chordae. A remodeling annuloplasty is performed in all cases since the annulus is often dilated and always fragile as a result of the endocardial resection.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy for the Right Ventricle and Conduction Tissue</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical principles for right-sided lesions and long-term recurrence prevention include:
* **Preservation of Conduction Tissue:** If the disease process involves the right side of the heart, it is recommended that a thin layer of fibrosis be left in the area of the conduction tissue to avoid postoperative atrioventricular block.
* **Systematic Right Ventricular Exploration:** If during preoperative investigation the disease process seems limited to the left ventricular cavity, the right ventricle must still be systematically explored. It is not unusual to observe the beginning of a similar fibrotic process near the apex that requires excision.
* **Tricuspid Annuloplasty Calibration:** If there is no visible pathological process, the tricuspid orifice should be measured and compared to the measurement of the surface area of the "antero-posterior leaflet" delineated by exerting traction on the anterior papillary muscle (see Chapter 19, Fig. 19-5). If the annulus is one size larger than the size that covers the exposed anteroposterior leaflet tissue, a ring should be implanted.
* **Advanced Valvular Replacement:** In patients with advanced valvular lesions, a reconstructive procedure may not be feasible and a conventional valve replacement is required.
* **Medical Follow-up:** After the surgery and during the follow-up period, eosinophil counts should be carefully assessed and hypereosinophilia treated as well as occasional parasitosis to avoid recurrence.
* **Timing of Surgery:** Taking into consideration the excellent long-term results of the surgical excision of endocardial fibrosis, it is advisable to perform surgery as early as possible, before extensive involvement of the valvular apparatus—that is, as soon as the diagnosis has been established and the apical obstruction has been documented.

</div></details>

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Operative Mortality and Surgical Series</summary><div class="details-content">

Because of the rarity of this disease, most clinical series include a small number of patients:
* **Early Resection Series (1990):** In 1990 we reported an early series of 32 cases, 22 of the patients with biventricular involvement. All patients were NHYA class III or IV and all were treated by endocardial resection and valve replacement. Six patients (19%) died in the postoperative period because of cardiac failure or valve-related complications.
* **Reconstructive Series (1987-1990):** In a second series of 12 patients who underwent surgery between 1987 and 1990, the atrioventricular valves could be preserved and repaired using temporary detachment of the posterior leaflet and reconstructive techniques. All patients survived and, as in the first series, no recurrency of the endocardial fibrosis was observed.
* **Metras Experience (1985):** An earlier experience was reported by Métras and colleagues in 1985; their results showed the absence of recurrent endocardial fibrosis in all patients who had a complete endocardectomy (55 patients).

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### First Operations by Professor Charles Dubost (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Your mentor, Professor Charles Dubost, performed the world’s first operation for endomyocardial fibrosis. Did you attend this surgery and how did it develop?

**AC:** I assisted Professor Dubost for his second case. It was a striking operation. He excised the mitral valve and literally dissected en bloc the fibrous formation from the ventricular wall, which after excision looked like an extensive bloody wound. Particularly frightening was when he approached the apex because it was so thin that a small amount of traction on the fibrous formation invaginated the left ventricle. I thought the ventricle would rupture or bleed after the operation, but I was wrong.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Role of Cardiac MRI and Need for Biopsy (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Do you routinely use cardiac MRI in addition to echocardiography to confirm the diagnosis of endomyocardial fibrosis? Do you believe right ventricular endomyocardial biopsy is necessary?

**AC:** MRI is useful not only to confirm the diagnosis but also to assess more precisely the extent of the fibrosis and its relationship with the subvalvular apparatus. With the imaging techniques available today, in my opinion endomyocardial biopsy is not necessary to confirm the diagnosis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Surgery in Asymptomatic Apical Fibrosis (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Do you recommend surgical intervention in asymptomatic patients who have the obstructive form of this disease localized to the apex?

**AC:** Endomyocardial fibrosis is an evolutive disease. The fibrosis progressively extends from the apex towards the inflow chamber and the ventricular outflow tract, successively involving the papillary muscles, the chordae, and at a later stage the valvular tissue. An early operation excising all endocardial tissue including tissue that seems grossly normal avoids this progression and allows preservation of the valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Prevention of Conduction Block in Right Ventricular Fibrosis (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In patients with right ventricular endomyocardial fibrosis, what surgical strategies do you recommend to minimize the risk of postoperative heart block?

**AC:** I recommend not excising the endocardial tissue in the area of the bundle of His. Our experience has shown that it effectively prevents heart block without further extension of the fibrous tissue.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Feasibility of Valve Preservation with Subvalvular Involvement (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** In your experience, if the fibrosis involved the subvalvular apparatus, how often did you successfully perform an endocardiectomy with preservation of the atrioventricular valve?

**AC:** As our experience grew and we recommended early surgery, the preservation of the atrioventricular valve was achieved in almost all cases. Only a few cases have been referred to us too late to save the valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Role of Ventriculotomy in Endocardiectomy (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In the past, several case series have reported a left ventriculotomy approach to perform an endocardiectomy. Do you believe there is still an indication for this approach?

**AC:** I do not see any indication for this approach. After endocardiectomy, the muscular wall at the apex is thin and fragile. I would be afraid to excise and suture it. In addition, it is not necessary. The entire operation can be carried out through atriotomies with or without temporary detachment of one leaflet.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anticoagulation and Antiplatelet Protocol (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What protocol do you use for short- or long-term anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy postoperatively?

**AC:** I recommend a 6-month anticoagulation regimen followed by long-term antiplatelet therapy. In addition it is vital that hypereosinophilia be treated if present.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Probability and Determinants of Recurrent Fibrosis (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Following a successful endocardiectomy, what is the probability of recurrence of fibrosis? Have you been able to identify any risk factors?

**AC:** Recurrent fibrosis has been extremely rare in our practice. The two main risk factors are incomplete excision of the endocardium and persistent hypereosinophilia.

</div></details>
