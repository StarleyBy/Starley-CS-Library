# 24. Tricuspid Valve Malformations: Ebstein's Anomaly

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Morphological Hallmarks
Ebstein's anomaly is a rare congenital malformation accounting for approximately 0.5% of all congenital cardiac defects. Other tricuspid malformations (e.g., isolated annular dilatation, chordal elongation, leaflet agenesis, or parachute tricuspid valve) are exceptional. Unlike other congenital valvular diseases, Ebstein's anomaly involves both the tricuspid valve apparatus and the geometry and contractile function of the right ventricle.

The disease is defined by three cardinal features:
1. **Downward displacement** of the hinge lines of the septal and posterior leaflets into the right ventricle, associated with marked dilatation of the true fibrous annulus and a sail-like, tethered anterior leaflet.
2. **An atrialized portion of the right ventricle** situated between the true annulus and the displaced functional leaflet attachment.
3. **A severely reduced, malformed functional right ventricle** lacking a normal inlet component and exhibiting variable degrees of contractile impairment.

### Carpentier's Four-Tier Classification Matrix
Based on morphological spectrum and right ventricular involvement, Carpentier classified Ebstein's anomaly into four distinct types:
* **Type A:** Moderate downward displacement with preserved leaflet mobility; small, contractile atrialized chamber; normal or adequate functional right ventricular volume.
* **Type B:** Pronounced displacement with hypoplastic septal leaflet; large, thin, poorly contractile atrialized chamber; reduced functional right ventricle with preserved contractility; large, mobile anterior leaflet.
* **Type C:** Marked downward displacement with severely hypoplastic, adherent posterior and septal leaflets; very large, thin, noncontractile atrialized chamber; severely reduced functional right ventricle; restricted anterior leaflet motion with fibrous/muscular tethering to the ventricular wall.
* **Type D ("Tricuspid Sac"):** The leaflets form a continuous fibrous sac adherent to the ventricular myocardium throughout, sparing only the infundibulum; only the infundibular outflow tract functions as the right ventricle.

### Pathophysiological Dynamics and Clinical Presentation
* **Hemodynamics:** Severe tricuspid regurgitation (occasionally mixed with stenosis) combined with reduced functional right ventricular stroke volume.
* **Opposing Wall Motion:** Systolic contraction of the atrialized chamber during atrial diastole elevates right atrial pressure, promoting right-to-left shunting across an atrial septal defect (cyanosis).
* **Clinical Spectrum:** Neonates present with respiratory failure, severe cyanosis, and functional pulmonary atresia requiring prostaglandin therapy. Older children and adults exhibit exertional dyspnea, fatigue, right heart failure, and supraventricular arrhythmias (22%–42%), including Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome in 5%–10% of cases.
* **Diagnostic Workup:** Chest X-ray and CT demonstrate a massive globular cardiac silhouette; echocardiography and MRI establish leaflet anatomy, functional right ventricular volume, and the degree of left ventricular compression from paradoxical septal shift.

### Carpentier's Reconstructive Surgical Strategy
Rejecting historical transverse plication techniques (Hunter-Lillehei, Danielson) which critically reduce functional right ventricular volume, Carpentier established a four-step anatomical reconstruction:
1. **Temporary detachment and extensive mobilization** of the anterior and posterior leaflets with division of tethering subvalvular bands.
2. **Vertical (longitudinal) plication** of the atrialized ventricular chamber from within the cavity, safely avoiding epicardial right coronary artery branches while restoring physiological conical right ventricular geometry.
3. **Clockwise rotation and reattachment** of the mobilized leaflets to cover the tricuspid orifice.
4. **Remodeling prosthetic ring annuloplasty** to stabilize the true annulus and prevent late dilatation.
5. **Adjunct Procedures:** Bidirectional Glenn cavopulmonary shunt (diverting 35%–45% of systemic venous return directly to the pulmonary arteries to unload the compromised right ventricle); intraoperative division of accessory conduction pathways; primary valve replacement restricted strictly to rare Type D malformations (<5%).

### Long-Term Outcomes from Hôpital Broussais (1980–2002)
* **Cohort:** 191 consecutive patients undergoing reconstructive surgery.
* **Hospital Mortality:** 9% overall, dropping to <4% in the modern surgical era.
* **20-Year Actuarial Survival:** 82 ± 5%.
* **Functional Recovery:** 84% of surviving patients in NYHA functional class I or II.
* **20-Year Freedom from Reoperation:** 88.6%.
* **Conduction Complications:** 5% permanent complete atrioventricular block requiring pacemaker implantation.

Tricuspid valve malformations are very rare. The most frequent is the anomaly described by Ebstein in 1866, which represents only 0.5% of patients with congenital heart disease. Other tricuspid valve malformations are exceptional. They include annular dilatation, papillary muscle elongation, leaflet agenesis, absence of papillary muscle, or "parachute" valve. Tricuspid valve malformations rarely require surgery within the first years of life.

When surgery becomes necessary because of severe regurgitation and cardiac enlargement, valve reconstruction is always possible using the techniques used for similar anomalies of the mitral valve. The Ebstein's anomaly is very different because it involves both the tricuspid valve and the right ventricle.

## SURGICAL ANATOMY</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Key Morphological Features

Ebstein's anomaly has three characteristic features ([Fig. 24-1](#fig-24-01)):

1. **Abnormal Tricuspid Valve:** Comprising downward displacement of the septal and posterior leaflets (a, b) where they attach to the right ventricle as well as severe enlargement of the tricuspid annulus. Although normally attached to the annulus, the anterior leaflet is not normal: it is significantly enlarged with a sail-like configuration and various degrees of interchordal space obliteration, and it is attached to the ventricular wall by fibrous bands.
2. **Atrialized Right Ventricle:** An atrialized portion of the right ventricle above the attachment of the displaced posterior and septal leaflets (inlet portion) (c).
3. **Right Ventricular Malformation:** A malformation of the right ventricle, which is markedly reduced in size beyond the tricuspid valve, does not have an inlet chamber, and may present with different degrees of reduced contractility.

#### Carpentier's Anatomical Classification

Depending on the severity of the anatomical lesions, four types of Ebstein's anomaly have been described ([Fig. 24-2](#fig-24-02), [Table 24-1](#table-24-01)):

* **Type A:** The downward displacement of the septal and posterior leaflets is moderate and the leaflets have retained some mobility. The anterior leaflet is well developed. The atrialized chamber is small and contractile. There is adequate right ventricular volume.
* **Type B:** The downward displacement of the posterior and septal leaflets is more pronounced. The septal leaflet is hypoplastic. The atrialized chamber is large, thin, fibromuscular, and poorly contractile. The right ventricle is small with preserved contractility. The anterior leaflet is large, moves freely, and has no or limited interchordal space obliteration.
* **Type C:** The posterior and septal leaflets are markedly displaced downward and may be severely hypoplastic and adherent to the ventricle ([Fig. 24-3](#fig-24-03)). The atrialized chamber is very large, with a thin, noncontractile wall and branches of the right coronary artery flowing on its epicardial surface. The functional right ventricle is very small with a severely reduced contractility (a). The anterior leaflet, which may be partially muscular, has restricted motion resulting from interchordal space obliteration and has fibrous and/or muscular bands that attach the ventricular aspect of the valve to the ventricular wall (b). The posterolateral and anteroseptal commissures are well delineated. The communication between the atrialized chamber and the functional right ventricle may be restrictive and often displays a severe prolapse of its anterior edge. The left ventricle is small because of the paradoxical motion of the ventricular septum.
* **Type D:** This anomaly is also named "Tricuspid sac" because the leaflets form a continuous sac of fibrous tissue partially adherent to the right ventricle except in the infundibular area. Communication between the fibrous sac and the infundibulum of the ventricle is achieved by a restrictive hole. The ventricular wall, to which the fibrous sac is adherent, is thin and poorly contractile. The only functional part of the right ventricle is the infundibulum.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Hemodynamic Consequences

The hemodynamic consequence of Ebstein's malformation is predominantly tricuspid regurgitation ([Fig. 24-4](#fig-24-04)). Associated tricuspid stenosis is occasionally present.

Tricuspid valve dysfunction in association with reduced right ventricular volume and opposing atrial contraction (i.e., diastolic in the atrium and systolic in the atrialized chamber) increase the pressure in the right atrium.

<span id="fig-24-04"></span>![Figure 24-4. Pathophysiology of Ebstein's anomaly: tricuspid regurgitation, reduced right ventricular volume, opposing atrialized chamber contraction, and right-to-left shunt.](images/_page_249_Figure_24-4.jpeg)

#### Shunting and Arrhythmogenesis

The increased right atrial pressure, in association with the atrial septal defect, produces cyanosis from right-to-left shunting, cardiac arrhythmias, and right heart insufficiency, particularly in types C and D.

Opposing wall motion between the true right atrium and the atrialized ventricular chamber impairs forward diastolic filling, producing severe systemic venous congestion.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Neonatal Manifestations

In neonates symptoms of respiratory failure and cardiac failure with cyanosis are predominant. High pulmonary resistance with impairment of right ventricular function and tricuspid regurgitation generate a "functional pulmonary atresia."

Medical management includes prostaglandin infusion and mechanical ventilation until the natural decline of the pulmonary vascular resistance allows anterograde pulmonary blood flow. Clinical manifestations are directly correlated with anatomical types.

#### Presentation in Children and Adults

In children and adults dyspnea is the major cause of disability. Discrete cyanosis and cardiac arrhythmias are observed with great frequency. The incidence of arrhythmias ranges between 22% and 42% of patients, 5% to 10% of whom have Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome.

Auscultation reveals various anomalies, such as a double first sound and a systolic murmur at the apex without reinforcement at inspiration. An electrocardiogram typically shows a right electrical axis, an increased P-R interval, and a right bundle branch block with polyphasic QRS complexes in leads V2 to V4.

Congestive heart failure occurs from poor right ventricular contractility and appears at a later stage as a result of tricuspid insufficiency and/or ventricular obstruction. The obstruction results from interchordal space obliteration, separation of the inflow and outflow chambers by a restrictive orifice, and reduced contractility of the right ventricle.

## PREOPERATIVE WORKUP</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Noninvasive Imaging

Both chest X-ray and computed tomography (CT) scan show a typical globular aspect of the cardiac silhouette resulting from severe dilatation of the right atrium and the atrialized chamber ([Fig. 24-5](#fig-24-05)).

<span id="fig-24-05"></span>![Figure 24-5. Preoperative chest radiography of Ebstein's anomaly: marked cardiomegaly with characteristic globular cardiac silhouette.](images/_page_250_Figure_24-5.jpeg)

Two-dimensional echocardiography and transesophageal echocardiography allow assessment of the level of attachment of the leaflets, the number and position of papillary muscles, and the motion of the anterior leaflet, all of which aid in the determination of the type of malformation and the expected difficulty of the valvular reconstruction.

Echocardiography also helps identify associated congenital anomalies, the most frequently encountered being an atrial septal defect. The evaluation of right ventricular function is difficult by echocardiography alone.

#### Magnetic Resonance Imaging

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) clearly shows the large atrialized chamber with the small right ventricle and reduced size of the left ventricle ([Fig. 24-6](#fig-24-06)).

<span id="fig-24-06"></span>![Figure 24-6. Magnetic resonance imaging of Ebstein's anomaly: (a) axial four-chamber view showing massive atrialized chamber and compressed LV; (b) short-axis view of the small functional right ventricle.](images/_page_250_Figure_24-6.jpeg)

MRI provides accurate volumetric evaluation of the functional right ventricular cavity and quantifies the paradoxical septal shift compressing the left ventricular chamber.

## SURGICAL INDICATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Symptomatic Patients and Arrhythmias

Surgical treatment is indicated in the presence of severe tricuspid regurgitation associated with NYHA functional class III or IV, progressive cardiomegaly with a cardiothoracic ratio > 65%, or desaturation/cyanosis.

Patients with supraventricular tachycardia and flutter should be operated on even in the presence of minimal symptoms because of the risk of sudden cardiac death. In these patients, the accessory conduction pathways must be interrupted during surgery.

#### Asymptomatic Patients

Indications for asymptomatic patients are not usual except when the cardiothoracic ratio is greater than 65% and the reconstructive procedure is considered feasible by an experienced surgeon.

Early operation before irreversible right ventricular myocardial dysfunction develops prevents late postoperative right heart failure and improves long-term functional survival.

</div></details>

## SURGICAL TECHNIQUE</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Principles of Carpentier's Technique

Contrary to the classic Lillehei-Hunter-Hardy operation or the variation by Danielson that consists of a transverse plication of the atrialized chamber, Carpentier's technique consists of a sliding plasty of the anterior and posterior leaflets and a vertical (longitudinal) plication of the atrialized chamber to restore not only the function of the tricuspid valve but also the geometry and function of the right ventricle.

The surgery is performed through a median sternotomy with bicaval cannulation and hypothermic cardiopulmonary bypass. Myocardial protection is a key issue in surgical operations for Ebstein's anomaly. When proceeding to longitudinal plication of the atrialized chamber, care should be taken not to injure branches of the right coronary artery.

The main features of Carpentier's technique comprise:
1. Temporary detachment and mobilization of the anterior and posterior leaflets.
2. Longitudinal plication of the atrialized chamber.
3. Clockwise rotation and reattachment of the anterior and posterior leaflets.
4. Prosthetic ring annuloplasty.

#### Step-by-Step Reconstructive Procedure

The steps of the procedure are depicted in [Fig. 24-7](#fig-24-07) and [Fig. 24-7 cont'd](#fig-24-07-cont):

* **Exposure and Mobilization:** A right atriotomy is performed parallel to the atrioventricular groove. The tricuspid valve is exposed. The anterior and posterior leaflets are detached from the annulus, preserving a 2 mm margin of leaflet tissue for subsequent reattachment. Fibrous bands and abnormal muscular attachments connecting the ventricular aspect of the anterior leaflet to the ventricular wall are carefully divided, restoring leaflet mobility.
* **Longitudinal Plication of Atrialized Chamber:** Plication of the thin, atrialized portion of the right ventricle is performed longitudinally from the apex of the atrialized chamber to the true annulus. Interrupted pledgeted mattress sutures or continuous sutures are placed from inside the cavity, taking care to avoid deep bites that could compromise the right coronary artery branches on the epicardial surface. This vertical plication excludes the atrialized chamber and restores the conical geometry of the functional right ventricle.
* **Leaflet Rotation and Reattachment:** The mobilized anterior leaflet is rotated clockwise along the true tricuspid annulus to cover the valve orifice. The posterior leaflet is reattached adjacent to the anterior leaflet.
* **Ring Annuloplasty:** A Carpentier-Edwards prosthetic tricuspid ring or a flexible ring is implanted to stabilize the annulus and remodel its orifice. In infants and young children, a biodegradable ring or suture annuloplasty is utilized to allow future somatic growth.

<span id="fig-24-07-cont"></span>![Figure 24-7 (continued). Carpentier's reconstructive technique: (d) exclusion of atrialized chamber; (e) clockwise rotation of mobilized leaflets; (f) prosthetic ring annuloplasty and closure of atrial septal defect.](images/_page_252_Figure_24-7_cont.jpeg)

#### Surgical Technique in Type A Anomaly

Type A Ebstein's anomaly has a well-developed anterior leaflet and moderate downward displacement. The repair is simpler ([Fig. 24-8](#fig-24-08)):

1. Commissurotomy between the anterior and posterior leaflets if commissural fusion exists.
2. Limited longitudinal plication of the small atrialized chamber.
3. Prosthetic ring annuloplasty with an appropriately sized ring ([Table 24-2](#table-24-02)).

#### Management of Type D Anomaly and Cavopulmonary Shunt

In Type D Ebstein's anomaly ("Tricuspid sac"), valve tissue is severely deficient and adherent to the ventricular wall. In most instances, valve replacement is preferable in Type D anomaly. A ventricular longitudinal plication or partial resection is associated, depending upon the volume and contractility of the right ventricle. Whenever a valve replacement is carried out, the prosthetic valve must be inserted above the coronary sinus to prevent complete atrioventricular block.

Right ventricular dysfunction is one of the strongest predictors of negative outcome in patients with Ebstein's anomaly. A bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (Glenn anastomosis) between the superior vena cava and the right pulmonary artery is a valuable adjunct procedure. It reduces right ventricular preload by approximately 35% to 45%, decreases right ventricular volume overload, and facilitates weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass in high-risk patients.

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Survival and Hemodynamics

From 1980 to 2002, 191 patients were treated using this reconstructive approach (Chauvaud et al.). Hospital mortality was 9% overall, which decreased significantly to less than 4% in the recent surgical era.

Actuarial survival at 20 years was 82 ± 5% ([Fig. 24-9](#fig-24-09)). 84% of surviving patients were in NYHA functional class I or II ([Fig. 24-10](#fig-24-10)). Only 7% had a grade 3+ residual tricuspid valve regurgitation, which was usually well tolerated when an adjunct bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt was performed.

Reoperations occurred in 8% ($n = 16$) of patients, resulting in an actuarial rate of freedom from reoperation of 88.6% at both 10 and 20 years ([Fig. 24-11](#fig-24-11)).

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Surgical Experience and Historical Evolution

**FF:** Ebstein's anomaly is so rare that most surgeons have very limited experience. Can you tell us about your personal experience and surgical evolution?

**AC:** In the 1970's I contributed to cooperative programs with several surgical teams in various medical institutions in Europe. Ebstein's anomaly was one of the numerous complex valve diseases for which I was asked to operate. In the early days I was puzzled by the results of the classic technique, which was associated with significant hospital mortality and variable long-term outcome. Looking critically at these patients, I recognized that right ventricular function had a major impact on the outcome of these patients. Furthermore, it seemed to me that transverse plication of the atrialized chamber had a deleterious effect on patient outcome by reducing the volume of the remaining right ventricle. I thought that an ideal operation should not only restore good tricuspid valve function but also reestablish normal geometry of the right ventricle.

#### Indications in Asymptomatic Patients

**DA:** Do you recommend an early operation in asymptomatic patients?

**AC:** The main issue with this malformation is the fact that patients with advanced forms of the disease present with signs of severe right ventricular dysfunction at the time of operation, which increases the hospital mortality and negatively impacts long-term survival. I am strongly in favor of early operations in asymptomatic patients, but only under the condition that the surgeon has the expertise to perform a valve reconstruction rather than a valve replacement.

#### Diagnostic Thresholds and MRI Volumetry

**FF:** In asymptomatic patients, do you use any diagnostic criteria, such as the degree of cardiomegaly, to indicate the operation?

**AC:** Echo and MRI are important components of the workup of these patients. In the presence of a cardiothoracic ratio of 65% or more and/or a reduction of right ventricular volume of 30% or more on MRI, I recommend an operation even in the absence of symptoms.

#### Right Coronary Artery Protection

**DA:** Can you further elaborate on the risk of right coronary artery injury during the longitudinal plication of the atrialized chamber?

**AC:** I have not seen a case of coronary artery kinking so far, and the risk appears more theoretical than real. The branches of the right coronary artery, however, can be damaged during longitudinal plication of the atrialized chamber. For this reason, the plication is performed from the inside of the cavity, and sutures are placed carefully to avoid deep bites that could compromise the coronary vessels on the epicardial surface.

#### Bidirectional Cavopulmonary Shunt Indication

**FF:** Bidirectional cavo-pulmonary shunt is a useful adjunct in patients with right ventricular dysfunction. Do you have any quantitative data with respect to right ventricular function, such as ejection fraction on MRI, to further stratify patients who may benefit from this associated procedure?

**AC:** Since MRI and echocardiography provide limited quantitative information, several other preoperative elements are used to determine patients who would benefit from a shunt, such as type D malformation, severity of regurgitation, pulmonary artery pressures, volume and contractility of the remaining right ventricle. Intraoperatively, the difficulty of weaning the patient from cardiopulmonary bypass is the most common indication for performing a shunt.

#### Tricuspid Annuloplasty Ring Sizing

**FF:** Do you use a ring annuloplasty in all patients?

**AC:** In all patients except in young children, it is preferable to use a ring to stabilize the repair long term. In most instances, 32- to 34-mm rings can be used, which are large enough to avoid transvalvular gradients.

#### Papillary Muscle Reimplantation

**FF:** How often have you used a papillary muscle reimplantation as an adjunct technique?

**AC:** I have used papillary muscle reimplantation in only five or six cases to correct severe anterior leaflet prolapse or tethering. I found this adjunct technique more reliable than the use of artificial chordae because of the variable distension of the right ventricle.

#### Surgical Ablation of Accessory Pathways

**DA:** Can you elaborate on the effect of the reconstructive procedure on cardiac arrhythmia?

**AC:** Because of the detachment of the anterior and posterior leaflets, the accessory conduction pathways located in the tricuspid annulus are severed; this explains why supraventricular arrhythmias are eliminated in more than 50% of patients.

#### Permissible Degree of Residual Regurgitation

**FF:** What degree of residual tricuspid regurgitation do you accept following the reconstruction?

**AC:** Some degree of residual tricuspid regurgitation is frequently observed in types C and D, which does not compromise the overall functional benefit usually observed after the reconstructive operation. The intraoperative evaluation by echocardiography of residual regurgitation should not be the only criteria. Intraoperative evaluation by the surgeon is critical to assess how the anterior and reattached posterior leaflets cover the orifice area. Filling the ventricle with saline after cross-clamping the pulmonary artery is also useful to diagnose a significant residual prolapse or tethering of the leaflets. In the presence of an adequate amount of leaflet tissue, mild to moderate residual regurgitation is acceptable in types C and D.

#### Hemodynamic Protocol for Weaning from Bypass

**DA:** Can you provide some details regarding your protocol to wean these patients from cardiopulmonary bypass, particularly those with severe right ventricular dysfunction?

**AC:** The protocol for weaning the patient from cardiopulmonary bypass is extremely important. A common mistake is filling the heart to reach a normal systolic arterial pressure of around 100 mm Hg. On the contrary, the heart should be kept underfilled with a mean arterial pressure of 60 to 70 mm Hg, which is often sufficient to maintain a good urine output. The filling of the heart should be very progressive. If the patient does not tolerate ventricular filling, inotropic agents can be administered at moderate dosage. If hemodynamics and urine output remain suboptimal, an intraaortic balloon pump should be inserted, particularly in patients with compromised left ventricular function. Finally, a cavo-pulmonary shunt should be strongly considered, mostly for patients with type D Ebstein's anomaly.

#### Reconstructive Preservation vs Valve Replacement

**DA:** Do you think patients with type D Ebstein's anomaly should undergo primary valve replacement rather than a reconstructive procedure that may be associated with significant degrees of residual tricuspid regurgitation?

**AC:** Contrary to other groups, valve replacement has been rarely used in our group, although I recognize that it is a valuable method to solve the problem of regurgitation. Valve replacement is associated with potential valve-related complications that I consider more deleterious than some degrees of residual regurgitation. In practice, valve replacement is performed in redo operations and in a few selected cases of type D Ebstein's anomaly. The total incidence of valve replacement has been less than 5% in our experience.
