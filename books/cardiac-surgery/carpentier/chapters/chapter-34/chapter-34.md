# Chapter 34. Primary Valve Tumors

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Primary cardiac valve tumors represent an exceptionally rare clinical entity with distinct pathological and surgical features:
1. **Incidence and Histology:** Primary valve tumors account for approximately 10% of all primary cardiac tumors. Papillary fibroelastoma is by far the most frequent tumor arising from the cardiac valves (accounting for 75% of valve tumors and 7% to 8% of all cardiac neoplasms), followed by myxoma and fibroma. Malignant neoplasms (sarcoma, histiocytoma) and hemangiomas are exceedingly rare.
2. **Topographical Distribution:** Left-sided valves are predominantly affected. In papillary fibroelastoma, the aortic valve (especially the non-coronary cusp) and the anterior mitral leaflet or chordae are the primary sites; tricuspid involvement is rare and pulmonary involvement is exceptional. In valvular myxoma, the tricuspid and mitral valves are the most common locations, predominantly arising from the atrial side of the leaflets.
3. **Thromboembolic Hazard:** Left-sided tumors carry an ominous risk of recurrent systemic embolization (transient ischemic attack, stroke, retinal artery occlusion, and coronary embolization leading to acute myocardial infarction), often unrelated to tumor size.
4. **Reconstructive Priority:** Because the vast majority of these lesions are benign and well circumscribed, valve reconstruction rather than prosthetic replacement is the standard of care, preserving native leaflet architecture.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiographic Identification:** Transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography represent the primary imaging modalities. Papillary fibroelastoma presents as a small (usually < 20 mm), solitary, highly mobile mass attached to the leaflet or subvalvular apparatus by a short pedicle with a classic "sea anemone" frond-like appearance. Valvular myxoma appears as a larger, regular mass attached to the atrial surface of the leaflet, occasionally causing orifice obstruction.
* **Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** Differentiates tumors from non-neoplastic masses (thrombi, infective vegetations). Myxomas exhibit high extracellular water content and heterogeneous enhancement with areas of necrosis, hemorrhage, or calcification.
* **Pathological Characteristics:**
  * *Papillary Fibroelastoma:* Grossly mimics a sea anemone; histology demonstrates branching avascular papillary projections with a central dense connective tissue core rich in elastic fibers, lined by hyperplastic endothelial cells ([Fig. 34-3](#fig-34-03)).
  * *Valvular Myxoma:* Polypoid or villous architecture; histology shows acid-mucopolysaccharide-rich myxoid stroma containing characteristic stellate and polygonal cells ([Fig. 34-6](#fig-34-06)).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Indications for Surgery:** Prompt surgical intervention is indicated in all patients (symptomatic or asymptomatic) with left-sided valve tumors due to the imminent risk of systemic embolization. Right-sided tumors are operated upon when symptomatic, mobile, or causing hemodynamic obstruction.
* **Myocardial Protection & Exposure:** The heart is approached through aortotomy or atriotomy under cardioplegic arrest. Direct manipulation or palpation of the heart prior to cross-clamping is strictly avoided to prevent intraoperative tumor fragment embolization.
* **Resection Techniques for Papillary Fibroelastoma:**
  1. *Pedunculated Lesions:* Careful shave excision of the tumor off the endocardial surface of the leaflet or chordae. The implantation base is treated with a drop of 0.6% glutaraldehyde for 2 minutes followed by copious saline irrigation to destroy residual micro-cells and prevent recurrence.
  2. *Broad-Based or Infiltrating Lesions:* Full-thickness excision of the underlying leaflet tissue with a 2 to 3 mm margin of healthy tissue, followed by autologous glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial patch reconstruction.
* **Resection Techniques for Valvular Myxoma:**
  1. *Complete Excision:* En bloc excision of the myxoma including its stalk and a 2 to 3 mm cuff of healthy leaflet tissue at its base of implantation.
  2. *Leaflet Repair:* Direct suture closure of small defects; tailored pericardial patch reconstruction for larger defects.
  3. *Adjunct Annuloplasty:* Remodeling ring annuloplasty is indicated when chronic regurgitation has induced significant annular dilatation.
  4. *Chamber Exploration:* Systematic inspection of the ipsilateral ventricle, contralateral chambers, and interatrial septum is mandatory to exclude synchronous tumors or multicentricity.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Intraoperative Fragmentation:** Inadvertent manipulation of the fragile tumor before aortic cross-clamping can cause fatal stroke or coronary embolization.
* **Recurrence from Inadequate Margins:** Historically, conservative shaving without chemical ablation or full-thickness margins resulted in tumor recurrence. Full-thickness resection with a 2 to 3 mm safety margin or topical 0.6% glutaraldehyde application prevents local relapse.
* **Leaflet Distortion from Excessive Tension:** Direct primary closure of large leaflet defects induces severe restriction and regurgitation; tension-free patch augmentation with autologous pericardium is mandatory.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## PATHOLOGY AND CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Primary cardiac valve tumors are rare and comprise about 10% of all cardiac tumors, which are rare themselves.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Incidence and Histological Varieties</summary><div class="details-content">

The most common tumors are papillary fibroelastoma followed by myxoma and fibroma. Extremely rare are hemangioma, sarcoma, and histiocytoma.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clinical Manifestations and Embolic Risk</summary><div class="details-content">

In asymptomatic patients the diagnosis is suspected often incidentally during a systematic echocardiography.

In symptomatic patients, stroke or myocardial infarction attributable to peripheral embolization and congestive heart failure due to valvular dysfunction are the most common manifestations. Surgery is recommended in symptomatic as well as asymptomatic patients with left-sided tumors because of the high risk of embolization.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## PAPILLARY FIBROELASTOMA</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac papillary fibroelastoma is the most common tumor arising from cardiac valves (75%), accounting for 7% to 8% of all cardiac tumors.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomical Localization and Pathology</summary><div class="details-content">

These are benign, small, and avascular tumors often involving the left-sided valves with the aortic valve affected more commonly than the mitral valve. The non-coronary leaflet of the aortic valve and the anterior mitral leaflet are the most common locations of this tumor. In the latter case, the tumor can develop at the level of the valvular tissue or the chordae ([Fig. 34-1](#fig-34-01)). The tricuspid valve is rarely involved ([Fig. 34-2](#fig-34-02)), the pulmonary valve exceptionally. Left-sided tumors have a tendency for cerebral and retinal embolization.

The diagnosis is made by echocardiography and/or magnetic resonance imaging, which often shows a solitary, mobile mass attached to a leaflet by a short pedicle. Gross pathological analysis reveals the tumor to have the appearance of a sea anemone with multiple papillary fronds.

Upon histological examination, the tumor presents with a central core of dense connective tissue ([Fig. 34-3](#fig-34-03)). The central core is surrounded by a layer of hyperplastic endocardial cells, connective tissue, and elastic fibers.

### Surgical Techniques: Shaving, Excision, and Patch Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

The tumor is approached by aortotomy or left or right atriotomy after cardioplegic arrest to avoid embolization.

The removal of the tumor can be carried out either by shaving it off the endocardial surface of the valve leaflet, whenever the tumor is pedicled, or by excision of the leaflet tissue attaching the tumor. In the first scenario, we recommend treatment of the area of implantation with a drop of glutaraldehyde 0.6% during 2 minutes followed by generous rinsing to avoid recurrence. In the second scenario, the leaflet is reconstructed using a glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch.

The risk of recurrence is extremely low after complete excision of this tumor.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## MYXOMA</summary><div class="details-content">

Intracardiac myxomas are benign tumors that rarely originate from valves.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomical Features and Clinical Course</summary><div class="details-content">

The tricuspid and mitral valves are the most common locations of these tumors (respectively [Fig. 34-4](#fig-34-04) and [Fig. 34-5](#fig-34-05)). Most tumors are located on the atrial side of the leaflets ([Fig. 34-5](#fig-34-05)). The tumor can grow rapidly and obstruct the valvular orifice ([Fig. 34-4](#fig-34-04)).

Myxomas of the aortic and pulmonary valves have been occasionally described.

In symptomatic patients, the presenting symptoms include valvular dysfunction with obstructive lesions, peripheral embolization, and constitutional symptoms. The latter are more often observed in intracavitary myxomas compared to valvular myxomas.

### Diagnostic Imaging and Histology</summary><div class="details-content">

On echocardiography the diagnosis is often suspected following the detection of a regular mass attached to the atrial side of a leaflet with a short pedicle ([Fig. 34-5](#fig-34-05)). However, this examination does not allow discrimination between a tumor, a thrombus, or a vegetation.

Magnetic resonance imaging permits further differentiation by showing tissue with high extracellular water content and a heterogeneous appearance with areas of necrosis, hemorrhage, and calcification.

Valvular myxomas appear polypoid or villous, and histological analysis typically discloses myxoid stroma with a characteristic acid-mucopolysaccharide matrix and polygonal or stellate cells ([Fig. 34-6](#fig-34-06)).

### Surgical Excision and Leaflet Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical treatment consists of the excision of the tumor including its base of implantation to minimize the risk of recurrence.

The resulting defect is repaired primarily or with leaflet patching depending on its size. An associated remodeling annuloplasty is performed whenever the annulus is severely dilated, particularly in those patients with long-lasting valvular dysfunction.

Careful inspection of the ventricular cavity and the interatrial septum is recommended to rule out a second manifestation of the tumor.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mechanisms and Prevention of Valve Tumor Recurrence (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** How often have you seen recurrent valve tumors?

**AC:** In the past, it was not uncommon to see patients with recurrent tumors. This was due to two reasons: the first was incomplete excision to preserve cardiac structures; the second was the unawareness of the mechanism of recurrence. Today, we know that even a small number of residual tumoral cells can proliferate, leading to a large recurrent tumoral mass in a short time. Accordingly, early operation and a large excision of not only the pedicle of the tumor but also a 2- to 3-mm margin of healthy tissue are mandatory. Respecting this rule, the risk of recurrence is minimal unless there is an unrecognized second localization.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Experience with Malignant Cardiac Valve Tumors (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What has been your experience with malignant cardiac valve tumors?

**AC:** During the era of cardiomyoplasty between 1985 through 1995, I have treated three cases of malignant cardiac valve tumors. Two cases involved the tricuspid valve with extension to the septum. One involved the pulmonic valve and extended into the right outflow tract. After tumor excision, the heart was reconstructed by biological valve replacement and cardiomyoplasty with good midterm results.

</div></details>
