# Chapter 2. Valve Analysis: “The Functional Classification”

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### The Babel Syndrome in Valvular Pathology
* **Semantic Chaos:** Historical terminology created immense confusion through synonyms (e.g., flail leaflet, overshooting leaflet, and leaflet prolapse; or Barlow, billowing, ballooning, floppy, and myxomatous valve).
* **Conceptual Conflation:** Specialists frequently conflated etiology (Barlow's disease), morphology/lesions (billowing, chordal elongation), and dysfunction (leaflet prolapse).
* **Clinical Imperative:** A unified, precise vocabulary is essential for cardiologists, echocardiographers, and cardiac surgeons to communicate, standardize research, and guide operative management.

### The Pathophysiological Triad
* **Architectural Concept:** Proposed by Alain Carpentier in 1983, the triad clearly distinguishes three tiers of valvular pathology ([Table 2-1](#table-02-01)):
  1. **Etiology (The cause of the disease):** Determines long-term prognosis and medical therapy ([Table 2-3](#table-02-03)).
  2. **Lesions (Result of the disease):** Governs the selection of specific surgical reconstructive techniques ([Table 2-4](#table-02-04)).
  3. **Dysfunction (Result of lesions):** Directs the overall surgical repair strategy ([Table 2-5](#table-02-05)).
* **Clinical Metaphor:** Analogous to traumatology: vehicular collision (etiology) $\rightarrow$ femoral fracture (lesion) $\rightarrow$ inability to ambulate (dysfunction).
* **Comprehensive Diagnostic Formula:** E.g., Barlow's disease (etiology) $\rightarrow$ chordal elongation (lesion) $\rightarrow$ Type II-P2 regurgitation (dysfunction).

### The Annular Plane and Functional Classification
* **Fundamental Reference Plane:** Leaflet motion is evaluated strictly relative to the plane of the annulus during the cardiac cycle ([Fig. 2-1](#fig-02-01), [Fig. 2-2](#fig-02-02)):
  * **Type I (Normal Leaflet Motion):** Leaflet excursion has normal amplitude; leaflet margins remain at the annular level during closure. Regurgitation results from annular dilatation or leaflet perforation/tear.
  * **Type II (Excess Leaflet Motion / Leaflet Prolapse):** The free edge of one or more leaflets overrides the plane of the annulus during closure. Caused by chordal elongation/rupture or papillary muscle elongation/rupture.
  * **Type III (Restricted Leaflet Motion):**
    * **Type IIIa (Restricted Opening):** Diastolic restriction of AV valves (or systolic restriction of semilunar valves); caused by leaflet/chordal thickening, fusion, or calcification. Leads to stenosis and/or regurgitation.
    * **Type IIIb (Restricted Closure):** Systolic restriction of AV valves; caused by apical and lateral tethering from ischemic papillary muscle displacement or left ventricular dilatation. Leads to regurgitation.

### Expert Surgical Insights (Carpentier, Adams, Filsoufi)
* **Dysfunction Precedes Lesion Analysis:** The reconstructive surgeon's objective is restoring normal valve function rather than normal anatomy. Focusing on dysfunction prevents missing associated lesions (e.g., repairing ruptured chordae while overlooking adjacent elongated chordae).
* **Billowing vs. Prolapse:** Excess tissue alone produces a billowing leaflet whose free margin remains below the annular plane. Billowing is not a dysfunction per se, but increases mechanical stress and naturally evolves into prolapse.
* **Fallacy of "Type I-Type II":** Regurgitation with prolapse and annular dilatation is Type II dysfunction with annular dilatation as an associated lesion, not a dual functional type.
* **Measurement vs. Metaphor:** "Prolapse" is a scientific, quantifiable term (measured in millimeters), whereas "flail" is a descriptive metaphor describing severe chordal rupture.
* **The Foremost Surgical Error:** Haste. A systematic, meticulous intraoperative analysis of all segments and lesions is paramount before initiating reconstruction.

## PRINCIPLES OF VALVE ANALYSIS</summary><div class="details-content">

During the cardiac cycle the heart’s four valves ([Fig. 2-1](#fig-02-01)) channel blood flow in a single direction: from the atria to the ventricles and from the ventricles to the aorta and pulmonary artery. The numerous diseases that can alter the structure and function of these valves should be identified by a process of "valve analysis" involving cardiologists and surgeons.

The challenge is to establish a precise diagnosis to recommend the most appropriate therapeutic option: continued medical therapy, percutaneous valve procedure, valve replacement, or valve reconstruction. This crucial choice implies that both surgeons and cardiologists use the same terminology and share the same understanding of valvular pathology, which has not been the case for decades.

### The "Babel Syndrome" in Valvular Pathology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Historical Ambiguity and Terminological Chaos</summary><div class="details-content">

In Genesis, the Bible tells us that when the people building the Tower of Babel ceased to speak the same language, they could not work together anymore. Yet the "Babel syndrome" often prevails in medicine when different terms are introduced by different specialists to define similar findings.

A sterling example of this is found in the multiple terminologies used to describe mitral valve pathology. Terms such as prolapse, flail, partial flail, redundant, overshooting, stretching, elongation, floppy, billowing, ballooning, Barlow, dysplasia, myxoid, and myxomatous, for example, have different meanings for different specialists.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Sources of Semantic Confusion</summary><div class="details-content">

The confusion comes first from the fact that several of these terms are synonyms. For example, flail leaflet, overshooting leaflet, and leaflet prolapse are synonyms. Other synonyms are Barlow, billowing, ballooning, myxomatous valve, and mitral valve prolapse. Another source of confusion comes from the fact that for some specialists a given term such as "prolapse" means a dysfunction (leaflet prolapse) while for others it refers to a disease (mitral valve prolapse). The same chaotic situation applies to the term "floppy valve," which is used to define either a valve morphology, or a dysfunction, or a disease. Similar confusion exists in tricuspid and aortic valve diseases, in which many of the previously listed terms are used without clear distinction.

If surgeons and cardiologists do not speak the same language, how can they work together, proceed to a comprehensive valve analysis, and present valuable clinical data? These difficulties emphasize a pressing need to define precisely terminology in valve pathology.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Pathophysiological Triad</summary><div class="details-content">

A clarification can be obtained by using a pathophysiological triad with a sound distinction between the terms describing valve etiology (i.e., the cause of the disease), valve lesions resulting from the disease, and valve dysfunction resulting from the lesions ([Table 2-1](#table-02-01)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### <span id="table-02-01"></span>TABLE 2-1: Pathophysiological Triad</summary><div class="details-content">

| Component | Clinical Definition | Impact on Clinical Decision-Making |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Etiology** | The cause of the disease | Determines medical management and long-term prognosis |
| **Lesions** | Structural damage resulting from the disease | Dictates the selection of specific surgical techniques |
| **Dysfunction** | Functional disturbance resulting from the lesions | Directs the overall reconstructive strategy |

###### Conceptual framework introduced by Alain Carpentier (1983).

</div></details>

Classifying terms into these three groups permits selection of one single term among several synonyms. Taking as an example the case of degenerative mitral valve diseases, [Table 2-2](#table-02-02) outlines our selection based on historical, scientific, and semantic considerations.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### <span id="table-02-02"></span>TABLE 2-2: Application of the Pathophysiological Triad in Degenerative Mitral Valve Diseases</summary><div class="details-content">

| Triad Level | Recommended Standard Terminology | Deprecated Synonyms and Ambiguous Terms |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Etiology** | **Barlow’s disease**<br>*(Fibroelastic deficiency should be recognized as a distinct cause of degenerative valve disease)* | Myxoid valve, myxomatous valve, billowing valve, floppy valve, mitral valve prolapse |
| **Lesions** | **Leaflet billowing**<br>**Chordae elongation** | Leaflet stretching, distension, ballooning, overshooting leaflet<br>Chordae stretching, chordae distension |
| **Dysfunctions** | **Leaflet prolapse** | Flail leaflet, partial flail, overshooting leaflet, floppy valve, mitral valve prolapse |

</div></details>

A comprehensive understanding of valvular pathology implies clear distinction between etiology, lesions, and dysfunctions. This triad can be applied to all cardiac valves.

The pathophysiological triad facilitates communication between cardiologists, echocardiographers, and surgeons and greatly clarifies clinical investigations. In addition, it has significant clinical relevance for the individual patient because long-term prognosis depends upon etiology, repair strategy depends upon dysfunction, and surgical techniques depend upon lesions.

## ETIOLOGY AND VALVULAR LESIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Etiology of Valvular Diseases</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac valves can be affected by numerous diseases ([Table 2-3](#table-02-03)). Primary valve diseases involve the valvular tissue. Secondary valve diseases affect the supporting structures of the valves—that is, the ventricles for the mitral and tricuspid valves and the aorta and pulmonary artery for the aortic and pulmonary valves, respectively.

The determination of the etiology of valvular disease is important because it helps to establish the medical treatment, which should precede or follow valve reconstruction. In addition, it is the single most important predictor of long-term prognosis.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Valvular Lesions</summary><div class="details-content">

Any of the previously listed diseases can cause lesions affecting one or several components of the heart valves: the annulus, the leaflets, and the supporting structures ([Table 2-4](#table-02-04)).

These lesions may be complex, multiple, and associated, making a comprehensive description by echocardiography difficult. This difficulty is overcome by a "functional approach," which focuses attention on the dysfunctions resulting from lesions rather than on the lesions themselves.

|:---|:---|:---|
| **Annulus** | Annular dilatation | Annular dilatation |
| **Leaflets / Cusps** | • Leaflet perforation<br>• Leaflet tear<br>• Leaflet thickening<br>• Vegetations<br>• Commissure fusion<br>• Calcification | • Leaflet perforation<br>• Leaflet distension<br>• Vegetations<br>• Commissure detachment<br>• Commissure fusion<br>• Calcification |
| **Subvalvular Apparatus** | • Chordae rupture<br>• Chordae elongation<br>• Chordae thickening<br>• Chordae fusion<br>• Papillary muscle rupture<br>• Papillary muscle elongation | *Not applicable* |

## VALVE DYSFUNCTION: THE FUNCTIONAL CLASSIFICATION</summary><div class="details-content">

In the late 1970's, as the techniques of valve reconstruction developed, it became clear that it was no longer sufficient to classify the valvular pathologies in the classical three groups: valve stenosis, valve regurgitation, and combined stenosis and regurgitation. The attempts to more precisely describe valve pathology by an exhaustive anatomical description of the lesions proved to be too complex to be practical.

This "anatomical approach" was progressively abandoned and more attention was placed on the valve dysfunction resulting from these lesions. This led to the development of a functional classification ([Table 2-5](#table-02-05)). The functional classification, introduced before echocardiography became available in the operating room, resulted in significant simplification of a complex field and proved to be particularly important for the reconstructive surgeon whose primary aim was to restore normal function rather than normal anatomy of the valve.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### <span id="table-02-05"></span>TABLE 2-5: Carpentier’s Functional Classification</summary><div class="details-content">

| Functional Class | Leaflet Kinetic Description | Predominant Hemodynamic Result |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Type I** | Normal leaflet motion | Valve regurgitation |
| **Type II** | Excess leaflet motion (leaflet prolapse) | Valve regurgitation |
| **Type III** | Restricted leaflet motion | Valve regurgitation, stenosis, or both |
| **Type IIIa** | Restricted leaflet motion during opening | Stenosis, mixed stenosis and regurgitation |
| **Type IIIb** | Restricted leaflet motion during closure | Valve regurgitation |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Functional Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

The "functional approach" of valvular disease is based on analysis of the motion of the leaflets by echocardiography and direct inspection by the surgeon. Three functional types are described depending upon whether the motion of the leaflets is normal (Type I), increased (Type II), or restricted (Type III). Restricted leaflet motion may occur mainly during the opening of the valve (Type IIIa) or during valve closure (Type IIIb) ([Table 2-5](#table-02-05), [Fig. 2-2](#fig-02-02)).

Types I and II valve dysfunctions result in valve regurgitation, whereas Type III may result in valve regurgitation, stenosis, or both.

<span id="fig-02-02"></span>![](_page_8_Figure_2-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2-2**

Assessing the type of dysfunction in valvular disease is important because it helps surgeons identify the lesions causing the dysfunction ([Table 2-6](#table-02-06)).

* **Type I: Valve dysfunction with normal leaflet motion.** In Type I valve dysfunction, the course of the leaflets between systole and diastole has normal amplitude. The valve is regurgitant because of either leaflet perforation or lack of leaflet coaptation, a consequence of annular dilatation.
* **Type II: Valve dysfunction with excess leaflet motion (leaflet prolapse).** In Type II dysfunction, the motion of one or more leaflets is increased and the free edge of one or several leaflets overrides the plane of the orifice during valve closure. The hemodynamic consequence is a regurgitation. A leaflet prolapse of the mitral or tricuspid valve may be due either to chordae rupture or elongation or to papillary muscle rupture or elongation. Leaflet prolapse of the aortic or pulmonary valves may result from leaflet rupture, edge distension, or commissure detachment.
* **Type III: Valve dysfunction with restricted leaflet motion.** In Type III dysfunction, the motion of one or more leaflets is limited, either during valve opening and closure (Type IIIa), leading to various degrees of valve stenosis and regurgitation, or during valve closure (Type IIIb), leading to valve regurgitation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### <span id="table-02-06"></span>TABLE 2-6: Valve Dysfunctions and Corresponding Lesions</summary><div class="details-content">

| Valve Dysfunction (“Functional Classification”) | Corresponding Lesions: Mitral and Tricuspid Valves | Corresponding Lesions: Aortic and Pulmonary Valves |
|:---|:---|:---|
| **Type I: Normal Leaflet Motion** | • Annular dilatation<br>• Leaflet perforation or tear<br>• Vegetations | • Annular dilatation<br>• Leaflet perforation or tear<br>• Vegetations |
| **Type II: Leaflet Prolapse** | • Chordae rupture<br>• Chordae elongation<br>• Papillary muscle rupture<br>• Papillary muscle elongation | • Leaflet rupture<br>• Leaflet stretching<br>• Commissure detachment<br>• Free edge distension |
| **Type III: Restricted Leaflet Motion**<br><br>*(Type IIIa: Restricted opening)*<br><br>*(Type IIIb: Restricted closure)* | • Leaflet thickening<br>• Commissure fusion<br>• Chordae thickening<br>• Chordae fusion<br>• Calcification<br>• Ventricular aneurysm<br>• Ventricular fibrous plaque<br>• Ventricular dilatation | • Leaflet thickening<br>• Commissure fusion<br>• Calcification<br>• Sino-tubular junction dilatation |

</div></details>

The "functional classification" of valvular diseases allows the echocardiographer to assess and localize valvular dysfunctions. It provides valuable information to the surgeon, who can then proceed to a full inventory of the lesions in the areas where a dysfunction has been identified. The complexity of valvular lesions contrasts with the simplicity of the resulting valvular dysfunctions.

The functional classification complements the classical assessment of the hemodynamic consequences of valvular lesions, helping to define the indications for surgery, particularly as it relates to valve reparability.

The functional classification is the foundation of valve analysis. It serves as a guideline to achieve a successful valve reconstruction.

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Clinical Role of the Triad (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Dr. David H. Adams (DA):** You emphasized the importance of differentiating etiology, lesion, and dysfunction. Many reports in the literature fail to make such a distinction, leading to confusion. Can you further clarify the role of this triad?

**Prof. Alain Carpentier (AC):** In most clinical scenarios, a similar triad is used naturally. Let me take the example of a fracture of the leg following a car accident: the trauma is the etiology, the fracture is the lesion, and the incapacity to walk is the dysfunction. This is the approach we should follow to make a comprehensive description of a valve disease.

A comprehensive description of mitral valve regurgitation, for example, would be as follows: this patient has Barlow’s disease (etiology), a Type II-P2 regurgitation (dysfunction), the cause of which is a chordal elongation (lesion). This simple description gives the surgeon all the information she or he needs to reconstruct the valve, to assess the difficulty of the operation, and to predict its outcome. This triad is also instrumental in clinical investigations to categorize the groups of patients to be studied, avoiding the confusion that may result from comparing, for example, an etiology group such as Barlow’s disease with a dysfunction group such as anterior leaflet prolapse.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Origins and Principles of the Functional Approach</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Annular Plane as the Fundamental Reference (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**Dr. Farzan Filsoufi (FF):** What led you to the concept of the functional approach before the era of routine echocardiography?

**AC:** The fundamental principle I came upon was the importance of the annular plane as a reference to describe leaflet motion. This was achieved intraoperatively by mobilizing the leaflet segments with nerve hooks to assess their motion in relation to the plane of the annulus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Universal Reference Across All Valves (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** The annular plane is fundamental to assess mitral valve dysfunction. Is the same true for the aortic and the tricuspid valves?

**AC:** The annular plane is fundamental regardless of the valve being assessed. It serves as the reference to define the position and motion of the margin of leaflets.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Functional Approach vs. Anatomical Approach (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** How does the "functional classification" help guide surgical strategy?

**AC:** The "functional classification" allows a "functional approach" to valve reconstruction. It is useful for the surgeon because the aim of a reconstructive operation is to restore a normal valve function rather than a normal valve anatomy.

Let us take the example of a leaflet prolapse caused by chordae rupture: an anatomical approach based on the recognition of the most visible lesion would guide the surgeon to repair only the ruptured chordae with the risk of ignoring a frequently associated adjacent chordae elongation; by contrast, the functional approach leads the surgeon to focus his attention on the prolapse and therefore to identify not only the chordae rupture but also the associated chordae elongation also causing the prolapse.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Excess Tissue, Billowing, and Prolapse (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Can excess tissue cause a valve dysfunction in the absence of other lesions?

**AC:** Excess tissue produces a billowing valve but not a valve dysfunction as long as the free edges of all valvular segments remain below the plane of the mitral valve orifice. However, excess tissue carries an ongoing risk of dysfunction resulting from excess tension on the leaflet tissue and excess traction on chordae. Leaflet prolapse is a natural evolution of a billowing valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Fallacy of Hybrid Dysfunctions (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Some surgeons describe an anterior leaflet prolapse with annular dilatation as "Type I-Type II dysfunction." Can you make a comment?

**AC:** By definition, a Type I mitral regurgitation refers to a regurgitation with normal leaflet motion. If the leaflet motion is normal, it cannot be excessive (Type II) or restricted (Type III). The surgeons to whom you refer want to emphasize the frequently associated annular dilatation present in Type II dysfunction, but by doing so they introduce confusion between dysfunction and lesions.

The correct description they should use in the example you have provided would be the following: Type II anterior leaflet (dysfunction) caused by chordae rupture (lesion) with an associated annular dilatation (associated lesion).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Terminology: Prolapse vs. Flail (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Some people use the term "flail" instead of prolapse. Why do you prefer prolapse?

**AC:** Some echocardiographers use the term "flail" because it describes quite well what they see in severe prolapse caused by chordae rupture: the leaflet segment "flails" like a sail with a broken sheet. The problem is that all leaflet prolapses are not flail leaflets. A moderate or mild prolapse does not "flail," particularly when it results from chordae elongation.

If you want to express the degree of prolapse, it is better to measure it in millimeters than to use flail, partial flail, and so on. Prolapse is therefore a better term because it covers all conditions. In addition, as opposed to flail, it is a truly scientific term.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Association of Functional Types and Operative Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Association of Different Functional Types (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What about possible associations of different functional types?

**AC:** Each valvular segment of the atrioventricular valves or each leaflet of the aortic valve may have a normal function or a specific dysfunction. For example, in mitral valve regurgitation caused by rheumatic valvular disease, a common association is a Type II anterior leaflet and a Type IIIa posterior leaflet; in other words, the association of a prolapse of the anterior leaflet and a diastolic restricted leaflet motion of the posterior leaflet.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Surgical Pitfalls and the Peril of Haste (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What is the most frequent mistake you have observed while watching others perform valve reconstruction? Are there any other pitfalls?

**AC:** The haste. Most surgeons are eager to begin the reconstruction without completing a systematic analysis of the function of all valvular segments, followed by a comprehensive inventory of all lesions. Personally, even after more than 40 years of experience in valve reconstruction, I still take several minutes to analyze the valve, to develop a strategy, and to decide which techniques I am going to use.

Another major pitfall is not respecting the three basic principles of valve reconstruction or applying them incompletely: for example, partial instead of complete remodeling annuloplasty, or techniques that restrict leaflet motion or provide a suboptimal surface of coaptation.

</div></details>
