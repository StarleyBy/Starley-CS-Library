# Chapter 25. Rheumatic Valvular Disease

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Global Burden and Epidemiological Divergence
Before the mid-20th century, acute rheumatic fever (ARF) represented one of the most devastating cardiovascular epidemics worldwide and was the leading cause of military draft rejection in the United States during World War II. Following the introduction of sulfonamides and the widespread clinical adoption of penicillin in the 1940s (from 1940 onward) and 1950s, rheumatic heart disease (RHD) was largely eradicated across developed nations. However, sporadic outbreaks still emerge in industrialized countries due to virulent new strains of group A *Streptococcus*. 

Conversely, rheumatic heart disease remains a massive public health crisis and the foremost etiology of acquired valvular pathology in developing nations. Fostered by overcrowded living conditions, poverty, and limited access to primary antimicrobial prophylaxis, its true burden is substantially underestimated: contemporary systematic echocardiographic screening surveys demonstrate a subclinical disease prevalence up to 10 times higher than that identified by routine clinical examination alone.

### Clinical Presentation and the Pathognomonic Triad
Jean-Baptiste Bouillaud famously encapsulated the natural history of the illness: *"Rheumatic disease enters the throat, licks the joints, and bites the heart."* The initial pharyngeal infection caused by Lancefield group A beta-hemolytic *Streptococcus* is clinically silent in more than one third of patients; consequently, the absence of a verified history of acute pharyngitis never excludes a rheumatic etiology. Transient systemic manifestations—migratory polyarthritis, subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum, and Sydenham's chorea (Jones criteria)—resolve without residual disability, whereas cardiac involvement ("the bite") drives long-term morbidity and mortality.

In chronic rheumatic mitral regurgitation, echocardiography and intraoperative surgical inspection reveal a characteristic diagnostic triad:
1. **Type IIIa restricted leaflet motion** predominantly affecting the posterior leaflet, contrasting sharply with discrete Type II prolapse of the anterior leaflet.
2. **Specific structural valvular lesions**, including commissural fusion with diastolic doming ("hockey-stick" deformity), fibrous leaflet thickening, and extensive chordal fusion.
3. **Associated multivalvular disease**, typically involving the aortic or tricuspid valves.

Differential diagnosis with congenital mitral malformations is guided by patient age (murmurs detected before age 2 years strongly suggest a congenital origin) and the presence of associated intracardiac anomalies.

### Histopathology and Progressive Morphological Evolution
* **Acute Exudative Phase:** Characterized by acute pancarditis. Interstitial connective tissue exhibits intense edema, eosinophilia, fibrinoid necrosis, and collagen bundle fragmentation. Multinucleated histiocytic aggregates form pathognomonic Aschoff bodies within the myocardium. Interstitial valvulitis and endocarditis promote secondary cellular infiltration and the deposition of small, translucent, verrucous vegetations along leaflet coaptation lines, where repetitive mechanical impact exacerbates the local inflammatory cascade.
* **Chronic Cicatricial Phase:** Active lesions heal through progressive, relentless fibroblastic proliferation and collagen accumulation. Diffuse fibrous thickening and retraction shorten the leaflets. The chordae tendineae undergo marked thickening, shortening, and interchordal fusion, producing a rigid, retracted, funnel-shaped subvalvular apparatus. Commissural fusion restricts orifice opening, while secondary dystrophic calcification infiltrates the scarred commissures, leaflets, and annulus, transforming regurgitant lesions into mixed or stenotic disease.

### Surgical Indications and Timing
* **Acute Rheumatic Carditis:** Historically deemed an absolute contraindication to surgery due to the fear of reactivating carditis and encountering suture pull-out in friable tissue, Alain Carpentier revolutionized management in the 1970s by demonstrating that acute regurgitation is predominantly Type I (annular dilatation and leaflet perforation) without advanced subvalvular scarring. Today, patients with acute rheumatic carditis and refractory pulmonary edema unresponsive to aggressive medical therapy have an absolute indication for emergency reconstructive surgery. Responding patients are maintained on high-dose corticosteroids and penicillin, deferring elective surgery until the acute inflammatory reaction subsides.
* **Chronic Rheumatic Mitral Stenosis:** Surgical or percutaneous intervention is indicated for symptomatic patients (NYHA class II–IV, orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, pulmonary edema), complications (systemic thromboembolism, hemoptysis, right ventricular failure), or asymptomatic patients with severe resting pulmonary hypertension (PASP >50 mm Hg at rest or >60 mm Hg with exercise) or anticipated pregnancy.
* **Selection of Interventional Modality:** Percutaneous balloon dilatation has largely replaced closed surgical commissurotomy in pliable valves devoid of calcification or subvalvular disease. Open surgical reconstruction (commissurotomy, chordal fenestration, secondary chordal resection) is preferred for valves with significant subvalvular fusion or localized calcification. Closed transventricular commissurotomy remains a valid, cost-effective option in low-resource environments where disposable balloon catheters are cost-prohibitive.
* **Feasibility of Type IIIa Reconstruction:** Feasibility is dictated by leaflet pliability and subvalvular preservation ([Table 25-2](#table-25-02)). Pliable leaflets with minimal subvalvular lesions yield excellent reparability; rigid leaflets with extensive subvalvular calcification and fusion generally necessitate prosthetic valve replacement.
* **Prognostic Impact of Atrial Fibrillation:** Atrial fibrillation marks a critical turning point in the natural history of rheumatic mitral disease, precipitating sudden hemodynamic decompensation and markedly increasing thromboembolic risk. Surgery should ideally be performed prior to permanent atrial fibrillation or significant left ventricular remodeling.

### Reconstructive Surgical Principles and Techniques
1. **Mitral Reconstruction:**
   * *Leaflet Mobilization:* Systematic incision of fused commissures down to the annulus without creating regurgitation, accompanied by surgical division of underlying fused papillary muscle heads.
   * *Subvalvular Reconstruction:* Extensive resection of restrictive secondary chordae and meticulous interchordal fenestration of thickened marginal chordae to restore free margin diastolic excursion.
   * *Leaflet Thinning and Pericardial Extension:* Rigid or thickened leaflet bellies undergo shaving/thinning. Severely retracted leaflets (particularly the posterior leaflet) are augmented with glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patches. Posterior patch height must not exceed 20 mm to prevent systolic anterior motion (SAM) and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction ("curtain effect").
   * *Prosthetic Remodeling Annuloplasty:* Sizing must be guided by anterior leaflet surface area rather than intercommissural distance to prevent functional postoperative stenosis. Ring sizes below 30 mm in adult females, 32 mm in adult males, and 28 mm in children must be strictly avoided.
2. **Concomitant Aortic Reconstruction:** Leaflet extension of all three aortic cusps using tailored glutaraldehyde-fixed autologous pericardial strips or the Ross procedure is preferred in young patients. In combined mitral and aortic disease, mechanical aortic valve replacement combined with mitral valve repair is strongly preferred over double valve replacement.
3. **Concomitant Tricuspid Reconstruction:** Severe or moderate functional tricuspid regurgitation is treated with a rigid or semi-rigid prosthetic remodeling ring. Dilated annuli exceeding sizer dimensions by more than one size warrant annuloplasty even in the absence of severe regurgitation.

### Postoperative Management and Secondary Prevention
* **Anticoagulation:** Warfarin therapy targeting an INR of 2.0–3.0 is maintained for 1 to 2 months postoperatively in patients in sinus rhythm, and extended indefinitely for chronic atrial fibrillation, left atrial enlargement, mural thrombosis, or following the Maze procedure.
* **Secondary Antibiotic Prophylaxis:** Intramuscular benzathine penicillin G (1.2 million units for adults, 0.6 million units for children every 3 to 4 weeks) is mandatory throughout life or at least until age 40 in endemic regions. Standard antimicrobial prophylaxis is strictly indicated prior to all dental and invasive procedures to prevent subacute bacterial endocarditis.

### Long-Term Clinical Outcomes
* **European Adult Cohort (Hôpital Broussais, Paris; $N = 951$, Mean Age 25.8 Years, Follow-up 7–29 Years):**
  * Hospital mortality: 2.0%.
  * Actuarial survival: 89% ± 19% at 10 years, 82% ± 18% at 20 years.
  * Thromboembolic event rate: 0.4% per patient/year.
  * Linearized reoperation rate: 2.0% per patient/year (freedom from reoperation: 82% ± 19% at 10 years, 55% ± 25% at 20 years). Reoperation was significantly higher in Type III dysfunction (2.7%/pt-yr) compared to Type II (1.9%/pt-yr, $p < 0.05$).
* **Asian Pediatric Cohort (Ho Chi Minh City Heart Institute; $N = 130$, Mean Age 11.8 Years):**
  * Hospital mortality: 0.7%.
  * 6-year freedom from reoperation: 96.8%; 89% showed no significant stenosis or regurgitation on late echocardiography.
* **Aortic Valve Leaflet Extension Cohort ($N = 89$, Mean Age 16 Years):**
  * Operative mortality: 1.1%.
  * 7-year freedom from reoperation: 85% ± 5%, confirming pericardial reconstruction as a durable bridge in young rheumatic patients.

Before 1950 rheumatic fever was one of the most common epidemics in the world, with considerable socioeconomic consequences. As an example, acute rheumatic fever was the most common cause of draft rejection in the United States Armed Forces during World War II. Dramatic changes took place when in the mid-1940s (from 1940 onward) sulfanilamides were found to be efficient in the treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis, the cause of rheumatic fever. Since then, with the widespread use of antibiotics, this disease has been practically eradicated in developed countries although a resurgence may appear from time to time because of the emergence of new strains of *Streptococcus*.

By contrast, the prevalence remains high in developing countries, where progress has been more limited because of unfavorable socioeconomic conditions and the financial burden of prophylaxis. In populations at risk, systematic echocardiography screening showed that the prevalence is much greater than previously thought and 10 times higher than the number of cases determined by clinical screening.

Today the incidence of rheumatic fever varies considerably from one country to another, and from one social community to another. It remains the main cause of valvular disease worldwide.

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Historical Perspective and Initial Infection

"Rheumatic disease enters the throat, licks the joints and bites the heart."* This aphorism from Jean-Baptiste Bouillaud, the French physician who first demonstrated the relationship between this disease and its valvular manifestations, summarizes the clinical characteristics.

*\*“Le rhumatisme articulaire aigu entre par la gorge, lèche les articulations et mord le coeur.”*

Although its pathophysiology is still not fully understood, it has been proven that the rheumatic process is caused by a streptococcus group A pharyngitis. Of note, more than one third of the infections that trigger rheumatic fever are asymptomatic. Therefore in the presence of valvular disease, a rheumatic origin cannot be ruled out in patients who have no history of sore throat. Other associated manifestations of rheumatic disease should be systematically investigated.

#### Systemic Manifestations and Diagnostic Triad

In the acute phase of rheumatic fever, transient episodes of polyarthritis or polyarthralgia are a common feature. As pointed out by Bouillaud, they only "lick the joints" and do not leave any residual disability. Other possible transient clinical manifestations—such as subcutaneous nodules, erythema marginatum, or chorea (all part of Jones criteria)—are suggestive of rheumatic fever.

> **Clinical Principle:** The absence of previous typical manifestations of rheumatic fever or throat infection does not rule out the possible rheumatic origin of a valvulitis.

In contrast to these systemic manifestations, rheumatic fever "bites the heart," and the cardiac involvement is responsible for the mortality associated with this disease. Cardiac manifestations can develop either during the acute phase or later during the chronic phase of the disease. In the acute phase, pancarditis symptoms are always present and type I mitral regurgitation is generally observed.

In the chronic phase, establishing the diagnosis of rheumatic fever is more difficult. In patients with valvular manifestations, the rheumatic origin is suggested by echocardiography and intraoperative examination ([Fig. 25-1](#fig-25-01)).

#### Pathognomonic Valvular Findings and Differential Diagnosis

For example, in the presence of mitral valve regurgitation (a), three findings are strongly in favor of the diagnosis of rheumatic valvular disease:
1. **Type IIIa mitral valve dysfunction** predominant on the posterior leaflet, contrasting with discrete prolapse of the anterior leaflet (b);
2. **Specific valvular lesions**, that is, commissural fusion with doming of the leaflet in diastole, leaflet thickening, and chordae fusion (c); and
3. **Associated aortic or tricuspid valve lesions**.

A congenital malformation of the mitral valve may occasionally make it more difficult to establish a differential diagnosis. The young age of the patient is particularly suggestive of a malformation if a cardiac murmur has been diagnosed before the age of 2 years. Also suggestive is the presence of associated cardiac malformations. Identification of rheumatic valvular disease has important consequences because it implies a potential recurrence of the disease.

> **Pathological Feature:** The association of a type IIIa dysfunction of the posterior leaflet and a limited type II of the anterior leaflet is characteristic of a rheumatic mitral valve.

## PATHOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Acute Exudative and Vegetative Reactions

In the acute phase of rheumatic disease, exudative inflammatory reactions involve the connective tissue of the heart, brain, joints, and skin. The interstitial connective tissue is edematous and eosinophilic with fragmentation and separation of the collagen fibers. Mononuclear cells and in particular multinucleated histiocytes form Aschoff bodies in the myocardium but not in other organs.

Similar inflammatory reactions involve the valvular endocardium and interstitial tissue of the valve leaflets and chordae. Fibrinoid necrosis and secondary cell infiltration lead to the formation of small, translucent, verrucous vegetations along the closure line of the leaflets. The presence of verrucae at the coaptation line of the leaflets rather than at their free edge is explained by the fact that the inflammatory reaction is aggravated by the impact of repetitive valve closure.

#### Chronic Cicatrization, Fibrosis, and Calcification

In the chronic phase, the acute inflammatory lesions heal by progressive fibrosis with continuous collagen accumulation ([Fig. 25-2](#fig-25-02)). Diffuse fibrous thickening and retraction of the leaflets develop. The chordae tendineae shorten and fuse together, resulting in a funnel-shaped subvalvular apparatus.

Fibrous commissural fusion leads to reduced mobility and orifice narrowing. Dystrophic calcification develops in scarred areas, involving commissures, leaflets, and annulus, further aggravating valve stenosis.

## SURGICAL INDICATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Acute Phase of Rheumatic Fever

Until the early 1970s cardiac surgery was considered to be contraindicated during the acute phase of rheumatic disease. Surgery was delayed until the inflammatory process had subsided because of fear of reactivating the rheumatic process and because friable tissues were considered unsuitable for reconstructive surgery.

In the 1970s, based on the principle that valvular regurgitation in this acute phase is mainly type I (annular dilatation and leaflet perforation) with no or minimal leaflet retraction or chordae lesions ([Fig. 25-3](#fig-25-03)), Alain Carpentier showed that reconstructive surgery is feasible and provides good results. Since then, it has been demonstrated that the risk of death with medical treatment during this acute phase is greater than the surgical risk. Today, acute rheumatic carditis with severe refractory heart failure is an indication for emergency surgery. However, in patients who respond to medical treatment, surgery should be delayed until the inflammatory process has subsided.

> **Surgical Rule:** Valvular surgery is not contraindicated in patients with an active rheumatic inflammatory process not responding to medical therapy.

#### Chronic Phase of Rheumatic Fever: Mitral Stenosis

Mitral valve stenosis with signs of pulmonary venous hypertension—such as orthopnea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea, and pulmonary edema—is an indication for surgical intervention. Other indications are complications such as systemic embolization, hemoptysis, and the development of right heart failure.

Intervention should also be considered if asymptomatic mitral valve stenosis exists in the presence of severe pulmonary arterial hypertension (pulmonary artery systolic pressure greater than 50 mm Hg at rest or 60 mm Hg during exercise) or in women contemplating pregnancy.

#### Choice of Interventional Modality

The type of intervention that is recommended for mitral valve stenosis has been widely discussed. The choice is between percutaneous balloon dilatation,* closed surgical commissurotomy, open surgical commissurotomy, and valve replacement. The decision depends upon the severity of the valvular and subvalvular lesions, the presence of subvalvular calcifications, and the presence of associated valve lesions.

The development of balloon dilatation has drastically reduced the indications for closed surgical commissurotomy. Balloon dilatation is indicated in the presence of pliable leaflets without significant subvalvular lesions or calcifications. In the presence of severe subvalvular lesions, extensive chordae thickening or fusion, open surgical repair with commissurotomy and subvalvular reconstruction is preferred.

*\*NOTE: The term "balloon dilatation" is preferred to "balloon commissurotomy," which is sometimes used.*

> **Prognostic Marker:** Atrial fibrillation is a turning point in the evolution of mitral valve disease, reducing the functional capacity, aggravating ventricular function, and increasing the risk of thromboembolic events.

#### Chronic Mitral Regurgitation and Feasibility Assessment

In patients with mitral valve regurgitation, medical treatment has a limited and transient effect. In patients with severe regurgitation, surgery should be performed before ventricular enlargement or the onset of atrial fibrillation. "Atrial fibrillation represents a turning point in the history of a mitral valve disease": it reduces cardiac output, has a deleterious effect on ventricular function, and favors thromboembolic complications.

Growing emphasis has been placed in recent years on the significant advantage of operating on asymptomatic patients to prevent the onset of complications. The timing of surgery is strongly influenced by the likelihood of valve reconstruction. The likelihood of a reconstructive operation depends upon the severity of valvular lesions and the surgeon's experience ([Table 25-2](#table-25-02)). This emphasizes the importance of a precise valve analysis by an experienced team.

#### Indications in Associated Aortic Valve Disease

Aortic valve regurgitation requires valvular surgery when symptoms develop or when asymptomatic patients present with left ventricular enlargement (left ventricular end-systolic diameter > 50 mm or > 25 mm/m²) or impaired left ventricular ejection fraction (< 50%).

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Mitral Valve Lesions

The surgical techniques used to treat mitral valve disease have been extensively described in Chapter 13. Several specific issues pertaining to rheumatic valve disease should be emphasized:

* **Leaflet Tissue Quality and Quantity:** The feasibility of valve reconstruction is based on the quality and the amount of leaflet tissue. Leaflet thickening and rigidity can be addressed by leaflet thinning and decalcification, but leaflet retraction requires patch extension with glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium.
* **Subvalvular Apparatus Mobilization:** In the presence of valve stenosis, special attention must be paid to the subvalvular apparatus. Extensive division of secondary chordae and fenestration of marginal chordae are essential to restore leaflet mobility.
* **Prosthetic Ring Selection:** Selection of an annuloplasty ring is of critical importance. In children or young adults, we recommend avoiding under-sizing the ring to prevent postoperative stenosis. Rings 30 to 32 mm in women, 32 to 34 mm in men, and 28 to 30 mm in children are recommended. The surgeon should not hesitate to proceed to a leaflet enlargement to respect this recommendation.

#### Aortic Valvular Lesions

Severe aortic regurgitation requires valve repair or a Ross procedure in young patients. Leaflet extension using autologous pericardium is the technique of choice.

Mild to moderate aortic regurgitation associated with mitral valve disease may progress over time. In selected patients, associated aortic valvuloplasty should be considered.

Severe aortic valve dysfunction that requires valve replacement in patients undergoing mitral valve reconstruction represents a challenging situation. In young patients, we prefer mechanical aortic valve replacement with mitral valve reconstruction over double valve replacement. Every effort should be made to preserve the native mitral valve.

#### Tricuspid Valve Lesions

In associated moderate or severe tricuspid valve dysfunction, the tricuspid valve should be repaired during the same procedure. In minimal tricuspid valve regurgitation or significant right atrial enlargement without regurgitation, the tricuspid valve should be explored through a right atriotomy.

In the presence of annular dilatation more than one size greater than the sizer covering the anterior leaflet tissue, a remodeling annuloplasty is recommended. In young patients, when functional tricuspid regurgitation is present with annular dilatation, suture annuloplasty may be considered.

## POSTOPERATIVE MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Anticoagulation Strategy

In patients with sinus rhythm, Coumadin should be given for 1 to 2 months postoperatively unless contraindicated. Patients with an enlarged left atrium or thrombus formation or patients recovering from a Maze procedure require a longer period of anticoagulation therapy.

In patients with atrial fibrillation who have benefited from cardioversion, a long-lasting restoration of sinus rhythm is necessary before discussing the discontinuation of anticoagulation treatment.

#### Long-Term Antimicrobial and Endocarditis Prophylaxis

In all patients, long-term prophylactic treatment with penicillin is indicated, particularly in countries with a high prevalence of rheumatic fever.

Finally, knowing that subacute bacterial endocarditis, a potential long-term complication, is not prevented by penicillin treatment alone, specific standard antibiotic prophylaxis is recommended before any invasive procedure, including dental care.

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Mitral Valve Reconstruction

Very long-term results of mitral valve reconstruction in rheumatic valvular disease were extensively analyzed in patients older than 15 years who underwent surgery at the Broussais Hospital in Paris (European study) and in children younger than 15 years who underwent surgery at the Vietnam Heart Institute in Ho Chi Minh City (Asian study).

#### European Study (Adults)

In this series, 951 patients surgically treated between 1970 and 1994 were followed for a period of 7 to 29 years. Mean patient age was 25.8 ± 18 years. A total of 37% of the patients were in NYHA functional class II, 57% in class III, and 10% in class IV; 62% had sinus rhythm. A total of 36 patients (4%) underwent surgery during the acute phase of rheumatic disease.

Functional classification based on leaflet motion showed that 7% of the patients were type I (normal leaflet motion), 33% type II (leaflet prolapse), 36% type IIIa (diastolic restricted leaflet motion), and 24% type II/III, i.e., prolapse of the anterior leaflet associated with restricted leaflet motion of the posterior leaflet. #### Asian Study (Children)

This study analyzed the results of mitral valve reconstruction in 130 children who were surgically treated at the Heart Institute in Ho Chi Minh City between 1992 and 1997. The patients' ages ranged from 3 to 15 years with a mean age of 11.8 ± 2.8 years. Of these children, 85% had rheumatic valve disease and 13% had congenital mitral valve malformation.

Valve analysis showed 11% type II (leaflet prolapse), 60% type III (restricted leaflet motion), and 29% type II/III (associated anterior leaflet prolapse and posterior restricted leaflet motion). There was one in-hospital death (0.7%); after 6 years 96.8% of the patients were free from reoperation, and 89% showed no signs of significant residual stenosis or regurgitation at echocardiography.

#### Aortic Valve Reconstruction

From September 1992 to December 2002, 89 consecutive patients with a mean age of 16 ± 5 years underwent valve reconstruction with leaflet extension of the three leaflets. A total of 80 patients had pure aortic insufficiency, and 9 had mixed aortic disease; 29 patients (31%) and 60 patients (69%) had additional mitral and/or tricuspid valve disease.

Aortic valve repair consisted of free edge aortic cusp extension using three rectangular strips of glutaraldehyde-treated fixed autologous pericardium. A total of 29 patients (31%) had isolated aortic valve repair, 39 patients (44%) had combined aortic and mitral procedures, and 21 patients (23%) had triple valve repair.

Operative mortality was 1.1%. Actuarial freedom from reoperation at 7 years was 85% ± 5%. Residual mild or moderate aortic insufficiency was present in 22% of patients at late follow-up. This study demonstrates that aortic valve reconstruction with autologous pericardial leaflet extension is a valuable alternative in a young rheumatic population.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Congenital Malformations Mimicking Rheumatic Disease

**FF:** What are the most common congenital malformations that mimic rheumatic valve disease?

**AC:** Papillary muscle commissure fusion may mimic rheumatic valve disease because one or two commissures may be completely fused. However, it can be differentiated from rheumatic valve disease because the fusion is not caused by inflammation but by the close relationship between the papillary muscle and the commissure.

#### Differentiating Degenerative and Rheumatic Calcification

**DA:** Many surgeons classify patients with mitral valve disease and leaflet calcification as having rheumatic disease. Can you differentiate degenerative leaflet calcification from true rheumatic disease?

**AC:** Calcification is not a specific process but a secondary process that can occur in any long-lasting mitral valve disease. The area of calcification is influenced by the etiology. In rheumatic valve disease, the process of calcification begins at the commissures and extends to the leaflet tissue. By contrast, in degenerative valve disease, the calcification involves primarily the annulus with extension to the base of the leaflet, the basal chordae, and occasionally the tip of one or two papillary muscles. In addition, specific lesions observed intraoperatively help differentiate these diseases.

#### Surgical Timing in Acute Rheumatic Carditis

**FF:** In the past, patients in the acute phase of rheumatic fever were treated medically. Surgery was delayed until the acute inflammatory process subsided. Did the introduction of valve reconstruction change this policy?

**AC:** About 40 years ago, a young Algerian child with acute rheumatic fever and mitral regurgitation was presented to me by Prof. Jean Lenègre, the renowned French cardiologist. This child had been treated with extensive antiinflammatory drugs and antibiotics for 2 months with no significant recession of the inflammatory symptoms and was in refractory pulmonary edema. Surgery was performed as an emergency life-saving procedure. A large ring annuloplasty and decortication of the pericardium were performed. The immediate postoperative recovery was uneventful and the patient had no further symptoms of rheumatic carditis. Over the years, we operated on 36 patients during the acute phase of rheumatic carditis with similar success.

This experience changed our point of view and we demonstrated that surgery was not contraindicated during this period. Because of the severity of the hemodynamic condition, we decided to proceed with a valve operation. The valve could be repaired, and after the surgery we were surprised to observe that the spectacular hemodynamic improvement was associated with a rapid resolution of the inflammatory syndrome. This was an important observation that showed the influence of hemodynamic conditions on the evolution of the inflammatory process, a finding confirmed by several authors. These findings led to the recommendation of performing valve surgery in the acute phase of the disease if significant improvement has not occurred using medical therapy.

#### Indications for Closed Surgical Commissurotomy

**DA:** Are there any situations in which closed surgical commissurotomy is indicated?

**AC:** Balloon dilatation is as efficient as a closed surgical commissurotomy. However, it is an expensive procedure because of the cost of disposable balloons; therefore a closed commissurotomy with a mechanical dilator still has a role in developing countries.

#### Acceptable Postoperative Echocardiographic Gradient

**FF:** In the normal size adult, what is the maximum mean gradient you would accept on echocardiography after mitral valve reconstruction?

**AC:** This is a difficult issue because of the variability of this parameter, depending upon loading condition, heart rate, and cardiac output. After surgery under more normal physiological conditions, we commonly observe a reduction of the transmitral gradient. Intraoperatively, the size of the implanted ring is an important consideration as much as the pliability of the leaflet tissue. I am not very concerned with a mean gradient less than 8 mm Hg in a patient with pliable tissue and a ring equal to or greater than 30 mm in females and 32 mm in males as opposed to the same gradient in a patient who has a smaller ring and thickened leaflets.

#### Role of Artificial Chordae in Rheumatic Disease

**DA:** Are there any indications to use artificial chordae replacement in rheumatic valve disease?

**AC:** In patients with thickened marginal and secondary chordae, it may be useful to extensively resect these chordae for optimal leaflet mobilization; this may require supporting the free edge with artificial chordae. However, if valvular lesions are too advanced, it is often preferable to proceed with valve replacement surgery.

#### Leaflet Patch Extension and Ring Sizing

**DA:** Leaflet patch extension increases the height of the anterior leaflet more than the intercommissural distance. How does it affect ring selection strategy?

**AC:** This depends upon whether the leaflet is enlarged transversally or vertically. The size of the ring should be based upon the size of the enlarged leaflet, which is measured with sizers. Care should be taken to carefully measure the new intercommissural distance and the new anterior leaflet height to decide whether a Classic ring is preferred, because the extremities of this type of ring can be bent to conform to the new configuration of the anterior leaflet. If the posterior leaflet has been enlarged, the total amount of leaflet tissue is a more important parameter affecting ring selection than the intercommissural distance. After injection of saline into the ventricular cavity, the sizers are used to measure the total amount of leaflet tissue closing the orifice.

#### Target Dimensions for Posterior Leaflet Extension

**DA:** How much leaflet extension should we target on the posterior leaflet?

**AC:** To avoid a curtain effect, which obstructs the mitral valve orifice, extension of the posterior leaflet should not be too long or extensive. The reconstructed height of the posterior leaflet should be less than 20 mm and the transverse length of the patch should be limited.

#### Assessment of Leaflet Peeling

**FF:** What is your opinion about leaflet peeling, which consists of the en bloc removal of the fibrotic process covering the leaflet?

**AC:** It is an interesting approach widely used by Prof. Kumar in India. I tried it several times in Africa and Southeast Asia. You can effectively peel the belly of the anterior leaflet, but the peeling is less complete in the coaptation zone and the posterior leaflet because of the indentations. The main concern is the recurrence of valve fibrosis.

#### Freedom from Reoperation vs. Echocardiographic Criteria

**DA:** Your use of freedom from reoperation as a criterion to evaluate the success of reconstructive valve surgery has been criticized as being rather crude when compared to echocardiography findings. What is your response?

**AC:** My response is that freedom from reoperation is a more objective criterion. Provided that the follow-up is complete, the need for reoperation is a definite endpoint whereas echocardiographic findings are subjective and examiner dependent. An echocardiographic follow-up is ideal but may be difficult to obtain and a serious limitation in developing countries.

#### Cohort Size in Early Decades of Surgical Experience

**DA:** Another comment regarding your reports on long-term results has been that your studies involved a relatively small number of patients during the first two decades of data collection.

**AC:** The primary reason is that in the first decade I performed surgery on a limited number of patients. This number grew rapidly as my experience increased and complementary techniques developed during the second decade. A second reason for the limited number of patients is the fact that we could not obtain accurate data from patients returning to Africa, so we excluded all patients coming from this continent. As a result, the follow-up was more complete but the number of patients was more limited. Our current follow-up in Asia confirms our original observation.

#### Public Health Strategy in Developing Countries

**FF:** What health policy decisions are required to decrease the prevalence of rheumatic diseases in developing countries?

**AC:** In the past century, rheumatic valve disease has been nearly eradicated in developed countries. There is no reason that the same result cannot be achieved in this century for the rest of the world. Resources must be directed at improving living conditions and providing greater access to health care and widespread antibiotic coverage for all children.
