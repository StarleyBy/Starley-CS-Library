# 12. Techniques in Type II Commissural and Bileaflet Prolapse

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical Spectrum and Pathological Anatomy
* **Etiology and Prevalence:** Commissural leaflet prolapse is typically observed in bacterial endocarditis (chordae rupture) and degenerative valve disease (Barlow's disease with chordae elongation).
* **Classification by Extent:**
  * *Limited Prolapse:* Confined to the commissural leaflet and measuring $\le 5$ mm of the leaflet margin.
  * *Extensive Prolapse:* Involves the commissural leaflet and extends into one or both paracommissural areas (A3/P3 or A1/P1).
* **Functional Role of the Commissure:** The commissure is a vital functional component of the mitral valve. Surgical reconstruction must restore a functional 5-mm bridge of coapting tissue rather than simply obliterating the commissure, which leads to orifice stenosis, flow turbulence, fibrosis, and late calcification.

### Surgical Strategy for Commissural Prolapse
* **Limited Prolapse (<=5 mm):**
  * *Triangular Resection:* Preferred over historical McGoon plication because it removes infected or degenerated tissue prone to late fibrous retraction. Executed with height greater than base, reconstructed with interrupted 5-0 sutures with knots on the ventricular side.
  * *Chordae Shortening:* Indicated in billowing commissures with chordal elongation.
* **Extensive Prolapse from Chordae Rupture:**
  * *Posterior Commissure (gap 5 to 10 mm):* Quadrangular resection + annular plication + "magic stitch" to recreate a 5-mm coaptation surface + ring annuloplasty.
  * *Posterior Commissure (gap >10 mm):* Quadrangular resection + sliding leaflet technique (detachment of P3 and adjacent A3, compression sutures, sliding advancement) + magic stitch.
  * *Anterior Commissure:* Plication must be avoided because deep annular plication risks compressing or kinking the circumflex coronary artery. Sliding leaflet plasty is mandatory.
* **Extensive Prolapse from Chordae or Papillary Muscle Elongation:**
  * *Barlow's Disease:* Papillary muscle sliding plasty or papillary muscle shortening without leaflet resection to correct both prolapse and tissue billowing.

### Systematic Strategy for Bileaflet Prolapse
* **Orderly Reconstruction Sequence:**
  1. *Posterior Leaflet Resection (P2):* Performed first to provide wide exposure of subvalvular structures.
  2. *Annular Decalcification:* Performed if annular calcification is present.
  3. *Posterior Papillary Muscle Reconstruction:* Papillary muscle sliding plasty, shortening, or chordal transfer/replacement using a newly established reference point.
  4. *Anterior Papillary Muscle Reconstruction:* Identical procedures applied to the anterior papillary head.
  5. *Restoration of Posterior Leaflet Continuity:* Annular plication or sliding plasty after measuring remnant height (<20 mm) and gap.
  6. *Annuloplasty Ring Sizing:* Sized strictly to the surface area of the anterior leaflet.
  7. *Saline Testing and Refinement:* Inspection under saline injection; minor residual prolapse managed by limited triangular resection or the concertina technique.
* **Role of Papillary Shortening:** Papillary muscle shortening is uniquely advantageous in bileaflet prolapse as it simultaneously corrects anterior, posterior, and commissural prolapse while reducing leaflet billowing.

## INTRODUCTION AND GENERAL PRINCIPLES</summary><div class="details-content">

A commissural leaflet prolapse is a valve dysfunction typically seen in bacterial endocarditis or in degenerative valvular diseases.

The cause of the prolapse is either chordae rupture in bacterial endocarditis or chordae elongation in degenerative diseases.

#### Classification and Anatomical Boundaries

The extent of the prolapse can be defined as limited or extensive. A limited prolapse involves the commissural leaflet and measures 5 mm or less of the leaflet margin ([Fig. 12-1](#fig-12-01), a). An extensive commissural prolapse involves the commissural leaflet and either one or both paracommissural areas (b).

The technique of reconstruction depends upon the extent of the prolapse and the lesions causing the prolapse ([Table 12-1](#table-12-01)).

#### Objectives and Principles of Commissural Preservation

Whatever the technique used, the aim is not only to correct the regurgitation but also to restore optimal function of the commissure; to avoid turbulence, fibrous proliferation, or calcification; and to achieve long-term function.

The commissure is an important component of the mitral valve, the reconstruction of which is a condition for normal long-term function.

## LIMITED PROLAPSE</summary><div class="details-content">

A limited prolapse involving <=5 mm of the commissural edge is treated by triangular leaflet plication ([Fig. 12-2](#fig-12-02)) (i.e., McGoon's plication) or preferably by triangular resection (inset).

Resection is preferred because it removes a potential cause of recurrent infection: leaflet retraction and fibrosis. A plication can be used in case of small prolapse and noninfected tissue.

In both techniques, two 4-0 sutures are placed at the limits of the prolapsed area.

#### Surgical Technique of Limited Resection

Gentle traction allows visualization of the triangle of tissue to be resected or plicated (a). The height of the triangle should be slightly greater than the base.

The edges of the resection are approximated using interrupted 5-0 monofilament suture (b). Whenever the tissue is resistant enough, it is preferable to position the knots on the ventricular side.

In patients with a billowing commissure and prolapse caused by chordae elongation, either of the techniques of chordae shortening described in Chapter 10 can be used. In patients with endocarditis, an annuloplasty is necessary only if the annulus is dilated.

**FIGURE 12-2** Limited commissural prolapse reconstruction: traction on stay sutures defining the resection triangle with height exceeding base (a); approximation of edges with interrupted 5-0 monofilament sutures with ventricular knots (b); alternative triangular leaflet resection removing pathological tissue (inset).

## EXTENSIVE PROLAPSE RESULTING FROM CHORDAE RUPTURE</summary><div class="details-content">

Usually seen in bacterial endocarditis, an extensive commissural prolapse resulting from chordae rupture requires a quadrangular resection involving all pathological tissue plus a 2-mm ridge in the case of endocarditis ([Fig. 12-3](#fig-12-03)).

After the quadrangular resection has been completed, leaflet continuity is restored using either an annular plication or a sliding leaflet technique, depending upon the commissure involved and the extent of the gap.

The optimal techniques in commissural prolapse with billowing leaflets aim at correcting both the prolapse and the billowing.

#### Posterior Commissure Reconstruction (Gap 5 to 10 mm)

At the posterior commissure (a) 5- to 10-mm gap is treated by annular plication (b), which approximates the leaflet remnants (c).

Then a magic stitch is used to restore a surface of commissural coaptation (d).

#### The Magic Stitch Technique and Annular Ring

The fixation of the commissural edges by one or two nearby secondary chordae may be useful to further increase the coaptation (e). The resection is completed by a ring annuloplasty (f).

The goal of the "magic stitch" is to restore a normal 5-mm bridge of commissural tissue prolonged by a large surface of leaflet coaptation.

<span id="fig-12-03"></span>![](_page_137_Figure_12-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 12-3** Extensive prolapse at the posterior commissure (gap 5 to 10 mm): quadrangular resection of ruptured area with 2-mm clear margins (a); annular plication with 2-0 mattress suture (b); approximation of leaflet remnants (c); placement of the magic stitch to create a functional coaptation surface (d); reinforcement with adjacent secondary chordae (e); completion with remodeling annuloplasty ring (f).

#### Posterior Commissure Reconstruction (Gap >10 mm)

A prolapse greater than 10 mm at the posterior commissure requires a quadrangular resection and a sliding leaflet technique ([Fig. 12-4](#fig-12-04)).

The P3 segment and if necessary a few millimeters of A3 are detached from the annulus (a, b).

Whenever distended, the commissural edge of the anterior leaflet is treated by an elective triangular resection to restore tension (b, c). Compression sutures are placed to reduce the gap (c).

#### Suture Placement and Annular Reconstruction

Leaflet remnants are secured to the annulus while exerting traction on the leaflet tissue to facilitate the sliding (d-f).

One or two magic stitches are used to reconstruct the surface of coaptation (g), leaving the commissure open (h).

A remodeling ring annuloplasty is used in all cases to reinforce the commissural reconstruction (i).

<span id="fig-12-04"></span>![](_page_138_Figure_12-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 12-4** Sliding leaflet technique for extensive commissural prolapse (gap >10 mm): detachment of P3 and adjacent A3 from annulus (a, b); selective triangular resection of distended anterior leaflet edge and placement of annular compression sutures (b, c); advancement and fixation of remnants to annulus under sliding traction (d, e, f); placement of magic stitches restoring coaptation while preserving an open commissure (g, h); implantation of remodeling annuloplasty ring (i).

#### Technical Considerations at the Anterior Commissure

At the anterior commissure, after a large valvular resection the plication technique should be avoided as a large annular plication could compromise the circumflex artery.

A sliding leaflet technique is preferred in all extensive reconstructions at the anterior commissural level.

Mobilization and sliding advancement of adjacent leaflet segments reliably close the defect without requiring deep annular bites that could distort or occlude the circumflex artery.

## EXTENSIVE PROLAPSE RESULTING FROM CHORDAE ELONGATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Chordae elongation is a cause of extensive prolapse mainly seen in Barlow's syndrome ([Fig. 12-5](#fig-12-05)).

This lesion (a) is treated by a papillary muscle sliding plasty (b) or a papillary muscle shortening (c) without leaflet resection, both techniques extensively described in Chapter 10.

The choice between these two techniques depends upon the anatomy of the papillary muscles. Whenever the area of prolapse is also severely billowing because of significant excess tissue, a leaflet resection may be used to correct both the prolapse and the billowing.

## EXTENSIVE PROLAPSE RESULTING FROM PAPILLARY MUSCLE ELONGATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Extensive prolapse resulting from papillary muscle elongation is observed in congenital malformation or chronic ischemic mitral valve regurgitation.

The techniques of papillary shortening have also been extensively described in Chapter 10.

Restoring normal papillary muscle position realigns the subvalvular tension apparatus and re-establishes competent leaflet coaptation.

## BILEAFLET PROLAPSE</summary><div class="details-content">

Extensive bileaflet prolapse ([Fig. 12-6](#fig-12-06)) should be treated using a combination of the techniques described for anterior, posterior, and commissural prolapse.

In the setting of extensive bileaflet prolapse, the reconstruction should follow an orderly fashion:

* The resection of a segment of the posterior leaflet, usually P2, is carried out first to facilitate access to the subvalvular structures.
* Decalcification of the annulus is performed if calcification is present (see Chapter 9).
* Exposure of the posterior papillary muscle and determination of a new reference point facilitate either chordae transfer (Figs. 10-4 to 10-7) or papillary muscle sliding plasty or shortening (Figs. 10-10 and 10-11) to correct both the prolapse and also the billowing found in Barlow's disease.
* The same procedures are used at the anterior papillary muscle.
* Restoration of the posterior leaflet continuity is carried out by an annular plication or a sliding plasty after careful measurement of the height of the leaflet remnants and the length of the gap (Figs. 11-1 to 11-6).
* A ring is selected by measuring the anterior leaflet.
* After ring implantation, a saline test allows visualization of occasional imperfections, which are corrected according to techniques described in Chapter 8.
* Occasional limited residual prolapse is corrected by a localized leaflet triangular resection (Fig. 8-25) or the concertina technique (Fig. 10-13).

#### Role of Papillary Muscle Shortening in Bileaflet Prolapse

In bileaflet prolapse the technique of papillary muscle shortening is particularly indicated since it does correct anterior, posterior, and commissural prolapse at once in a harmonious fashion.

In addition, it reduces billowing, a cause of recurrent regurgitation.

Bileaflet prolapse is almost always amenable to valve reconstruction provided that leaflet tissue is pliable.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Diagnostic Precision and Imaging Modalities

**FF: How precise is echocardiographic assessment of commissural regurgitation?**
> **AC:** Commissural regurgitation is difficult to recognize by transthoracic echocardiography. Transesophageal echocardiography, particularly the transgastric view, provides more accurate information relating to the commissural areas. Nonetheless, it may be difficult to distinguish between commissural leaflet prolapse and leaflet perforation. This may be a dysfunction where three-dimensional echo could play a significant role in the diagnosis.

#### Selection Criteria: Annular Plication vs. Sliding Plasty

**FF: Can you summarize the main indications for annular plication or leaflet sliding plasty following commissural leaflet resection?**
> **AC:** Following the resection, if the gap measures 10 mm or less, an annular plication is preferred because of its simplicity and efficacy. If the gap measures more than 10 mm, particularly at the anterior commissure, a sliding plasty is preferred to avoid a large plication and possible injury to the circumflex artery.

**DA: A Barlow's valve has a large orifice area. Do you agree that a limited commissural prolapse can be treated by two or three sutures advancing the commissure medially without compromising valve function?**
> **AC:** Although it is not an optimal solution because it compromises normal commissural function, it may be a valuable adjunct, particularly in a complex Barlow's valve with secondary lesions.

#### Annuloplasty Indications in Commissural Prolapse

**FF: Is a prosthetic ring mandatory whenever you have carried out a limited commissural leaflet resection with annular plication?**
> **AC:** A ring is theoretically not necessary because this technique effectively corrects the regurgitation, but the etiology should be considered. In acute bacterial endocarditis, it is usually not necessary to use a ring because the annulus is not dilated. By contrast, in chronic endocarditis or degenerative valvular disease, the disease process involves not only the commissural segment but also the entire annulus with a potential for secondary dilatation. In this case, it is preferable to ensure the long-term stability of the annulus with a remodeling annuloplasty ring.

**DA: Does your operative strategy differ for commissural prolapse caused by Barlow's disease as opposed to that caused by fibroelastic deficiency?**
> **AC:** In fibroelastic deficiency, the usual lesion causing a commissural prolapse is the rupture of one or two chordae. The techniques used are either a limited leaflet resection with restoration of the commissure or a simple chordae transfer or replacement. In Barlow's syndrome, the commissural prolapse is generally due to chordae elongation and is associated with a commissural tissue billowing. The technique of choice is preferably a papillary muscle sliding plasty or a papillary muscle shortening plasty. Both techniques have the advantage of correcting not only the prolapse but also the billowing while preserving the commissural function.

#### Functional Principles of Commissural Reconstruction

**FF: Clarify the advantage of the magic suture technique over commissural closure in commissural prolapse.**
> **AC:** The magic stitch reconstructs the actual commissure by restoring a deeper surface of coaptation and therefore a functional structure. By contrast, closing the commissure excludes a part of the valve from normal function. Restoring the function of the commissure rather than closing it is important to preserve full leaflet motion, which in turn prevents turbulence, fibrous retraction, and occasional future commissural calcification.

**DA: Is there a scenario where you would consider a Wooler-Reed type asymmetrical annuloplasty?**
> **AC:** This technique was useful in the past in the absence of a better alternative. Today, it should be regarded as a palliative technique. It overnarrows the orifice, does not support the entire annulus, and excludes the commissural area from normal function, leading to secondary lesions like those seen in the early years of cardiac surgery. I believe this technique has no indications for use today.

#### Management of Large Commissural Leaflets and Bileaflet Interactions

**DA: Describe your technique for treating prolapse of a well-developed commissural leaflet.**
> **AC:** This condition is generally observed in Barlow's syndrome with a prolapse resulting from chordae elongation. The corresponding commissural papillary muscle is usually easily identified. The preferred technique is to proceed to a sliding plasty or shortening plasty of the papillary muscle as opposed to resection. In the scenario of commissural leaflet prolapse associated with a hypoplastic adjacent posterior leaflet segment, the commissural leaflet can be used to extend the surface area of the hypoplastic segment by suturing them together.

**DA: In bileaflet prolapse, why specifically does a posterior leaflet repair also correct a minimal anterior leaflet prolapse?**
> **AC:** More precisely, it does increase the surface of coaptation but it does not correct the discrepancy between the levels of the free edges of two opposing leaflets. As long as the height of the surface of coaptation is greater than 5 mm, a few millimeters discrepancy is acceptable. If not, it is advisable to correct this discrepancy, which requires a rather simple technique: either a small triangular resection or a chordae transfer or replacement on the anterior leaflet.

#### Durability Factors and Long-Term Mechanisms of Failure

**DA: In type II dysfunction, why is a posterior leaflet reconstruction regarded as more durable than an anterior leaflet reconstruction?**
> **AC:** In a sailboat, the great sail and the spinnaker wear faster than the jibs because they have a larger surface area. Similar to the great sail, the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve has more tissue than the posterior leaflet segments, which can be compared to three jibs. Therefore significantly more tension is exerted on the anterior leaflet. A restored large surface of leaflet coaptation minimizes this tension.

**FF: What is the most common mechanism of late recurrent prolapse after valve reconstruction?**
> **AC:** The most frequent disease-related mechanisms are the onset of bacterial endocarditis with chordae rupture or a continuous degenerative process resulting in chordae elongation or rupture. Ensuring a large surface of coaptation and remodeling the annulus with a ring minimize the latter risk. The most common technique-related mechanism is chordae abrasion by suture material placed in contact with native chordae at the level of a papillary muscle. Another possibility is chordae abrasion by artificial chordae crossing the route of native chordae. Avoiding suture misplacement minimizes these risks.

**FF: Why is bileaflet prolapse associated with a higher rate of reoperation?**
> **AC:** As far as reconstructive valve surgery techniques are concerned, the overall rate of reoperation is very low: 0.4% per patient per year in degenerative valve disease over a period of follow-up extending up to 25 years. However, we did find a rate difference between bileaflet prolapse and posterior leaflet prolapse, with freedom from reoperation at 20 years of 82% and 97%, respectively. Nevertheless, 82% of patients not requiring reoperation is a very reasonable statistic, especially considering that our patient population includes more than 30 years of evolutive surgical techniques. Since the early years of cardiac surgery, great progress has been made that may further improve this statistic. Particularly important in this regard have been intraoperative echocardiography guidance, a more rigorous correction of minor prolapse, and disease-specific rings. All three of these measures contribute to providing a larger surface of coaptation, which minimizes the increased risk of failure in bileaflet prolapse.
