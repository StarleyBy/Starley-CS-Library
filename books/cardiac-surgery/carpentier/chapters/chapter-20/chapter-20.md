# 20. Surgical Anatomy and Physiology

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Functional Integration of the Aortic Root
The aortic valve is an integral component of the aortic root, which bridges the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) and the ascending tubular aorta. Functionally, the aortic root directs large stroke volumes under laminar conditions with minimal resistance while sustaining optimal coronary perfusion throughout the cardiac cycle. The root is deeply nested within the central fibrous skeleton of the heart, bounded anteriorly by the pulmonary root and posteriorly by the fibrous junction between the mitral and tricuspid valves.

### Structural Triad: Annulus, Sinuses, and Sinotubular Junction
The aortic root is bounded proximally by the ventriculo-aortic junction (anatomic/hemodynamic annulus) and distally by the sinotubular junction (STJ). The three sinuses of Valsalva display distinct anatomical asymmetry: the right coronary sinus is the largest, followed by the non-coronary sinus, with the left coronary sinus being the smallest ([Table 20-1](#table-20-01)). Rather than a planar ring, the true hemodynamic annulus is a three-dimensional crown-shaped scalloped collagenous structure following the attachments of the three semilunar cusps. The spaces below the commissures are occupied by fibrous interleaflet (subcommissural) triangles, which transmit ventricular pressure and provide essential compliance.

### Microscopic Leaflet Architecture and Valve Dynamics
Each semilunar leaflet features three distinct zones: the basal hinge, the load-bearing belly, and the translucent coaptation lunula with its central nodule of Arantius. Histologically, leaflets comprise three specialized layers: the fibrosa (dense circumferential collagen transmitting diastolic load to the commissures), the spongiosa (glycosaminoglycan-rich matrix absorbing compressive stress), and the ventricularis (elastic lamina facing the outflow tract). Commissural fenestrations are common in adults and functionally competent when located above the coaptation line.

Aortic valve opening and closing are governed by the generation of vortices within the sinuses of Valsalva (the Bellhouse mechanism). During systole, vortex circulation prevents the leaflets from impacting the aortic wall, sustains laminar flow toward the coronary ostia, and initiates early, smooth leaflet closure before the onset of reverse diastolic flow.

## ARCHITECTURE OF THE AORTIC ROOT</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Topographical Relationships and Boundaries
The aortic valve is one component of the aortic root, which connects the left ventricular outflow tract to the ascending aorta. The function of the aortic root is to guide the unidirectional flow of large volumes of blood with minimal resistance while maintaining laminar flow. Another function is to optimize coronary blood flow. These functions require complex dynamic interactions between different anatomical components that may be affected by pathological processes.

The aortic root is deeply anchored within the base of the heart between the pulmonary root anteriorly and the mitral and tricuspid valves posteriorly ([Fig. 20-1](#fig-20-01)). The junction between the atrioventricular valves and the aortic root is occupied by the fibrous skeleton of the heart including the two fibrous trigones.

## SURROUNDING STRUCTURES AND CONDUCTION AT RISK</summary><div class="details-content">

#### The Subaortic Curtain and Fibrous Skeleton
The subaortic segment, which forms the junction between the left ventricular outflow tract and the base of the anterior mitral leaflet, constitutes the mitro-aortic intervalvular fibrosa or subaortic curtain.

This fibrous continuum is bordered by the right and left fibrous trigones. The central fibrous body provides structural anchoring to the aortic root and protects against progressive annular enlargement during systole.

#### Conduction System Topography and Membranous Septum
The conduction system exhibits a critical relationship with the aortic root ([Fig. 20-9](#fig-20-09)). The bundle of His emerges through the central fibrous body and runs along the inferior margin of the membranous septum.

<span id="fig-20-09"></span>![](_page_212_Figure_20-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20-9**

*[Figure 20-9](#fig-20-09). Relationship between the aortic root and the cardiac conduction system. The bundle of His courses along the inferior border of the membranous septum immediately beneath the commissure between the right coronary and non-coronary leaflets.*

This critical landmark is situated immediately beneath the commissure between the right coronary and non-coronary leaflets. Deep surgical sutures placed in this area, or aggressive debridement of calcification near this commissure, carries a substantial risk of complete atrioventricular block.

## ANATOMY AND HISTOLOGY OF THE AORTIC LEAFLETS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Gross Morphology and Functional Zones
The aortic valve comprises three semilunar leaflets: right coronary, left coronary, and non-coronary ([Fig. 20-10](#fig-20-10)). Each leaflet is anatomically divided into three distinct zones: the basal attachment, the load-bearing belly (body), and the coaptation lunula.

<span id="fig-20-10"></span>![](_page_213_Figure_20-10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20-10**

*[Figure 20-10](#fig-20-10). Morphological features of an aortic leaflet: basal hemicircular attachment, load-bearing belly, crescent-shaped coaptation lunula, and the central nodule of Arantius.*

The free edge of each leaflet displays a central nodular thickening known as the nodule of Arantius. Extending laterally from the nodule of Arantius to the commissures are the lunulae—crescent-shaped, extremely thin, and translucent zones that form the coaptation surface during diastole.

#### Coaptation Geometry and Apposition
The effective surface of coaptation is critical for maintaining valve competence under high systemic diastolic pressure gradients ([Fig. 20-11](#fig-20-11)).

<span id="fig-20-11"></span>![](_page_213_Figure_20-11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20-11**

*[Figure 20-11](#fig-20-11). Geometric coaptation parameters: coaptation height, effective height, and zone of apposition along the free edges of adjacent leaflets.*

During diastole, adjacent lunulae press firmly against each other, distributing the stress across a vertical height of several millimeters rather than along a single contact line.

#### Trilaminar Microscopic Architecture
Histologically, each aortic leaflet consists of three distinct layers: the fibrosa, spongiosa, and ventricularis ([Fig. 20-12](#fig-20-12)).

<span id="fig-20-12"></span>![](_page_213_Figure_20-12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20-12**

*[Figure 20-12](#fig-20-12). Trilaminar microscopic architecture of the aortic leaflet: fibrosa (dense collagen bundles oriented circumferentially facing the aorta), spongiosa (glycosaminoglycan-rich ground substance), and ventricularis (elastin-rich layer facing the ventricular cavity).*

- The **fibrosa** faces the aortic lumen and contains dense, circumferentially aligned type I collagen bundles that transmit diastolic loads to the commissures and annulus.
- The **spongiosa** is a central layer rich in glycosaminoglycans and proteoglycans that cushions mechanical shear and compressive stress between adjacent layers.
- The **ventricularis** faces the ventricular outflow tract and contains abundant elastin fibers oriented radially, enabling rapid recoil and expansion during opening and closing cycles.

#### Commissural Architecture and Leaflet Fenestrations
The commissures represent the narrow vertical lines where adjacent semilunar leaflets meet the aortic wall and reach the sinotubular junction ([Fig. 20-13](#fig-20-13)).

<span id="fig-20-13"></span>![](_page_213_Figure_20-13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20-13**

*[Figure 20-13](#fig-20-13). Commissural architecture and coaptation line. Leaflet free edges meet at the commissural apex at the level of the sinotubular junction.*

In adults, small fenestrations of the leaflet tissue are frequently observed near the commissural insertions. These fenestrations are positioned above the line of coaptation and are normally supported by the coapting adjacent leaflet, thereby remaining hemodynamically silent in the absence of leaflet elongation or prolapse.

## FLUID MECHANICS AND DYNAMIC FUNCTION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Bellhouse Vortex Mechanism
The fluid mechanics of the aortic valve during systole and diastole were elucidated by Bellhouse and Bellhouse ([Fig. 20-14](#fig-20-14)).

<span id="fig-20-14"></span>![](_page_214_Figure_20-14.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20-14**

*[Figure 20-14](#fig-20-14). The Bellhouse vortex mechanism in the sinuses of Valsalva: during systole, blood circulating within the sinuses forms stable vortices that prevent leaflets from hitting the aortic wall and initiate early leaflet closure at the end of ejection.*

As forward flow accelerates through the triangular open orifice during peak systole, fluid circulating within the sinuses of Valsalva forms stable toroidal vortices. These vortices generate a continuous transverse pressure gradient across the leaflets. This prevents the delicate leaflets from colliding with or adhering to the aortic sinus wall and directs blood smoothly into the coronary ostia.

At the deceleration phase of systole, the sinus vortices naturally guide the leaflet free edges toward the center of the lumen, initiating smooth, early closure before any significant reverse flow can occur.

#### Cyclic Dynamic Deformations of the Root
The aortic root is a dynamic, living structure that undergoes complex cyclic three-dimensional deformations throughout the cardiac cycle ([Fig. 20-15](#fig-20-15)).

<span id="fig-20-15"></span>![](_page_214_Figure_20-15.jpeg)

**FIGURE 20-15**

*[Figure 20-15](#fig-20-15). Dynamic deformations of the aortic root during the cardiac cycle: systolic downward translation of the annulus and radial expansion of the sinotubular junction facilitating low-resistance ejection.*

During early isovolumetric contraction, the aortic annulus undergoes downward displacement toward the ventricular apex, initiating leaflet separation. Concurrently, the sinotubular junction expands radially by 10% to 15%, reducing the transvalvular gradient and minimizing shear stress on the leaflet tissue. In diastole, the root elongates and recoils, optimizing coronary perfusion and distributing hemodynamic stress across the fibrous skeleton.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Comparative Anatomy Across Species
> **DA:** To develop the first valvular bioprostheses, you extensively studied the aortic valves of pigs and calves. Compared to these two species, how different is the anatomy of the human aortic valve?
>
> **AC:** There are more anatomical similarities between the aortic valve of these three species than between their respective body morphology! The main difference is that in animals the muscular interventricular septum extends to the right coronary leaflet and forms a ridge of muscular tissue supporting that leaflet. The second difference is the presence of bone formation in the fibrous skeleton of the heart, particularly in calves. These anatomical differences significantly complicated the mounting of porcine valvular bioprostheses into a stent. For this reason, among others, I adopted the pericardial tissue at a later stage.

#### Annular to Sinotubular Junction Ratios
> **FF:** Several studies have reported different ratios between annular and sino-tubular junction circumferences. Could you comment on that?
>
> **AC:** Several authors, including Tirone David and Raymond Cartier, have shown that this ratio varies according to age. In young people, the annular circumference is about 10% larger than the sino-tubular circumference, and the ratio is less than 1. In the elderly, the sino-tubular junction tends to dilate and its circumference becomes equal to or larger than the annular circumference, with the ratio approximating or exceeding 1.

#### Functional Impact of Sinotubular Dilatation
> **DA:** What is the functional relationship between the sino-tubular junction and the crown of the commissures?
>
> **AC:** The tips of the commissures are attached to the sino-tubular junction. In supravalvular stenosis, the sino-tubular junction is hypertrophied, making it difficult to expose the commissures and the coronary ostia without creating regurgitation. By contrast, a dilatation of the sino-tubular junction displaces the commissures outward, causing type IIIb valve dysfunction with aortic regurgitation.

#### Structural Nature of the Lunulae
> **DA:** Are lunulae distinct structures or simply a designation of the coaptation zones?
>
> **AC:** They are distinct structures with a crescent shape extending from the commissures to the nodulus of Arantius. The extreme thinness of the lunulae facilitates leaflet closure and compensates for the small surface of coaptation. The leaflet tissue is sometimes perforated near the commissures.

#### Significance of Commissural Fenestrations
> **FF:** Do these fenestrations cause any valve dysfunction?
>
> **AC:** They usually do not leak because they are opposed by the adjacent leaflet or positioned above the line of coaptation.

#### Biomechanical Role of Subcommissural Triangles
> **FF:** What is the role of the subcommissural triangles?
>
> **AC:** The subcommissural triangular structures provide optimal elasticity to the aortic annulus. As such, they play a significant role in absorbing the stress and organizing the flow pattern through the outflow tract and the aortic valve.

#### Rationale for Creating Pseudosinuses
> **FF:** What is your opinion about the creation of pseudosinuses in aortic root reconstruction or in conduits used for root replacement?
>
> **AC:** Leonardo da Vinci showed that the sinuses of Valsalva play a major role in the closure of the aortic leaflets and the organization of flow during both systole and diastole. It is therefore appropriate to create sinuses in aortic root reconstruction or valve conduits.

#### Systolic Translation of the Aortic Annulus
> **DA:** What is the significance of the downward displacement of the aortic annulus during systole?
>
> **AC:** The downward displacement of the aortic annulus during systole most probably results from the isovolumetric contraction of the ventricle in early systole. It initiates the opening of the aortic valve.
