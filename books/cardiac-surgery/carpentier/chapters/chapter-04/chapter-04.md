# Chapter 4. Operative Management

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Intraoperative Monitoring & Setup
* **Routine Monitoring:** Standard central venous line, arterial line, and urinary bladder catheterization are mandatory for all patients.
* **Hemodynamic Catheterization:** Pulmonary artery catheter (Swan-Ganz) monitoring is specifically reserved for high-risk patients presenting with elevated pulmonary artery hypertension or severely depressed ventricular function.
* **Defibrillation & Ventilation:** External defibrillator adhesive pads are placed before surgical draping in all minimally invasive procedures, right thoracotomy approaches, and reoperations. A double-lumen endotracheal tube is mandatory for right thoracotomy to ensure complete decompression of the right lung.
* **Gaseous Field Flooding:** Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) insufflation into the surgical field at a flow rate of 4 L/min is instituted at least 30 minutes prior to cardiac cavity opening and maintained throughout open-heart instrumentation.
* **Transesophageal Echocardiography (TEE):** Mandatory in 100% of reconstructive cases: pre-bypass TEE defines the functional mechanism of dysfunction and associated pathology; post-bypass TEE evaluates coaptation length, excludes residual regurgitation or systolic anterior motion (SAM), and confirms absolute de-airing.

### Specialized Surgical Instrumentation & Materials
* **Retraction Systems:** Dedicated sternal retractor equipped with self-retaining blades provides stable, multi-axis atrial wall exposure without distorting annular geometry; atraumatic aortic retractors for root exposure.
* **Tissue Manipulation:** Fine nerve hooks for systematic leaflet analysis and segment-by-segment hook testing; atraumatic tissue forceps; specialized papillary muscle clamps for deep ventricular exposure; heavy-jaw needle holders and fine scissors.
* **Suture Selection:** 
  * *Annuloplasty Ring:* Braided 4-0 polyester sutures mounted on $3/8$ curvature needles to ensure wide, secure tissue bites and prevent ring dehiscence.
  * *Leaflet, Chordal & Papillary Plasties:* Resilient polyamide (nylon) monofilament sutures (5-0 for leaflet reconstruction and chordal transfer; 4-0 with $3/8$ needle for annular reconstruction, cusp reattachment, and papillary muscle splitting). Polyamide provides pliability, elasticity, lack of memory, and requires only 4 knots.
  * *Polypropylene Warning:* Stiff polypropylene monofilament is discouraged in intraventricular reconstruction due to rigidity, elongation risk, sliding knots, and sharp cut ends causing leaflet abrasion.

### Surgical Approaches & Less Invasive Surgery
* **Median Sternotomy:** The universal standard for primary and reoperative valve procedures. The historical 20-cm incision is replaced by a cosmetic 8- to 10-cm skin incision with full sternotomy. Sternal retractor midpoint must align precisely with the right atrial appendage.
* **Right Anterolateral Thoracotomy:** Fourth intercostal space incision (12–15 cm) with the patient rotated 30 degrees to the left; indicated for primary cosmetics in selected women or reoperations.
* **Ministernotomy:** Upper ministernotomy (sternal notch to 3rd or 4th intercostal space) for aortic valve operations; Lower ministernotomy (xyphoid to 2nd intercostal space) for mitral and tricuspid operations.
* **Right Lateral Minithoracotomy:** 5-cm incision in the 4th intercostal space with video and robotic port assistance.
* **Quality Mandate:** Less invasive techniques must never compromise the quality or completeness of valve repair. A suboptimal repair or valve replacement performed merely to achieve a smaller incision is unacceptable surgical practice.

### Redo Surgical Principles
* **Preoperative Imaging:** Multi-slice chest computed tomography (CT) to map retrosternal adhesions, distance from the sternum to the right ventricle and aorta, and internal mammary artery graft course. Coronary angiography to visualize patent bypass grafts.
* **Reentry Safety:** Pre-draping defibrillator pads; optional peripheral femoral/axillary vessel exposure before saw reentry. Saw division from xyphoid to manubrium with upward sternal retraction.
* **Catastrophic Bleeding Protocol:** Immediate cessation of sternal dissection, release of retractors, emergency femoral cannulation, and institution of CPB to decompress the heart. Hypothermia down to 20°C if temporary circulatory arrest is required. Immediate ventricular venting if aortic regurgitation causes acute left ventricular distension.

### Cardiopulmonary Bypass & Myocardial Protection
* **Cannulation:** Ascending aortic cannulation after epiaortic ultrasound screening; bicaval venous cannulation with caval snares (total CPB) assisted by kinetic vacuum drainage to prevent warm systemic return.
* **Cardioplegia:** Cold blood cardioplegia is mandatory for prolonged reconstructions (> 2 hours). Intermittent antegrade cardioplegia via an aortic root needle combined with retrograde cardioplegia via the coronary sinus.
* **Hypothermia & Monitoring:** Tepid systemic hypothermia (28° to 30°C); topical iced saline; continuous myocardial septal temperature monitoring maintained strictly below 15°C (or redosing every 20 minutes).

### Left Atrial Closure & Systematic De-airing
* **Atrial Closure Safeguards:** Placement of a temporary transmitral vent into the left ventricle to maintain mitral incompetence during suturing. Avoid right pulmonary artery injury, superior vena cava narrowing, and roof tearing (suture bites < 1 cm).
* **Three-Step De-airing Protocol:**
  1. *Passive Retrograde:* Aorta cross-clamped, atrial vent loosened at zenith, heart filled, lungs ventilated, gentle ventricular massage, brief apical venting.
  2. *Passive Antegrade:* Aorta clamped, atrial suture tensioned closed, root suction active, heart filled, right coronary ostium compressed, ventilation active.
  3. *Active Antegrade:* Aorta unclamped with initial 5-beat clamp cycles, heavy root suction active, gentle ventricular contraction, TEE monitoring until zero microbubbles remain.

### Weaning from Cardiopulmonary Bypass
* **Normothermia & Pacing:** Core rewarming to $\ge 35^\circ\text{C}$; routine epicardial atrial and ventricular temporary pacing wires.
* **Hemodynamic Targets:** Low-normal initial filling pressure; left atrial pressure (LAP) or pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) maintained strictly $< 20\text{ mm Hg}$.
* **Inotropic Support & Counterpulsation:** If systemic blood pressure is $< 80\text{ mm Hg}$ with LAP $> 20\text{ mm Hg}$, intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) is preferred over toxic vasopressor/inotropic escalation. Milrinone and inhaled nitric oxide for severe pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular failure.

## PREOPERATIVE SETUP AND INTRAOPERATIVE MONITORING</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Routine and Hemodynamic Monitoring</summary><div class="details-content">

In the operating room, standard monitoring techniques are systematically established in all patients undergoing valve reconstructive procedures. These include continuous electrocardiography, systemic arterial blood pressure monitoring via a radial or femoral arterial line, central venous pressure catheterization, and a urinary bladder drainage catheter.

Swan-Ganz pulmonary artery catheter monitoring is particularly useful in high-risk patients presenting with elevated pulmonary artery systolic pressure, pulmonary vascular resistance, or depressed ventricular function. In routine cases of uncomplicated degenerative valve disease with preserved ventricular performance, central venous line monitoring is sufficient.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Defibrillation, Gas Flooding, and Echocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

An external defibrillator is placed in all patients undergoing minimally invasive surgery, right thoracotomy, and redo operations prior to sterile draping. A double-lumen endotracheal tube is mandatory in patients undergoing an anterolateral or lateral right thoracotomy to permit unilateral lung deflation and optimal exposure. Continuous carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) insufflation into the surgical field at a flow rate of 4 L/min is instituted at least 30 minutes before opening cardiac chambers to displace ambient air with high-solubility gas.

Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is mandatory in all patients undergoing valve reconstruction. Pre-bypass TEE confirms the functional classification of regurgitation, assesses regional and global biventricular function, and screens for occult secondary lesions. Post-bypass TEE is vital to assess the exact quality of reconstruction, measure coaptation depth, identify residual leaks, monitor biventricular unloading, and confirm complete de-airing.

</div></details>

## SURGICAL INSTRUMENTATION AND SUTURE MATERIALS</summary><div class="details-content">

In reconstructive valve surgery, specialized surgical instruments are indispensable to provide atraumatic tissue handling, precise valve analysis, and deep ventricular visualization ([Fig. 4-1](#fig-04-01)).

In aortic valve surgery, specialized atraumatic aortic retractors provide clear visualization of the aortic root and cusp coaptation geometry without tearing delicate aortic wall tissues. In mitral and tricuspid valve surgery, a specially designed sternal retractor equipped with self-retaining blades retracts the atrial wall and provides optimal exposure without distorting annular architecture.

Nerve hooks are critical instruments for systematic valve analysis, allowing the surgeon to assess leaflet mobility, evaluate chordal elongation or rupture, and test individual scallop motion. Additional instruments include atraumatic gripping forceps for leaflet manipulation, a specialized papillary muscle clamp to facilitate exposure of the subvalvular apparatus within the left ventricle, a long needle holder with large jaws, and specialized scissors.

<span id="fig-04-01"></span>![](_page_15_Figure_4-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4-1**

Suture material requires special consideration:
* **Annuloplasty Ring Implantation:** Braided 4-0 sutures are recommended. A $3/8$ curvature needle rather than a semicircular needle is strongly recommended to ensure large, deep tissue bites through the fibrous annulus, thereby preventing ring dehiscence.
* **Leaflet and Subvalvular Reconstruction:** Resilient polyamide (nylon) suture material is ideal for leaflet, chordal, and papillary muscle reconstruction. Polyamide monofilament offers exceptional pliability, elasticity, and lack of memory, and requires only 4 secure knots.
* **Suture Gauges:** For leaflet reconstruction and chordal transfer, 5-0 polyamide sutures are preferred. For annular reconstruction, leaflet reattachment to the annulus, and papillary muscle plasties, 4-0 polyamide sutures with $3/8$ curvature needles are used.
* **Warning against Polypropylene:** The widely used polypropylene suture material is less adaptable for intracardiac valve reconstruction because of its rigidity, sliding characteristics, and protruding knots with sharp cut ends that can cause leaflet abrasion and impair coaptation.

## SURGICAL APPROACHES</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Median Sternotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

Median sternotomy remains the preferred approach in patients undergoing valve surgery as well as in the reoperative setting ([Fig. 4-2](#fig-04-02)).

In this era of less invasive surgery, the conventional 20-cm skin incision is no longer necessary. A smaller, cosmetic 8- to 10-cm skin incision is far less traumatic yet provides adequate exposure to perform a complete sternotomy with optimal visualization. Special attention must be given to careful dissection of substernal tissues in order to avoid inadvertently opening the pleural spaces.

**FIGURE 4-2**

Proper positioning of the sternal retractor is obtained by ensuring that the midpoint between the two central anchoring mechanisms is aligned with the right atrial appendage ([Fig. 4-3](#fig-04-03)). Faulty cephalad or caudal placement impairs exposure of the interatrial groove and left atrium.

### Right Anterolateral Thoracotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

This approach is used in mitral valve surgery for cosmetic reasons in select primary patients or in redo operations ([Fig. 4-4](#fig-04-04)).

It is strictly contraindicated in patients with previous right-sided thoracic surgery, moderate to severe aortic regurgitation, or severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. The patient is rotated 30 degrees to the left side. A 12- to 15-cm right anterolateral thoracotomy is performed through the fourth intercostal space. A double-lumen endotracheal tube is used to decompress the right lung.

Before sterile draping, an external defibrillator is placed for subsequent cardiac defibrillation. The opening of the thorax is limited to the minimal amount required for atrial or superior vena cava cannulation. Cannulation of the ascending aorta is possible in most patients.

### Less Invasive Approaches and Ministernotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

Less invasive surgery is attractive because of numerous claimed advantages: better cosmetics, less bleeding, decreased risk of infection, reduced pain, and shorter hospital stay. Certain risks have been emphasized, however, such as a higher incidence of stroke and vascular complications.

Actually, the main potential disadvantage of less invasive mitral valve surgery is the execution of a suboptimal operation as a result of suboptimal exposure (such as performing a valve replacement instead of a valve repair, or a palliative repair instead of a comprehensive reconstruction). Excellent exposure is a fundamental prerequisite in reconstructive valve surgery. The incision may be large or small; the critical point is that it must provide clear visualization of all valve structures regardless of their depth.

Ministernotomy may be used both for aortic and for mitral or tricuspid valve surgery:
* **Upper Ministernotomy:** Extends from the sternal notch to the third or fourth right intercostal space ([Fig. 4-5](#fig-04-05)); it is particularly indicated for aortic valve surgery.
* **Lower Ministernotomy:** Extends from the xyphoid process to the second right intercostal space ([Fig. 4-6](#fig-04-06)); it is indicated for mitral and tricuspid valve surgery.

<span id="fig-04-05"></span>![](_page_17_Figure_4-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4-5**

<span id="fig-04-06"></span>![](_page_17_Figure_4-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4-6**

Access to the mitral valve is achieved through the interatrial groove or through the atrial septum. Cannulation of the ascending aorta is possible, particularly when using an upper ministernotomy. Peripheral arterial cannulation is advisable if access to the ascending aorta is limited or severe aortic calcifications are present. Venous cannulation is performed either percutaneously (via right femoral and internal jugular veins) or directly through the right atrium. Vacuum-assisted drainage is essential to ensure adequate venous return.

Right lateral minithoracotomy ([Fig. 4-7](#fig-04-07)) was the approach used to perform the very first cases of mini-invasive mitral valve reconstruction. Today, it is used routinely with excellent results by specialized groups. The incision is limited to 5 cm and an access port is placed in the fourth intercostal space. This approach requires specialized long-shaft instrumentation, video assistance, and optional robotic telemanipulation.

<span id="fig-04-07"></span>![](_page_18_Figure_4-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4-7**

In all minimally invasive procedures, three mandatory safety rules must be followed:
1. Placement of external defibrillator adhesive pads before surgical draping.
2. Placement of ventricular epicardial temporary pacing wires before weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass.
3. Careful preservation of distal limb arterial perfusion whenever peripheral femoral cannulation is employed.

## REOPERATIVE SURGERY (REDO OPERATIONS)</summary><div class="details-content">

The most common scenario of redo operations involves aortic or mitral valve surgery after prior coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Other common clinical scenarios include redo mitral valve reconstruction or replacement, mitral surgery after prior aortic valve replacement, and isolated tricuspid valve surgery after previous left-sided corrections.

The cardinal surgical rule in redo surgery is to limit mediastinal dissection to the absolute minimum necessary to complete the intracardiac procedure safely. Several surgical approaches may be utilized depending on the valve involved and the nature of the previous operations.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Redo Median Sternotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Surgical Planning and Safe Sternal Reentry</summary><div class="details-content">

Median sternotomy is the preferred approach for all reoperative valvular procedures, particularly in patients undergoing multiple valve surgery. There may be some exceptions, however, such as multiple previous coronary bypass surgeries, recent sternotomy, or a history of mediastinitis.

Repeat sternotomy requires extreme vigilance to avoid catastrophic injury to the adherent right ventricle, ascending aorta, or patent coronary bypass grafts. Preoperative multi-detector chest CT is essential to visualize the retrosternal space and graft anatomy. Coronary angiography precisely localizes patent bypass conduits. External defibrillator pads are systematically positioned before draping.

In high-risk cases with minimal retrosternal clearance, repeat sternotomy should be preceded by surgical exposure and vascular control of the femoral or axillary vessels. The sternotomy is performed from the xyphoid process upward toward the manubrium while exerting upward traction on the divided sternal edges. All retrosternal adhesions are freed prior to placing the retractor.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Management of Catastrophic Reentry Bleeding and Alternative Approaches</summary><div class="details-content">

If significant mediastinal hemorrhage occurs during reentry, dissection is immediately halted, sternal spreaders are loosened, emergency femoral cannulation is completed, and cardiopulmonary bypass is instituted to decompress the heart. If necessary, profound hypothermia (20°C) is established to allow brief periods of circulatory arrest. In patients with concomitant aortic regurgitation, immediate left ventricular decompression via transseptal vent or apical vent is mandatory to avoid fatal ventricular distension.

Upper ministernotomy is useful in redo aortic valve surgery. A right anterolateral thoracotomy is strongly preferred in patients with patent internal mammary artery grafts crossing the midline or those with a history of sternal wound infection or mediastinitis requiring mitral or tricuspid procedures.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## CARDIOPULMONARY BYPASS AND MYOCARDIAL PROTECTION</summary><div class="details-content">

In routine reconstructive valve operations, cardiopulmonary bypass is established between the ascending aorta and both vena cavae ([Fig. 4-8](#fig-04-08)).

Epiaortic ultrasound examination of the ascending aorta is recommended to rule out atherosclerotic plaques at the planned cannulation and cross-clamping sites before placing the aortic cannula. The superior and inferior vena cavae are snared with tourniquet tapes to achieve total cardiopulmonary bypass and prevent warm systemic blood from entering the right heart. Venous return is augmented by kinetic vacuum-assisted drainage.

<span id="fig-04-08"></span>![](_page_19_Figure_4-8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4-8**

Myocardial protection is best achieved by cold blood cardioplegia. Warm blood cardioplegia, widely used in coronary artery bypass grafting, is not recommended in reconstructive valve surgery because the operative cross-clamp time frequently extends beyond 2 hours.

The aorta is cross-clamped with care. Intermittent antegrade cold blood cardioplegia is delivered through an aortic root needle that is connected simultaneously to the cardioplegia delivery line and to a root suction line used for subsequent de-airing ([Fig. 4-9](#fig-04-09)). Retrograde cardioplegia via a transatrial coronary sinus catheter is a crucial adjunct, particularly in complex reconstructions and prolonged ischemic arrests.

<span id="fig-04-09"></span>![](_page_19_Figure_4-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4-9**

Myocardial preservation is supplemented by tepid systemic hypothermia between 28° and 30°C and topical hypothermia with iced saline slush. Direct continuous monitoring of myocardial septal temperature is recommended, maintaining septal temperature strictly below 15°C. In the absence of continuous temperature monitoring, cold blood cardioplegia must be redosed every 20 minutes in an antegrade or retrograde fashion.

In patients with dense adhesions or an intensely calcified "porcelain" aorta prohibiting cross-clamping, the procedure can be performed without cardioplegic arrest on a fibrillating heart at 30°C core hypothermia (if the aortic valve is competent) or under deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (if significant aortic regurgitation is present). Continuous apical ventricular venting, Trendelenburg position, leftward table rotation, and continuous $CO_2$ insufflation are vital to prevent cerebral air embolism.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## LEFT ATRIAL CLOSURE AND SURGICAL PITFALLS</summary><div class="details-content">

Following completion of mitral valve reconstruction, special attention must be directed to left atriotomy closure ([Fig. 4-10](#fig-04-10)).

A transmitral cannula is placed through the mitral valve orifice into the left ventricle to render the valve incompetent. This cannula is connected to the heart-lung machine and serves as an active vent only in special circumstances (such as acute aortic insufficiency or ventricular distension). Most of the time, it remains clamped, functioning strictly as a passive stent to hold the mitral leaflets apart and allow retrograde air evacuation.

<span id="fig-04-10"></span>![](_page_20_Figure_4-10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4-10**

During left atrial closure, five major technical pitfalls must be avoided:
* **Right Pulmonary Artery Entrapment:** The right pulmonary artery can be inadvertently caught by sutures placed at the superior aspect of the atriotomy. This complication is prevented by dissecting and separating the artery from the roof of the atrium.
* **Superior Vena Cava Distortion:** The cavo-atrial junction may be narrowed by excessively deep suture bites. Clear surgical exposure of the junction is required.
* **Tearing of the Atrial Roof:** Tissue laceration occurs if suture bites are too large or tension is excessive. The width of each suture bite must be kept under 1 cm.
* **Internal Catheter Entrapment:** Suture lines must avoid catching pacing wires, Swan-Ganz catheters, or retrograde coronary sinus lines.
* **Vent Mattress Suture:** Once the running closure is completed, a large mattress suture is placed around the transmitral vent, to be tied snugly after vent removal.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SYSTEMATIC DE-AIRING PROTOCOL</summary><div class="details-content">

Although continuous carbon dioxide insufflation has significantly reduced the clinical incidence of macro- and micro-air embolism, systematic mechanical de-airing remains one of the most critical phases of every valve operation ([Table 4-1](#table-04-01)).

The comprehensive method developed by Carpentier provides complete de-airing of the two cardiac chambers where air naturally accumulates (the left ventricular apex and the left atrium) while completely avoiding apical puncture or mobilization of the left ventricular apex.

<span id="fig-04-11"></span>![](_page_21_Figure_4-11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 4-11**

De-airing is executed in three successive, coordinated stages ([Fig. 4-11](#fig-04-11)):

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 1. Passive Retrograde De-airing through Left Atrial Opening</summary><div class="details-content">
* The ascending aorta remains securely cross-clamped.
* The left ventricular vent and aortic suction lines remain clamped.
* The mattress suture around the left atrial vent is loosened to allow air and blood collected at the highest point (zenith) of the atrium to escape freely.
* Caval snares are released and the heart is filled by reducing venous line drainage.
* The lungs are fully inflated by the anesthesiologist.
* Gentle mechanical ballottement of the left ventricle and left atrial appendage dislodges air trapped between trabeculae and papillary muscles.
* Complementary apex de-airing is achieved by brief, low-vacuum suction on the ventricular vent line, maintaining pericardial blood over the atriotomy to prevent air entry.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 2. Passive Antegrade De-airing through Aortic Root Suction</summary><div class="details-content">
* The aorta and left ventricular vent remain clamped.
* The mattress suture around the left atrial vent is pulled under tension to completely seal the atrial incision.
* Suction on the aortic root line is opened while maintaining passive cardiac filling. A surgical finger gently compresses the right coronary ostium to prevent air from entering the right coronary artery.
* Active bilateral mechanical lung ventilation is re-established.
* Gentle manual massage of the left ventricle facilitates displacement of air into the aortic root.
* Most of the residual air in the aortic root and ascending aorta is evacuated antegrade through the root vent.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 3. Active Antegrade De-airing on Beating Heart</summary><div class="details-content">
* The aortic cross-clamp is released while keeping the left ventricular vent clamped and the left atrial incision tightly closed under blood immersion.
* Heavy active suction is maintained on the aortic root suction line.
* Right and left heart filling is transiently reduced to prevent acute ventricular overdistension.
* During the first 5 cardiac contractions, the ascending aorta is briefly cross-clamped intermittently to force intracardiac air into the root vent.
* If ventricular distension occurs, the ventricular vent may be opened intermittently on low suction. Transmitral venting must remain strictly clamped to avoid atmospheric air aspiration.
* Aortic root suction is maintained throughout systemic rewarming until complete CPB weaning.
* Continuous TEE monitoring is performed; only after TEE confirms that all microbubbles have cleared from the apex and atrium is the ventricular vent removed.

</div></details>

## WEANING FROM CARDIOPULMONARY BYPASS AND POST-BYPASS CARE</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Separation from Cardiopulmonary Bypass and Pacing</summary><div class="details-content">

Weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass is conducted in a slow, progressive manner only after the patient's core body temperature has reached at least 35°C and the myocardium has regained stable rhythm and vigorous contractility.

Temporary epicardial atrial and ventricular pacing wires are inserted systematically in every patient to maintain optimal rate and atrioventricular synchrony. Acute ventricular dilation immediately after bypass is severely deleterious; filling pressures must be maintained low, tolerating a transiently lower systemic blood pressure rather than overloading the ventricles.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Hemodynamic Management, Inotropes, and Mechanical Support</summary><div class="details-content">

If myocardial contractility is impaired, fluid administration must be strictly restricted to avoid left or right ventricular overdistension. Left atrial pressure (LAP) or pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) must remain strictly below 20 mm Hg. In patients with severe left ventricular dysfunction, if systemic arterial blood pressure remains $< 80\text{ mm Hg}$ and LAP exceeds $> 20\text{ mm Hg}$, an intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) should be instituted immediately rather than escalating toxic doses of sympathetic inotropic drugs.

In patients with severe pulmonary hypertension and secondary right ventricular failure, intravenous milrinone infusion is initiated prior to bypass separation. Resultant systemic vasodilation and hypotension may require the addition of alpha-adrenergic vasopressors (norepinephrine) or balanced inotropes (epinephrine). In selected cases, inhaled nitric oxide is administered to reduce pulmonary vascular resistance selectively.

Comprehensive post-bypass TEE is mandatory to verify complete de-airing, evaluate ventricular kinetics, and rigorously evaluate the reconstructed valve.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Minimally Invasive & Robotic Surgery: Scope and Limitations (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: You have pioneered mini-invasive and robotic surgery. What role should they play today in the context of reconstructive valve surgery?

**AC:** Less invasive approaches present an irresistible attraction to patients because of several perceived advantages: less pain, reduced bleeding, reduced length of hospitalization, and better cosmetic result. The drawback is that the operation is unquestionably more difficult.

For this reason, the main limitation of this approach is actually the risk of performing suboptimal operations: valve replacement instead of valve repair and palliative valve repair instead of a reconstructive valve operation. Other limitations have been highlighted, such as a higher incidence of stroke or ischemia of the leg.

##### Quality Preservation and Impact on Conventional Surgery

There is no question that small incisions are always preferable, but not at the expense of the quality of the valve operation. This is sound information that surgeons owe to their patients in light of their experience. No surgeon should feel guilty for not using mini-invasive approaches, but a surgeon can be blamed for performing a suboptimal valve operation for the sake of mini-invasive surgery.

In fact, the most important contribution of less invasive surgery is that the concept has strongly influenced all cardiac operations. Most surgeons today perform an optimal operation with significantly reduced length of conventional incisions and a much more limited opening of the sternum. As a result, pain, bleeding, infection, and length of hospitalization have been very significantly reduced when compared to the past.

#### DA: In 1999 you pioneered computer-assisted cardiac surgery. What is your feeling today about this approach?

**AC:** It is potentially a promising approach still under investigation by few groups. Computer-assisted valve surgery will develop in the future provided that some persisting drawbacks are resolved: the currently available device is cumbersome and lacks tactile feedback, the operation is unavoidably long, and the high costs of the system and disposable materials preclude a widespread application.

25 years ago, I predicted that this technology would be fully available and used routinely in the cardiac surgery operating room in 2020. This prediction might be possible provided that future advances minimize these drawbacks.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Air Embolism Prevention and Management (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Why have you always emphasized air embolism as a major cause of complication in patients undergoing valvular surgery?

**AC:** Air embolism may be responsible for arrhythmia, low cardiac output, and stroke. These complications are very often attributed to the patient’s related risk factors such as advanced myocardial disease or the presence of valvular or aortic calcification. The influence of these risk factors should be considered only after having ruled out potential air embolism.

The diagnosis may be obvious in the case of poor de-airing maneuvers, visible air bubbles in the coronary arteries, and major ischemic electrocardiogram changes. The diagnosis, however, may be difficult in the presence of coronary artery disease or possible mechanical obstruction. It is important to remember that clinical manifestations of air embolism can be delayed for as long as several hours following arrival in the intensive care unit.

##### Management Protocol and Prevention

It is the responsibility of the surgeon to systematically consider this complication because an early diagnosis allows immediate measures to be instituted that minimize the consequences of this complication. In the operating room, these measures consist of prolonging the extracorporeal support, reassessing the presence of air bubbles in the left atrial and ventricular cavities, repeating the complete de-airing process, and maintaining a high perfusion pressure. In the early hours of intensive care unit stay, if air embolism is considered to be the cause of a stroke, hyperbaric oxygen treatment is recommended.

The key responsibility of the surgeon is to prevent air embolism by systematically applying the different steps of the de-airing process under transesophageal echo guidance. The systematic use of $CO_2$ insufflation is recommended as an additional preventive measure.

### Surgical Approach Indications: Redo, Beating Heart & Transseptal (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: When do you use a right thoracotomy in the setting of a redo operation?

**AC:** I favor a right thoracotomy in patients having had multiple previous operations or mediastinitis and in cases of severe adhesions between the heart and the sternum documented by CT scan. Another potential indication is a patent internal mammary artery crossing the midline.

#### DA: . . . and in the setting of a primary operation?

**AC:** I would consider a right thoracotomy for women expressing cosmetic concerns.

#### FF: Is there an indication for beating heart valve surgery?

**AC:** Beating heart valve surgery is a good option for isolated tricuspid regurgitation, particularly in patients with poor left and right ventricular function.

In the past, I also used this method in patients with type IIIb ischemic regurgitation and poor ventricular function because it provided better protection of the myocardium and possible assessment of leaflet motion on a beating heart. Of course, this required particular attention to prevention of air embolism by keeping the heart empty at all times. Today, with excellent myocardial protection and the assessment of type IIIb regurgitation by echocardiography, I no longer use this technique.

#### FF: What are the potential advantages of total cardiopulmonary bypass, which requires the cannulation and snaring of both superior and inferior vena cavae?

**AC:** Total cardiopulmonary bypass prevents the return of warm blood into the heart, ensuring better myocardial preservation. This is particularly germane in long-lasting, complex valve procedures.

#### DA: When do you use a transseptal approach?

**AC:** I use this approach less frequently than in the past because the interatrial groove approach I favor today provides excellent exposure, even in a small left atrium. The self-retaining retractor specially designed for the left atrial approach does not alter the geometry of the mitral valve.

I still occasionally use a transseptal approach in redo operations with unusually severe adhesions or whenever a very large left atrium severely displaces the mitral valve to the left.

### Technical Complications: Exposure, Left Atrial Tear, and Peripheral Vessels (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: If suboptimal exposure is obtained after the left atrial approach you described, what can be done to improve it?

**AC:** First, make sure that the sternal retractor and the retracting atrial blades are well positioned. A blade introduced too deep in the atrium may displace the mitral valve annulus backward. Second, place two traction stitches at the commissures.

If under these conditions the exposure is still suboptimal, as commonly seen in redo mitral operations where the mitral valve apparatus is hardly visible because of its apical displacement, four maneuvers may further improve the exposure:
1. The first is to completely release all pericardial adhesions, including the posterior atrioventricular (AV) groove.
2. The second maneuver is to exert upward traction on the tapes snaring the two vena cavae.
3. The third is to extend the left atrial incision behind the inferior vena cava.
4. The fourth is to add a 1-cm-long incision extending perpendicularly from the middle of the left atrial incision toward the fossa ovalis involving the atrial septum and the right atrium.

It is amazing to see how much these simple maneuvers enhance the mitral valve exposure.

#### FF: How do you handle a tearing of the dome of the left atrium discovered after discontinuation of the heart-lung machine?

**AC:** The three possible causes of this type of tear are a direct injury to the dome by the extremity of the aortic clamp or a tear caused either by a retractor blade or by suture bites that are too large during left atriotomy closure.

If the tear is large, it should not be fixed by direct suturing using pledgetted mattress sutures because the excess tension on fragile tissues could further enlarge the tear. It is preferable to reinstitute cardiopulmonary bypass and to dissect and visualize the dome in order to analyze the lesion and its proximity to the aortic root.

If the lesion is well identified and far enough from the aortic root, it can be closed with a patch to cover the tear rather than using pledgetted mattress sutures to approximate the edges of the tear. If the tear is near the aortic root, it is preferable to reopen the left atrium and to fix it from inside the atrium using a patch.

#### FF: Can you elaborate on potential complications linked to peripheral cannulation?

**AC:** They may lead to disasters. Ischemia of the limb following arterial cannulation can be expected in long-lasting operations and in young people with small femoral arteries. The risk can be minimized by carefully preserving the distal perfusion of the femoral artery and gentle manipulation of the artery.

The use of a small Dacron graft, sutured end-to-side to the artery for antegrade and retrograde perfusion, is highly recommended in long-lasting operations and small arteries.

#### FF: What about venous complications?

**AC:** Venous complications are due to perforation of the common femoral or iliac veins by the guide wire or the cannula itself. They should be prevented by controlling the progression of the wire and cannula under echo guidance.

When these complications occur, they are often diagnosed because of the difficulty of advancing the wire or the cannula. They can be treated by compression at the femoral or lower iliac veins. This compression should be maintained until the end of the operation and confirmed by absence of retroperitoneal hematoma.

### Residual Regurgitation & Future Transcatheter Valve Technologies (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Do you have a different strategy for residual regurgitation depending on the reconstructed valve?

**AC:** In theory, any residual leak detected by TEE on the operating table should be corrected immediately. However, there are a few rare exceptions:
* After aortic valve reconstruction, a trivial (1+) central leak without cusp pathology can be left as such.
* After tricuspid valve reconstruction, a mild (1+) tricuspid regurgitation in patients with a dilated right ventricle and pulmonary hypertension can also be left without further reconstruction.
* After mitral valve reconstruction, a mild residual regurgitation (1+) is acceptable in type IIIb regurgitation, or in a patient with poor left ventricular function or bad general condition.

Besides these rare exceptions, my opinion is that a residual regurgitation should not be left uncorrected. In most instances TEE allows precise localization of the leak and occasionally determination of the functional type. Knowing the exact location of the leak, the surgeon can readily examine the valve at this level and usually recognize the cause of the leak.

The correction of the leak is generally straightforward and takes only a few minutes. It is worth spending this additional time to obtain a perfect and therefore long-lasting result. However, because the core temperature is usually normal at this time of the operation, special care should be taken to protect the myocardium by an additional dose of cardioplegia.

#### DA: What future role do you see for interventional cardiology valve procedures?

**AC:** I see a great future for transcutaneous aortic valve delivery, provided that the incidence of residual leak is minimal.

By contrast, I foresee only a few indications (e.g., life-saving operations) for percutaneous mitral valve repair procedures. Of course, there will be some attempt to broaden the indications of these mitral valve procedures, accepting suboptimal and temporary results. However, this will be regarded with increasing attention and critical view by patients, who are increasingly aware of the already proven long-lasting results of reconstructive techniques.
