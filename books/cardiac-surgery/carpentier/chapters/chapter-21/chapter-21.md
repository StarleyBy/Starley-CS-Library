# 21. Pathophysiology, Valve Analysis, and Surgical Indications

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### The Pathophysiological Triad
According to the Carpentier reconstructive philosophy, a rigorous distinction must be maintained between etiology (the cause of the disease), lesions (the anatomical alterations resulting from the disease), and dysfunctions (the physiological consequences resulting from the lesions). While etiology dictates the biological substrate and long-term durability, the surgical reconstructive strategy is determined strictly by the specific lesions and resulting leaflet dysfunctions.

### Etiologic Spectrum: Primary vs Secondary Involvement
Aortic valve diseases are broadly categorized into primary valve diseases (direct pathological involvement of the valve leaflets themselves, including congenital bicuspid malformations, rheumatic carditis, calcific degenerative sclerosis, endocarditis, and trauma) and secondary valve diseases (pathological dilatation or disruption of the functional aortic root and supporting structures, such as aortic annular ectasia, ascending aortic aneurysms, and acute aortic dissections).

### Carpentier Functional Classification of Aortic Regurgitation
The functional classification characterizes leaflet motion relative to the aortic annular plane during diastole:
- **Type I (Normal Leaflet Motion):** Regurgitation results from annular dilatation, leaflet perforation, or infective vegetations, with the free edges remaining above the annular plane.
- **Type II (Leaflet Prolapse / Excess Motion):** Regurgitation is caused by transverse displacement of the leaflet free edge below the annular plane during diastole due to commissural detachment, leaflet tears, or free-edge distension.
- **Type III (Restricted Leaflet Motion):** Subdivided into **Type IIIa**, exhibiting restricted leaflet excursion in both systole and diastole (opening and closure) due to commissural fusion, thickening, or calcification; and **Type IIIb**, demonstrating diastolic tethering and restricted closure secondary to outward commissural displacement from severe sinotubular junction dilatation.

### Hemodynamic Severity Stratification and Surgical Decision-Making
Comprehensive assessment combines qualitative color Doppler parameters (jet width percentage of the left ventricular outflow tract, vena contracta width) and quantitative indices (regurgitant volume, regurgitant fraction, effective regurgitant orifice area) for aortic regurgitation, alongside continuous-wave Doppler peak/mean gradients and continuity equation valve areas for aortic stenosis. Because of the limited surface of coaptation and the biological availability of nonthrombogenic alternatives, reconstructive surgery is selectively indicated for pliable leaflets, aortic root aneurysms (valve-sparing procedures), discrete perforations, and non-calcified prolapsing leaflets, whereas calcific stenosis and severely retracted rheumatic leaflets predominantly necessitate prosthetic valve replacement.

#### Principles of Pathophysiological Distinction
The same systemic and degenerative diseases that affect the atrioventricular (mitral and tricuspid) valves may also affect the aortic valve. According to the pathophysiological triad ([Table 21-1](#table-21-01)), a clear distinction should be made between etiology, lesions, and dysfunctions.

Etiologic determination is essential to assess the feasibility of durable valve reconstruction, establish the long-term prognosis, and prescribe appropriate postoperative medical therapy.

#### Predominant Etiologic Patterns and Epidemiology
The three most common etiologies encountered in clinical practice ([Figure 21-1](#fig-21-01)) are bicuspid valve malformation (a), rheumatic valvular disease (b), and calcified degenerative aortic valvular disease (c).

<span id="fig-21-01"></span>![Figure 21-1. The three most common etiologies of aortic valve disease: (a) congenital bicuspid valve malformation, (b) rheumatic valvular disease with commissural fusion and retraction, and (c) calcified degenerative aortic valvular disease.](images/_page_217_Figure_21-1.jpeg)

In developing countries, rheumatic valvular disease remains the predominant etiology, whereas in developed countries, degenerative valve sclerosis leading to calcific aortic stenosis is observed with increasing frequency because of the aging of the population.

Besides the characteristic gross lesions recognized during direct surgical inspection, several complementary clinical factors contribute to determining the etiology of aortic valve disease: patient age, detailed medical history, geographical origin, socioeconomic status, and clinical presentation. Integrating this clinical information with comprehensive transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography findings allows reliable preoperative identification of the etiology in the vast majority of aortic valve diseases.

## FUNCTIONAL CLASSIFICATION OF AORTIC VALVE DYSFUNCTION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### The Functional Approach to Valve Regurgitation
The functional approach by echocardiography provides precise, real-time physiological information regarding leaflet mobility to the operating surgeon, whose primary objective is to restore normal valve mechanics and competence.

Aortic valve regurgitation is classified according to the amplitude of leaflet motion and the diastolic position of the free edge of each leaflet in relation to the aortic annular plane, which serves as the constant reference landmark. Three fundamental types of aortic valve dysfunction are recognized ([Table 21-4](#table-21-04)).

<span id="table-21-04"></span>
| Table 21-4. Carpentier's Functional Classification of Aortic Valve Regurgitation |
| :--- |
| **Type I: Normal Leaflet Motion** |
| Leaflet excursion between systole and diastole is normal; free edges remain above the annular plane in diastole |
| **Type II: Leaflet Prolapse (Excess Leaflet Motion)** |
| Diastolic displacement of the leaflet free edge underneath the annular plane |
| **Type III: Restricted Leaflet Motion** |
| • **Type IIIa:** Restricted leaflet opening and closure (systolic and diastolic restriction) |
| • **Type IIIb:** Restricted leaflet closure only (diastolic tethering) |

#### Type I Dysfunction: Normal Leaflet Motion
In Type I aortic valve regurgitation, the course of the leaflets between systole and diastole has a normal amplitude, and the free edge of the leaflets during diastole is well positioned above the annular plane ([Figure 21-2](#fig-21-02)).

<span id="fig-21-02"></span>![Figure 21-2. Type I aortic valve regurgitation with normal leaflet motion. The amplitude of leaflet excursion is physiological, and the free edge is positioned above the annular plane during diastole; regurgitation results from central coaptation deficit due to annular dilatation, leaflet perforation, or infective vegetations.](images/_page_218_Figure_21-2.jpeg)

The regurgitation in Type I dysfunction results primarily from a central lack of coaptation between the leaflets, which is the direct consequence of aortic annular dilatation. Type I regurgitation may also result from localized leaflet tissue destruction, such as tears, perforations, or infective vegetations.

#### Type II Dysfunction: Leaflet Prolapse
Typically, leaflet prolapse is a valve dysfunction in which the free edge of a leaflet is displaced underneath the annular plane during diastole. The resulting lack of leaflet apposition produces an eccentric regurgitant jet, which runs obliquely under the nonprolapsing leaflets into the left ventricular outflow tract.

The precise degree of prolapse must be assessed by echocardiography and intraoperatively by the "reference point method," comparing the level of the free edge of the prolapsed leaflet with that of adjacent, nonprolapsed reference leaflets. The goal of reconstructive surgery is to reposition the free edge of each prolapsed leaflet at the physiological level in order to restore a secure 2 to 3 mm height of coaptation. Leaflet prolapse may be caused by commissural detachment, leaflet tears, or free-edge fibrous distension.

#### Type III Dysfunction: Restricted Leaflet Motion
In Type III aortic regurgitation, the mobility of one, two, or all three leaflets is pathologically restricted:
- **Type IIIa:** Leaflet motion is limited during both opening and closure. This pattern is typically observed in calcified aortic valve stenosis, senile valve sclerosis, or chronic rheumatic valve disease. Characteristic lesions include commissural fusion, diffuse leaflet fibrous thickening, and calcification.
- **Type IIIb:** Regurgitation displays leaflet tethering caused by outward displacement of the commissures resulting from severe sinotubular junction dilatation. The leaflets are pliable, but their diastolic closure is mechanically hindered by excessive radial tension.

#### Aortic Valve Stenosis
Valvular stenosis presents with varying degrees of hemodynamic severity depending upon the number of leaflets and commissures involved, the intrinsic rigidity of the leaflet tissue, and the spatial extent of dystrophic calcification.

#### Correlation Between Dysfunctions and Underlying Lesions
The functional classification applies individually to each of the three aortic leaflets, which can be affected differently in terms of both motion and severity. This allows the echocardiographer and the surgeon to localize the dysfunction and pair it with its causative anatomical lesion ([Table 21-5](#table-21-05)).

<span id="table-21-05"></span>
| Table 21-5. Valve Dysfunctions and Corresponding Anatomical Lesions |  |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Valve Dysfunction** | **Corresponding Anatomical Lesions** |
| **Type I:** Normal leaflet motion | Annular dilatation; Leaflet perforation; Infective vegetation |
| **Type II:** Leaflet prolapse | Leaflet rupture or distension; Commissural detachment |

## HEMODYNAMIC CONSEQUENCES AND SEVERITY GRADING</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Pathophysiology of Aortic Regurgitation
Aortic valve regurgitation produces a significant widening of the systolic-diastolic pressure difference (increased pulse pressure), the magnitude of which serves as a classical clinical criterion for the severity of valve regurgitation. Specifically, a diastolic aortic pressure less than half of the systolic pressure indicates severe, hemodynamically significant aortic regurgitation.

As an obligate consequence of diastolic regurgitant flow, the end-diastolic volume of the left ventricle increases substantially. The clinical tolerance of the regurgitant volume depends upon the compliance and contractile reserve of the left ventricle to compensate for the diastolic volume overload. In the late stages of chronic disease, progressive left ventricular dilatation and myocardial systolic dysfunction ensue.

#### Qualitative and Quantitative Grading of Regurgitation
Echocardiographic color Doppler study allows precise qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the severity of valvular regurgitation ([Table 21-6](#table-21-06)).

<span id="table-21-06"></span>
| Table 21-6. Grading of Aortic Regurgitation (Modified from Douglas et al.) | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Qualitative Parameters** |  |  |  |
| Doppler jet width (% of LVOT diameter) | <25% | 25%–65% | >65% |
| Vena contracta width (cm) | <0.3 | 0.3–0.6 | >0.6 |
| **Quantitative Parameters (Cath or Echo)** |  |  |  |
| Regurgitant volume (ml per beat) | <30 | 30–59 | ≥60 |
| Regurgitant fraction (%) | <30% | 30%–49% | ≥50% |
| Regurgitant orifice area (cm²) | <0.20 | 0.20–0.29 | ≥0.30 |

Qualitative assessment utilizes the relative width of the color Doppler jet across the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) and the vena contracta width. Quantitative parameters—regurgitant volume, regurgitant fraction, and effective regurgitant orifice area—provide rigorous confirmation of hemodynamic impact and dictate the timing of operative intervention.

#### Pathophysiology of Aortic Stenosis
Aortic valve stenosis produces a mechanical barrier to left ventricular ejection, resulting in a marked elevation of peak systolic left ventricular pressure. Over time, this chronic pressure overload induces concentric left ventricular hypertrophy. In advanced or decompensated aortic stenosis, secondary left ventricular dilatation and heart failure develop.

#### Hemodynamic Quantification of Stenosis Severity
The severity of aortic valve stenosis is determined by transvalvular pressure gradients measured with continuous-wave Doppler ultrasound using the simplified Bernoulli equation. Both instantaneous peak and mean transvalvular gradients are recorded. In minimally symptomatic or borderline cases, hemodynamic evaluation during exercise testing provides valuable functional insight. Aortic jet velocity and the calculated aortic valve area (determined via the continuity equation) complete the hemodynamic evaluation ([Table 21-7](#table-21-07)).

<span id="table-21-07"></span>
| Table 21-7. Grading of Aortic Stenosis (Modified from Zoghbi et al.) | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Jet velocity (m/sec) | <3.0 | 3.0–4.0 | >4.0 |
| Mean transvalvular gradient (mmHg) | <25 | 25–40 | >40 |
| Effective aortic valve area (cm²) | >1.5 | 1.0–1.5 | <1.0 |

## SURGICAL INDICATIONS AND STRATEGY</summary><div class="details-content">

#### General Indications for Surgical Intervention
Surgical intervention is indicated in all symptomatic patients presenting with severe aortic valve disease. Furthermore, operation is indicated in asymptomatic patients with severe disease before irreversible left ventricular dilatation (end-systolic dimension >50 mm or >25 mm/m²) or systolic dysfunction (ejection fraction <50%) occurs.

Unlike mitral valve disease, in which reconstructive valve surgery represents the undisputed primary surgical option, aortic valve disease is most commonly treated by prosthetic valve replacement. This disparity stems from two fundamental realities:
1. The unfavorable anatomical characteristics of the aortic valve, which features a very limited surface of coaptation and high diastolic closing pressures;
2. The availability of reliable, nonthrombogenic substitutes, notably the pulmonary autograft (Ross operation) in children and young adults, and modern biological prostheses in older patients.

#### Reconstructive Indications in Aortic Regurgitation
In aortic valve regurgitation, historical repair techniques have been associated with variable results because of limited long-term reliability. Nonetheless, clear indications for reconstructive valve surgery exist:
- Traumatic cusp tears or avulsions;
- Acute or healed bacterial endocarditis with discrete perforations but preserved leaflet pliability;
- Aortic regurgitation associated with ventricular septal defect (VSD) in children and adolescents;
- Selected pliable rheumatic valves in younger patients where anticoagulation is contraindicated or undesirable;
- Valve-sparing aortic root replacement (reimplantation or remodeling procedures) in patients with aortic root or ascending aortic aneurysms with morphologically normal cusps.

#### Strategy in Valvular Aortic Stenosis
Severe calcific aortic valve stenosis in adult patients requires definitive valve replacement surgery, because surgical attempts at adult aortic valve repair (debridement, ultrasonic decalcification) have yielded poor durability and rapid restenosis. The sole exception is congenital aortic valve stenosis in infants and young children, where surgical commissurotomy successfully relieves obstruction while preserving native growth potential.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: What are your respective indications for aortic valve reconstruction, biological valve replacement, or mechanical valve replacement in young patients?
**AC:** Three factors should be taken into consideration: the type of lesion, the patient's socioeconomic condition, and the patient's personal preference.

As far as lesions are concerned, congenital malformations and aortic root disease with pliable leaflets can benefit from a valve repair or a valve-sparing operation. In rheumatic valvular disease, knowing that an aortic valve reconstruction has limited durability, better alternatives are the Ross operation or a prosthetic valve replacement. Whenever the patient requires or favors a nonthrombogenic valve substitute, a new-generation valvular bioprosthesis may be considered.

#### FF: Can an aneurysm of a sinus of Valsalva produce aortic valve regurgitation?
**AC:** An aneurysm of a sinus of Valsalva may develop without aortic valve regurgitation until the intercommissural distance or the sinotubular circumference dilates to such a point that a lack of apposition of the free edge is produced.

#### FF: What are the possible causes of pressure gradients at the different levels of the left ventricular outflow tract?
**AC:** There are multiple causes depending on the anatomical level of the aortic root:
- At the left ventricular outflow tract level, one may encounter an infra-annular fibrous ring, asymmetric septal hypertrophy, excess mitral valve tissue, or systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the anterior mitral leaflet.
- At the leaflet level, pressure gradients result from extensive calcification or leaflet fibrous retraction and thickening.
- At the supravalvular level, the most common cause is congenital supravalvular stenosis.

#### DA: In what circumstances do you favor a bio-Bentall procedure to a valve-sparing procedure?
**AC:** I recommend the bio-Bentall procedure in the following two circumstances: for elderly patients and for patients with severely depressed left ventricular function. In bicuspid valves, the appropriate role of the valve-sparing procedure remains to be determined.
