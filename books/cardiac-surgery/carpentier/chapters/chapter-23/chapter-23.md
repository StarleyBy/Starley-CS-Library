# 23. Mitral Valve Malformations

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Spectrum and Diagnostic Challenges of Congenital Mitral Malformations
Mitral valve malformations in the pediatric population are rare, diverse, and anatomically complex anomalies that frequently coexist with other congenital cardiovascular defects. Unlike acquired valve pathology, congenital lesions involve embryonic dysgenesis of any component of the mitral apparatus: annulus, leaflets, chordae tendineae, and papillary muscles. Echocardiography and magnetic resonance imaging are essential to establish accurate anatomic detail and hemodynamic severity. The strategic timing of surgical intervention seeks to postpone operation as long as safely possible to allow the child and mitral annulus to grow, while avoiding irreversible pulmonary vascular disease, refractory heart failure, and growth retardation.

### Carpentier's Four-Tier Functional Classification
Congenital mitral malformations are systematically organized into four distinct functional categories:
- **Type I (Normal Leaflet Motion):** Primary regurgitant lesions including isolated annular dilatation, cleft anterior leaflet (isolated or partial atrioventricular canal type), and localized leaflet agenesis or defects.
- **Type II (Leaflet Prolapse / Excess Motion):** Regurgitation resulting from elongation or agenesis of chordae tendineae, or elongation of papillary muscles.
- **Type III (Restricted Leaflet Motion with Normal Papillary Muscles):** Stenotic or mixed lesions characterized by commissural-papillary fusion, anomalous short chordae, or obstructive excess valvular/supravalvular tissue in the presence of two well-formed papillary muscles.
- **Type IV (Restricted Leaflet Motion with Abnormal Papillary Muscles):** Severe malformations characterized by primitive or deformed subvalvular apparatus, including the true parachute mitral valve (single papillary muscle receiving all chordae), hammock valve (anomalous muscular arcade with absent interchordal spaces and direct leaflet insertion), and papillary muscle hypoplasia or agenesis.

### Reconstructive Surgical Techniques
Surgical repair principles mirror the philosophy of acquired reconstruction adapted to fragile pediatric tissues:
- **Type I Repairs:** Circular annuloplasty or tailored prosthetic ring implantation (using size 22 to 26 mm rings, which do not inhibit subsequent subvalvular annular growth); direct closure of anterior leaflet clefts with interrupted sutures; and pericardial patch closure for leaflet agenesis.
- **Type II Repairs:** Chordal shortening, chordal transposition, creation of artificial chordae, and papillary muscle shortening or repositioning.
- **Type III Repairs:** Commissurotomy with wedge excision of fibrous tissue, extensive interchordal fenestration, resection of supravalvular fibrous rings, and autologous glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial patch leaflet extension.
- **Type IV Repairs:** Splitting of the solitary papillary muscle in parachute valves with aggressive interchordal fenestration; and fenestration of muscular arcades with leaflet mobilization in hammock valves.

### Long-Term Clinical Outcomes from Hôpital Broussais
In a historical landmark series of 155 children surgically managed between 1970 and 1991, valve reconstruction was successfully achieved in 94% of patients presenting with stenosis and 93% with regurgitation, with hospital mortalities of 26% (early era pulmonary hypertension) and 5%, respectively. In a subsequent series of 145 consecutive children (mean age 5.7 ± 3 years, follow-up to 26 years), reconstructive surgery was possible in 95% of patients (138 children), including 91 ring annuloplasties and 21 pericardial patch leaflet extensions. Actuarial freedom from reoperation was 86 ± 6% at 5 years, 80 ± 8% at 10 years, and 68 ± 17% at 15 years.

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION AND DIAGNOSTIC ASSESSMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Congenital Mitral Valve Stenosis
Mitral valvular malformations are rare, diverse, and complex anomalies. They are often associated with other cardiac malformations and are frequently recognized late because of the lack of awareness of their possible existence.

Congenital mitral valve stenosis belongs to the category of anomalies that cause pulmonary venous hypertension. For this reason, the malformation is generally diagnosed and treated early in childhood, within the first 3 to 4 years of life. Clinical manifestations include recurrent heart failure, dyspnea on exertion, failure to thrive, and tachypnea.

The clinical diagnosis of mitral valve stenosis is suggested by a characteristic apical diastolic murmur associated with right ventricular heave and left atrial hypertrophy on electrocardiography. The diagnosis may be challenging in the presence of an associated systolic murmur, which may or may not be related to concomitant defects. Atrial fibrillation is extraordinarily rare in children even after prolonged disease progression.

#### Timing of Surgical Intervention in Stenosis
Echocardiography is essential to establish the diagnosis and define the exact anatomic type of mitral valve stenosis. Cardiac catheterization is usually performed to assess pulmonary vascular resistance, evaluate ventricular functional volumes, and identify associated anomalies.

The optimal age for surgical intervention depends on physical disability, growth retardation, and the severity of pulmonary hypertension. The expected difficulty of the operation, attributable to the fragility of the leaflet tissue and the diminutive size of the mitral valve annulus in infants, makes it preferable to delay surgery as long as safely possible. However, emergency or semi-elective surgery may become mandatory within the first months or years of life because of intractable pulmonary hypertension or life-threatening heart failure.

#### Congenital Mitral Valve Regurgitation
Congenital mitral valve regurgitation frequently results in repeated lower respiratory tract infections, congestive cardiac failure, and physical growth retardation. With adequate medical decongestive therapy, mitral valve regurgitation is slightly better tolerated hemodynamically than mitral valve stenosis. Consequently, children typically undergo surgery at a later age, approximately 6 years.

Surgery should be delayed as late as feasible to allow the implantation of an annuloplasty ring of near-normal size ([Table 23-1](#table-23-01)). In practice, repeated episodes of cardiac failure, progressive left ventricular enlargement, or pulmonary artery hypertension dictate the timing of operative intervention.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY: CARPENTIER'S FUNCTIONAL CLASSIFICATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### The Four Functional Categories
The diagnosis of the precise type of malformation is difficult because of the numerous anatomical variants. Similar to acquired mitral valve disease, comprehensive echocardiography and magnetic resonance imaging permit classification of congenital malformations into four functional groups ([Figure 23-1](#fig-23-01) and [Table 23-2](#table-23-02)).

These diagnostic modalities facilitate accurate identification of the malformation ([Table 23-2](#table-23-02)) and guide the systematic functional approach to surgical valve reconstruction.

Echocardiography is also critical to quantify the severity of valvular stenosis or regurgitation, measure the annular dimensions against nomograms of normal size, and evaluate ventricular chamber volumes and systolic function. Associated cardiovascular lesions must be systematically cataloged.

#### Type I: Normal Leaflet Motion
Three primary anomalies are included in this functional category: isolated annular dilatation, cleft leaflet, and localized leaflet defect or agenesis. All three entities produce pure valvular regurgitation.

#### Isolated Annular Dilatation
Isolated annular dilatation ([Figure 23-2](#fig-23-02)) is a rare but well-defined malformation. The annulus is severely dilated, leading to central coaptation failure despite normal leaflet architecture and mobility ([Figure 23-2](#fig-23-02), a). The treatment consists of annular remodeling by circular annuloplasty or prosthetic ring annuloplasty ([Figure 23-2](#fig-23-02), b).

<span id="fig-23-02"></span>![Figure 23-2. Isolated congenital mitral annular dilatation: (a) severe annular dilatation with central coaptation deficit despite preserved leaflet mobility; (b) surgical remodeling via prosthetic ring annuloplasty restoring leaflet apposition.](images/_page_231_Figure_23-2.jpeg)

#### Cleft Anterior Leaflet
A cleft anterior leaflet is a congenital division that splits the anterior leaflet into two distinct hemivalves ([Figure 23-3](#fig-23-03)). Two distinct forms must be carefully differentiated: the true isolated cleft and the atrioventricular canal type cleft.

<span id="fig-23-03"></span>![Figure 23-3. Congenital cleft of the anterior mitral leaflet: (a) isolated cleft oriented toward the aortic valve; (b) cleft associated with partial atrioventricular canal oriented toward the ventricular septum; (c, d) surgical reconstruction by edge approximation with interrupted sutures.](images/_page_231_Figure_23-3.jpeg)

The true isolated cleft ([Figure 23-3](#fig-23-03), a) is oriented directly toward the aortic valve and left fibrous trigone, whereas the cleft associated with an atrioventricular canal defect ([Figure 23-3](#fig-23-03), b) is oriented toward the mid-portion of the ventricular septum. Both forms produce significant regurgitation and are repaired by reapproximating the edges of the cleft with interrupted 5-0 monofilament sutures ([Figure 23-3](#fig-23-03), c, d).

#### Leaflet Agenesis and Defects
Congenital leaflet agenesis or localized tissue deficiency involves either the anterior or posterior leaflet ([Figure 23-4](#fig-23-04)). The resulting lack of tissue produces severe regurgitation ([Figure 23-4](#fig-23-04), a). Reconstruction requires augmentation of the deficient segment using a tailored autologous pericardial patch treated with 0.6% glutaraldehyde ([Figure 23-4](#fig-23-04), b).

<span id="fig-23-04"></span>![Figure 23-4. Congenital leaflet defect and agenesis: (a) segmental absence of leaflet tissue producing major regurgitation; (b) reconstructive patching with glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium restoring leaflet surface.](images/_page_232_Figure_23-4.jpeg)

#### Type II: Leaflet Prolapse
In Type II malformations, the free edge of one or both leaflets overrides the plane of the annulus during ventricular systole ([Figure 23-5](#fig-23-05)). The underlying lesions include elongation of chordae tendineae ([Figure 23-5](#fig-23-05), a), elongation of papillary muscles, or localized absence (agenesis) of chordae.

<span id="fig-23-05"></span>![Figure 23-5. Type II congenital leaflet prolapse: (a) severe anterior leaflet prolapse secondary to chordal elongation; (b) reconstruction utilizing chordal shortening and remodeling annuloplasty.](images/_page_232_Figure_23-5.jpeg)

Reconstruction entails restoring normal leaflet height by chordal shortening, chordal transposition, or implantation of polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) neo-chordae, systematically combined with a stabilizing annuloplasty.

#### Type III: Restricted Motion with Normal Papillary Muscles
In Type III malformations, leaflet excursion during diastole and/or systole is restricted despite the presence of two distinct, anatomically normal papillary muscles ([Figure 23-6](#fig-23-06)).

<span id="fig-23-06"></span>![Figure 23-6. Type III congenital restricted leaflet motion: (a) restricted diastolic opening due to anomalous short chordae and commissural fusion; (b) restoration of leaflet excursion via commissurotomy and interchordal fenestration.](images/_page_233_Figure_23-6.jpeg)

The causative lesions comprise congenital commissural fusion ([Figure 23-7](#fig-23-07), a), abnormally short and thickened chordae ([Figure 23-7](#fig-23-07), b), or fusion of the papillary muscle tip directly to the leaflet commissure.

<span id="fig-23-07"></span>![Figure 23-7. Commissural and chordal pathology in Type III malformations: (a) congenital commissural fusion; (b) anomalous short and thickened primary chordae tethering the leaflet belly.](images/_page_233_Figure_23-7.jpeg)

Surgical correction requires precise commissurotomy, longitudinal splitting of fused papillary muscles, and interchordal fenestration.

#### Supravalvular Mitral Ring
A discrete fibrous membrane or supravalvular ring may encircle the mitral orifice on its atrial aspect ([Figure 23-8](#fig-23-08)). This membrane arises immediately above the hinge line of the leaflets and causes severe inflow obstruction ([Figure 23-8](#fig-23-08), a).

<span id="fig-23-08"></span>![Figure 23-8. Congenital supravalvular mitral ring: (a) circumferential fibrous diaphragm located immediately above the mitral leaflets obstructing left ventricular inflow; (b) complete surgical excision of the membrane exposing intact leaflets below.](images/_page_234_Figure_23-8.jpeg)

Complete circumferential excision of the supravalvular membrane with fine scissors relieves the stenosis while preserving the underlying delicate leaflet tissue ([Figure 23-8](#fig-23-08), b).

#### Accessory Valvular Tissue and Double Orifice
Accessory mitral leaflet tissue ([Figure 23-9](#fig-23-09)) presents as an anomalous mobile mass of fibrous tissue originating from the leaflet belly or subvalvular apparatus, producing left ventricular outflow tract obstruction or transmitral obstruction ([Figure 23-9](#fig-23-09), a). Resection of the accessory tissue relieves the gradient ([Figure 23-9](#fig-23-09), b).

<span id="fig-23-09"></span>![Figure 23-9. Accessory mitral valve tissue: (a) anomalous fibrous mass obstructing left ventricular outflow; (b) surgical resection of accessory tissue restoring unhindered outflow tract.](images/_page_234_Figure_23-9.jpeg)

Double mitral valve orifice ([Figure 23-10](#fig-23-10)) is characterized by a central bridge of fibrous or muscular tissue dividing the mitral opening into two separate orifices ([Figure 23-10](#fig-23-10), a).

<span id="fig-23-10"></span>![Figure 23-10. Double-orifice mitral valve: (a) central fibrous bridging dividing the mitral orifice into two functional components; (b) intraoperative surgical view.](images/_page_234_Figure_23-10.jpeg)

<span id="fig-23-10-cont"></span>![Figure 23-10 (continued). Double-orifice mitral valve: (c) assessment of individual orifice competence; (d) reconstructive approach preserving both functional orifices without transecting the supporting bridge.](images/_page_235_Figure_23-10_cont.jpeg)

Surgical management must respect the central bridge if each orifice has an independent subvalvular support, addressing only associated leaflet prolapse or annular dilatation ([Figure 23-10](#fig-23-10), c, d).

#### Type IV: Restricted Motion with Abnormal Papillary Muscles
Type IV malformations represent severe developmental abnormalities of the ventricular papillary muscle apparatus ([Figure 23-11](#fig-23-11)).

<span id="fig-23-11"></span>![Figure 23-11. Type IV malformations with abnormal papillary muscles: (a) true parachute mitral valve with single dominant papillary muscle; (b) cross-sectional echocardiographic view.](images/_page_235_Figure_23-11.jpeg)

<span id="fig-23-11-cont"></span>![Figure 23-11 (continued). Type IV malformations with abnormal papillary muscles: (c) hammock valve with muscular arcade obliterating the subvalvular space; (d) direct insertion of leaflets into ventricular trabeculations.](images/_page_236_Figure_23-11_cont.jpeg)

In the true parachute mitral valve ([Figure 23-11](#fig-23-11), a), all chordae tendineae converge and insert into a single, solitary papillary muscle. In the hammock valve or anomalous muscular arcade ([Figure 23-11](#fig-23-11), c, d), the papillary muscles are fused into a confluent muscular ridge directly attached to the leaflet margins, obliterating the subvalvular interchordal spaces.

## RECONSTRUCTIVE SURGICAL TECHNIQUES</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Exposure and Annular Sizing
Exposure is achieved through a standard left atriotomy in the interatrial groove or transseptally via the right atrium in the presence of an associated atrial septal defect. Great care must be taken to avoid tearing the extremely delicate infant leaflet tissue.

The normal diameter of the mitral annulus for the patient's body surface area must be determined using Rowlatt's nomogram ([Table 23-1](#table-23-01)). This measurement serves as the guide for annuloplasty sizing.

#### Techniques in Type I Malformations
For isolated annular dilatation in children older than 5 years, a prosthetic remodeling ring (size 22 to 26 mm) is implanted ([Figure 23-12](#fig-23-12)). In infants younger than 5 years, a circular annuloplasty using vertical mattress sutures of 2-0 braided polyester is preferred to avoid complete circular rigidity.

<span id="fig-23-12"></span>![Figure 23-12. Surgical techniques in Type I malformations: (a) exposure of dilated annulus; (b) placement of annuloplasty sutures; (c) sizing with pediatric sizers; (d) ring seating; (e) saline testing demonstrating competent coaptation.](images/_page_237_Figure_23-12.jpeg)

For cleft anterior leaflets, interrupted 5-0 monofilament sutures are placed from the base of the cleft toward the free margin ([Figure 23-13](#fig-23-13)), leaving a 1 to 2 mm zone of free margin untied to prevent leaflet tethering and restriction.

<span id="fig-23-13"></span>![Figure 23-13. Surgical closure of anterior leaflet cleft: placement of interrupted fine sutures from the annular base toward the free edge, restoring continuity without creating radial tension.](images/_page_238_Figure_23-13.jpeg)

Whenever tissue deficiency is present, an autologous pericardial patch treated with 0.6% glutaraldehyde for 10 minutes is tailored to reconstruct the defect ([Figure 23-14](#fig-23-14)). Remodeling annuloplasty should be performed in most mitral valve malformations to prevent future annular dilatation.

<span id="fig-23-14"></span>![Figure 23-14. Reconstruction of congenital leaflet defect: augmentation of deficient anterior leaflet tissue using an autologous glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial patch combined with ring annuloplasty.](images/_page_238_Figure_23-14.jpeg)

#### Techniques in Type II Malformations
In Type II prolapse, elongated chordae are shortened by trenching into the papillary muscle tip or by folding plication ([Figure 23-15](#fig-23-15)).

<span id="fig-23-15"></span>![Figure 23-15. Reconstructive techniques in Type II leaflet prolapse: papillary muscle plication and chordal shortening restoring physiological coaptation height.](images/_page_239_Figure_23-15.jpeg)

When chordae are absent, artificial chordae of 5-0 or 6-0 expanded PTFE (Gore-Tex) are secured between the fibrous core of the papillary muscle and the free edge of the prolapsed leaflet.

#### Techniques in Type III Malformations
In Type III restricted leaflet motion, commissurotomy is performed by sharply incising the fused commissures to within 2 mm of the annulus ([Figure 23-16](#fig-23-16)).

<span id="fig-23-16"></span>![Figure 23-16. Surgical techniques in Type III malformations: (a) commissurotomy of fused commissures; (b) interchordal fenestration of thickened, fused subvalvular structures; (c) mobilization of papillary muscle attachments.](images/_page_240_Figure_23-16.jpeg)

Extensive interchordal fenestration is carried out by excising fibrous tissue between fused secondary and tertiary chordae, thereby liberating the subvalvular inflow tract.

When severe fibrous retraction is present, autologous glutaraldehyde-treated pericardial leaflet extension is performed ([Figure 23-17](#fig-23-17)). A transverse incision is made at the base of the retracted leaflet, and a tailored pericardial patch is sutured in place with continuous 5-0 monofilament suture to significantly enlarge the surface of coaptation.

<span id="fig-23-17"></span>![Figure 23-17. Extensive pericardial leaflet extension in congenital restricted mitral leaflets: (a) baseline restricted leaflet; (b) transverse incision at the base; (c) insertion of tailored oval pericardial patch; (d) suturing with continuous monofilament; (e) restored coaptation surface.](images/_page_241_Figure_23-17.jpeg)

#### Techniques in Type IV Malformations
In true parachute mitral valves, the solitary papillary muscle is split longitudinally into two distinct muscular columns down to the ventricular wall ([Figure 23-18](#fig-23-18)).

<span id="fig-23-18"></span>![Figure 23-18. Surgical reconstruction of parachute mitral valve: (a) single papillary muscle with crowding of chordae; (b) longitudinal splitting of the papillary muscle into two separate heads combined with aggressive interchordal fenestration to enlarge the central opening.](images/_page_242_Figure_23-18.jpeg)

Deep interchordal fenestration must be combined with papillary splitting to separate the anterior and posterior chordal groups and relieve the severe subvalvular stenosis.

In hammock valves, muscular bridges obstructing the subvalvular inflow are divided and fenestrated ([Figure 23-19](#fig-23-19)), liberating the tethered leaflet margins and creating a functional funnel into the left ventricular cavity.

<span id="fig-23-19"></span>![Figure 23-19. Reconstruction of hammock valve (anomalous muscular arcade): division of anomalous muscular insertions and creation of interchordal fenestrations restoring leaflet mobility.](images/_page_243_Figure_23-19.jpeg)

#### Early Surgical Series (1970–1991)
Excluding complex atrioventricular canal defects, single ventricle, and hypoplastic left heart syndrome, our clinical experience encompasses hundreds of children undergoing surgery for congenital mitral malformations before age 12.

In an early series of 155 children surgically treated between March 1970 and August 1991 (50 with congenital mitral stenosis, 105 with congenital mitral regurgitation), valve reconstruction using the techniques described in this chapter was successfully achieved in 94% of patients in the stenotic group and 93% in the regurgitant group.

Hospital mortality was 26% in the stenotic cohort (primarily attributable to severe, long-standing pulmonary arterial hypertension in early infancy) and 5% in the regurgitant cohort.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: As you initiated your career in adult cardiac surgery, what led you to become interested in congenital mitral valve malformations?
**AC:** When I began my practice in cardiac surgery in 1965, the surgical mortality for congenital mitral valve disease was prohibitive, often exceeding 30% to 40% worldwide, and prosthetic valve replacement in infants was almost uniformly fatal.

I realized that the surgical principles of valve analysis and functional reconstruction that I was developing for adult degenerative and rheumatic disease could be adapted to pediatric anatomy. With the collaboration of pioneering pediatric cardiologists, we demonstrated that reconstructing the native valve preserves ventricular geometry and allows children to survive and grow.

#### DA: What progress needs to be made to improve the results of surgical treatment of congenital mitral valve disease?
**AC:** Mitral valve stenosis has the worst prognosis among congenital malformations, particularly in infants presenting with severe pulmonary hypertension. Progress depends on three factors: earlier echocardiographic detection before irreversible pulmonary vascular changes occur; refinement of micro-surgical instruments and suture materials suited for delicate infant tissues; and continued improvement in postoperative intensive care management of pulmonary hypertensive crises.

#### FF: How do you explain the rarity of atrial fibrillation in children with mitral valve disease?
**AC:** Atrial fibrillation results from long-lasting mitral disease causing progressive atrial stretch, interstitial fibrosis, and electrical remodeling. In children, even with severe hemodynamic overload, the atrial myocardium has not experienced decades of chronic tension and maintains electrical homogeneity, preventing the wavelets of reentry that trigger and perpetuate fibrillation.

#### FF: When do you approach the mitral valve through the right atrium and an incision of the atrial septum?
**AC:** Unless a large atrial septal defect is already present, I believe the standard left atriotomy in the interatrial groove provides the cleanest, most direct visualization of the mitral valve without distorting the geometry of the annulus. The transseptal approach through the fossa ovalis is reserved for infants with a small left atrium, complex associated right-sided anomalies, or whenever visualization via the groove is anatomically constrained.

#### DA: Is there a role for an annuloplasty band, which might be preferred to a ring, to respect the growth of the child?
**AC:** In children younger than 5 years, an annuloplasty band or a circular suture annuloplasty is preferred because a rigid closed ring would permanently fix the circumference. However, in children older than 5 years, a prosthetic ring (sizes 22 and 26 mm) is exceptionally well tolerated. The valve apparatus and ventricular cavity continue to develop below and in spite of the ring, and when the child reaches adult size, the small ring can easily be removed and replaced with a full adult-sized ring if necessary.

#### DA: What is the long-term durability of a glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch used for leaflet extension in children?
**AC:** The very long-term durability of glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium has exceeded our original expectations. When properly fixed in 0.6% glutaraldehyde for 10 minutes, the collagen fibers are cross-linked, preventing both early tissue retraction and late aneurysmal stretching. In our 15-year and 20-year follow-up studies, calcification of these pericardial extensions was remarkably low compared to commercial heterografts.

#### FF: Is there any indication for valve replacement in children with a mitral valve malformation?
**AC:** Mechanical valve replacement should be avoided as far as humanly possible in children. It should be reserved solely for cases where reconstructive attempts have failed or where there is total agenesis of the leaflet and papillary apparatus incompatible with repair. In children younger than 2 years, mechanical valve replacement carries an early and late mortality exceeding 20% to 30%, alongside severe risks of anticoagulation-related hemorrhage and valve thrombosis.

#### FF: Whenever a valve replacement is needed what type of valve should be used?
**AC:** Despite continuous progress made in valve processing, valvular bioprostheses should be avoided in children because of the persistent risk of early accelerated calcification within 2 to 4 years. Mitral homografts led to disappointing results in our hands, suffering rapid degeneration. I have no personal experience with the so-called Mitral Ross procedure and do not advocate it. Whenever replacement is unavoidable, a modern low-profile mechanical bileaflet prosthesis remains the most reasonable alternative.

#### FF: Can you elaborate on tricuspid regurgitation associated with congenital mitral valve malformation in children?
**AC:** With the exception of atrioventricular canal malformations and very long-standing, neglected mitral valve malformations, associated tricuspid regurgitation is rare in children. Whenever the right atrium and right ventricle are severely dilated secondary to pulmonary hypertension, the tricuspid valve should be explored intraoperatively. If annular dilatation is present, an annuloplasty should be added to prevent postoperative right heart failure.

#### FF: In asymptomatic children with severe valvular regurgitation, what criteria do you use for surgical intervention?
**AC:** Severe valvular regurgitation may be well tolerated for a long time in children because of favorable ventricular compliance. Progressive cardiac enlargement on serial imaging and progressively increasing pulmonary artery pressures measured noninvasively or by catheterization are the two critical criteria that prompt surgical intervention before irreversible myocardial damage occurs.

#### DA: You mention that a ring implanted in a young child can be replaced by a larger ring at adulthood.
**AC:** This may be surprising, but our extensive reoperative experience has shown that the subvalvular apparatus and the base of the heart develop below and in spite of the ring, giving the opportunity to implant a larger adult ring at reoperation. If there is insufficient leaflet tissue to accommodate the adult ring, the procedure is combined with pericardial leaflet extension.

#### FF: In your experience, what is the likelihood of valve reconstruction in children with Type III dysfunction?
**AC:** In children every technical option should be pursued to avoid mechanical valve replacement. The liberal and aggressive use of pericardial leaflet extension, combined with commissurotomy and interchordal fenestration, allows successful and durable valve reconstruction in approximately 70% of Type III congenital malformations.

#### FF: What residual transmitral gradient do you believe necessitates valve replacement surgery?
**AC:** A residual mean transmitral gradient greater than 8 mm Hg measured post-bypass by direct pressure monitoring or transesophageal echocardiography indicates significant residual obstruction and necessitates immediate revision or valve replacement.
