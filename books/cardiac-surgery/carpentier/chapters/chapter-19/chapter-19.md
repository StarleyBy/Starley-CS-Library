# 19. Reconstructive Techniques

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Principles of Tricuspid Valve Reconstruction
Reconstruction of the tricuspid valve is governed by the same three fundamental principles established for the mitral valve: preserving or restoring normal leaflet motion, restoring an optimal orifice area, and remodeling the annulus while preserving its dynamic flexibility. The surgical approach is optimized through a right circular atriotomy, which prevents damage to atrial conduction tissue, minimizes supraventricular arrhythmias, and provides direct visual exposure of all three leaflets and the subvalvular apparatus.

### Remodeling Annuloplasty and Sizing Standards
Annular dilatation in functional tricuspid regurgitation is primarily treated by remodeling annuloplasty using a Carpentier-Edwards classic or 3D Physio prosthetic ring. The fundamental rule of ring selection is that the size is chosen according to the surface area of available leaflet tissue (measured by exerting gentle traction on the anterior papillary muscle to unfurl the anterior and posterior leaflets), rather than the dilated annular circumference. Annular sutures must be placed with meticulous precision: passing through the hinge of the septal leaflet posterior to its midpoint to safely avoid the bundle of His in Koch's triangle, and maintaining orientation toward the right ventricle in the aortic segment to prevent injury to the non-coronary sinus of Valsalva.

### Spectrum of Reconstructive Techniques by Dysfunction Type
- **Type I Dysfunction:** Remodeling annuloplasty for annular dilatation; glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patching for leaflet perforations and tears.
- **Type II Dysfunction (Prolapse):** Triangular leaflet resection (if defect involves < 10% of leaflet area); chordal transposition from adjacent leaflet tissue; artificial PTFE chordae; papillary muscle sliding plasty or concertina shortening for elongated muscles; and papillary muscle reimplantation into a septal trench for ruptured heads.
- **Type IIIa Dysfunction (Diastolic Restriction / Stenosis):** Direct triple commissurotomy, division and fenestration of fused chordae, secondary chordae resection, and autologous pericardial patch extension for retracted leaflets.
- **Type IIIb Dysfunction (Systolic Tethering):** Undersized remodeling ring annuloplasty (1–2 sizes smaller than sizer) to compensate for right ventricular papillary displacement and leaflet tenting.
- **Pacemaker/Defibrillator Lead Lesions:** Annular trench positioning for adherent leads with annular/leaflet continuity restoration; lead extraction and epicardial lead placement for infected or non-mobilizable leads.

### Clinical Evidence and Long-Term Durability
Long-term clinical follow-up of 1,345 patients at Hôpital Broussais demonstrated superior durability of remodeling ring annuloplasty compared to semicircular suture annuloplasty (de Vega): a 10-year reoperation rate of 0.6% versus 6.4%, with documented preservation of valve competence and hemodynamics.

## SURGICAL EXPOSURE AND ANNULAR SIZING</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Surgical Exposure via Right Circular Atriotomy
Reconstruction of the tricuspid valve should be guided by the same basic principles described for the mitral valve: (1) preserve or restore full leaflet motion, (2) create a large surface of coaptation, and (3) remodel the annulus.

Surgical exposure of the tricuspid valve is achieved through a circular atriotomy made 1 to 2 cm away from the right atrioventricular groove ([Fig. 19-1](#fig-19-01)). The circular atriotomy's low incision aims at preserving the atrial conduction tissue with the goal of minimizing the risk of supraventricular arrhythmia. It also provides optimal exposure because of more direct access to the tricuspid valve. In addition, the upper curvature of the atriotomy is ideal for holding a self-retaining retractor blade.

#### Systematic Valve Analysis
A systematic analysis of the three leaflets using nerve hooks is performed to identify the mechanism and type of valve dysfunction ([Fig. 19-2](#fig-19-02)).

*[Figure 19-2](#fig-19-02). Systematic valve analysis using a nerve hook. Gentle traction on each leaflet systematically identifies areas of prolapse, chordal elongation, or restricted leaflet motion.*

#### Remodeling Annuloplasty Principles
Type I regurgitation due to annular dilatation is best treated by annular remodeling. The prosthetic ring remodels the annulus, restores the normal shape of the orifice, and increases the surface of coaptation while preserving leaflet mobility.

Carpentier-Edwards Classic and 3D rings and sizers are available in sizes 26 to 36 mm ([Table 19-1](#table-19-01)). The relative flexibility of the ring allows its adaptation to the three-dimensional configuration and complex motion of the annulus.

#### Ring Selection and Leaflet Sizing Technique
The size of the prosthetic ring is chosen according to the surface area of the anterior leaflet or, more practically, the surface area of the leaflet tissue that can be unfurled by exerting gentle traction on the anterior papillary muscle ([Fig. 19-3](#fig-19-03)).

<span id="fig-19-03"></span>![](_page_195_Figure_19-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-3**

*[Figure 19-3](#fig-19-03). Unfurling the tricuspid leaflet tissue. Gentle traction exerted on the head of the anterior papillary muscle exposes the available leaflet surface area.*

The measurement of the entire surface area of the anterior leaflet requires its complete unfurling, which is difficult because of the dual attachment of this leaflet to the anterior and conal papillary muscles. Exerting traction on the head of the anterior papillary muscle and measuring the surface area of the exposed leaflet tissue, which corresponds to a large portion of the anterior leaflet and a small portion of the posterior leaflet, provides an equivalent measurement with a much easier technique ([Fig. 19-4](#fig-19-04)).

<span id="fig-19-04"></span>![](_page_195_Figure_19-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-4**

*[Figure 19-4](#fig-19-04). Application of the Carpentier sizer to the exposed leaflet tissue. The sizer is matched against the surface area of the unfurled leaflet tissue.*

#### Three-Step Sizing Protocol
Ring selection follows three successive measurements ([Fig. 19-5](#fig-19-05)):
1. The first is the measurement of the anterior leaflet surface area using a sizer (a).
2. The second is the measurement of the height of the septal leaflet (b).
3. The third is the confirmation that the selected sizer covers the exposed leaflet tissue (c).

<span id="fig-19-05"></span>![](_page_196_Figure_19-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-5**

*[Figure 19-5](#fig-19-05). Comprehensive three-step sizing protocol: (a) measurement of the anterior leaflet surface area; (b) verification of the septal leaflet height; (c) confirmation of orifice coverage with the chosen sizer; (d) matching leaflet tissue surface area to optimal ring size.*

The size of the ring is selected from the surface area of the leaflet tissue, not from the annular circumference.

A final measurement is used to cover the portion of the exposed leaflet tissue (d). If the surface area of the leaflet tissue is less than that of the preselected sizer, a smaller sizer should be tried and vice versa. The measured leaflet surface area indicates the optimal orifice area and therefore the size of ring to be selected.

## RING IMPLANTATION AND ANNULAR REMODELING</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Suture Placement Technique and Conduction Safety
Once the ring has been selected, a series of 3-0 horizontal mattress sutures are placed at equidistant points around the tricuspid annulus.

To place these sutures properly within the annulus, the leaflet tissue must be grasped transversally and pulled away from its attachment to visualize its hinge ([Fig. 19-6](#fig-19-06)). The first septal suture should be placed posterior to the midline of the septal leaflet attachment, through the very hinge of this leaflet to prevent injury to the bundle of His. The following septal mattress suture is placed in the same manner towards the posteroseptal commissure. Directing the needle through the annulus toward the ventricular cavity and then back through the annulus ensures that these bites have maximal strength.

<span id="fig-19-06"></span>![](_page_197_Figure_19-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-6**

*[Figure 19-6](#fig-19-06). Safe placement of annular sutures along the septal leaflet hinge. Sutures are placed posterior to the midline of the septal leaflet, strictly through the hinge, avoiding the bundle of His in Koch's triangle.*

#### Suture Placement Around the Free Wall Annulus
The mattress sutures corresponding to the posterior and anterior leaflets are placed within the annulus (i.e., 2 mm from the atriovalvular junction) ([Fig. 19-7](#fig-19-07)).

<span id="fig-19-07"></span>![](_page_198_Figure_19-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-7**

*[Figure 19-7](#fig-19-07). Suture placement along the posterior and anterior annulus. Suture bites are placed 2 mm from the atriovalvular junction. Inset: Schematic rectangular needle maneuvers illustrating forehand and backhand suture techniques.*

A schematic rectangular presentation of the annulus illustrates the commonly used needle maneuvers and positions. For the two horizontal sides backhand sutures are used and forehand sutures for the two vertical sides.

In the aortic segment of the annulus, special care should be taken not to injure the aortic root. This can be achieved by visualizing the hinge of the anterior segment and by placing suture with the tip of the needle always oriented towards the right ventricle.

#### Suture Passage Through the Prosthetic Ring
Sutures are then passed through the sewing band of the selected ring ([Fig. 19-8](#fig-19-08)).

<span id="fig-19-08"></span>![](_page_199_Figure_19-8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-8**

*[Figure 19-8](#fig-19-08). Passing sutures through the sewing band of the prosthetic ring: equal spacing is maintained along the septal segment (a), while reduced spacing is used along the anterior and posterior segments (b-d) to achieve plication and remodeling.*

Equal spacing is used for sutures of the septal leaflet (a) while reduced spacing is used for sutures that arise from the posterior and anterior leaflets, especially for those arising from the commissures (b-d). The relative flexibility of the ring allows its adaptation to the three-dimensional configuration and complex motion of the annulus.

#### Ring Seating and Competence Testing
The prosthetic ring is lowered into position, thus remodeling the annulus and ensuring adequate surface of coaptation while preserving full mobility of the leaflets.

The normal geometry of the closure line is tested by using a bulb syringe to inject saline solution into the ventricular cavity ([Fig. 19-9](#fig-19-09)). A regular line of closure indicates that a large surface of coaptation has been restored (a), which is confirmed by echocardiography (b).

<span id="fig-19-09"></span>![](_page_199_Figure_19-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-9**

*[Figure 19-9](#fig-19-09). Testing valve competence after ring annuloplasty: (a) saline injection into the right ventricular cavity demonstrates a uniform, symmetric line of coaptation; (b) post-repair echocardiography confirms adequate coaptation length.*

## RECONSTRUCTIVE TECHNIQUES FOR SPECIFIC LESIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Leaflet Perforation or Other Tissue Defects
Lesions such as leaflet perforation, vegetation, or tears may also cause type I dysfunction. Leaflet perforation or vegetation requires first the resection of all infected tissues and a 2-mm ridge of normal tissue; then the leaflet is patched using glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium ([Fig. 19-10](#fig-19-10)). A leaflet tear is repaired by suturing or patching. A ring annuloplasty is usually indicated as an adjunct procedure.

<span id="fig-19-10"></span>![](_page_200_Figure_19-10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-10**

*[Figure 19-10](#fig-19-10). Repair of tricuspid leaflet perforation. Following debridement of infected tissue with a 2-mm clear margin, the defect is closed using a glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch.*

#### Type II Dysfunction: Prolapse Management
Most techniques described for mitral valve prolapse are applicable to the tricuspid valve.

Limited chordae rupture or chordae elongation can be treated by leaflet triangular resection provided that the resection involves less than one tenth of the leaflet surface area ([Fig. 19-11](#fig-19-11)); otherwise, patching is necessary.

<span id="fig-19-11"></span>![](_page_200_Figure_19-11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-11**

*[Figure 19-11](#fig-19-11). Triangular resection of prolapsing leaflet tissue. Indicated for localized prolapse involving less than one tenth of leaflet surface area.*

Extensive prolapse resulting from bacterial endocarditis ([Fig. 19-12](#fig-19-12)) requires first the resection of all infected tissue (a) and then restoration of leaflet continuity by annular plication and leaflet suturing (b) or patching (c).

<span id="fig-19-12"></span>![](_page_200_Figure_19-12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-12**

*[Figure 19-12](#fig-19-12). Surgical reconstruction in extensive infective endocarditis: (a) radical excision of infected tissue; (b) annular plication and direct primary approximation; (c) pericardial patch reconstruction of large defects.*

#### Chordal Reconstruction: Transposition and Neo-Chordae
Extensive chordae rupture requires chordae transposition ([Fig. 19-13](#fig-19-13)) using a segment of an adjacent leaflet or using artificial chordae ([Fig. 19-14](#fig-19-14)).

*[Figure 19-13](#fig-19-13). Chordal transposition. A small segment of an adjacent normally supported leaflet containing secondary chordae is transferred to support the flail segment.*

**FIGURE 19-14**

*[Figure 19-14](#fig-19-14). Implantation of artificial PTFE (Gore-Tex) chordae to correct extensive chordal rupture.*

#### Papillary Muscle Elongation and Shortening Techniques
Extensive chordae elongation and papillary muscle elongation ([Fig. 19-15](#fig-19-15)) can be treated by papillary muscle sliding plasty (a) or the concertina technique (b).

<span id="fig-19-15"></span>![](_page_201_Figure_19-15.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-15**

*[Figure 19-15](#fig-19-15). Techniques for papillary muscle shortening: (a) papillary muscle sliding plasty; (b) concertina shortening technique.*

#### Papillary Muscle Rupture Reimplantation
Papillary muscle rupture can be treated by papillary muscle reimplantation ([Fig. 19-16](#fig-19-16)). The papillary muscle remnant attaching the chordae is trimmed from all muscular tissue, leaving a small fibrous cuff 3 to 4 mm in diameter (a). A 5 mm long × 4 mm deep trench is created in the septum (b) at a distance from the annulus that allows positioning of the corresponding leaflet free edge at the level of the reference leaflet.

The two arms of a 4-0 monofilament suture first secured to the fibrous cuff are passed through each edge of the trench (c). Pulling the two ends of this suture effectively buries the cuff into the trench. The suture is tied. Two additional 4-0 sutures are then placed to close the trench, carefully avoiding close contact with the chordae to prevent chordae abrasion (d).

<span id="fig-19-16"></span>![](_page_202_Figure_19-16.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-16**

*[Figure 19-16](#fig-19-16). Papillary muscle reimplantation into a septal trench: (a) trimming the muscular head to leave a 3-4 mm fibrous cuff; (b) creation of a 5 × 4 mm trench in the interventricular septum; (c) suture placement burying the cuff into the trench; (d) closure of the trench margins.*

#### Type IIIa Dysfunction: Commissurotomy and Leaflet Extension
Commissurotomy of the three commissures is performed under direct visualization with division of fused chordae ([Fig. 19-17](#fig-19-17), a). Further leaflet mobilization is achieved by resection of secondary chordae. When the leaflets are significantly retracted, leaflet extension is performed after commissurotomy and fenestration of the supporting chordae (b). Normal cusp apposition is then obtained by insertion of a prosthetic ring.

<span id="fig-19-17"></span>![](_page_202_Figure_19-17.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-17**

*[Figure 19-17](#fig-19-17). Reconstructive techniques in Type IIIa stenosis/restriction: (a) direct triple commissurotomy and division of fused chordae; (b) autologous pericardial leaflet extension following chordal fenestration.*

#### Type IIIb Dysfunction: Undersized Annuloplasty
An undersized ring annuloplasty can be used to repair type IIIb tricuspid regurgitation with severe tethering of otherwise normal leaflet tissue resulting from right ventricular dilatation ([Fig. 19-18](#fig-19-18)). One or two smaller sizes are used depending upon the severity of ventricular dilation. Extreme forms may require anterior leaflet augmentation by patching.

<span id="fig-19-18"></span>![](_page_203_Figure_19-18.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-18**

*[Figure 19-18](#fig-19-18). Undersized ring annuloplasty in Type IIIb regurgitation with severe tethering. Downsizing by 1 to 2 ring sizes compensates for lateral displacement of papillary muscles.*

Type IIIb tricuspid regurgitation with severe tethering resulting from fibrotic retraction of the chordae tendineae, as seen in some rheumatic disease or endomyocardial fibrosis, can be treated by resection of scar tissue followed by patch extension and restoration of leaflet continuity.

#### Management of Lesions Caused by Pacemaker or Defibrillator Leads
Pacemaker or defibrillator leads may become adherent to leaflets, either the septal or the posterior leaflet, or both, with scar tissue impairing leaflet mobility ([Fig. 19-19](#fig-19-19), a). More rarely, a lead can cause leaflet perforation or a tear. Infected leads are recognized by the presence of vegetations.

<span id="fig-19-19"></span>![](_page_203_Figure_19-19.jpeg)

**FIGURE 19-19**

*[Figure 19-19](#fig-19-19). Management of pacemaker and defibrillator lead-induced pathology: (a) fibrous lead encapsulation and leaflet entrapment; (b) relocation of the lead into a 3-4 mm annular trench with reconstruction of annular and leaflet continuity.*

In the case of leaflet perforation, the usual repair technique consists of an incision of the posterior leaflet extended to the annulus. The lead is positioned within a 3- to 4-mm deep incision of the annulus, and the continuity of both the annulus and the leaflet is restored (b). A prosthetic ring may be necessary if the annulus is dilated.

#### Lead Extraction and Epicardial Pacing Strategy
When a leaflet tear is present, the previously described technique is used if the lead can be separated from the leaflet tissue. If the lead cannot be mobilized, it must be resected. The valve is then repaired and epicardial leads are implanted.

Finally, in the case of infection, all the leads are removed, the infected tissues are extensively excised, and the leaflets are repaired by sliding plasty or leaflet patching. It is rare to find valves altered to the point of being nonreparable.

## CLINICAL RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### The Broussais Long-Term Experience (1,345 Patients)
In 1983 we reviewed our conclusions from a large series of 1345 tricuspid valve repairs at Hôpital Broussais between 1971 and December 1982. Two different techniques of tricuspid valve repair were used by two different groups of surgeons so that a comparative evaluation could be done.

Valve repair using reconstructive techniques and a remodeling annuloplasty ring was performed on 1210 patients whereas valve repair using reconstructive techniques and a semicircular annuloplasty according to de Vega was performed on 135 patients.

#### Comparative Freedom from Reoperation
The incidence of reoperation at 10 years was 0.6% in the prosthetic ring annuloplasty group and 6.4% in the semicircular annuloplasty group.

#### Hemodynamic Follow-Up Investigations
For further evaluation, 30 patients (15 with functional and 15 with organic disease) treated by prosthetic ring annuloplasty were subjected to follow-up catheterization, angiograms, dye-dilution curves, and intracardiac phonocardiograms. A good result with no significant valve stenosis or regurgitation was observed in all but one patient, who had severe organic tricuspid disease.

The superiority of remodeling annuloplasty and valve reconstructive procedures has been further confirmed by other studies.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Advantages of Right Circular Atriotomy
> **DA:** What are the potential advantages of a circular atriotomy compared to the conventional atriotomy parallel to the atrioventricular groove?
>
> **AC:** The circular atriotomy’s low incision aims at preserving the atrial conduction tissue with the goal of minimizing the risk of supraventricular arrhythmia. It also provides optimal exposure because of more direct access to the tricuspid valve. In addition, the upper curvature of the atriotomy is ideal for holding a self-retaining retractor blade.

#### Evolution of Tricuspid Sizing Techniques
> **FF:** In the past, you have recommended measurement of the surface area of the anterior leaflet of the tricuspid valve for ring selection. More recently, however, you have recommended measuring parts of the surface area of both the anterior and posterior leaflets. Can you comment on this?
>
> **AC:** The measurement of the entire surface area of the anterior leaflet requires its complete unfurling, which is difficult because of the dual attachment of this leaflet to the anterior and conal papillary muscles. I found that exerting traction on the head of the anterior papillary muscle and measuring the surface area of the exposed leaflet tissue, which corresponds to a large portion of the anterior leaflet and a small portion of the posterior leaflet, provides an equivalent measurement with a much easier technique.

#### Critique of Palliative Suture Annuloplasties
> **FF:** Are there any circumstances in which you recommend the use of a palliative technique such as de Vega repair?
>
> **AC:** The de Vega annuloplasty is a narrowing, not a remodeling, annuloplasty, which explains why it led to suboptimal long-term results. Considering the unpredictability of the progression of annular dilatation, I usually recommend the use of a prosthetic ring. In children, I perform a commissuroplasty at the anteroposterior and posteroseptal commissures to avoid a ring.

#### Rationale for Ring Downsizing
> **DA:** Do you ever downsize the ring?
>
> **AC:** The fundamental principle of remodeling annuloplasty is remodeling, not narrowing, the annulus according to the surface area of the leaflet tissue. The one exception is type IIIb tricuspid regurgitation, in which some degree of downsizing (one or two ring sizes, depending upon the degree of ventricular dilatation) is necessary to compensate leaflet tethering.

#### Indications for Surgical Correction
> **DA:** You recommend a very liberal approach for the correction of moderate functional tricuspid regurgitation. Are there any clinical or intraoperative scenarios where you do not correct a tricuspid regurgitation?
>
> **AC:** My answer may be surprising because I recommend that any documented tricuspid regurgitation should be corrected whatever the degree of regurgitation, including mild regurgitation in patients with pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular dysfunction, or right atrial dilatation.

#### Rationale for Three-Dimensional Flexible Rings
> **FF:** You recommend a 3D ring for the tricuspid valve to match the shape of the tricuspid annulus. What is the rationale behind this recommendation?
>
> **AC:** The tricuspid annulus is a fragile structure, particularly at the attachment of the septal leaflet. In addition, it is subject to complex deformations during the cardiac cycle. An ideal ring structure should be flexible enough to follow the morphological changes rather than imposing a predetermined shape to the annulus.

#### Valvular Tissue vs Orifice Area Concept
> **DA:** How do you define the relationship between the amount of valvular tissue and the orifice area?
>
> **AC:** By measuring the surface area of valvular tissue using sizers, you determine the theoretical size of the annulus, just like measuring the surface area of a door gives you the size of the frame supporting the door. This theoretical size is the size of the ring to be selected. An orifice that can hold a sizer that is two sizes larger should be repaired.

#### Dreyfus Criterion and Annular Prophylaxis
> **FF:** Do you believe mild tricuspid annular dilatation without regurgitation is a sufficient criterion to perform an annuloplasty on patients undergoing mitral valve surgery?
>
> **AC:** We all have seen patients who developed late tricuspid regurgitation despite a successful mitral valve reconstruction and the absence of tricuspid regurgitation at the time of mitral valve surgery. To address this dilemma, Dreyfus has suggested that significant annular dilatation of the tricuspid valve without regurgitation should benefit from a remodeling annuloplasty. I fully adhere to this concept, which implies systematic measurement of the tricuspid orifice and available leaflet tissue in mitral valve surgery.

#### Bicuspidization Technique of Kay
> **DA:** Do you ever use the bicuspidization technique described by Dr. Kay?
>
> **AC:** The bicuspidization of the tricuspid valve is a palliative technique that plicates the posterior segment of the annulus and the corresponding posterior leaflet. This leaflet is therefore excluded from valve function. The plicated leaflet tissue becomes fibrotic and may become calcified in the long term. I no longer use this now outdated technique.

#### Choice of Prosthesis in Re-Replacement
> **FF:** Does the presence of a mechanical valve on the left side impact your choice of prosthesis when the tricuspid valve needs to be replaced?
>
> **AC:** The technique of leaflet extension with glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium usually avoids the need for tricuspid valve replacement.

#### Valve Choice in Extensive Endocarditis
> **FF:** Yes, but if you are forced to replace the valve because of extensive endocarditis, for example, what would you do?
>
> **AC:** I would implant a bioprosthesis because of the high risk of thrombus formation with a mechanical valve in the tricuspid position. This is a personal preference.

#### Management of Atrioventricular Conduction Block
> **DA:** Following a tricuspid annuloplasty, does the onset of a third-degree atrioventricular block require any particular response?
>
> **AC:** Most of the AV blocks following a tricuspid annuloplasty are multifactorial, related to cardioplegia, topical cooling, and/or tissue distension by retractors. Most of them resolve within a week. Therefore they do not require a second look provided that the surgeon is confident that the suture placement respected the area of the bundle of His.

#### Asymptomatic Severe Tricuspid Regurgitation
> **DA:** Is there a role for surgery in patients with isolated asymptomatic severe tricuspid regurgitation?
>
> **AC:** There are circumstances where a tricuspid regurgitation occurs as an isolated valve disease, such as bacterial endocarditis, trauma, chronic pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular infarction, and pacemaker implantation. Although in these circumstances the tricuspid valve regurgitation may be tolerated for a long period, surgery is indicated as soon as increasing volume or dimension of right cardiac chambers occurs. Clinical signs of right-sided heart failure significantly increase the risk of operative mortality.

#### Residual Mild Regurgitation Post-Annuloplasty
> **DA:** Following tricuspid annuloplasty, some patients present with residual mild regurgitation. What is the explanation?
>
> **AC:** The most common cause of this condition is transient right ventricular dilatation and dysfunction, which usually resolves or remains stable. Whenever the regurgitation is permanent, a potential explanation is a type IIIb regurgitation that has not been corrected by adequate downsizing.

#### Prophylactic Annuloplasty in Heart Transplantation
> **FF:** What is your opinion about systematic tricuspid annuloplasty at the time of cardiac transplantation?
>
> **AC:** A randomized study has shown that systematic annuloplasty was associated with preserved tricuspid valve function in patients with cardiac transplantation. The absence of postoperative tricuspid regurgitation facilitates the medical management of this high-risk group of patients. This is particularly important if the recipient has increased pulmonary artery pressure.
