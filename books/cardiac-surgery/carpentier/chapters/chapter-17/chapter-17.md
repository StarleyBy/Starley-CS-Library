# 17. Surgical Anatomy and Physiology

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Anatomical Landmarks and Right Atrial Architecture
* **Right Atrial Components:** Curved posterior caval groove continuous with the superior and inferior vena cavae, smooth interatrial septum containing the triangle of Koch, and trabeculated anterior wall extending into the right atrial appendage.
* **Atrio-Valvular Junction:** The boundary between the right atrium and tricuspid valve is sharply demarcated by structural transitions, lipid deposits, and coloration shifts.
* **Single Fibrous Trigone Anchor:** Unlike the mitral annulus which is anchored between two symmetric fibrous trigones, the tricuspid annulus attaches to only one fibrous trigone (the right/posteromedial fibrous trigone), making the remainder of its circumference predominantly muscular and dynamic.

### Tricuspid Annulus Geometry and Dynamics
* **Non-Planar "Wave Form":** The tricuspid annulus possesses a complex 3D wave-like architecture rather than a flat or simple saddle shape, featuring an anterior-septal peak and posterior depression.
* **Dynamic Cyclic Deformation:**
  * Diastolic orifice area: $7.1 \pm 1.3\text{ cm}^2$ (grossly circular);
  * Systolic orifice area: $5 \pm 1\text{ cm}^2$ ($13 \pm 1\%$ circumference reduction, $20\text{--}30\%$ area reduction);
  * Complex 3D kinematics combining circumferential contraction, segmental mobility, and axial torsion along the anteroposterior axis.
* **Critical Structures at Risk:**
  1. *Aortic Root / Non-Coronary Sinus of Valsalva:* Separated from the aortic segment of the tricuspid annulus by only a few millimeters of fibrous tissue;
  2. *Atrioventricular Conduction Axis (Bundle of His):* Penetrates beneath the membranous septum at the apex of the triangle of Koch;
  3. *Right Coronary Artery:* Courses within the right atrioventricular groove adjacent to the anterior and posterior annular segments.

### Leaflets, Subvalvular Apparatus, and Conduction
* **Leaflet Architecture:** Three main leaflets of unequal dimensions: anterior (largest, height 25 mm), posterior (intermediate, scalloped), and septal (smallest, least mobile, attached to the septum).
* **Dual Chordal Function:** Primary (marginal), secondary (strut), and tertiary (basal) chordae. Beyond preventing systolic prolapse, the subvalvular apparatus actively facilitates diastolic leaflet opening ("active opening valve").
* **Triangle of Koch Boundaries:** Tendon of Todaro (superior limb), septal annulus (inferior limb), and coronary sinus ostium (base), converging at the membranous septum.

## RIGHT ATRIAL MORPHOLOGY AND TRICUSPID JUNCTION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Topographical Organization of the Right Atrium

The right atrium consists of a curved posterior groove continuous with the superior and inferior vena cavae, a flat interatrial septum, a trabeculated anterior wall, and the right atrial appendage ([Fig. 17-1](#fig-17-01)).

The posterior groove is separated from the anterior wall by the crista terminalis. The interatrial septum is characterized by the fossa ovalis, the limbus of the fossa ovalis, and the triangle of Koch.

The Eustachian valve guards the inferior vena caval orifice, while the Thebesian valve protects the coronary sinus ostium.

**FIGURE 17-1** Surgical anatomy of the right atrium and tricuspid valve: curved posterior caval groove, smooth interatrial septum, triangle of Koch, and trabeculated anterior wall with right atrial appendage.

#### The Atrio-Valvular Boundary and Surgical Exposure

The junction between the right atrium and the tricuspid valve is usually well-delineated by the change in color and structure ([Fig. 17-2](#fig-17-02)) at the junction between the smooth atrial endocardium and the valvular tissue.

This delineation is accentuated by a yellowish line of adipose tissue, which is particularly visible at the level of the anterior and posterior leaflets.

In contrast, at the level of the septal leaflet, this junction is less apparent because the endocardium directly bridges the atrial and ventricular septal musculature.

#### Segmental Dissection of the Annular Perimeter

The annulus of the tricuspid valve is not visible from an atrial view. It can be exposed only by surgical dissection separating the atrial muscle from the valve leaflets.

The annulus fibrosus is not visible from an atrial view because it is deeper and covered by atrial musculature.

The annulus is a heterogeneous, almost virtual structure composed of intermixed fibrous, fibroelastic, and muscular tissue.

## ANNULAR GEOMETRY, DYNAMICS, AND STRUCTURES AT RISK</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Three-Dimensional "Wave Form" Architecture

The tricuspid annulus has an asymmetrical, non-planar configuration best described as a "wave form" ([Fig. 17-3](#fig-17-03)).

The highest position is the portion of the aortic segment adjacent to the right fibrous trigone and non-coronary aortic sinus.

The lowest position corresponds to the posterior segment and posteroseptal commissure. This non-planar contour is accentuated during ventricular systole by ventricular contraction.

<span id="fig-17-03"></span>![](_page_176_Figure_17-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 17-3** Non-planar "wave form" configuration of the tricuspid annulus: 3D saddle/wave architecture showing elevated anterior-septal peak and depressed posterior segment.

#### Cyclic Annular Dynamics and Measurements

The shape of the annulus varies throughout the cardiac cycle ([Fig. 17-4](#fig-17-04), [Table 17-1](#table-17-01)). During diastole, it is grossly circular (a) with a transverse diameter of 35 mm and an anteroposterior diameter of 30 mm.

During systole, the orifice contracts concentrically by 13 percent in circumference and 20 to 30 percent in area, assuming an elliptical wave form (b).

The concentric reduction of the tricuspid valve orifice during systole results from the contraction of the right and left ventricles and the bulging of the aortic root. In addition to concentric deformation, the tricuspid annulus displays three-dimensional deformation at the anteroseptal commissure.

<span id="fig-17-04"></span>![](_page_177_Figure_17-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 17-4** Cyclic dynamics and 3D deformation of the tricuspid annulus: circular diastolic geometry (a) transitioning into an elliptical, concentrically reduced systolic wave form with axial torsion (b).

#### Critical Anatomical Structures at Risk

Two anatomical structures close to the annulus are at risk during tricuspid valve surgery ([Fig. 17-5](#fig-17-05)): (1) the non-coronary sinus of Valsalva, especially in the aortic segment, and (2) the atrioventricular conduction bundle of His in the apex of the triangle of Koch.

The right coronary artery is also at potential risk where it courses in the right atrioventricular groove.

Sutures placed along the anterior and posterior segments must remain superficial to avoid coronary arterial compromise.

## LEAFLETS AND SUBVALVULAR APPARATUS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Leaflet Morphology and Segmental Dimensions

The three leaflets of the tricuspid valve have different sizes and shapes ([Fig. 17-6](#fig-17-06), [Table 17-2](#table-17-02)). The anterior leaflet is larger than the posterior leaflet, which is larger than the septal leaflet.

The anterior leaflet is primarily attached to the right ventricular outflow tract, the posterior leaflet to the muscular wall of the right ventricle, and the septal leaflet to the septum.

The anterior leaflet has a semicircular shape with an average height of 25 mm. The free edge of the valve may present an indentation.

#### Dual Role of the Subvalvular Mechanism

The subvalvular apparatus fulfills two distinct functional roles ([Fig. 17-7](#fig-17-07)): one is to maintain leaflet coaptation and prevent prolapse during systole; the other is to facilitate the opening of these leaflets during diastole ("active opening valve").

To achieve these two functions, the papillary muscles are oriented in a direction perpendicular to the plane of the leaflets in diastole.

During systole, the papillary muscles contract, pulling the leaflets towards the apex of the right ventricle, which ensures stable planar coaptation.

<span id="fig-17-07"></span>![](_page_179_Figure_17-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 17-7** Dual functional mechanism of the tricuspid subvalvular apparatus: prevention of systolic leaflet prolapse and active diastolic opening ("active opening valve") via papillary muscle contraction.

#### Chordae Tendineae Classification and Papillary Muscle Anatomy

Three types of chordae can be described depending upon their attachment on the leaflets ([Fig. 17-8](#fig-17-08)): primary (marginal) chordae inserting into the free edge (1), secondary (strut) chordae inserting into the ventricular surface (2), and tertiary (basal) chordae originating directly from the ventricular wall (3).

The chordae tendineae can also be described in relation to their origin from the papillary muscles and their insertion on different segments of the valve ([Fig. 17-9](#fig-17-09)).

The anterior papillary muscle is the largest and most constant, while the posterior papillary muscle is frequently bifid or trifid. The septal papillary muscles (papillary muscle of Lancisi) are small and multiple.

## CONDUCTION SYSTEM AND THE TRIANGLE OF KOCH</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Topographical Boundaries of Koch's Triangle

The triangle of Koch is the key surgical landmark for avoiding injury to the atrioventricular conduction tissue ([Fig. 17-10](#fig-17-10)).

Its boundaries comprise the tendon of Todaro superiorly, the attachment of the septal leaflet inferiorly, and the ostium of the coronary sinus at its base.

The apex of the triangle corresponds to the central fibrous body and the membranous septum, where the atrioventricular node transitions into the penetrating bundle of His.

#### Surgical Safety Rules in Annular Suture Placement

To prevent postoperative complete atrioventricular block, all annular sutures placed in the vicinity of the septal annulus must avoid the apex of Koch's triangle.

Sutures along the septal annulus should be placed superficially into the leaflet hinge tissue rather than deeply into the muscular septum.

At the level of the coronary sinus, annuloplasty sutures should be transitioned onto the atrial side or terminated anterior to the tendon of Todaro.

**FIGURE 17-10** Surgical boundaries of the triangle of Koch and conduction axis: tendon of Todaro (superior), septal annulus (inferior), and coronary sinus ostium (base), meeting at the membranous septum where the bundle of His penetrates the ventricular septum.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### The Aortic Segment and Aortic Root Relationships

> **FF:** What is the significance of the aortic segment that you identified?
>
> **AC:** I believe it is useful to direct the attention of cardiac surgeons to the proximity of this segment with the aortic root because it has important practical implications. Only a few millimeters of fibrous tissue separate the aortic segment of the tricuspid annulus from the aortic root, particularly in the elderly. Therefore improper orientation of a suture could result in aortic sinus or leaflet injury. If this occurs, the aortic valve should be inspected through an aortotomy to recognize and repair the lesion.

> **FF:** Does the pathology of the aortic root affect the geometry or structure of the tricuspid valve?
>
> **AC:** It certainly does in two pathological processes: (1) aneurysms of the aortic root and (2) bacterial endocarditis of the aortic valve with annular abscess involving the area between the right coronary cusp and the non-coronary cusp.

#### Tricuspid Dimensions and the "Wave Form" Annulus

> **DA:** Is there a "normal size" for the tricuspid valve?
>
> **AC:** The size of the tricuspid orifice is related to the body surface area and therefore varies from patient to patient. More important is the "normal" relationship between the size of the tricuspid orifice and the surface area of the leaflet tissue. This relationship is altered in pathological conditions either by a dilated annulus or by leaflet tissue shrinkage and should be restored by remodeling annuloplasty. The size of the selected ring should be based on the surface area of the leaflet tissue and not arbitrarily be the same in all patients.

> **FF:** Does the tricuspid annulus have a saddle shape like the mitral annulus?
>
> **AC:** Numerous studies in my laboratory have shown that the tricuspid annulus should be defined not only by its shape but also by its dynamics. The dynamics of the tricuspid annulus are more complex than those of the mitral valve. The tricuspid annulus combines three types of motion: (1) circumferential, with successive contraction and dilatation of the orifice; (2) segmental, with four different segments following a different pattern of motion; and (3) axial, with torsion of the annulus along the anteroposterior axis of the orifice. The resulting geometry of the annulus is not a saddle shape but a complex and constant changing segmental movement evoking that of waves. Thus the term "wave form" seems more appropriate than saddle shape for the tricuspid valve.

#### Asymmetrical Dynamics and the Anteroseptal Commissure

> **FF:** How do you explain the complex asymmetrical motion of the tricuspid annulus as opposed to that of the mitral valve?
>
> **AC:** The fundamental difference between the two valves is the fact that the tricuspid annulus is attached to a single fibrous trigone while the mitral valve annulus is attached symmetrically to both fibrous trigones. Most of the circumference of the tricuspid annular attachment is muscular as opposed to the posterior two-thirds circumference of the mitral annulus. This explains the more complex and asymmetrical pattern of annular contraction of the tricuspid valve, with the posterior trigone serving as the "point d'ancrage," that is, the fixation point of the tricuspid annulus.

> **DA:** Can you further clarify the functional anatomy of the anteroseptal commissure?
>
> **AC:** Observing a beating heart either in the laboratory or during open-heart surgery permits visualization during the cardiac cycle of torsion of the tricuspid orifice along its anteroposterior axis, which crosses the anteroseptal commissure. The resulting complex motion of this commissure is influenced by the surrounding anatomical structures: the posteromedial trigone is resistant and plays the role of a hinge, the membranous septum is a weak structure, and the septal annulus is the less mobile annular segment. The tricuspid Physio ring has been designed to follow these 3D deformations.

#### Commissural Leaflets, Papillary Digits, and Conduction Variations

> **DA:** What is the implication of tricuspid commissural leaflets?
>
> **AC:** Commissural leaflets are always present with a variable amount of tissue. They may be small and difficult to identify but there is always some leaflet tissue to achieve a connection between two adjacent leaflets so that the coaptation zone never reaches the annulus. A practical implication is that a commissurotomy should never be extended to the annulus.

> **FF:** What are the differences between the mitral and tricuspid valves regarding the attachment of the papillary muscles on the ventricle?
>
> **AC:** The ventricular attachment of the papillary muscles of the tricuspid valve frequently presents several digits, which are parts of the complex muscular bands of the right ventricle. These finger-like structures are not as strong as those found in the attachment of the papillary muscles of the mitral valve, explaining the higher frequency of tricuspid valve papillary muscle detachment following blunt chest trauma.

> **DA:** Can you describe the variations of the conduction system?
>
> **AC:** In normal hearts, the main variation is the relationship between the bundle of His and the membranous septum. The bundle may perforate or surround caudally the membranous septum. Multiple variations result from abnormal accessory pathways and/or abnormal anatomy of the interventricular septum, particularly the various types of atrioventricular canal defects.
