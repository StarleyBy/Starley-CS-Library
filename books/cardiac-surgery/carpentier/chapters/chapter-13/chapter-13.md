# 13. Techniques in Type IIIa: Diastolic Restricted Leaflet Motion

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical Spectrum and Pathological Anatomy
* **Etiology:** The vast majority of valvular lesions with diastolic restricted leaflet motion (Type IIIa) have a rheumatic origin.
* **Pathological Components:** Restriction results from commissural fusion, leaflet thickening, chordal fibrosis and shortening, and secondary calcification.
* **Clinical Presentations:** Manifests as pure mitral stenosis, pure mitral regurgitation, or combined stenosis and regurgitation.
* **Commissural Fusion Grades:**
  * *Grade I:* Partial commissural fusion with preservation of commissural chordae.
  * *Grade II:* Complete commissural fusion with visible delineation between anterior and posterior leaflets.
  * *Grade III:* Complete commissural fusion with obliteration of anatomical delineation.

### Surgical Strategy for Mitral Stenosis
* **Commissurotomy:** Incision initiated 5 mm from the annulus toward the orifice, preserving a 5-mm peripheral tissue bridge near the annulus to prevent commissural regurgitation.
* **Subvalvular Mobilization:**
  * Resection of thickened, retracted secondary chordae.
  * Fenestration of fused marginal chordae by removing triangular fibrous tissue wedges.
  * Splitting of the papillary muscle down to the ventricular wall until a subcommissural orifice admitting a cardiotomy sucker is created.
  * *4-mm Chordal Rule:* At least one marginal chord must be preserved every 4 mm along the free edge.
* **Annular Geometry and Ring Annuloplasty:**
  * Normal kidney-shaped annulus with preserved function: Commissurotomy alone corrects stenosis without causing regurgitation.
  * Dilated/circular annulus: Commissurotomy releases centrifugal opposing forces and induces severe regurgitation; a remodeling ring annuloplasty is mandatory. Minimum ring size: 30 mm in females, 32 mm in males.

### Surgical Strategy for Type IIIa Regurgitation and Leaflet Augmentation
* **Feasibility Framework ([Table 13-1](#table-13-01)):** Governed by anterior leaflet pliability/surface area and the severity of subvalvular fibrosis/calcification.
* **Associated A2 Prolapse:** Prolapse of the free edge of A2 caused by posterior or lateral displacement of paramedial chordae is corrected by chordal repositioning and re-anchoring with 5-0 monofilament sutures.
* **Autologous Pericardial Leaflet Extension:**
  * *Preparation:* Glutaraldehyde treatment (0.6% buffered solution for 10 minutes followed by a 5-minute saline rinse).
  * *Posterior Leaflet Extension:* Transverse incision 5 mm from the annulus; patch tailored to restore a posterior leaflet height of 15 to 20 mm (+2 mm suture margin); continuous 4-0 suture to annulus and 5-0 suture to leaflet edge. Enables sizing a ring one size larger.
  * *Anterior Leaflet Extension:* Vertical or transverse incisional patch augmentation with localized calcification debridement.
* **Remodeling Annuloplasty Philosophy:** "The larger the ring, the better the long-term result." Sizing targets: 32 to 34 mm in males, 30 to 32 mm in females, 28 to 30 mm in children. Classic ring preferred in rheumatic pathology.
* **Saline Testing and Residual Leak Correction:**
  * Commissural leak: Magic stitch or secondary chordal fixation.
  * Indentation leak: Direct 5-0 suture or triangular pericardial patch.
  * A2 prolapse: Secondary chordal transposition or artificial chordae.

## VALVE STENOSIS</summary><div class="details-content">

The great majority of valvular diseases with diastolic-restricted leaflet motion have a rheumatic origin. The limitation of the motion of the leaflets is due to commissural fusion, leaflet thickening, chordae fibrosis, and occasionally calcifications.

These lesions produce either pure mitral valve stenosis or mitral regurgitation or combined valve stenosis and regurgitation.

#### Clinical Context and Evolution of Surgical Dilatation

Mitral valve stenosis was the first mitral valve dysfunction to be treated surgically by closed commissurotomy. Open commissurotomy was implemented when extracorporeal circulation became available.

Today, most patients with mitral valve stenosis are treated by percutaneous balloon dilatation or a transcutaneous mechanical dilator.

Surgeons, however, may have to treat this condition if the valve stenosis is not amenable to balloon dilatation, if there is a combined stenosis and regurgitation, or if a previous valve dilatation has failed.

#### Pathological Grades of Commissural Fusion

In valve stenosis, the fibrotic lesions are predominant at the commissures and in the zone of leaflet coaptation. The fused commissures are categorized according to three grades of increasing severity ([Fig. 13-1](#fig-13-01)).

Grade I is a partial fusion of the commissures with preservation of commissural chordae. Grade II is a complete fusion of the commissures with a visible delineation between the anterior and the posterior leaflets. Grade III is a complete fusion of one or two commissures with no visible delineation between the anterior and posterior leaflets.

The fusion of the commissures generates turbulence, the main cause of platelet-fibrin deposits and ultimately calcification. The underlying subvalvular apparatus is fibrotic and sometimes retracted to the point that the head of the corresponding papillary muscle is adherent to the commissure.

**FIGURE 13-1** Three grades of commissural fusion in rheumatic mitral valve stenosis: Grade I partial fusion with preserved commissural chordae (left); Grade II complete fusion with visible delineation between leaflets (center); Grade III complete fusion with total obliteration of anatomical delineation (right).

#### Annular Morphology in Mitral Stenosis

Two types of mitral valve stenosis can be observed:
1. Those with a normal systolic kidney-shaped annulus;
2. Those with a dilated and circular annulus.

The annulus fibrosus usually has a normal kidney shape during systole with the anteroposterior diameter smaller than the transverse diameter ([Fig. 13-2](#fig-13-02), a). In this case, a commissurotomy does not produce a regurgitation (b).

Occasionally the annulus is dilated, with the anteroposterior diameter equal to or greater than the transverse diameter (c). In this case, a commissurotomy may lead to further annular dilatation by releasing the opposing resistance caused by the centrifugal forces of the stenotic valvular orifice (d).

<span id="fig-13-02"></span>![](_page_144_Figure_13-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-2** Annular geometry in mitral valve stenosis: normal kidney-shaped annulus where commissurotomy relieves stenosis without regurgitation (a, b); dilated circular annulus where commissurotomy unmasks or exacerbates severe regurgitation requiring prosthetic ring stabilization (c, d).

#### Commissural Fusion: Commissurotomy

Commissural fusion is treated by commissurotomy ([Fig. 13-3](#fig-13-03)). To avoid commissural leakage, the technique should preserve a 5 mm long segment of valvular tissue near the annulus equivalent to the commissural leaflet.

The main difficulty in performing a commissurotomy is the identification of a commissural line--the delineation between the anterior and the posterior leaflets. This is relatively easy in grade I and grade II commissural fusion but more difficult whenever the line of commissurotomy is not apparent, as in grade III fusion.

In this case, traction on the main chordae of the anterior leaflet opposite to the commissure creates a furrow, which delineates the limit between the anterior and the posterior leaflets (a).

#### Incision and Chordal Exposure

Using a knife with a sharp triangular blade, the incision begins 5 mm from the annulus and proceeds towards the mitral valve orifice (b). At 5 mm from the orifice, the incision is interrupted.

The commissural chordae are then identified and exposed with a nerve hook or a right angle clamp passed around the corresponding papillary muscle (c). The incision is then completed, leaving at least one chordae on each margin of the commissural opening (d).

#### Papillary Splitting and Fenestration

The corresponding papillary muscle is split, leaving a little more thickness on the anterior leaflet side than on the posterior leaflet side. The splitting of the papillary muscle is extended downward until a subcommissural orifice is created. This orifice should be large enough to admit the extremity of a cardiotomy sucker (e).

Whenever possible, the splitting of chordae or papillary muscle is completed by a "fenestration," that is, removal of a triangular piece of tissue to avoid early refusion (f, g).

<span id="fig-13-03"></span>![](_page_145_Figure_13-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-3** Surgical technique of open commissurotomy: identification of commissural furrow under chordal traction (a); incising leaflet tissue beginning 5 mm from annulus (b); exposing underlying commissural chordae (c); completing incision leaving chordal support on both margins (d); splitting papillary muscle to admit cardiotomy sucker (e); chordal and papillary fenestration excising triangular fibrous wedges to prevent refusion (f, g).

#### Subvalvular Lesions: Chordae Resection and Fenestration

Once completed, the commissurotomy provides enhanced access to the subvalvular apparatus.

Leaflet mobilization is completed by resecting thickened secondary chordae and by splitting or "fenestrating" fused marginal chordae ([Fig. 13-4](#fig-13-04), a).

#### Secondary Chordae Resection Technique

The secondary chordae to be resected are identified with a nerve hook (b). Their attachment to the papillary muscle is cut first. The attachment to the leaflet is then exposed by gentle traction and also cut (inset).

During this maneuver, care should be taken to avoid cutting an adjacent marginal chordae inadvertently and to avoid excess traction, which may lead to leaflet perforation. If a defect is made in the leaflet tissue, it is closed by direct suture or occasionally a small piece of autologous pericardium.

#### The 4-mm Marginal Chordae Preservation Rule

All thickened chordae can be resected without hesitation provided that at least one marginal chordae is preserved every 4 mm along the leaflet free edge.

Whenever the distance between two marginal chordae is greater than 4 mm, a secondary chordae of appropriate length should be transposed to secure the free edge of the leaflet.

Fused marginal chordae participating in the subvalvular obstruction are not cut, but "fenestrated" by removing a triangular piece of fibrous tissue and splitting the corresponding papillary muscle.

<span id="fig-13-04"></span>![](_page_146_Figure_13-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-4** Subvalvular chordal management: mobilization of subvalvular apparatus via secondary chordae resection and marginal chordae fenestration (a); nerve hook isolation of secondary chordae (b); division of chordal origin at papillary muscle and insertion at leaflet base (inset).

#### Annular Deformation: Ring Annuloplasty

In mitral valve stenosis, a ring annuloplasty is not necessary whenever the ventricular function is preserved, the atrial enlargement is moderate, and the mitral orifice has a normal ovoid kidney shape with no significant annular dilatation ([Fig. 13-5](#fig-13-05), a).

In this condition, a commissurotomy usually corrects the mitral stenosis without producing regurgitation (b, c).

In contrast, in the setting of annular dilatation (d), a commissurotomy may lead to further annular dilatation and lack of leaflet coaptation (e). This mechanism explains, at least in part, the mitral valve regurgitation sometimes seen after a successful commissurotomy.

#### Prosthetic Ring Sizing in Stenosis

A remodeling ring annuloplasty is therefore mandatory in mitral valve stenosis with a dilated annulus and severely dilated left atrium to avoid post-commissurotomy regurgitation (f).

A "Classic ring" specifically designed for rheumatic valvular disease is preferred but a Physio ring can also be used if the height of the anterior leaflet fits that of the sizer preselected according to the intercommissural distance.

Rings smaller size than 30 mm in females and 32 mm in males should be avoided. In valve stenosis with annular dilatation, a commissurotomy may result in valve regurgitation, and a prosthetic ring is mandatory to restore adequate surface of coaptation.

<span id="fig-13-05"></span>![](_page_147_Figure_13-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-5** Annuloplasty indications in mitral stenosis: normal annular shape maintaining competency post-commissurotomy (a, b, c); dilated annulus progressing to severe regurgitation following relief of stenosis (d, e); mandatory stabilization with remodeling annuloplasty ring (f).

## VALVE REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Type IIIa valve regurgitation, whether or not associated with some degree of stenosis, presents specific lesions involving each component of the valve. These complex lesions raise the question of the feasibility of valve reconstruction.

Although preoperative echocardiography allows some determination of a possible treatment plan, the decision is actually "l'affaire du chirurgien" based on a careful analysis of the valve during the operation ([Table 13-1](#table-13-01)).

Two key features have a direct impact on this decision: (1) the surface area and pliability of the anterior leaflet and (2) the severity of the subvalvular lesions.

#### Reconstructive Feasibility Matrix

The decision to proceed with repair versus replacement in Type IIIa dysfunction is structured by the anatomical matrix of leaflet pliability versus subvalvular compromise ([Table 13-1](#table-13-01)).

<span id="table-13-01"></span>

| Leaflet Pathology / Morphology | Minimal Subvalvular Lesions | Severe Subvalvular Lesions and Calcifications |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pliable and Large Leaflets** | Repair feasible | Probably not feasible |
| **Poorly Pliable Leaflets** | Probably feasible (with leaflet extension) | Not feasible |

**TABLE 13-1** Feasibility of Valve Reconstruction in Diastolic Restricted Leaflet Motion.

#### Anatomical Components of Type IIIa Regurgitation

The most common valvular lesions in type IIIa are annular dilatation, commissural fusion, leaflet thickening, and chordae thickening and fusion ([Fig. 13-6](#fig-13-06)).

In addition, a rarely described, but frequently associated dysfunction is a minor prolapse of the free edge of A2 resulting from the free edge distension and posterolateral displacement of the paramedial chordae (c).

In most instances the free edge of A2 near the midline is deprived of chordal support. Guided by the valve analysis and applying the "one lesion one technique principle," each specific lesion is addressed.

<span id="fig-13-06"></span>![](_page_148_Figure_13-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-6** Pathological anatomy of Type IIIa regurgitation: annular dilatation, commissural fusion, leaflet thickening, and chordal fusion (a, b); associated minor prolapse of A2 caused by distension and posterolateral displacement of paramedial chordae depriving midline margin of support (c).

#### Leaflet-Restricted Motion: Leaflet Mobilization

Leaflet mobilization in type IIIa regurgitation is carried out by commissurotomy and management of subvalvular lesions as described in [Figures 13-3](#fig-13-03) and [13-4](#fig-13-04).

Chordae resection should preserve at least one chordae every 4 mm at the leaflet margin. Leaflet extension facilitates leaflet mobility and allows implantation of a larger ring.

#### Associated A2 Prolapse: Chordae Repositioning

Whenever an associated A2 prolapse is present, the technique to be used depends upon the mechanism of the prolapse ([Fig. 13-7](#fig-13-07)).

Prolapse resulting from a predominant posterior displacement of the paramedial chordae (a) is corrected by securing the leaflet free edge to the displaced chordae using one or two 5-0 monofilament sutures (b).

This maneuver may require a limited wedge resection of fibrous tissue at the attachment of the chordae to facilitate the mobility of the leaflet (c).

#### Management of Lateral Chordal Displacement

Leaflet prolapse resulting from a predominant lateral displacement of the paramedial chordae (d) is treated by chordae repositioning.

This is achieved by detaching the chordae from the leaflet (e) followed by reattachment to the leaflet free edge using 5-0 monofilament sutures (f) near the midline.

Precise chordal re-anchoring restores uniform edge tension across the entire coaptation length of A2.

<span id="fig-13-07"></span>![](_page_149_Figure_13-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-7** Correction of associated A2 prolapse: posterior displacement of paramedial chordae (a) treated by free margin tacking with 5-0 sutures (b) and localized wedge resection of fibrotic tissue (c); lateral displacement of paramedial chordae (d) managed by detachment (e) and medial transposition to free edge (f).

#### Leaflet Thickening, Retraction, and Calcification: Leaflet Extension

Leaflet extension was proposed as early as 1954 by Shumway and Lewis and first used clinically by Frater in 1965. Glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium patch extension was introduced in 1980 by Carpentier and colleagues.

Leaflet extension is used whenever secondary chordae resection and marginal chordae fenestration are insufficient to adequately mobilize the leaflets.

An appropriately sized patch compensates for the lack of tissue and improves leaflet pliability. In addition, it makes it possible to use a prosthetic ring that is one to two sizes larger. Depending upon the type and extent of the lesions, patch extension may involve the posterior or the anterior leaflet.

#### Pericardial Patch Extension of the Posterior Leaflet

Pericardial patch extension of the posterior leaflet is carried out after incising the leaflet transversally 5 mm from and parallel to the annulus ([Fig. 13-8](#fig-13-08)). The extent of the incision depends upon the extent of leaflet retraction (a).

Taking advantage of the exposure of the ventricular side of the leaflet, all secondary chordae are resected, carefully preserving the marginal chordae (b).

A semilunar autologous pericardial patch is treated for 10 minutes in 0.6% buffered glutaraldehyde and then thoroughly rinsed in saline for 5 minutes.

#### Patch Tailoring and Suture Technique

The height of the patch (c) is calculated to provide a reconstructed posterior leaflet, which has a height of 15 to 20 mm (d). When tailoring the patch, it is important to add a circumferential 2-mm margin for suturing.

Starting from the midline towards the commissures, the patch is sutured to the annulus using a 4-0 monofilament continuous suture and to the leaflet tissue using a 5-0 monofilament continuous suture (d).

The use of locked bites on the leaflet side is helpful to avoid the purse-string effect. A ring is selected that is one size larger than the surface area of the anterior leaflet (e, f) to take advantage of the posterior leaflet extension.

<span id="fig-13-08"></span>![](_page_150_Figure_13-8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-8** Pericardial patch extension of posterior leaflet: transverse incision 5 mm from annulus (a); exposure and resection of subvalvular secondary chordae (b); tailoring of glutaraldehyde-treated semilunar pericardial patch with 2-mm seam allowance (c); continuous suture fixation with 4-0 to annulus and 5-0 locked suture to leaflet edge, restoring 15 to 20 mm posterior leaflet height (d); selection and implantation of prosthetic ring one size larger than anterior leaflet sizer (e, f).

#### Pericardial Patch Extension of the Anterior Leaflet

Pericardial patch extension of the anterior leaflet ([Fig. 13-9](#fig-13-09)) is carried out similarly to that of the posterior leaflet; however, the anterior leaflet is incised vertically (a) or transversally (b) depending upon the anterior leaflet geometry.

The spontaneous opening created by the incision gives a useful indication about the size and the shape of the patch to be used.

In case of localized leaflet calcification (c), a resection of the calcified area can be performed as part of the leaflet augmentation. A ring is selected according to the surface area of the reconstructed leaflet (d, e).

<span id="fig-13-09"></span>![](_page_151_Figure_13-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-9** Pericardial patch extension of anterior leaflet: vertical incision for asymmetric transverse restriction (a); transverse incision for symmetric longitudinal restriction (b); localized decalcification with patch reconstruction (c); annuloplasty ring selection matched to expanded anterior leaflet dimensions (d, e).

## REMODELING ANNULOPLASTY</summary><div class="details-content">

A large orifice area and optimal leaflet pliability are essential to achieve long-term function.

A ring should be implanted even if the valve appears competent after leaflet mobilization or extension; this is because the annulus has been affected by the disease and therefore must be corrected to prevent further annular dilatation ([Fig. 13-10](#fig-13-10)).

Ring selection is based on the measurement of the anterior leaflet. Whenever the surgeon is indecisive between two sizes, he or she should choose the larger size, particularly if a Classic ring is used.

#### Ring Sizing Rules with Leaflet Augmentation

As already stated, whenever the posterior leaflet has been augmented (a), the ring selected should be one size larger than the sizer that covers the surface area of the anterior leaflet (b).

Whenever the anterior leaflet has been augmented by patch enlargement (c), the ring selected should be the same size as the sizer that covers the entire anterior leaflet including the patch (d).

The optimal ring sizes are 32 to 34 mm in males, 30 to 32 mm in females, and 28 to 30 mm in children. Smaller "pediatric" rings can be used in children with congenital malformations with pliable leaflets. The larger the ring, the better the long-term result, and the liberal use of leaflet extension enables the surgeon to respect this rule.

<span id="fig-13-10"></span>![](_page_152_Figure_13-10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-10** Annuloplasty ring sizing principles in Type IIIa: posterior leaflet augmentation permitting selection of a ring one size larger than anterior leaflet template (a, b); anterior leaflet patch enlargement requiring ring matched to expanded total anterior area (c, d).

## SALINE TEST: MANAGEMENT OF RESIDUAL LEAKS</summary><div class="details-content">

After the repair has been completed and the ring lowered into position, a saline test is performed to assess leaflet pliability and to observe occasional residual leaks ([Fig. 13-11](#fig-13-11)).

Using a bulb syringe, leaflet pliability is assessed by a jet of saline directed towards the anterior leaflet (a).

If the leaflet easily opens, the pliability is judged satisfactory. If the leaflet remains partially closed (b), and the post-bypass echocardiography suggests significant residual stenosis, a valve replacement should be strongly considered.

<span id="fig-13-11"></span>![](_page_152_Figure_13-11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-11** Intraoperative saline testing of leaflet pliability: pressurized saline jet testing anterior leaflet opening mobility (a); failure of opening indicating severe persistent tissue rigidity predisposing to valve replacement (b).

#### Analysis of Residual Leak Patterns

Residual leaks must be carefully analyzed. Three localizations and mechanisms are common ([Fig. 13-12](#fig-13-12)):
1. Commissural leak;
2. Indentation leak;
3. A2 prolapse.

#### Correction of Commissural and Indentation Leaks

A commissural leak may be observed whenever the commissurotomy has been extended too far towards the annulus or whenever the slightly rigid edges of the commissure do not coapt properly (a). The leak is corrected by augmenting the surface of coaptation either by securing the commissural edges to secondary chordae (inset 1) and/or by employing the "magic suture technique" (inset 2).

An enlarged or retracted indentation may be another cause of residual leak (b). After resecting the chordae attached to the edges of the indentation so as to mobilize the tissue, a 5-0 suture is used to close the gap and eliminate the leak (inset 1).

If the edges of the indentation are too rigid and the indentation is too large, a small triangular pericardial patch is used after resection of thickened tissues (inset 2).

#### Identification and Repair of Unmasked A2 Prolapse

A minor A2 prolapse may have been unrecognized at the mitral valve analysis. After valve reconstruction, this dysfunction is identified by noting the direction of the regurgitant jet (c) and/or by comparing the level of the free edge of A2 and P2 with nerve hooks (inset 1).

It is preferably corrected by secondary chordae transposition (inset 2) or by securement of artificial chordae to the edge of A2.

<span id="fig-13-12"></span>![](_page_153_Figure_13-12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 13-12** Troubleshooting residual leaks: commissural leak (a) corrected by chordal hitching (inset 1) or magic stitch (inset 2); indentation leak (b) repaired by direct closure (inset 1) or triangular pericardial patch (inset 2); unmasked A2 prolapse (c) diagnosed by level comparison (inset 1) and treated by chordal transposition or replacement (inset 2).

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Management of Commissural Calcification

**FF: What is your repair strategy when calcifications involve the commissural area?**
> **AC:** Localized and superficial platelet and calcium deposits in the commissure do not preclude a commissurotomy. However, extensive and deep calcium formation in the commissural area is a contraindication to valve reconstruction. Attempts to decalcify these lesions have been associated with disappointing results in my experience. Because of the deep penetration of calcium within the leaflet tissue, residual regurgitation is frequent and progressive. The repair does not last as long as a bioprosthetic valve replacement, the alternative nonthrombogenic valve operation.

**FF: If a commissurotomy appears to be placed in the wrong direction, what do you do?**
> **AC:** From time to time, after full leaflet mobilization it is possible to observe that the new commissure is not ideally placed. In this condition, it is preferable to redo a commissurotomy in the right location and to close the first incision with interrupted sutures.

#### Subvalvular Management and Papillary Splitting

**DA: Do you routinely resect all secondary chordae in type IIIa dysfunction?**
> **AC:** I resect those that are thickened, shortened, or fused; this includes most of the chordae in rheumatic valvular disease.

**FF: When splitting a papillary muscle to release a subvalvular stenosis, you often leave more papillary muscle tissue on the anterior leaflet side than on the posterior leaflet side. What is the rationale?**
> **AC:** This takes into consideration the fact that the load on the anterior leaflet is significantly greater than that on the posterior leaflet. If the papillary muscle does not seem to be large enough, it can be split into two halves of equal thickness.

#### Leaflet Extension Mechanics and SAM Prevention

**FF: Is there a risk of systolic anterior motion (SAM) after posterior leaflet patch extension?**
> **AC:** I have never seen an SAM after posterior leaflet patch extension in rheumatic valvular disease; this is probably due to the fact the height of the anterior leaflet is reduced by the rheumatic process and the leaflet tissue is less pliable. The height of the expanded posterior leaflet can be larger than the normal 15 mm but should not exceed 20 mm.

**DA: What is the most common cause of suboptimal early results in type IIIa dysfunction?**
> **AC:** The most common cause is postrepair valve stenosis, which is facilitated by two factors: the first is the residual rigidity of the leaflet tissue despite leaflet mobilization; the other is a ring size that is too small. Since our impact on residual rigidity is limited, one should maximize the orifice area by selecting a ring as large as possible and by generous use of leaflet extension.

**FF: What maximum height of patch extension do you recommend?**
> **AC:** The total height of the extended leaflet should not exceed 20 mm. It is wrong to believe that a larger patch will enhance the mobility of the leaflet. It is exactly the contrary. A patch that is too large may produce a redundant immobile posterior leaflet that partially obstructs the valvular orifice.

#### Prosthetic Ring Selection: Classic vs. Physio

**DA: What are the specific indications of the Classic versus Physio ring in type IIIa dysfunction?**
> **AC:** The respective selection is based on the morphology and measurement of the anterior leaflet. Often in rheumatic disease, the height of the anterior leaflet is slightly reduced compared to normal. In this case, the Classic ring would be preferred because it has been specifically designed to accommodate this feature. In most instances, however, a normal relationship is found between the height and the transverse dimension of the anterior leaflet. In this circumstance, a Physio ring is preferred.

#### Management of Postrepair Residual Regurgitation

**FF: Does the age of the patient impact your approach to treat postrepair residual mitral regurgitation in the setting of type IIIa dysfunction?**
> **AC:** In general, a residual regurgitation should lead to an intraoperative analysis. In most instances, one of the simple additional procedures described in this chapter solves the regurgitation. If this cannot be achieved, the question arises whether it would be preferable to immediately replace the valve. My clinical experience has shown that a mild residual regurgitation can be tolerated for a long time whenever a ring has been implanted to remodel and stabilize the annulus. In contrast, a moderate residual regurgitation has the tendency to get worse. It is therefore preferable to replace the valve. The age of the patient does not influence this decision.

#### Etiology and Disease Evolutivity

**DA: Does the etiology of the valvular disease--such as rheumatic, lupus, or irradiation--alter your approach in type IIIa dysfunction?**
> **AC:** The etiology certainly influences the indications. For each etiology, two important factors should be considered: the severity of the lesions and the evolutivity of the disease. By "evolutivity," I mean the pace of the progression of the disease as determined by a careful assessment of the evolution at regular intervals. In the settings of acute rheumatic valvular disease, lupus, or irradiation, the valvular lesions and their evolutivity may suggest valve replacement as the first choice. However, I have used valve reconstruction to successfully treat patients with favorable lesions, in particular preserved mobility of the anterior leaflet. The evolutivity of the disease under appropriate medical treatment is the key factor in the decision making for the choice of the surgical procedure. If the evolutivity is well controlled by medical treatment and the lesions favorable, I tend to reconstruct the valve, particularly in young patients. Otherwise, I replace it. In all instances, I take the patient's opinion as my first consideration, because of the increased risk of long-term reoperation versus anticoagulation in these settings.
