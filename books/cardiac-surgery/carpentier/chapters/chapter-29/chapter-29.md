# Chapter 29. Ischemic Valvular Disease

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Pathophysiological Paradigm and Mechanism of Regurgitation
Ischemic valvular disease defines secondary valve regurgitation caused by ventricular ischemia or myocardial infarction, strictly excluding preexisting organic valvular disease in coronary patients. Although the leaflet tissue initially appears histologically preserved, referring to this condition as purely "functional regurgitation" is clinically misleading, as it erroneously implies reversibility without surgery and overlooks the presence of severe organic remodeling within the subvalvular apparatus, annulus, and left ventricular myocardium:
1. **Myocardial Etiology:** Ischemic mitral regurgitation (IMR) is fundamentally a ventricular myocardial disease producing secondary valvular incompetence. Regurgitation is driven by localized or global ventricular wall motion abnormalities, progressive chamber dilatation, and displacement of papillary muscles away from the annulus.
2. **Failure of Papillary Muscle Theory:** Landmark experimental sheep studies in the 1980-s by Alain Carpentier proved that isolated formaldehyde infiltration into the posterior papillary muscle alone failed to produce mitral regurgitation ([Fig. 29-1](#fig-29-01)). Regurgitation required extending the infiltration to the adjacent ventricular wall, establishing that papillary muscle displacement secondary to regional ventricular dyssynergy—rather than isolated muscular paralysis—is the primary mechanism of ischemic regurgitation.

### Functional Classification Spectrum
Ischemic mitral regurgitation spans three distinct functional types under Carpentier's classification:
* **Type I (Normal Leaflet Motion, < 10%):** Incomplete leaflet coaptation occurring with normal leaflet mobility, typically observed in basal myocardial infarctions. Regurgitation results from localized annular dilatation predominating in the posteromedial segments (P2, P3, and posterior commissural segments).
* **Type II (Leaflet Prolapse):** Valvular incompetence resulting from partial rupture, complete rupture, or ischemic elongation with post-infarction fibrosis of a papillary muscle. It predominantly affects the posteromedial papillary muscle due to its vulnerable, single-vessel blood supply from the posterior descending artery. Complete rupture is an acute catastrophe; partial rupture or elongation presents subacutely or chronically.
* **Type IIIb (Systolic Restricted Leaflet Motion, > 80%):** By far the most common mechanism, resulting from apical and lateral displacement of the posteromedial papillary muscle and posterolateral wall motion abnormalities. The resulting tethering severely restricts systolic coaptation of the P2, P3, and posterior commissural segments, creating a characteristic "seagull deformation" (*effet de mouette*) of the anterior leaflet. In anterior infarctions, global left ventricular dilatation causes symmetric bileaflet tethering.

### Preoperative Diagnostics and Surgical Indications
* **Echocardiography & Doppler Jet Direction:** Preoperative and intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) identifies the precise mechanism and quantifies tethering parameters (tenting area, coaptation depth). The Doppler jet orientation is pathognomonic: central in Type I and anterior MI Type IIIb; eccentric and directed opposite to the prolapsing leaflet in Type II ([Fig. 29-2](#fig-29-02)); posteriorly directed over the restricted P3 segment in posterolateral MI Type IIIb ([Fig. 29-3](#fig-29-03), [Fig. 29-4](#fig-29-04)). Anesthesia-induced afterload reduction often downgrades regurgitation severity intraoperatively.
* **Surgical Indications in Acute Regurgitation:** Severe acute ischemic MR presenting with cardiogenic shock or pulmonary edema refractory to intensive medical therapy and intraaortic balloon counterpulsation (IABP) mandates emergency surgical intervention.
* **Surgical Indications in Chronic Regurgitation:** Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) alone does not correct moderate-to-severe chronic ischemic MR. Concomitant mitral valve reconstruction is indicated in patients undergoing CABG who exhibit mild-to-severe chronic MR to prevent progressive ventricular remodeling and late mortality.

### Reconstructive Principles and Surgical Strategy
* **Sequence & Myocardial Protection:** Cardiopulmonary bypass with bicaval cannulation, antegrade/retrograde cold blood cardioplegia, and topical hypothermia. Complete coronary revascularization is performed first to prevent traumatic avulsion of bypass grafts or atrioventricular junction injury when the heart is retracted to expose the circumflex or posterior descending coronary branches.
* **Type I Repair:** Rigid or semi-rigid remodeling annuloplasty downsized by one size to restore an abundant surface of coaptation and protect against late annular dilatation.
* **Type II Repair:** Complete papillary muscle rupture generally warrants mitral valve replacement with preservation of the complete subvalvular apparatus. Partial head rupture or elongation is reconstructed by papillary muscle reimplantation into a muscular trench, PTFE artificial neo-chordae, or plication/shortening, always supported by a downsized remodeling annuloplasty.
* **Type IIIb Repair:** Implantation of an undersized remodeling prosthetic ring (downsized by 1–2 sizes, e.g., Carpentier-Edwards Physio ring or specific Carpentier-McCarthy-Adams IMR ETlogix ring) restores coaptation by shortening the anteroposterior diameter and drawing the posterior annulus toward the displaced coaptation line. When extensive dyskinetic akinesia or aneurysm is present, ventricular reconstruction (Dor/Jatene procedure) and papillary muscle relocation are combined with annuloplasty. In cases of massive bileaflet tethering or extreme LV dilatation (LVEDD > 65 mm), chordae-sparing valve replacement may be preferable.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY</summary><div class="details-content">

The term "ischemic valvular disease" defines valve regurgitation due to ventricular ischemia or ventricular infarction. This definition excludes preexisting valvular diseases in patients presenting with coronary artery disease. The leaflet tissue often appears normal. For this reason, the valve dysfunction is sometimes called "functional regurgitation," a terminology that might suggest a potential reversibility of the regurgitation (which is rarely the case) and the absence of organic lesions (which is never the case).

The regurgitation is actually the result of mitral valve lesions involving the annulus, the papillary muscle, and the configuration of the left ventricle, which are parts of the mitral valve complex. In addition, the leaflet tissue can be affected by secondary lesions in long-lasting progression of mitral regurgitation. Whatever semantic considerations it is important to describe the regurgitation using the functional classification, which serves as a guideline to valve reconstruction.

> *An ischemic valvular disease is a myocardial disease producing a valve dysfunction.*

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Type I Mitral Regurgitation (Normal Leaflet Motion)</summary><div class="details-content">

Type I mitral regurgitation refers to a lack of leaflet coaptation with normal leaflet motion. This relatively rare condition (less than 10% of ischemic mitral regurgitation cases) is seen in basal infarctions. The lack of leaflet coaptation is due to an annular dilatation predominant in the posteromedial segments of the annulus (i.e., P2, P3, and PC segments).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Type II Mitral Regurgitation (Leaflet Prolapse)</summary><div class="details-content">

Type II ischemic mitral valve regurgitation is rarely observed today. The usual cause is papillary muscle rupture or elongation. In most instances, the lesion involves the posteromedial papillary muscle, which has a more tenuous vascularization than the anterolateral papillary muscle.

Complete rupture of a papillary muscle is much less frequent nowadays than in the past because of improved management of acute myocardial infarctions and early revascularization by interventional cardiology. Today, type II ischemic mitral regurgitation is generally due to partial rupture of a papillary muscle or to papillary muscle elongation following infarction and fibrosis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Type IIIb Mitral Regurgitation (Systolic Restricted Leaflet Motion)</summary><div class="details-content">

Type IIIb mitral regurgitation is by far the most frequent valve dysfunction following ventricular ischemia or infarction. In the past, the main cause of regurgitation was thought to be "papillary muscle dysfunction." Experimental studies in our laboratory in the 1980-s using sheep demonstrated that papillary muscle dysfunction caused by formaldehyde injection of the posterior papillary was unable to produce mitral valve regurgitation ([Fig. 29-1](#fig-29-01)). A significant regurgitation could only be obtained by extending the formaldehyde infiltration to the adjacent myocardium, resulting in wall motion abnormality. This observation has now been validated as the main mechanism of ischemic mitral valve regurgitation.

<span id="fig-29-01"></span>![](images/_page_300_Figure_29-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 29-1**

It is well accepted that type IIIb ischemic mitral regurgitation results from lateral and apical displacement of the papillary muscles, mostly the posteromedial papillary muscle and its supporting ventricular wall. The resulting tethering typically involves the P2, P3, and PC leaflet segments of the mitral valve (see Fig. 14-1). A tethering can also be seen at the belly of the anterior leaflet attributable to excess traction on one or more main chordae. We use the term "effet de mouette" (seagull deformation) to describe the image of the anterior leaflet seen at echocardiography.

The severity and localization of leaflet tethering vary according to the type of myocardial infarction. In anterior myocardial infarction the dilatation is usually global with leaflet tethering involving both the anterior and the posterior leaflets. In posterolateral myocardial infarction, leaflet tethering is predominant in the P2, P3, and PC segments. There is not always a perfect correlation between the degree of papillary muscle displacement and the severity of mitral regurgitation because of variations in myocardial contractility and loading conditions.

> *Type IIIb ischemic mitral valve regurgitation results from complex changes in left ventricular configuration, papillary muscle position, and annular dimensions.*

In long-lasting type II and type IIIb ischemic regurgitation, annular dilatation is often observed as an associated lesion. The dilatation is predominant in the P3 and PC areas with a pronounced asymmetry of the annulus. Here again, the degree of deformation does not necessarily correlate with the severity of mitral regurgitation.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## CLINICAL PRESENTATION</summary><div class="details-content">

A myocardial infarction can result in the appearance of ischemic mitral valve regurgitation immediately or at a later time.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Acute Ischemic Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute ischemic mitral regurgitation results from papillary muscle rupture or ventricular motion abnormality in the setting of acute myocardial infarction. The clinical presentation varies widely depending upon the functional type and severity of mitral regurgitation and the extent of myocardial damage.

For example, a limited myocardial infarction resulting in papillary muscle necrosis and rupture (type II dysfunction) causes a severe mitral regurgitation, leading to pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock. This usually occurs within the first week after a myocardial infarction. Of note, the same time frame applies to ventricular septal rupture, making the differentiation between these two entities clinically difficult.

In septal rupture, the systolic murmur is loud and predominant at the left sternal border, whereas in ischemic mitral regurgitation the murmur is softer and predominant at the apex, without thrill. Because of the rapid equilibration of left ventricular and atrial pressures, a murmur of ischemic mitral regurgitation may not be audible. In this case echocardiography is particularly useful to make the right diagnosis. In type IIIb ischemic mitral acute regurgitation, the clinical presentation is often less dramatic. Most patients present with mild to moderate regurgitation as assessed by echocardiography.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Chronic Ischemic Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

Chronic ischemic mitral regurgitation results from progressive ventricular dilatation following myocardial infarction. Patients may have a clinical history of myocardial infarction in the absence of mitral regurgitation (or in the presence of mild regurgitation) that worsens over time. Severe ischemic mitral regurgitation can be asymptomatic or be associated with minimal symptoms for many years because the patient adapts his or her physical activity to the functional capacity of the heart.

The diagnosis is often made when the patient exhibits dyspnea on exertion, fatigue attributable to low cardiac output, or the onset of atrial fibrillation. Another common scenario is a patient presenting with acute ischemia and diagnosed with mitral regurgitation from previous silent infarction.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY AND CORONARY ANGIOGRAPHY</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Echocardiographic Assessment of Regurgitant Jet and Annular Geometry</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography allows assessment of the mechanism and severity of mitral valve regurgitation and ventricular size and function. The direction of the regurgitant jet on Doppler echocardiography gives some valuable information about the functional type of mitral regurgitation.

Type I mitral regurgitation displays a central jet. In type II, the jet is opposite to the prolapsing leaflet and papillary muscle rupture can be identified ([Fig. 29-2](#fig-29-02)).

<span id="fig-29-02"></span>![](images/_page_301_Figure_29-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 29-2**

In type IIIb, as a result of posterolateral myocardial infarction, the jet is typically directed posteriorly over the restricted P3 segment ([Fig. 29-3](#fig-29-03)). It can be a central jet, however, if there is massive annular dilatation.

<span id="fig-29-03"></span>![](images/_page_302_Figure_29-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 29-3**

In type IIIb mitral regurgitation caused by anterior myocardial infarction, both leaflets are often tethered with a central jet ([Fig. 29-4](#fig-29-04)). Echocardiography also permits identification of some of the lesions, such as papillary muscle rupture in type II, and assessment of the severity of leaflet tethering, the tenting area, and the depth of the displaced coaptation in type IIIb. Finally, quantitative measurement allows calculation of the regurgitant orifice area and volume.

<span id="fig-29-04"></span>![](images/_page_302_Figure_29-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 29-4**

The assessment of the severity of regurgitation, however, may be rendered difficult by the variations in the loading conditions. This explains why the degree of regurgitation is often downgraded by intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography caused by afterload reduction induced by general anesthesia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Coronary Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

A coronary angiography is performed in all patients. In patients with type II mitral regurgitation, an occlusion of the right coronary artery or the posterior descending branch is generally observed. Patients with type IIIb mitral regurgitation usually present with triple-vessel coronary artery disease.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SURGICAL INDICATIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Acute Ischemic Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

Severe acute ischemic mitral regurgitation requires emergency surgery in patients with cardiogenic shock and pulmonary edema who are refractory to medical therapy. In other patients, intensive medical therapy is instituted to optimize the hemodynamic status.

In patients with preserved systemic pressure, the medical regimen includes sodium nitroprusside and diuretics to reduce the regurgitant volume while concomitantly increasing the forward stroke volume. In cardiogenic shock, systemic hypotension limits the use of vasodilators. Intraaortic balloon counterpulsation is used to restore the mean arterial blood pressure and reduce the afterload while at the same time increasing the cardiac output. Another advantage of intraaortic balloon counterpulsation is that it helps avoid administration of excessive doses of inotropic agents.

Mild to moderate acute mitral regurgitation does not require urgent surgery since it may be partially or completely reversible with medical treatment and/or occasional percutaneous coronary revascularization.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Chronic Ischemic Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

In chronic ischemic mitral regurgitation, two clinical scenarios can be encountered. Some patients are referred to the surgeon for congestive heart failure resulting from moderate to severe mitral regurgitation. These patients should benefit from a valve reconstruction and a complete coronary revascularization.

Other patients who need complete coronary revascularization for symptomatic multivessel coronary disease may be diagnosed with an associated mild to moderate mitral regurgitation, raising the question of the necessity of valve repair. This condition carries an increased risk of long-term mortality and morbidity. Coronary revascularization alone does not reverse chronic mitral regurgitation. Thus patients with mild to severe mitral regurgitation should have concomitant mitral valve reconstruction and complete coronary revascularization.

Whenever preoperative risk factors suggest that the operative mortality could be prohibitive, the surgical procedure can be preceded by transcutaneous revascularization. In patients who present with a severe ventricular dysfunction, a myocardium viability evaluation by magnetic resonance imaging or thallium study is warranted, particularly in the absence of angina.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

When operating on a patient with ischemic mitral regurgitation, several conditions should be fulfilled before, during, or at the end of operation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Transesophageal Echocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Transesophageal echocardiography is an essential tool in the operating room. Before cardiopulmonary bypass, it allows confirmation of the functional type of the mitral regurgitation and assessment of myocardial contractility. After cardiopulmonary bypass, it allows the surgeon to control the result, to detect residual air, and to monitor ventricular filling.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Myocardial Protection</summary><div class="details-content">

Myocardial protection is achieved by both antegrade and retrograde blood cardioplegia, topical cooling, and mild to moderate systemic hypothermia. It is strongly recommended that two venous cannulas be used to snare the two venae cavae so that the heart is completely excluded from the circulation, thus avoiding early myocardial rewarming.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Myocardial Revascularization</summary><div class="details-content">

Myocardial revascularization should be carried out first to avoid injury to the atrio-ventricular junction by the prosthetic ring when the left ventricle is elevated to expose the circumflex artery and the posterior descending arteries.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Intraoperative Valve Analysis and Annular Geometry</summary><div class="details-content">

Valve analysis by the surgeon does confirm annular deformity if present but may not be reliable to confirm leaflet tethering in the arrested heart. The annulus is asymmetrically dilated in posterior myocardial infarction ([Fig. 29-5](#fig-29-05), a), and symmetrically dilated in anterior myocardial infarction (b).

<span id="fig-29-05"></span>![](images/_page_303_Figure_29-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 29-5**

Echocardiography is used to diagnose the presence and severity of leaflet tethering which must be taken into consideration by the surgeon. If echocardiography is not available, a saline test before repair will identify the jet of regurgitation and usually suggests restricted leaflet motion.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Left Ventricular Venting</summary><div class="details-content">

A left ventricular vent should be positioned and remain in the mitral valve until myocardial contractility has recovered. This is particularly important in the setting of aortic regurgitation or poor ventricular function.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SURGICAL TECHNIQUES</summary><div class="details-content">

Several techniques are applied depending upon the type of valve dysfunction and lesions.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Type I Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

Type I mitral regurgitation is treated by a remodeling annuloplasty, which is extensively described in Figs. 8-5 to 8-7 and in Figures 14-3 to 14-5. Three specific recommendations should be considered.

Downsizing the ring by one size, despite the absence of leaflet tethering, is recommended, taking into consideration a possible progression of recurrent regurgitation. In severe annular dilation, an overlapping or a double row suture technique should be employed to minimize the risk of ring dehiscence. In asymmetrical dilation, the midline of P2, not the midline of the posterior circumference, should correspond to the midline of the ring (see Figure 8-21).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Type II Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Complete Rupture of a Papillary Muscle</summary><div class="details-content">

Complete rupture of a papillary muscle usually requires a valve replacement with preservation of the subvalvular apparatus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rupture of One Head of a Papillary Muscle</summary><div class="details-content">

Such a rupture is associated with much less dramatic clinical manifestations, and is often surgically addressed during the chronic phase. Two alternative techniques are possible. A papillary muscle reimplantation can be attempted, provided that the supporting muscular wall has an acceptable contractility with minimal ventricular dilatation of the corresponding area (Fig. 10-15). Another alternative is to use artificial chordae to correct the prolapse. In all cases, it is recommended that a downsized remodeling ring annuloplasty be performed to reduce the tension on the reconstructed valve.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Partial Rupture at the Papillary Muscle Base</summary><div class="details-content">

In this type of lesion, one or two muscular bands attaching the base of the papillary muscle are ruptured because of myocardial necrosis. The resulting elongation produces a mild to moderate mitral regurgitation. It is preferable to wait several weeks before surgery, the time necessary for fibrotic transformation of the lesions. A technique similar to that described for papillary muscle rupture can be used with the difference that the base of the papillary muscle is preserved and buried into the trench.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Elongated Papillary Muscle</summary><div class="details-content">

Following a papillary muscle infarction, the healing process may induce complete fibrotic transformation with elongation leading to leaflet prolapse. In this setting, a plication of the papillary muscle or a resection of the appropriate extra length and reconstitution of the continuity of the remaining segments can be carried out (Fig. 10-16).

Although all of these techniques have been used successfully in many cases in our experience, it must be emphasized that a valve replacement may be preferable if the anatomical conditions are not favorable.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Type IIIb Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

In type IIIb mitral regurgitation, the techniques employed must address the lack of coaptation caused by leaflet tethering following papillary muscle displacement. The use of a downsized remodeling annuloplasty or an "ischemic ring" is mandatory to restore a reasonable surface of coaptation (see Chapter 14).

This technique, however, may be insufficient for the repair of a mitral valve regurgitation associated with a large dyskinetic plaque or an aneurysm. In this situation, the plaque or the aneurysm should be resected and the usually fibrotic papillary muscle is reimplanted within the suture line, which approximates the edges of the resection (Fig. 14-6). In the presence of severe bileaflet restricted motion, or left ventricular end diastolic dimensions greater than 65 mm, a chordae sparing valve replacement may be preferable.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Limits of Remodeling Annuloplasty and Adjunct Myocardioplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

Although some reverse remodeling of the base of the ventricle may be obtained by an undersized prosthetic ring annuloplasty, the ventricular chamber remains dilated and the papillary muscles displaced. Despite complete myocardial revascularization, the process of ventricular dilatation may progress, leading to more pronounced leaflet tethering and subsequent recurrent mitral regurgitation after several months or years of good valvular function.

Several techniques have been proposed to address this problem, including "papillary muscle slings" to approximate the two papillary muscles and myocardial contention. We have used the latter technique with promising results.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Early Operative Mortality</summary><div class="details-content">

The outcome of the surgical procedure in ischemic mitral regurgitation depends upon the acuity of the presentation, the mechanism of the regurgitation, and the underlined ventricular function.

In patients with type II dysfunction who undergo an emergent operation, the improvement in operative techniques and perioperative management have contributed in decreasing the operative mortality to 10% to 15%. In patients with type I and type IIIb dysfunction, an operative mortality of less than 5% can be expected following coronary revascularization and mitral valve remodeling annuloplasty.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Long-Term Survival</summary><div class="details-content">

The long-term survival depends upon the extent of the ischemic lesions. In type II regurgitation, the results are better than in type IIIb dysfunction because of the fact that type II mitral regurgitation usually presents with a more limited size of myocardial infarction.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Restricted Motion of the Anterior Leaflet (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: The most common mechanism in ischemic mitral regurgitation is restricted motion of the posterior leaflet. How often do you observe restricted motion of the anterior leaflet?

AC: In most cases of ischemic mitral regurgitation, although restricted leaflet motion is predominant in the P3 region, there may be a certain amount of restricted motion in the A3 and PC segments, particularly when severe lateral wall dysfunction is present. This condition can be treated with an Ischemic ring annuloplasty or with a downsized Physio ring. In the less frequent case of a large anterior infarction, a global remodeling may occur that restricts the leaflet motion of all leaflet segments.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Type II Dysfunction with Papillary Muscle Elongation (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: How often do you observe a type II dysfunction with papillary muscle elongation?

AC: In the prethrombolytic era, it was not uncommon to see papillary muscle elongation, particularly in the setting of a large lateral wall infarction. This lesion is rarely observed today because of early interventional revascularization. I occasionally see it in children with congenital coronary anomalies.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Repair of Mild to Moderate Type IIIb MR at Time of CABG (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Do you repair mild to moderate type IIIb ischemic mitral regurgitation at the time of coronary artery revascularization?

AC: Previous studies including yours have demonstrated that revascularization alone does not correct chronic mitral regurgitation. Additional reported clinical studies in the literature have shown the negative impact of mitral regurgitation, even mild, on long-term survival. Therefore, in the absence of a randomized trial, I still recommend that mitral regurgitation be corrected at the time of the revascularization, provided that the surgeon is experienced in mitral valve reconstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Valve Replacement with Subvalvular Preservation vs. Repair (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Some surgeons prefer to perform a valve replacement with preservation of the subvalvular apparatus instead of a valve repair, considering that the risk of residual or recurrent mitral regurgitation is eliminated with this technique. What do you think about this approach?

AC: Since there have been limited studies addressing this issue, I can only say the following: if the lesions are favorable for an annuloplasty, it is preferable to remodel the annulus with a downsized remodeling ring. In the setting of severe bileaflet tethering, a valve replacement may be a valuable alternative but, contrary to a reconstructed native valve, the preservation of the subvalvular tissue does not provide an active opening for the artificial valve the cause of a less favorable hemodynamics.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Closure of P2-P3 Indentation in Type IIIb MR (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Should a P2-P3 indentation be closed in type IIIb ischemic mitral regurgitation?

AC: Considering the importance of indentations in valve function, I personally do not close the P2-P3 indentation during my primary reconstruction except when the saline test shows a residual leak at this level following the remodeling annuloplasty.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Chordal Resection and Artificial Chordae for P3 Mobilization (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: In the aim of mobilizing P3, some advocate the resection of primary and secondary chordae and their subsequent replacement with artificial chordae. What is your opinion of this technique?

AC: I do not use this technique. I believe it is simpler and more reliable to use an Ischemic ring or an undersized Physio ring to compensate for the lack of tissue in the P3 area.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Annuloplasty Ring Selection in Anterior Myocardial Infarction (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: Would you use a Classic ring in the setting of anterior myocardial infarction?

AC: I take into consideration the localization of the leaflet tethering. If all the leaflet segments are tethered as in most anterior myocardial infarctions, a downsized Physio ring can be used. In the rare situation of an asymmetrical tethering predominant at the P1 and anterior commissural segments, I prefer using a Classic ring with manual reduction of the curvature of the commissural portion of the ring.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Adjunct Ventricular Reconstruction (Jatene-Dor) (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### DA: When do you add a ventricular procedure to your remodeling annuloplasty in ischemic mitral regurgitation?

AC: A remodeling annuloplasty in ischemic mitral regurgitation is effective in correcting valvular regurgitation. In addition, this procedure may also facilitate reverse remodeling of the left ventricle, as shown by Robert Dion’s group. However, we should keep in mind that an ischemic mitral regurgitation is primarily a myocardial disease. Its evolution depends upon the possible development of ventricular dilatation. In the setting of a well-documented ventricular aneurysm, a Jatene-Dor procedure should be added.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Risk of Mitral Stenosis with Downsized Annuloplasty (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### FF: Can you cause mitral valve stenosis by performing a downsized annuloplasty in type IIIb dysfunction?

AC: I have never seen clinically significant mitral valve stenosis resulting from a downsized annuloplasty, probably because I rarely use rings smaller than 26 mm. It has, however, been suggested that during exercise echocardiography some patients with extremely undersized rings may develop increased transvalvular gradients. For this reason, I personally think that the ring size should be selected according to the amount of leaflet tissue, not arbitrarily.

</div></details>

</div></details>
