# Chapter 35. Immune-Mediated Valvular Diseases

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Key Principles</summary><div class="details-content">

Immune-mediated systemic diseases can produce characteristic destructive and proliferative valvular lesions, predominantly involving the aortic and mitral valves:
1. **Underlying Pathologies:** The principal systemic autoimmune conditions affecting cardiac valves include systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), primary antiphospholipid syndrome (PAPS), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and ankylosing spondylitis; scleroderma, polymyositis, and dermatomyositis are rarely implicated.
2. **Pathomorphology & Leaflet Motion:** Chronic immune-complex deposition and active or smoldering inflammation result in leaflet thickening, fibroblastic proliferation, and retraction, classically manifesting as Carpentier Type IIIa dysfunction (restricted leaflet motion during systole and diastole).
3. **Libman-Sacks Endocarditis:** Characterized by multiple, irregular, nonbacterial verrucous vegetations with heterogeneous echogenicity located on the atrial surface of the mitral valve and the aortic side of the aortic leaflets. The presence of lesions on both opposing anterior and posterior mitral leaflets ("kissing vegetations") is diagnostic ([Fig. 35-1](#fig-35-01)).
4. **Thromboembolic Hazard:** Valvular vegetations, particularly in hypercoagulable states (such as PAPS or secondary antiphospholipid syndrome), carry a substantial risk of recurrent systemic embolization (transient ischemic attacks [TIAs] and ischemic strokes).
5. **Role of Surgery:** Mild to moderate regurgitation and active vegetations are primarily managed medically with antiinflammatory and aggressive anticoagulation regimens. Reconstructive valve surgery is reserved for recurrent embolization despite medical therapy or advanced hemodynamic regurgitation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Criteria & Thresholds</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiographic Identification:**
  * *Type IIIa Dysfunction:* Symmetrical or asymmetrical leaflet thickening and retraction with restricted systolic and diastolic excursion of mitral and aortic leaflets.
  * *Libman-Sacks Vegetations:* Numerous, irregularly contoured masses with heterogeneous echogenicity, without destructive valve perforation. The "kissing vegetations" sign involves both mitral leaflets simultaneously ([Fig. 35-1](#fig-35-01)).
  * *Rheumatoid Nodules:* Small, circular, well-delineated lesions with homogeneous echogenicity, distinguishing them from irregular Libman-Sacks vegetations.
  * *Aortitis in Ankylosing Spondylitis:* Direct visualization of ascending aorta and root dilation, root wall thickening/sclerosis, and cuspal thickening.
* **Histopathological Characteristics:**
  * *Active Vegetations:* Nonbacterial central core of fibrinoid necrosis and fibroblastic proliferation surrounded by mononuclear and polymorphonuclear inflammatory cells, accompanied by fibrin and platelet deposition.
  * *Healed Vegetations:* Devoid of active inflammatory cells, composed of central dense fibrosis surrounded by organized, hyalinized, or endothelialized thrombus.
  * *PAPS Lesions:* Intravalvular thrombosis, necrotic and hemorrhagic plaques, vascular proliferation, inflammatory infiltration, and progressive secondary calcification.
* **Hemodynamic Severity:** Regurgitation is graded by Doppler color flow mapping; mild to moderate regurgitation is managed conservatively, whereas severe symptomatic regurgitation necessitates surgical correction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Strategy & Decision Tree</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Indications for Surgical Intervention:**
  1. *Recurrent Thromboembolism:* Recurrence of systemic embolic events (predominantly transient ischemic attacks) despite optimal anticoagulation therapy.
  2. *Severe Hemodynamic Regurgitation:* Advanced leaflet retraction or secondary annular dilatation leading to severe symptomatic valve incompetence.
* **Mitral Valve Reconstruction in SLE / PAPS:**
  1. *Vegetation Excision:* Shave resection of healed or persistent vegetations from the atrial surface of the leaflets, preserving native leaflet substance.
  2. *Chemical Stabilization:* Application of a single drop of 0.6% glutaraldehyde to the debrided leaflet area for 1 or 2 minutes followed by saline irrigation to denature residual tissue and minimize local recurrence risk.
  3. *Leaflet Extension:* In cases with severe posterior leaflet retraction (Type IIIa dysfunction), patch extension using glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium is performed (see Chapter 19).
  4. *Subvalvular Mobilization:* Resection of thickened secondary chordae to restore leaflet mobility.
  5. *Annuloplasty:* Remodeling ring annuloplasty with a prosthetic ring to restore normal annular geometry and coaptation depth.
* **Aortic Valve Strategy:**
  * Valve repair is rarely feasible due to transmural cusp retraction and root distortion; aortic valve replacement is the standard procedure.
  * *Prosthesis Selection:* Biological tissue valves (bioprostheses) are favored over mechanical valves to avoid the high morbidity of lifelong anticoagulation in patients with systemic multi-organ disease and bleeding diatheses.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complications & Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Recurrent Thromboembolism:** Persisting active or healed vegetations on leaflets act as a persistent nidus for platelet and fibrin aggregation.
* **Anticoagulation Failure:** High-dose anticoagulation (warfarin target INR with adjunct aspirin in PAPS) must be maintained to prevent catastrophic thrombosis.
* **Progression of Systemic Aortitis:** In ankylosing spondylitis, inflammation may progress beyond the aortic root across the aorto-mitral curtain into the base of the anterior mitral leaflet, leading to secondary mitral insufficiency.
* **Bioprosthetic Degeneration vs. Bleeding:** Balancing the risks of accelerated tissue valve calcification/deterioration in young patients with active systemic inflammation against the hemorrhagic and thrombotic hazards of mechanical valves.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SYSTEMIC LUPUS ERYTHEMATOSUS</summary><div class="details-content">

Systemic lupus erythematosus is an immune complex–mediated inflammatory disease that can cause a variety of cardiac manifestations including valvular heart disease, coronary artery disease, myocarditis, and pericarditis. Valvular lesions, characterized by leaflet thickening and fibrosis, involve predominantly the aortic and mitral valves.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology, Echocardiography, and Libman-Sacks Endocarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Echocardiographic Features:** Echocardiography typically shows Type IIIa dysfunction with leaflet thickening of the aortic and mitral valves.
* **Libman-Sacks Vegetations:** Valvular vegetations (Libman-Sacks endocarditis) are commonly seen on the atrial side of the mitral valve and on the aortic side of the aortic valve.
* **Morphological Characteristics:** Typically, there are numerous vegetations with different sizes and irregular borders that produce heterogeneous echogenicity. Vegetations on both the anterior and posterior leaflets of the mitral valve, described as kissing vegetations, are characteristic ([Fig. 35-1](#fig-35-01)).
* **Thromboembolic Risk:** Thrombotic vegetations can be observed in patients who have a hypercoagulable state. These vegetations are at risk of embolization and may cause cerebrovascular accidents.

### Medical Therapy and Histopathology</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Medical Management:** The severity of valvular regurgitation, as seen on Doppler-color, is often mild to moderate and can be managed medically. Vegetations usually decrease in size or resolve with antiinflammatory therapy and an anticoagulation regimen. In rare occasions, advanced valvular lesions causing severe valvular regurgitation require surgical intervention.
* **Active Vegetations:** Histological studies show that active vegetations are nonbacterial and have a central core of fibrinoid necrosis and fibroblastic proliferation surrounded by mononuclear and polymorphonuclear cells with fibrin and platelet deposits.
* **Healed Vegetations:** In contrast, healed vegetations do not present with inflammatory cells and mainly appear as a central fibrosis surrounded by hyalinized or endothelialized thrombus formation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Reconstruction and Replacement</summary><div class="details-content">

Reconstructive valve surgery can be performed in selected cases:
* **Leaflet Extension:** If the predominant lesion is posterior leaflet retraction, leaflet patch extension with glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium followed by a remodeling annuloplasty should be performed.
* **Chordal Resection:** Resection of healed vegetations and thickened secondary chordae increases leaflet mobility.
* **Aortic Valve:** In the aortic position, valve replacement is the optimal surgical approach.

</div></details>

## PRIMARY ANTIPHOSPHOLIPID SYNDROME</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clinical Features, Histopathology, and Medical Management</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Valvular Manifestations:** Valvular manifestations in primary antiphospholipid syndrome are similar to those seen in systemic lupus erythematosus. Most patients present with Type IIIa dysfunction with leaflet thickening and eventually retraction affecting the mitral or aortic valve. In the mitral position, the involvement of the subvalvular apparatus is rare.
* **Histological Findings:** Histological analysis shows that valvular lesions are characterized by intravalvular thrombosis with necrotic and hemorrhagic plaques, vascular proliferation, and inflammatory cell infiltration. These lesions are commonly surrounded by fibrin and thrombus formation and may evolve toward calcification over time.
* **Medical Therapy:** Medical therapy including antiinflammatory therapy and a high-dose anticoagulation regimen with warfarin and aspirin is of paramount importance in these patients.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## RHEUMATOID ARTHRITIS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Valvular Lesions and Rheumatoid Nodules</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Valvular Involvement:** Rheumatoid arthritis can be at the origin of acute mitral and/or aortic valvulitis. These lesions can resolve under antiinflammatory therapy or evolve toward fibrosis with leaflet thickening and retraction. In most instances, valvular lesions cause mild regurgitation and patients are asymptomatic.
* **Echocardiographic Features:** Rheumatoid nodules can be observed on echocardiography. These small masses are circular and well-delineated with homogeneous echogenicity, and therefore can be differentiated from Libman-Sacks vegetations that have also been reported in rheumatoid arthritis.
* **Treatment:** Medical therapy is the treatment of choice.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## ANKYLOSING SPONDYLITIS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Aortitis and Valvular Involvement</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Aortitis of the Aortic Root:** The most common cardiovascular manifestation of ankylosing spondylitis is aortitis involving the ascending aorta and the aortic root with extension toward the aortic leaflets and annulus. The inflammatory process involves the entire thickness of the aorta and over time leads to aortic root sclerosis and dilatation.
* **Aortic Leaflet Thickening:** Aortic leaflet thickening is also observed as a result of fibrosis. The resulting aortic regurgitation is often mild to moderate and does not require surgical intervention.
* **Aorto-Mitral Extension:** In patients with long-lasting disease, the inflammatory process can progress to the aorto-mitral curtain and the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve, leading to mild to moderate degrees of valvular regurgitation. Similarly, surgical intervention in this particular scenario is rarely indicated.

</div></details>

## OTHER SYSTEMIC DISEASES</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Scleroderma, Polymyositis, and Dermatomyositis</summary><div class="details-content">

* **Rare Valvular Involvement:** Other systemic diseases—such as scleroderma, polymyositis, and dermatomyositis—rarely cause valvular diseases.
* **Vegetations and Embolization:** Mitral and aortic leaflet vegetations similar to those seen in Libman-Sacks endocarditis associated with systemic lupus erythematosus can occasionally be observed. They may cause peripheral embolization with an increased risk of stroke.
* **Medical Therapy:** Medical therapy including long-term anticoagulation is the appropriate treatment for these patients.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Referral Patterns of Immune-Mediated Valvular Diseases (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** How often have you observed the diseases described in this chapter?

**AC:** They are rarely seen by the surgeon, not because they are infrequent but because they are rarely referred to a surgeon. The physician and the specialist generally believe that patients with these diseases are beyond the scope of surgery, which is not always the case.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications and Technique for Libman-Sacks Vegetations (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In your experience, what has been the primary surgical indication to remove mitral valve Libman-Sacks vegetations?

**AC:** These vegetations are often localized on the atrial side of the posterior or anterior leaflet and have a tendency for embolization. They may resolve with appropriate anticoagulation treatment but they may also persist or even increase in size despite medical therapy. In practice, the main indication for their surgical removal is the recurrence of peripheral embolization, mainly transient ischemic attacks, despite effective anticoagulation therapy. Almost always, the vegetations can be removed and the valve preserved. After removing the vegetations, I like to apply a drop of glutaraldehyde on the leaflet tissue for 1 or 2 minutes at the site of the vegetations to minimize the risk of recurrence.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Prosthesis Selection and Bioprosthetic Durability in Inflammatory Disease (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Most patients with immune-mediated aortic valve disease present with type IIIa dysfunction and leaflet thickening or retraction requiring replacement. What type of prosthesis do you favor in this group of patients? Do you have any data regarding the long-term fate of bioprostheses in the presence of a long-lasting inflammatory syndrome?

**AC:** I favor bioprosthetic valves to avoid long-term anticoagulation therapy because these patients are exposed to multiple complications during their lifetime. However, I have limited experience with this type of surgery and I am not aware of any report of long-term bioprosthetic valve durability in this group of patients.

</div></details>
