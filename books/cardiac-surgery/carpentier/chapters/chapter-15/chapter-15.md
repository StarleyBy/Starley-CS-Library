# 15. Techniques in Systolic Anterior Leaflet Motion (SAM)

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical Definition and Historical Context
* **Definition:** Systolic Anterior Motion (SAM) defines the abnormal systolic displacement of the distal portion of the anterior mitral leaflet into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT).
* **Clinical Consequences:** Dynamic LVOT obstruction, significant ventriculo-aortic systolic pressure gradient, and secondary mitral regurgitation typically directed posterolaterally.
* **Historical Evolution:** First identified by echocardiographers in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM); recognized after mitral valve repair by Carpentier in 1980 following the introduction of ring annuloplasty.

### Etiological Prerequisites and Risk Stratification
* **The "Sine Qua Non" Mechanism:** Coexistence of excess leaflet tissue (particularly posterior leaflet height > 20 mm) and an undersized annuloplasty ring that displaces the coaptation line anteriorly into the outflow tract.
* **Major Risk Factors:**
  * Excess valvular tissue (Barlow's disease, redundant posterior leaflet).
  * Undersized annuloplasty ring relative to the surface area of the anterior leaflet.
* **Minor (Contributing) Risk Factors:** Narrow aorto-mitral angle (< 120 degrees), hyperkinetic small left ventricular cavity, basal septal hypertrophy (septal bulge), and anterior leaflet elongation.

### Preventive Strategy and Rescue Procedures
* **Intraoperative Prevention (The 15–20 mm Rule):** Posterior leaflet height must be strictly reduced to 15 to 20 mm using sliding plasty, posterior leaflet height reduction (basal ovoid resection), or folding plasty.
* **Annuloplasty Sizing:** Strictly avoid undersizing in the presence of tall leaflets or redundant tissue; select ring size based on true anterior leaflet surface area.
* **Immediate Intraoperative Algorithm:**
  1. Stop inotropes;
  2. Optimize volume loading (atrial filling pressures 18 to 22 mm Hg);
  3. Ensure atrioventricular synchrony and heart rate control.
* **Surgical Re-exploration:** If hemodynamically significant SAM persists with gradient > 50 mm Hg or regurgitation > 2+, perform basal ovoid resection leaving the ring in situ, or exchange for a larger ring.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY AND RISK FACTORS OF SAM</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Definition and Fundamental Mechanisms

The term "Systolic Anterior Motion" (SAM) defines a systolic displacement of the distal portion of the anterior leaflet of the mitral valve towards the outflow tract of the left ventricle. This abnormal motion, first described by echocardiographers in obstructive cardiomyopathy, was also observed following mitral valve reconstruction.

The consequence of SAM is a left outflow tract obstruction often associated with mitral valve regurgitation. In the early experience with valve reconstruction, this complication was relatively frequent, occurring in almost 10 percent of cases in some series.

We described the mechanism of SAM in our first paper published in 1988: the two major factors creating SAM were excess leaflet tissue and an undersized annuloplasty ring.

#### The Sine Qua Non Condition and Venturi Effect

Excess leaflet tissue and an undersized annuloplasty ring displace the line of coaptation anteriorly towards the left ventricular outflow tract ([Fig. 15-1](#fig-15-01)). The displaced coaptation line exposes the slack distal portion of the anterior leaflet to high-velocity flow during ventricular ejection.

Under the influence of Venturi forces and hydrodynamic drag, the anterior leaflet is drawn into the outflow tract, obstructing ventricular ejection and preventing complete coaptation.

This geometric disproportion between the amount of functional valvular tissue and the size of the reconstructed mitral annulus is the "sine qua non" condition required to trigger SAM.

#### Anatomical Risk Stratification

The two major risk factors for producing SAM after mitral valve repair ([Fig. 15-2](#fig-15-02)) are excess leaflet tissue (a) and an undersized ring (b).

Additional potential contributing factors, described by Mihaileanu and colleagues, include a narrow aorto-mitral angle, a hyperkinetic small ventricle, a bulging interventricular septum, and an abnormal configuration or elongation of the anterior leaflet ([Table 15-1](#table-15-01)).

Recognition of these risk factors during preoperative valve analysis allows the surgeon to select reconstructive techniques that proactively prevent SAM.

## HEMODYNAMIC ASSESSMENT AND ECHOCARDIOGRAPHY</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Flow Velocity and Hydrodynamic Drag Forces

The interaction between subaortic flow and the redundant anterior leaflet generates dynamic obstruction ([Fig. 15-3](#fig-15-03)).

As the left ventricular outflow tract narrows, flow velocity accelerates markedly.

This high-velocity flow creates a low-pressure zone that sucks the free edge of the anterior leaflet into the subaortic space, exacerbating the obstruction throughout peak systole.

<span id="fig-15-03"></span>![](_page_159_Figure_15-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15-3** Hemodynamic mechanism of SAM: anterior displacement of the coaptation point positioning the slack distal portion of the anterior leaflet into the high-velocity subaortic flow stream, triggering Venturi suction and drag forces.

#### Intraoperative Transesophageal Echocardiography

Transesophageal echocardiography performed immediately after weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass provides definitive diagnosis of SAM ([Fig. 15-4](#fig-15-04)).

The classic findings include systolic contact between the anterior mitral leaflet and the ventricular septum, high turbulent velocities in the outflow tract by continuous-wave Doppler, and a posteriorly directed jet of mitral regurgitation.

Because the anterior leaflet cannot coapt fully with the posterior leaflet, incomplete coaptation creates a regurgitant orifice pointing towards the posterior left atrial wall.

<span id="fig-15-04"></span>![](_page_159_Figure_15-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15-4** Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography of postrepair SAM: midesophageal long-axis view demonstrating systolic obstruction of the left ventricular outflow tract by the anterior mitral leaflet with severe turbulence.

#### Dynamic Nature and Medical Optimization

[Figure 15-5](#fig-15-05) shows a representative case of post-repair SAM with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (a) and severe mitral regurgitation displaying a posteriorly oriented jet (b).

Complete resolution of SAM and mitral regurgitation was obtained by the discontinuation of inotropic agents and optimization of left ventricular filling pressures (c–f).

Medical therapy consisting of volume administration, beta-blockade, and avoidance of vasodilators resolves this functional anomaly in most instances by enlarging the ventricular cavity and reducing ejection velocity.

## SURGICAL PREVENTION AND RESCUE TECHNIQUES</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Principles of Intraoperative Prevention

Most postoperative dynamic SAM disappears within days or weeks after the operation as the result of left outflow tract remodeling.

In the absence of significant improvement after appropriate medical therapy (for example, a persisting peak gradient greater than 50 mm Hg or a mitral valve regurgitation greater than 2+), a reoperation is indicated.

Prevention remains the superior strategy: the surgeon must systematically assess the height of the posterior leaflet and never undersize the annuloplasty ring when excess tissue is present.

#### The 15–20 mm Posterior Leaflet Rule

To prevent SAM, the height of the posterior leaflet should not exceed 15 to 20 mm.

Whenever the posterior leaflet is taller than 20 mm, height-reduction techniques must be incorporated into the reconstruction.

These include sliding leaflet plasty, triangular resection, or posterior leaflet height reduction via basal ovoid resection.

#### Rescue Techniques with Ring in Situ

Whenever an irreversible SAM has been diagnosed after a valve reconstruction of any type, different correction techniques are used ([Fig. 15-6](#fig-15-06)), depending upon the mechanisms identified by echocardiography.

Excess tissue of the posterior leaflet (a) requires the reduction of the height of any abnormal segment. This can be carried out by an ovoid leaflet resection at the base of this leaflet while leaving the ring in place (b).

The posterior incision of the leaflet tissue should leave a leaflet remnant height of 3 to 4 mm to facilitate suturing. Leaflet continuity is then restored using a 5-0 monofilament continuous suture (c).

<span id="fig-15-06"></span>![](_page_161_Figure_15-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15-6** Surgical rescue techniques for postrepair SAM with ring in situ: basal ovoid resection of excess posterior leaflet tissue leaving 3 to 4 mm rim for closure (a, b, c); ring exchange for a larger size in severe undersizing (d); targeted anterior leaflet height reduction or adaptation for anterior leaflet redundancy (e, f).

#### Ring Upsizing and Anterior Leaflet Management

When SAM is caused by an undersized ring (d), the ring should be removed and replaced by a ring of larger size.

In cases where SAM is related to an abnormally large anterior leaflet, the use of an altered Classic ring is recommended to conform to the configuration of the anterior leaflet (e).

A reduction of the height of the anterior leaflet can also be carried out without replacing the ring (f).

#### Posterior Leaflet Basal Ovoid Resection Technique

Posterior leaflet height reduction is achieved by removing an ovoid piece of excess leaflet tissue near the base of the posterior leaflet without leaflet detachment from the annulus ([Fig. 15-8](#fig-15-08)).

This technique selectively shortens the posterior leaflet height to the target of 15 to 20 mm, allowing the coaptation point to recede posteriorly away from the outflow tract.

Continuous 5-0 monofilament suture restores leaflet integrity without distorting the free edge or compromising marginal chordae.

<span id="fig-15-08"></span>![](_page_163_Figure_15-8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15-8** Surgical technique of posterior leaflet height reduction: transverse basal ovoid excision of redundant intermediate tissue with maintenance of annular ring fixation and reconstruction by continuous suture, restoring posterior leaflet height to <= 20 mm.

#### Anterior Leaflet Height Reduction Technique

When excess tissue of the anterior leaflet is the predominant cause of SAM, transverse or tailored resection of the anterior leaflet body can be performed ([Fig. 15-9](#fig-15-09)).

The incision is made in the clear zone of the anterior leaflet, and excess redundant tissue is excised.

Reconstruction with fine monofilament sutures restores optimal leaflet tension while preserving all primary marginal chordal insertions.

<span id="fig-15-09"></span>![](_page_163_Figure_15-9.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15-9** Technique of anterior leaflet height reduction: selective transverse resection or tailored plication of the anterior leaflet body preserving marginal chordal attachments in rare cases of isolated anterior redundancy.

## CLINICAL RESULTS AND ALGORITHM</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Systematic Clinical Outcomes

The application of these principles and techniques has practically eliminated SAM following valve reconstruction in our practice.

We investigated the efficacy of the surgical techniques developed to prevent SAM in a series of 82 patients undergoing surgery between 1988 and 1991.

Most of these patients had Barlow's disease with extensive bileaflet prolapse and severe excess valvular tissue. With the routine application of posterior leaflet height reduction and avoidance of ring undersizing, the incidence of SAM fell from nearly 10 percent to less than 1 percent.

<span id="fig-15-07"></span>![](_page_162_Figure_15-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 15-7** Postoperative echocardiographic confirmation following SAM correction: widely patent left ventricular outflow tract without accelerated flow velocities and restored planar leaflet coaptation.

#### Echocardiographic Confirmation of Success

Postoperative transesophageal echocardiography in these patients demonstrated widely patent left ventricular outflow tracts without systolic flow acceleration ([Fig. 15-7](#fig-15-07)).

The coaptation line was securely positioned in the posterior half of the left ventricle, well away from the subaortic flow stream.

Long-term follow-up confirmed stable valve competence without late outflow tract obstruction or recurrent mitral regurgitation.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Functional Classification and Nature of SAM

> **FF:** Using the functional classification, what type of dysfunction is SAM?
>
> **AC:** Excess posterior leaflet tissue in SAM limits the full deployment of the anterior leaflet, leaving the free edge below the plane of the orifice. The primary mechanism is therefore a type III dysfunction, in other words a restricted anterior leaflet motion. The systolic anterior motion of the distal part of the anterior leaflet is not a prolapse since its displacement is oriented toward the left outflow tract, not toward the mitral valve orifice. In addition, the free edge of this valve remains below the plane of the mitral valve annulus.

#### Management of Undersized Rings and Edge-to-Edge Repair

> **FF:** In the presence of SAM caused by a ring that is too small, can the removal of the ring be a sufficient maneuver for the correction of this dysfunction?
>
> **AC:** It does correct SAM but increases the risk of regurgitation because the diseased annulus is no longer remodeled and supported. It is therefore necessary to replace the ring with a properly sized ring and to verify that the height of the posterior leaflet is less than or equal to 20 mm. If both of these conditions are followed, SAM should be eliminated and the mitral valve regurgitation durably corrected.

> **FF:** Edge-to-edge repair has been used as a rescue procedure to correct SAM after valve repair. Can you comment on this?
>
> **AC:** The results using this approach as a rescue procedure have been generally disappointing. Another alternative proposed by Vanermen is posterior leaflet displacement in the ventricle with a short artificial chordae. A more tested and reliable rescue procedure for SAM is a transverse ovoid resection of a segment of either the posterior or the anterior leaflet followed by restoration of leaflet continuity. This can be easily achieved with the prosthetic ring in place.

#### Stepwise Algorithm for Postrepair SAM

> **DA:** Can you describe an algorithm to address the problem of SAM after valve reconstruction?
>
> **AC:** Four immediate actions should be undertaken:
> 1. Stop inotropic medications.
> 2. Fill the heart to achieve a maximum diastolic atrial pressure of 18 mm Hg whenever the preoperative left ventricular function is preserved, and up to 22 mm Hg if the patient presents with a significant preoperative left ventricular dysfunction.
> 3. Ensure atrioventricular synchrony.
> 4. Analyze by echocardiography the mechanism involved; assess the height of the posterior leaflet and the amount of tissue of the anterior leaflet and its relation to the size of the ring.

#### Three Post-Optimization Scenarios

> **AC:** Following these steps, three scenarios are possible:
>
> In the first scenario (disappearance of both SAM and mitral regurgitation and normalization of the ventriculo-aortic systolic gradient), no further surgical intervention is required and the patient may benefit from beta-blockers postoperatively.
>
> In the second scenario (persistence of SAM with minimal residual mitral regurgitation and significant reduction of the ventriculo-aortic gradient), the same conservative attitude may be used on the basis that in most cases outflow tract remodeling takes place in the follow-up period, leading to a spontaneous resolution of SAM. Because of the unpredictability of this progression and our current understanding of the mechanisms involved, I prefer not to leave an imperfect result if the exact mechanism has been clearly identified, particularly excess height of the posterior leaflet or too small ring size. In these cases, as in the third scenario (persistence of SAM with residual regurgitation and increased gradients), a second corrective operation should be carried out, which is usually fast and simple since it consists of posterior leaflet or anterior leaflet height reduction by an ovoid resection, or more rarely replacement with a larger ring.

#### Operating Room Observation Time and Sliding Plasty Comparison

> **DA:** How long should we wait in the operating room in the first or second scenario?
>
> **AC:** Once the actions described in the previous answer have been undertaken, the resolution of SAM is generally rapid, in less than 30 minutes. If resolution occurs or remains partial after this period, in the absence of residual regurgitation and a gradient over 20 to 30 mm Hg, the patient can be transferred to the intensive care unit for continuous monitoring. A complete resolution of SAM is usually confirmed during short-term or longer follow-up.

> **DA:** Describe the difference between posterior leaflet height reduction and sliding leaflet plasty.
>
> **AC:** Both techniques aim at preventing systolic anterior leaflet motion by removing a certain amount of excess tissue at the base of the posterior leaflet. Posterior leaflet height reduction is achieved by removing an ovoid piece of excess leaflet tissue near the base of the posterior leaflet without leaflet detachment from the annulus. A sliding leaflet plasty, by definition, implies an advancement of residual leaflet segments along the annulus, often following triangular leaflet resection to reduce leaflet height.
