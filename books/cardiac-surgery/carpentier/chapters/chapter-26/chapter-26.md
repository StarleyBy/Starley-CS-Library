# Chapter 26. Degenerative Mitral Valve Disease: Barlow's Disease and Fibroelastic Deficiency

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Spectrum of Degenerative Mitral Valve Pathology
Degenerative valvular disease represents the foremost etiology of mitral regurgitation in industrialized nations and constitutes the "daily bread" (*pain quotidien*) of the reconstructive valve surgeon. Historically obscured by heterogeneous nomenclature—such as "mitral valve prolapse," "floppy valve," "billowing valve," or "myxomatous disease"—degenerative mitral disease encompasses distinct clinical and histopathological entities rather than a uniform condition. Alain Carpentier established the fundamental dichotomy separating **Barlow's disease** from **fibroelastic deficiency (FED)**, alongside syndromic connective tissue disorders such as **Marfan's syndrome**.

Understanding this spectrum is critical because the surgical strategy, technical complexity, risk of postoperative systolic anterior motion (SAM), and choice of annuloplasty ring size diverge markedly between entities.

### Pathological and Clinical Dichotomy: Barlow vs. Fibroelastic Deficiency
A systematic comparison reveals opposite pathophysiological profiles ([Table 26-1](#table-26-01)):
* **Barlow's Disease (Excess Tissue and Myxoid Proliferation):**
  * *Demographics & Genetics:* Typically diagnosed before age 50; familial clustering with autosomal dominant transmission linked to loci on chromosomes 11, 13, and 16.
  * *Clinical Course:* Long, indolent, well-tolerated history (>10-year interval from diagnosis to operation) characterized by a midsystolic click and late systolic murmur (Cuffer, Barbillon, Reid, Barlow).
  * *Morphology:* Massive, circular annular dilatation (often requiring ≥34–36 mm rings); voluminous excess leaflet tissue (surface area 2 to 3 times normal) with diffuse, irregular billowing giving a classic "cauliflower" appearance; thickened, distended, or fused indentations; deep ventricular "cul-de-sac"; multiple annular disruptions/cracks with microthrombi and calcification; elongated, thickened, and chaotically anchored chordae forming a trabeculated mesh.
  * *Histopathology:* Disorganized leaflet architecture with profound mucopolysaccharide (acid proteoglycan) accumulation infiltrating the fibrosa, marked collagen attenuation, and disrupted elastic fibers.
* **Fibroelastic Deficiency (Tissue Deficiency and Chordal Rupture):**
  * *Demographics & Course:* Typically diagnosed in elderly patients (>50–60 years); non-familial; acute or subacute symptom onset (<5-year interval from diagnosis to operation), often presenting as acute pulmonary edema due to sudden chordal rupture.
  * *Morphology:* Normal or mildly dilated annulus (ring size usually ≤32–34 mm); thin, translucent, transparent leaflets without excess tissue, except for localized secondary myxoid degeneration strictly restricted to the prolapsing segment (most commonly the middle scallop of the posterior leaflet, P2); thin, elongated, and ruptured chordae.
  * *Histopathology:* Preserved trilaminar architecture; deficiency of collagen, elastin, and proteoglycans resulting in mechanical fragility and rupture under normal hemodynamic stress.
* **Marfan's Disease:** Fibrillin-1 (*FBN1*) gene mutation; tall stature, arachnodactyly, pectus deformities, ectopia lentis; characterized by thin, elongated, translucent leaflets, extreme annular dilatation, elongated chordae, and frequent coexistence of annuloaortic ectasia.

### Reconstructive Surgical Strategy
1. **Surgical Principles in Barlow's Disease:**
   * *Excess Tissue Reduction:* Leaflet resection combined with extensive sliding plasty of posterior leaflet remnants is essential to reduce posterior leaflet height below 15–20 mm, shifting the coaptation line posteriorly and preventing systolic anterior motion (SAM).
   * *Correction of Billowing and Prolapse:* Billowing leaflets must not be left untreated, as excess curvature increases wall tension according to Laplace's law and risks recurrent regurgitation. Techniques include papillary muscle sliding or shortening plasty, secondary chordal transfer, and artificial chordae.
   * *Remodeling Annuloplasty:* Annular dilatation is severe and circumferential. Large prosthetic rings (typically 34–40 mm Physio rings) sized strictly to the anterior leaflet surface area must be used. Undersizing must be avoided to prevent functional mitral stenosis and SAM.
   * *Management of Severe Annular Calcification:* Extensive decortication of calcified bars with en bloc resection and annular reconstruction, or the "magic suture" technique in high-risk patients.
2. **Surgical Principles in Fibroelastic Deficiency:**
   * *Targeted Segmental Resection:* Limited triangular or quadrangular resection of the prolapsing segment (P2) with preservation of adjacent indentations, followed by annular plication.
   * *Subvalvular Reconstruction:* Transposition of secondary chordae or polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) neo-chordae to support free margins.
   * *Remodeling Annuloplasty:* Sizing is standard (30–32 mm in females, 32–34 mm in males) using a prosthetic ring to prevent late redilatation. SAM is rare due to the absence of excess leaflet tissue.

### Long-Term Outcomes (20–25 Year Cohort Analysis)
Analysis of 162 consecutive patients surgically treated between 1970 and 1984 at Hôpital Broussais (Braunberger et al.) with up to 25-year follow-up demonstrated:
* **25-Year Actuarial Survival:** Approximately 50%, matching the survival of an age-matched general population.
* **Freedom from Reoperation:** 90% at 25 years for the overall cohort (linearized reoperation rate 0.4% per patient/year).
* **Parity of Durability:** Long-term durability was virtually identical between Barlow's disease and fibroelastic deficiency, validating the anatomical repair concept.
* **Functional Recovery:** Over 98% of surviving patients remained in NYHA functional class I or II after 15 to 25 years.

## BARLOW'S DISEASE</summary><div class="details-content">

#### History and Terminology

Barlow's disease is the most frequent degenerative valvular disease in patients younger than 60 years. It is a familial disease with a high prevalence of autosomal dominant transmission. Association of this disease with sites on chromosomes 11, 13, and 16 has been described.

#### Auscultatory Milestones and Seminal Observations

This disease displays unique clinical and pathological characteristics compared to other degenerative valvular diseases ([Table 26-1](#table-26-01)). The main clinical characteristic is a midsystolic click and/or a systolic apical murmur, first described by Cuffer and Barbillon. Both of these features were originally thought to be extracardiac until Reid postulated in 1961 that these two auscultatory features could be due to mitral regurgitation. Two years later, Barlow confirmed this hypothesis by left ventricular cineangiography and provided the first well-documented analysis of this disease, which was originally believed to be of rheumatic origin.

Mucoid degeneration producing billowing leaflets was actually found to be the main histochemical characteristic of the disease. Later, numerous new terms were introduced to describe the same abnormal morphology and dysfunction, such as excessive leaflet motion, mitral valve prolapse, billowing valve, floppy valve, valve flail, or prolapsed leaflets. These terms contribute to the confusion in terminology that prevails today. For the sake of clarity, we prefer the term "Barlow's disease"; this term has the advantage of recognizing the seminal contribution of the author while avoiding confusion with the term "prolapse," which actually defines a functional impairment rather than a specific pathological entity. The term "Barlow's disease" should be used exclusively to designate this specific disease in its typical or "forme fruste"* stage.

*\*“Forme fruste” designates a disease that presents with incomplete clinical or anatomical features.*

#### Clinical Presentation and Auscultatory Findings

The diagnosis of Barlow's disease is usually established early in life between the ages of 20 and 40 years. It is more common in females than in males. The typical findings in Barlow's disease are the midsystolic click believed to be caused by the sudden stretching of the chordae tendineae and the late systolic murmur caused by mitral regurgitation.

Patients are usually asymptomatic for long periods of time. Nonspecific symptoms such as atypical chest pain, palpitation, asthenia, dizziness, and syncope may be present. Palpitation is often associated with premature ventricular contractions or supraventricular arrhythmias. Mitral valve regurgitation develops slowly and is well tolerated for years or decades until left ventricular dilatation, atrial fibrillation, or ruptured chordae occur.

#### Echocardiographic Analysis

Echocardiography is the essential tool for the anatomical determination of Barlow's disease ([Fig. 26-1](#fig-26-01)).

<span id="fig-26-01"></span>![](images/_page_268_Figure_26-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 26-1**

The most characteristic echocardiographic feature of Barlow's valve is a severe dilatation of the mitral valve annulus associated with extensive and irregular billowing of both anterior and posterior leaflets into the left atrium during systole ([Fig. 26-1](#fig-26-01)). The leaflets are thickened and redundant, displaying increased echogenicity.

Color Doppler echocardiography demonstrates single or multiple eccentric regurgitant jets caused by leaflet prolapse. Transesophageal echocardiography provides detailed anatomical mapping of the prolapsing scallops, the presence of chordal elongation or rupture, and the degree of annular calcification.

#### Pathology and Structural Lesions

The annulus is severely dilatated and circular ([Fig. 26-2](#fig-26-02)). Excess leaflet tissue with extensive and irregular billowing of the bellies of the leaflets gives a cauliflower appearance to Barlow's valve (a, b). The leaflets are thickened as a result of a myxoid degenerative process that predominantly involves the coaptation zone.

The excess tissue is such that the total surface area of the leaflets is usually two to three times greater than the normal surface area, with predominant involvement of the posterior leaflet (c). The anterior leaflet typically measures 38 to 40 mm in transverse diameter and 30 mm or more in height.

<span id="fig-26-02"></span>![](images/_page_269_Figure_26-2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 26-2**

##### Annular and Subvalvular Morphological Alterations

The increased tension on the excess leaflet tissue (a consequence of Laplace's law) may produce severe lesions at the attachment of the leaflet to the annulus on both ventricular and atrial sides. On the ventricular side, the posterior leaflet attachment to the crest of the ventricular wall is displaced outward, forming the "cul-de-sac"* previously described. On the atrial side, the atrio-valvular junction may be disrupted with tiny cracks (a, arrows) often filled with platelet aggregates that eventually become calcified.

Indentations are distended or thickened and fused, and sometimes calcified (d). The chordae are grossly abnormal: some are elongated, others are shortened, some are thin, and others are thickened. The most characteristic aspect of the marginal and secondary chordae is their chaotic anchorage to the leaflets: their attachment to the ventricular side of the leaflets forms a trabeculated "mesh" that circumscribes small pockets of billowing tissue (e).

Extensive annular calcification may also occur, involving predominantly the posterior annulus with occasional extension to the entire circumference and the head of the papillary muscles. The tricuspid valve often displays similar morphological abnormalities as the mitral valve.

> **Pathological Principle:** The main pathophysiological characteristic of Barlow's valve is excess valvular tissue responsible for the billowing of the bellies of the leaflets into the left atrium during systole.

#### Surgical Indications in Barlow's Disease

Surgery is indicated in symptomatic patients presenting with severe mitral valve regurgitation (NYHA class II–IV). In asymptomatic patients, surgery should be considered in the presence of left ventricular enlargement (left ventricular end-systolic diameter ≥ 40 mm), left ventricular dysfunction (ejection fraction ≤ 60%), pulmonary hypertension (systolic pulmonary artery pressure > 50 mm Hg at rest), or atrial fibrillation.

Early surgery in asymptomatic patients before the onset of ventricular dilatation or atrial fibrillation is strongly recommended, provided the operation is performed by an experienced surgeon in a center with high repair rates and low operative risk.

#### Surgical Management: Valve Reconstruction Principles

The goal of reconstructive surgery in Barlow's disease is to correct both leaflet prolapse and leaflet billowing, reduce excess leaflet tissue, and restore normal valve geometry. Reconstructive surgery involves several sequential steps (see Chapter 11):

* **Leaflet Resection and Sliding Plasty:** When the posterior leaflet displays severe degenerative lesions, it should be corrected by resection of the prolapsing and billowing tissue, followed by leaflet sliding plasty to reduce the height of the remaining posterior leaflet tissue and prevent systolic anterior motion (SAM).
* **Subvalvular Apparatus Reconstruction:** Fused, shortened, or calcified papillary muscles are mobilized and managed by sliding or shortening techniques (Chapter 10). Elongated or ruptured chordae are addressed by chordal transfer or PTFE artificial chordae.
* **Prosthetic Remodeling Annuloplasty:** The annulus, involved in the degenerative process, must be stabilized using a prosthetic ring (Physio ring). Ring sizing must be strictly based on the surface area of the anterior leaflet. Sizing below 32–34 mm should generally be avoided in Barlow's disease to eliminate the risk of SAM and postoperative mitral stenosis.

<span id="fig-26-03"></span>![](images/_page_270_Figure_26-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 26-3**

#### Reconstructive Variations: Severe Bileaflet Billowing

Reconstruction of Barlow's valve with billowing and prolapse of both leaflets requires comprehensive repair techniques addressing both anterior and posterior leaflets (see Chapters 10 and 12). The strategy is to treat not only the prolapse but also the billowing tissue ([Fig. 26-3](#fig-26-03)).

Valve reconstruction in Barlow's disease is particularly challenging when extensive calcification of the annulus and papillary muscles is present. In these situations, the calcified tissue is removed en bloc (see Chapter 9). The annulus is then reconstructed using sliding plasty or patch techniques, followed by prosthetic ring implantation.

## MARFAN'S DISEASE</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Genetics and Valvular Involvement

Marfan's syndrome is an inherited connective tissue disorder caused by mutations in the fibrillin-1 gene (*FBN1*) on chromosome 15. It has an autosomal dominant pattern of inheritance with variable expressivity.

Cardiovascular manifestations are the major cause of morbidity and mortality. They include aortic root dilatation, aortic dissection, and mitral valve disease. Mitral valve involvement is present in up to 80% of patients with Marfan's syndrome and represents the primary cause of cardiac morbidity in children and adolescents.

<span id="fig-26-04"></span>![](images/_page_272_Figure_26-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 26-4**

#### Morphology and Surgical Strategy

In some patients, echocardiography findings may suggest Barlow's disease because of bileaflet billowing and severe annular dilatation. Contrary to Barlow's valve, the leaflets in Marfan's syndrome are thin, translucent, and transparent ([Fig. 26-4](#fig-26-04)). The chordae tendineae are thin, elongated, and fragile.

The surgical management of mitral valve disease in Marfan's syndrome follows the principles described for Barlow's disease. However, the surgery is more demanding because of the extreme friability of the tissues and the severe annular dilatation.

Prosthetic ring annuloplasty with a semi-rigid ring is mandatory to secure the annulus and prevent late redilatation. When aortic root replacement is required simultaneously, valve-sparing aortic root operations (David or Yacoub procedure) or composite graft replacement (Bentall procedure) are performed concurrently with mitral valve repair.

</div></details>

## OTHER VALVULAR CONNECTIVE TISSUE DISORDERS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Connective Tissue Fragility Syndromes

Other hereditary connective tissue disorders may present with degenerative mitral valve lesions mimicking Barlow's disease or fibroelastic deficiency. These include Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, osteogenesis imperfecta, and pseudoxanthoma elasticum.

In these syndromes, mitral regurgitation is caused by chordal elongation or rupture, annular dilatation, and leaflet billowing. Tissues are extremely fragile, requiring reinforced suture techniques and avoidance of excessive tension on suture lines.

#### Mucopolysaccharidoses and Metabolic Disorders

Hurler's syndrome and related mucopolysaccharidoses may present with valve thickening, leaflet rigidity, and restricted leaflet motion resembling combined degenerative and rheumatic pathology.

In these metabolic conditions, valve replacement is frequently preferred over reconstruction due to the progressive nature of the enzymatic defect and diffuse tissue infiltration.

</div></details>

## FIBROELASTIC DEFICIENCY</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Clinical Profile and Presentation

Fibroelastic deficiency is the second most common cause of degenerative mitral valve disease and the most frequent etiology in elderly patients (>60 years). Contrary to Barlow's disease or Marfan's disease, fibroelastic deficiency has no familial transmission.

The clinical onset is typically acute or subacute, characterized by sudden chordal rupture leading to massive mitral valve regurgitation and acute pulmonary edema. The interval between diagnosis and surgical intervention is short (<5 years), directly opposite to the long-standing clinical course of Barlow's disease ([Table 26-1](#table-26-01)).

<span id="fig-26-05"></span>![](images/_page_274_Figure_26-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 26-5**

#### Pathology and Valvular Characteristics

The hallmark of fibroelastic deficiency is a deficiency of collagen and elastic fibers as well as proteoglycans, leading to chordal thinning and rupture ([Fig. 26-5](#fig-26-05)). As opposed to Barlow's disease, there is no excess leaflet tissue.

The leaflets are thin, smooth, and transparent throughout the entire valve, except for the prolapsing scallop, which may display localized secondary thickening due to mechanical stress. The annulus is normal in size or only mildly dilated.

<span id="fig-26-06"></span>![](images/_page_274_Figure_26-6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 26-6**

The primary lesion is rupture of one or several chordae tendineae, most commonly involving the middle scallop of the posterior leaflet (P2) ([Fig. 26-6](#fig-26-06)). Less frequently, chordal rupture involves the anterior leaflet or the commissural areas.

> **Pathological Distinction:** Fibroelastic deficiency is characterized by thin, deficient leaflet tissue and chordal rupture, presenting the exact morphological opposite of the voluminous, redundant tissue seen in Barlow's disease.

#### Surgical Management of Fibroelastic Deficiency

The surgical repair of fibroelastic deficiency is straightforward and standardized (see Chapter 11). In most instances, it requires only a limited triangular or quadrangular resection of the flail P2 segment with preservation of adjacent indentations ([Fig. 26-7](#fig-26-07)).

<span id="fig-26-07"></span>![](images/_page_275_Figure_26-7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 26-7**

#### Annular Reconstruction and Sizing Strategy

Leaflet resection is preferable to remove the pathological flail area and reduce tension on the remaining leaflet tissue and chordae. After resection, the annulus is plicated and leaflet continuity is restored. A leaflet sliding plasty is rarely necessary in fibroelastic deficiency because there is no excess leaflet height, except when localized annular calcification is present (Chapter 9).

A prosthetic remodeling annuloplasty ring is always used because the annulus is involved in the degenerative process, creating a risk of late dilatation. Ring size is generally ≤34 mm (typically 30–32 mm in females and 32–34 mm in males). Systolic anterior motion is uncommon after annuloplasty in fibroelastic deficiency because there is no excess tissue.

> **Annuloplasty Standard:** The most common ring size used to repair fibroelastic deficiency is 32 to 34 mm in males and 30 to 32 mm in females.

In anterior leaflet prolapse, secondary chordal transposition from the anterior or posterior leaflet (Chapter 10) or artificial PTFE chordae replacement is used. Transposition of living autologous chordae has demonstrated outstanding long-term durability.

## RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### 25-Year Long-Term Outcomes (Braunberger et al.)

Long-term results from more than 20 years of reconstructive techniques in degenerative valvular diseases have been analyzed in 162 consecutive patients with Barlow's disease or fibroelastic deficiency who underwent surgery between 1970 and 1984 at Hôpital Broussais in Paris. Patient age varied between 22 and 77 years (mean 56 ± 10 years). Sixty percent of the patients were in NYHA class III or IV, and 45% had atrial fibrillation. More than 33% had anterior or bileaflet prolapse.

<span id="fig-26-08"></span>![](images/_page_275_Figure_26-8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 26-8**

#### Survival and Freedom from Reoperation

With follow-up extending up to 25 years, several critical observations were established:
* **Kaplan-Meier Survival:** The 25-year survival rate was approximately 50%, which was identical to the expected survival of an age-matched normal population.
* **Freedom from Reoperation:** Freedom from reoperation for the entire cohort was 90% at 25 years, representing a linearized reoperation rate of 0.4% per patient/year ([Fig. 26-8](#fig-26-08)). Reoperation rates were slightly higher when the anterior leaflet was involved ($p < 0.03$).
* **Etiological Durability Parity:** No difference in long-term repair durability was found between Barlow's disease and fibroelastic deficiency.
* **Functional Capacity:** All surviving patients but one were in NYHA functional class I or II at 15 to 25 years postoperatively.
* **Impact of Preoperative Class:** Advanced preoperative disability (NYHA class III/IV) did not negatively affect long-term repair durability.

#### Results in Marfan's Syndrome

In Marfan's disease, long-term studies are more limited because valve replacement was historically favored. Between 1986 and 1996, 33 consecutive patients with Marfan's syndrome underwent surgery with systematic attempts at valve reconstruction.

Only one patient required intraoperative valve replacement. There were two perioperative deaths (6%). Mean follow-up was 39 months (up to 122 months). Three late deaths occurred, two of which were related to late aortic dissection. Among survivors, valve reconstruction provided stable competence with freedom from reoperation.

## CONCLUSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Diagnostic and Therapeutic Synthesis

Degenerative mitral valve disease comprises distinct pathological entities with differing pathophysiological mechanisms, requiring precise anatomical diagnosis and tailored surgical techniques.

Barlow's disease, characterized by extensive excess tissue and bileaflet billowing, requires aggressive tissue reduction, subvalvular remodeling, and large annuloplasty rings. Fibroelastic deficiency, characterized by tissue deficiency and chordal rupture, requires limited segmental resection and stabilization with standard-sized rings.

When properly executed according to functional principles, mitral valve reconstruction provides near-normal life expectancy and exceptional long-term freedom from reoperation extending past a quarter-century.

</div></details>

## CLINICAL DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Differentiating Barlow's Disease from Fibroelastic Deficiency (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** What specific findings help differentiate Barlow's valve from fibroelastic deficiency?

**AC:** There are clinical and morphological characteristics that distinguish these two malformations. Clinical characteristics of Barlow's valve are the presence of click syndrome, the young age of the patient at the time of diagnosis, and the long-lasting and well-tolerated progression of the disease. Morphological characteristics of Barlow's valve are billowing leaflets caused by excess leaflet tissue and myxoid degeneration predominant in the ventricular portion of the leaflets.

By contrast, in fibroelastic deficiency, patients are older, the onset of symptoms is sudden due to chordal rupture, and the leaflets are thin and transparent without excess tissue.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Meaning of "Myxomatous Valve" (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Is the term "myxomatous valve" a synonym of Barlow's valve?

**AC:** Myxoid degeneration is a distinguishing feature of Barlow's valve and is predominant in the ventricular portion of the leaflets. However, myxoid degeneration can also be observed in fibroelastic deficiency, but only in areas of prolapse as a secondary lesion resulting from the long-lasting dysfunction. Therefore, the term "myxomatous valve" is a descriptive histological term, not an etiological synonym for Barlow's disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Diagnostic Identification of "Forme Fruste" Barlow's Disease (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** As a surgeon, how do you practically differentiate a "forme fruste"* Barlow's disease from fibroelastic deficiency on the basis of valve analysis?

**AC:** A practical mean of differentiation is to use the measurement of the anterior leaflet. If this measurement indicates that a ring smaller than 36 mm should be selected to correct the regurgitation, the diagnosis of Barlow's disease can be ruled out. If the selected ring is 36 mm or larger, the etiology is Barlow's disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Morphological Role of Posterior Leaflet Indentations (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Are deep indentations segmenting the posterior leaflet a characteristic of the valve deformity evident in Barlow's disease?

**AC:** Indentations of the posterior leaflet are normal structures that facilitate the opening of the valve. They may be larger than normal in patients with Barlow's disease because of excess tissue and excess tension on the edge of the indentation, but they are usually smaller as a result of myxoid tissue proliferation, fibrosis, and, more rarely, calcification.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anterior Papillary Muscle Fibrosis and Calcification (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What is your explanation for the greater tendency of the anterior papillary muscle to fibrose and calcify in Barlow's disease?

**AC:** Your observation is correct but the exact explanation is still obscure. My hypothesis is that any tissue or cells exposed to prolonged mechanical or biological injury may exhibit a disregulation of cellular phosphocalcium metabolism, a cause of calcification. In the case you mention, shear stress plays the major role.

The position of the head of the anterior papillary muscle is close to the left ventricular outflow tract and therefore is exposed to more turbulent flow and to higher shear stress. Increased tension on the subvalvular apparatus as a result of excess tissue contributes to the process of calcification involving the two papillary muscles.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Rationale for Repairing Billowing Leaflets (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** What led you to first recommend repair of not only leaflet prolapse but also leaflet billowing in Barlow's valve?

**AC:** I always pay great attention to mathematical considerations in valve reconstruction. According to the well-known Laplace's law, the tension on the leaflet tissue and chordae is proportional to the radius of a spherical structure. To minimize the risk of recurrent mitral regurgitation, it therefore seemed logical to reduce the exaggerated curvature of both leaflets by repositioning their free margins low in the ventricle. This can be ideally achieved by papillary muscle shortening.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anterior Leaflet Billowing and Surgical Options (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** I have observed that anterior leaflet billowing is often corrected by posterior leaflet sliding plasty and annuloplasty. Do you believe papillary muscle sliding plasty or shortening is always necessary?

**AC:** It depends upon the degree of billowing and the position of the free edge of the anterior leaflet during systole. A moderate billowing can effectively be minimized by a posterior leaflet sliding plasty. In more severe cases, either a papillary muscle sliding or a shortening plasty is necessary to correct leaflet billowing, thus achieving a large surface of coaptation, a condition for long-term durability.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Posterior Leaflet Management in Isolated Anterior Prolapse (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** In patients with Barlow's disease and isolated anterior leaflet prolapse, does the posterior leaflet require repair?

**AC:** Considering that the posterior leaflet is always billowing in Barlow's disease, I think the posterior leaflet needs correction, first to prevent SAM and second to reduce leaflet and chordae tension as I just pointed out.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Reconstructive Strategy in Marfan's Disease (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Do you advise valve reconstruction in a patient with Marfan's disease who has mild to moderate mitral regurgitation and is undergoing an aortic procedure?

**AC:** Patients with Marfan's disease often have surgery at a young age so that all valve dysfunction, even mild to moderate, should be treated during the same procedure to avoid addressing the mitral problem at a later stage. A valve reconstruction is the operation of choice in this situation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Annuloplasty Strategy in Dilated Ventricles (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** Tell me about your annuloplasty strategy in a late referred degenerative patient with a severely dilated left ventricle, particularly with associated type II and type IIIb dysfunction.

**AC:** In this rare circumstance, the prolapse should be treated first so as to position the free edges of both leaflets at the same level in the ventricle. Then the type IIIb dysfunction is addressed by using a downsized ring annuloplasty, selecting a ring one to two sizes smaller than would require measurement of the anterior leaflet.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Reconstructive Benefit in Left Ventricular Dysfunction (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** The potential benefit of valve reconstruction over replacement in patients with degenerative valve disease and left ventricular dysfunction has been discussed. What is your opinion?

**AC:** Valve reconstruction is preferable for two reasons: First, it preserves the whole valvular apparatus, a condition for improved ventricular function. Second, the native valve is an active opening valve as opposed to a prosthesis; therefore the hemodynamics across the valve are improved with reduced blood stasis and reduced risk of thromboembolism.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Strategy for Extensive Annular Calcification (DA & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**DA:** A common finding in Barlow's disease is a quasi-prolapse of the commissural area including A3, PC, and P3. In this setting, I usually favor the magic suture technique. What is your preferred strategy?

**AC:** The magic suture technique is a reasonable alternative provided there is not a significant excess of commissural tissue. If this is the case, however, it is preferable to perform a sliding plasty of the commissural papillary muscle, which effectively corrects both the prolapse and the billowing while preserving optimal commissural leaflet mobility.

### Nomenclature: Disease vs. Syndrome (FF & AC)</summary><div class="details-content">

**FF:** Why do you prefer the term disease instead of syndrome to qualify Barlow's and Marfan's diseases?

**AC:** The term syndrome was used in the early clinical era before other tools such as echocardiography, advanced histopathology, and genetics became available. Today the diagnosis of these conditions mainly relies on these investigation tools, which permit identification of a disease. Therefore the term disease seems to me to be more appropriate.
