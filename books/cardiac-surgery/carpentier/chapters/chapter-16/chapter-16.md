# 16. Adjunct Left Atrial Procedures

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Clinical Indications for Adjunct Procedures
* **Spectrum of Pathologies:** Patients undergoing mitral valve reconstruction frequently present with associated left atrial disorders:
  * Left atrial thrombus formation (appendage or parietal wall);
  * Extensive left atrial wall calcification ("porcelain atrium");
  * Giant left atrium with severe hemodynamic stasis;
  * Chronic or paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF).
* **Surgical Objective:** Restore normal left atrial hemodynamics, minimize thromboembolic stroke risk, reduce atrial volume, and restore sinus rhythm.

### Surgical Principles and Techniques
* **Left Atrial Appendage (LAA) Management:** Endoatrial direct suture closure is preferred over external ligation to eliminate residual pouches; autologous pericardial patch closure is indicated if the LAA orifice is calcified.
* **Thromboendocardiectomy:** En bloc excision of organized mural thrombus and calcified endocardium; raw or thinned atrial walls are lined with a glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch.
* **Porcelain Atrium:** Calcium is left in place if the endothelial surface is regular and thrombus-free; irregular, ulcerated calcium is dissected en bloc with the knife oriented against the calcium.
* **Left Atrial Volume Reduction:** Helicoidal plication (continuous 4-0 monofilament from LAA between pulmonary veins and mitral annulus to diaphragmatic wall) or "H-plication" of Guilmet/Deloche to prevent stasis.

### Concomitant Atrial Fibrillation Ablation
* **Pathophysiological Rationale:** AF increases stroke risk 6-fold and late mortality 2-fold; sinus rhythm restoration is mandatory.
* **Lesion Sets:**
  * *Left-Sided Maze:* Bilateral pulmonary vein isolation, connecting line to the P3 mitral annulus, and line to the left atrial appendage (approx 80% success in paroxysmal AF).
  * *Biatrial Maze:* Addition of intercaval line, right appendage-to-tricuspid line, atriotomy line, and coronary sinus isthmus lesion for chronic AF or flutter (approx 80% 1-year sinus rhythm).
* **De-Airing and Safety:** Systematic use of a transmitral vent tube through the atriotomy for passive decompression and thorough de-airing prior to aortic cross-clamp release.

## LEFT ATRIAL THROMBUS FORMATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Etiology and Clinical Presentation

Patients undergoing mitral valve reconstruction often require additional procedures to address associated left atrial disorders: left atrial thrombus formation, atrial calcification, giant left atrium, and atrial fibrillation.

Thrombus formation in the left atrium ([Fig. 16-1](#fig-16-01)) is frequently observed in long-lasting rheumatic valvular disease, triggered by an enlarged left atrium, atrial fibrillation, endocardial lesions, and low cardiac output.

In degenerative valvular disease, atrial thrombus formation can be observed in elderly patients or in those with low cardiac output or hematological disorders. Atrial fibrillation is the major contributing factor for atrial thrombus formation in these conditions.

<span id="fig-16-01"></span>![](_page_167_Figure_16-1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 16-1** Left atrial thrombus patterns in mitral valve disease: localized organized thrombus in the left atrial appendage (a); extensive parietal thrombus covering the posterior and lateral walls of the enlarged left atrium (b).

#### Left Atrial Appendage Thrombus and Closure Techniques

Left atrial appendage thrombus is the most common location (a). The thrombus is usually organized and adherent to the endothelium. It should be completely removed before closing the orifice of the atrial appendage.

The absence of an intraoperative visible thrombus does not preclude the existence of thrombus formation during the course of the disease, in particular in the presence of a large appendage, an irregular endocardial surface, or a history of stroke.

In these circumstances, the left atrial appendage should also be closed. The closure of the left atrial appendage is performed in most instances by endoatrial direct suturing. This technique is preferred to external ligation, which may not completely occlude the base of the appendage, a cause of recurrent thrombus formation. Whenever the orifice of the atrial appendage is calcified, the closure is carried out using a pericardial patch.

#### Extended Mural Thrombus and Thromboendocardiectomy

Extended thrombus covering a large surface of the left atrium can be approached using different techniques. Whenever the thrombus can be separated easily from the left atrial wall, it should be removed, excising occasional endothelial bands.

Whenever the thrombus cannot be separated easily, it can be left in place provided that its surface is regular.

Whenever an irregular surface with or without ulcerations or spots of calcification is present, the thrombus should be removed en bloc. This thromboendocardiectomy may leave areas of thin atrial tissue or a block of calcification, which should be covered by a large pericardial patch ([Fig. 16-2](#fig-16-02)).

## ATRIAL CALCIFICATION (PORCELAIN ATRIUM)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Pathology and Diagnostic Evaluation

Atrial calcification may or may not be associated with thrombus formation.

Extensive calcification may involve the entire atrium with the orifices of the mitral valve and pulmonary veins usually intact ([Fig. 16-3](#fig-16-03)).

This condition, often termed "porcelain atrium," is observed primarily in advanced rheumatic disease or in patients with dialysis-dependent chronic renal failure exhibiting altered phosphocalcium metabolism.

<span id="fig-16-03"></span>![](_page_168_Figure_16-3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 16-3** Massive left atrial calcification ("porcelain atrium"): lateral chest radiograph showing dense, circumferential calcification of the left atrial wall sparing the mitral and pulmonary vein orifices.

#### Surgical Strategy and Calcium Dissection Technique

If the surface is regular and free from thrombus, the calcium formation should be left in place.

If the surface is irregular with adherent thrombus, the calcium formation is preferably dissected from the atrial wall. The dissecting knife should be oriented so as to remain in contact with the calcium at all times.

Usually, calcification is minimal at the mitral valve orifice and the pulmonary valve ostia. At these levels, the endocardium is simply cut to remove the calcium en bloc. Following this procedure, it is advisable to reduce the left atrium size to improve atrial flow.

## VOLUME REDUCTION FOR GIANT LEFT ATRIUM</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Hemodynamic Consequences of Left Atrial Megalotriatrium

The volume of a severely enlarged left atrium should be reduced as much as possible to improve atrial flow and to minimize the risk of thrombus formation ([Fig. 16-4](#fig-16-04)).

A severely dilated left atrium leads to marked blood stasis, progressive wall thinning, and elevated risks of thromboembolism and low cardiac output syndrome.

A variety of surgical reduction techniques have been described to restore laminar flow and favorable chamber geometry.

<span id="fig-16-04"></span>![](_page_169_Figure_16-4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 16-4** Left atrial volume reduction techniques for giant left atrium: helicoidal plication along the posterior wall between the pulmonary veins and mitral annulus (a); "H-plication" with superior and inferior horizontal tiers connected by a vertical transverse bridge (b).

#### Helicoidal Plication and H-Plication Methods

The preferred technique is a helicoidal plication of the atrium using 4-0 monofilament continuous sutures starting at the appendage, progressing between the left pulmonary vein ostia and the posterior mitral annulus, and then plicating the diaphragmatic part of the left atrium (a).

Another more complex alternative is "H plication" of the left atrium (b). In this technique, one horizontal branch of plication involves the atrial tissue between the left pulmonary veins and the mitral valve. Care should be taken not to injure the circumflex vessels.

The other horizontal branch is the extended atriotomy. The transverse branch joins the two horizontal branches between the superior and the inferior right pulmonary veins. The left atriotomy is then closed with large sutures reinforced with Teflon strips.

## SURGICAL MANAGEMENT OF ATRIAL FIBRILLATION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Electrophysiological Basis and Stroke Risk

Recent advances in the understanding of the pathogenesis of atrial fibrillation have been critical for the development of the surgical treatment of this disorder. Atrial fibrillation is believed to arise from enhanced automaticity at one or more rapidly depolarizing foci or re-entry involving one or more circuits.

Haissaguerre and colleagues have shown that these foci originate predominantly at the origin of the pulmonary veins, particularly in patients with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation. Infrequently, foci may also occur in the right atrium, the superior vena cava, or the coronary sinus.

Patients with atrial fibrillation are at a 6-fold increased risk of stroke and a 2-fold increased risk of late mortality. Therefore every effort should be made at the time of valve reconstruction to restore sinus rhythm.

#### Evolution of Ablation Techniques and Left-Sided Maze

The classic Cox Maze III cut and sew technique is efficacious but associated with increased postoperative morbidity, particularly bleeding.

Today, the Cox Maze procedure has benefited from improved techniques ([Fig. 16-5](#fig-16-05)) using a variety of energy sources and lesion sets.

The lesion set we prefer isolates the pulmonary veins (a) with a connecting lesion between the inferior left pulmonary vein and the P3 region of the mitral annulus (b). A connecting lesion to the left atrial appendage may be associated (c).

<span id="fig-16-05"></span>![](_page_169_Figure_16-5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 16-5** Surgical ablation lesion sets for atrial fibrillation (Cox-Maze IV modification): left atrial lesion set comprising bilateral pulmonary vein isolation (a), mitral isthmus line to P3 annulus (b), and left atrial appendage isolation line (c); extended biatrial lesions including intercaval line (d), right atrial appendage to anterior tricuspid annulus (e), tricuspid commissural line (f), and coronary sinus isthmus lesion (g).

#### Biatrial Lesions and Clinical Outcomes

Right-sided lesions are added in cases of chronic atrial fibrillation or for the treatment of atrial flutter.

One lesion extends from the superior to the inferior vena cava (d), another lesion extends from the right atrial appendage to the anterior segment of the tricuspid annulus (e), a third lesion extends from the anteroposterior commissure of the tricuspid valve superiorly to the atriotomy (f), and the final lesion extends from the interatrial septum up to the caudal aspect of the coronary sinus (g).

Following this strategy, we have documented an 80 percent rate of normal sinus rhythm at 1-year follow-up.

## DISCUSSION</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Atrial Wall Calcification and Anticoagulation

> **DA:** What are the most common causes of atrial wall calcification?
>
> **AC:** The two conditions in which atrial calcification is observed are rheumatic valve disease and dialysis-dependent renal failure. In rheumatic patients, atrial calcification usually follows thrombus formation, which occurs frequently in patients with long-lasting atrial fibrillation and enlarged left atrium in the absence of anticoagulation treatment. In renal failure, calcification usually results from altered phosphocalcium metabolism.

> **FF:** In a patient in whom an atrial thrombus has been completely removed and the left atrial appendage has been closed while the valve has been effectively treated by reconstructive valve surgery, do you recommend the discontinuation of long-term anticoagulation?
>
> **AC:** The patient should be committed to anticoagulation at least for the first year. Then, anticoagulation can be discontinued under the following conditions: the patient remains in sinus rhythm with no thrombus at echo; there is no residual valve regurgitation or stenosis; and the patient has subnormal left atrial size and good ventricular function. This recommendation is particularly relevant in patients at risk for bleeding. However, even in these cases treatment with aspirin should be prescribed.

#### Patient Selection for the Maze Procedure

> **DA:** Are there any circumstances in which you would not perform a Maze procedure in patients with chronic atrial fibrillation undergoing mitral valve reconstruction?
>
> **AC:** The duration of atrial fibrillation is not a contraindication to a Maze procedure. In fact, many patients can be converted to sinus rhythm after many years of atrial fibrillation. Rather than the duration of atrial fibrillation, the conditions of the left atrium should be considered. Patients with a giant, fibrotic, and calcified left atrium are unlikely to be converted to sinus rhythm. In these circumstances, I do not perform a Maze procedure.

> **FF:** Do you always prefer a biatrial Maze procedure? Are there any circumstances where a left-sided Maze procedure is sufficient?
>
> **AC:** A left-sided Maze procedure may be sufficient in patients with paroxysmal atrial fibrillation and is associated with a success rate of approximately 80 percent. A biatrial Maze procedure is recommended in patients with persistent or chronic atrial fibrillation as well as in patients with a history of atrial flutter.

> **FF:** Should a Maze procedure be considered in patients receiving a mechanical mitral valve?
>
> **AC:** Despite the absence of well-documented data for this patient group, knowing that the risk of thromboembolism is increased in patients with atrial fibrillation, it is reasonable to perform a Maze operation in patients receiving a mechanical valve.

#### Transmitral Venting and De-Airing Protocol

> **FF:** For many years, you have recommended the placement of a transmitral left ventricular tube through the atriotomy at the time of closure. Are there any circumstances in which you would not use a ventricular vent?
>
> **AC:** I routinely use a transmitral tube in all open left-heart procedures to keep the mitral valve incompetent, allowing retrograde passive de-airing through the left atriotomy as the first step of the de-airing process. In addition, in patients with severe ventricular dysfunction the tube allows passive decompression of the left ventricle so that the surgeon is not obligated to vent it actively, a cause of penetration of air through the left atrium.

> **FF:** You have always emphasized de-airing cardiac chambers while the aortic cross-clamp is still in place. Can you explain your rationale?
>
> **AC:** I have often been surprised by the low incidence of neurological events in our center compared to what has been reported commonly in the literature. Because of the aging of the population, embolization from calcification involving the ascending aorta has been incriminated as the primary cause of this event. I believe that many neurological events and early right ventricular failure are due to air embolization to the head vessels and the right coronary artery. The incidence of these complications can effectively be reduced by the triphase approach I described in this chapter. The systematic use of intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography is instrumental to verify the completeness of the de-airing, which may take a prolonged time in some patients.

#### Prevention of Retraction Injuries and Postoperative Anticoagulation

> **DA:** Describe the possible injuries that can occur from the retraction of the left atrial wall with blades during surgery.
>
> **AC:** A systematic inspection of the atrial endothelium and wall should be undertaken before left atrial closure in order to detect the following possible injuries:
> 1. Endothelial tear;
> 2. Perforation of the interatrial septum;
> 3. Injury of the conduction tissue that can become evident on postoperative electrocardiogram.

> **FF:** What is your current postoperative anticoagulant regimen following a Maze procedure?
>
> **AC:** I remain precautious and in the absence of contraindications give the patients anticoagulants for 6 months regardless of the postoperative rhythm.
