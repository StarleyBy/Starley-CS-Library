# Pericardial Disease, Cardiac Tumours and Cardiac Trauma

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Pericardial Tamponade

*   **Clinical features:** Haemodynamic compromise, dyspnoea, tachypnoea, low pulse volume, **pulsus paradoxus** (fall in systolic BP >10 mm Hg on inspiration), raised JVP with prominent x descent but no y descent, muffled heart sounds, oliguria/anuria.
*   **Pulsus paradoxus mechanism:** Increased inspiratory venous return → right heart filling → interventricular septum displaces leftward (rigid pericardium) → reduced left heart filling → decreased cardiac output and BP.
*   **ECG findings:** Low voltage QRS complexes, **electrical alternans** (heart swinging in fluid-filled sac).
*   **CXR:** Symmetrical globular enlargement of the heart ("water bottle" shape).
*   **Echocardiography:** Large pericardial collection, swinging heart, early diastolic RV collapse, late diastolic RA collapse, marked respiratory variation (tricuspid >40%, mitral >25%).
*   **Treatment:** Paraxiphoid pericardiocentesis, subxiphoid drainage, thoracotomy with pericardial window, or re-opening of sternotomy (post-op).

### Constrictive Pericarditis

*   **Causes:** Idiopathic (most common in developed world), infection (TB, viral, fungal), mediastinal radiotherapy, post-cardiac surgery (0.2–0.3%), rare (tumour, drugs, sarcoidosis, amyloidosis, uraemia, trauma, MI).
*   **Clinical features:** Symptoms of left and right heart failure, raised JVP, ascites, peripheral oedema, hepatosplenomegaly, displaced apex beat, muffled heart sounds, narrowed pulse pressure, Kussmaul's sign.
*   **Diagnostic findings:**
    *   CXR: pericardial calcification, bilateral pleural effusions
    *   Echocardiography: impaired diastolic filling, thickened pericardium, dilated RA/IVC/hepatic veins, increased E:A ratio
    *   CT/MRI: thickened pericardium >4 mm (normal 1–2 mm)
    *   Cardiac catheterisation: equalisation of LVEDP and RVEDP (<5 mm Hg), square root sign (dip and plateau), RVEDP/RVSP ratio >1:3
*   **Differentiation from restrictive cardiomyopathy (RCM):**
    *   RCM more likely: pulmonary hypertension (PAP >50 mm Hg), reduced LV function (EF <40%), infiltrative process on biopsy
    *   CP more likely: equalisation of LVEDP/RVEDP (<5 mm Hg), Kussmaul's sign, RVEDP/RVSP ratio >1:3, thickened pericardium on imaging
*   **Pericardiectomy:** Complete removal of thickened pericardium and epicardium from ventricles and diaphragm, preserving both phrenic nerves. Operative mortality 10–15%, 5-year survival 70%.

### Restrictive Cardiomyopathy (SAILS)

*   **S**cleroderma
*   **A**myloidosis
*   **I**ron storage diseases (haemochromatosis)
*   **L**oeffler's eosinophilic endocarditis / endomyocardial fibrosis
*   **S**arcoidosis

### Cardiac Tumours

*   **Classification:** Primary tumours (75% benign, 25% malignant); secondary tumours (metastatic or direct spread) are **30 times more common**.
*   **Benign tumours:**
    *   **Myxoma** (most common benign in adults): 75% left atrium, 20% right atrium, 5% ventricles/valves
    *   **Lipoma:** Well-encapsulated, obese elderly females, right atrium
    *   **Fibroelastoma:** Cardiac valves (mitral/aortic), frond-like projections, embolisation risk
    *   **Rhabdomyoma:** Most common benign in children, multicentric, ventricles
*   **Malignant tumours:**
    *   **Angiosarcoma:** Atria, presents with caval/valvular obstruction, distant metastases common, median survival ~6 months
    *   **Rhabdomyosarcoma:** Multicentric, all locations, median survival ~12 months
*   **Carney's syndrome:** Autosomal dominant familial atrial myxoma; male predominance, earlier onset (childhood), multicentric (65%), recurrence (40%); associated with adrenocortical nodules, Sertoli cell tumours, spotty pigmentation.

### Cardiac Myxoma — Surgical Principles

*   **Approach:** Median sternotomy, bicaval cannulation, minimal cardiac manipulation
*   **Incision:** Left atriotomy, transseptal, or Dubost bi-atrial incision
*   **Resection:** En bloc with clear margins; interatrial septum defect closed with pericardial patch
*   **Irrigation:** Saline washout of residual cells (avoid cell-saver or bypass suction)
*   **Recurrence rate:** <5% (inadequate resection, tumour seeding, multicentric tumours)

### Traumatic Aortic Rupture

*   **Pathophysiology:** Deceleration injury → strain at anatomical fixation points. 80–90% at **aortic isthmus** (between left subclavian artery and ligamentum arteriosum). 80–90% die at scene; 50% of survivors die within 48 hours.
*   **Clinical features:** Consider in severe blunt thoracic trauma with upper rib (1st–3rd), sternal, or scapula fractures; retrosternal/interscapular pain; haemodynamic compromise; peri-aortic haematoma causing dysphagia, stridor, hoarseness.
*   **CXR findings:** Widened (>8 cm) superior mediastinum, obliterated aortic knob, left apical pleural cap, left haemothorax, tracheal/oesophageal deviation to right, left hilum displaced downwards.
*   **CT findings:** Aortic lumen disruption, contrast extravasation, peri-aortic haematoma.
*   **Surgical options:**
    *   **Clamp and sew:** No heparin, but paraplegia risk if clamp time >30 minutes
    *   **Gott shunt:** Heparin-bonded tubing from ascending aorta/LV apex to descending aorta/femoral artery
    *   **Left heart bypass:** Heparin-bonded tubing from LAA to femoral artery with centrifugal pump (~2 L/min), no systemic heparin
    *   **Total cardiopulmonary bypass:** Femoral vein to femoral artery, systemic heparin required
    *   **Endovascular stenting:** Consider as alternative

### Emergency Resuscitative Thoracotomy (ATLS)

*   **Indication:** Penetrating trauma (not blunt) within 10 minutes of PEA
*   **Approach:** Left anterolateral thoracotomy through 4th intercostal space
*   **Therapeutic options:** Pericardial evacuation, open cardiac massage, descending aortic clamping

### Blunt Cardiac Trauma

*   **Management:** ATLS guidelines, monitor with serial ECGs, echocardiography, cardiac enzymes, CXR, CT
*   **Potential injuries:** Left pleuropericardial tears, right atrial disruption (caval junctions), atrial/ventricular septal defects, aortic regurgitation

### Penetrating Cardiac Trauma

*   **Diagnosis:** Clinical signs (haemodynamic compromise, Beck's triad, Kussmaul's sign, pulsus paradoxus) and echocardiography
*   **Treatment:** Prompt decompression (paraxiphoid pericardiocentesis) then operative closure
*   **Access:** Left anterolateral thoracotomy (emergency) or median sternotomy (if time permits)
*   **Technique:** Open pericardium, evacuate clot, open cardiac massage, direct suture of lacerations (Teflon® strips for buttressing). Coronary artery lacerations may require ligation and bypass grafting.

## 1. Pericardial Tamponade</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Clinical Features

*   Haemodynamic compromise with acute dyspnoea and tachypnoea
*   Low pulse volume with pulsus paradoxus
*   Low or undetectable blood pressure
*   Raised jugular venous pulse (JVP) with prominent **x descent** but **no y descent**
*   Kussmaul's sign (inspiratory rise of JVP) is **not common**
*   Muffled heart sounds
*   Oliguria or anuria

#### Pulsus Paradoxus

Pulsus paradoxus is defined as an abnormally excessive fall in systolic blood pressure on inspiration **>10 mm Hg**.

![](_page_0_Figure_13.jpeg)

**Figure 1. Pulsus paradoxus.**

**Normal physiology:** On inspiration there is a slight fall in systolic blood pressure (<5 mm Hg).

**Mechanism in cardiac tamponade:**
The increased venous return that occurs on inspiration causes filling of the right heart, thereby displacing the interventricular septum to the left (rigid box with tamponade), resulting in less filling possible in the left heart, producing reduced cardiac output and systemic blood pressure.

**Other causes of pulsus paradoxus:**
*   Constrictive pericarditis
*   Status asthmaticus

#### Investigations

**ECG:**
*   Low voltage QRS complexes
*   **Electrical alternans** (due to the heart moving within a fluid-filled sac)

![](_page_1_Figure_7.jpeg)

**Figure 2. A) Chest radiograph and B) echocardiogram (apical 4 chamber) demonstrating a large pericardial collection. M-mode echocardiography demonstrating marked respiratory C) tricuspid valve and D) mitral valve inflow variation.**

**CXR:**
*   Symmetrical globular enlargement of the heart

**Echocardiography:**
*   Large pericardial collection
*   Heart swinging freely within the pericardial sac
*   Early diastolic collapse of the right ventricle
*   Late diastolic collapse of the right atrium
*   Marked respiratory tricuspid valve (>40%) and mitral valve (>25%) inflow variation

#### Treatment

Depending on the stability of the patient and the underlying cause:
*   Paraxiphoid pericardiocentesis
*   Subxiphoid drainage of the pericardial collection
*   Anterolateral thoracotomy drainage with a pericardial window
*   Re-opening of median sternotomy

## 2. Constrictive Pericarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Causes

*   **Idiopathic** — the major cause in the developed world
*   **Infection** — tuberculosis, viral (Coxsackie), fungal (Histoplasma)
*   **Mediastinal radiotherapy** — dose-dependent (e.g., for lymphoma)
*   **Post-cardiac surgery** — incidence 0.2–0.3%
*   **Rare causes** — tumour (mesothelioma), drugs (procainamide, hydralazine), sarcoidosis, amyloidosis, carcinoid syndrome, uraemia, trauma, myocardial infarction

#### Clinical Features

*   Symptoms of left and right heart failure: dyspnoea, fatigue, weakness, anorexia, peripheral oedema, ascites
*   Raised jugular venous pressure
*   Ascites
*   Peripheral oedema
*   Hepatosplenomegaly
*   Displaced apex beat
*   Muffled heart sounds
*   Narrowed pulse pressure
*   Kussmaul's sign
*   Pulsus paradoxus may be present

#### Diagnostic Findings

**CXR:**
*   Pericardial calcification
*   Bilateral pleural effusions

**Echocardiography:**
*   Impaired diastolic ventricular filling
*   Thickened echo-bright pericardium
*   Dilated right atrium, inferior vena cava, and hepatic veins

**Doppler echocardiography:**
*   Increased E:A ratio (rapid early filling and diastasis)
*   Decreased inspiratory flow reduction in the hepatic veins

**Coronary angiography:**
*   Radiation-induced coronary artery disease

**CT/MRI:**
*   Thickened pericardium **>4 mm** (normal thickness 1–2 mm)

![](_page_3_Figure_8.jpeg)

**Figure 3. A, B) Computed tomography and C) echocardiography demonstrating calcified pericardium associated with constrictive pericarditis (arrows). D) Echocardiography demonstrating a dilated inferior vena cava on subcostal transthoracic views.**

**Cardiac catheterisation — allows differentiation from restrictive cardiomyopathy:**
*   Equalisation (within 5 mm Hg) of raised left and right ventricular end-diastolic pressures at any phase of respiration
*   Equalisation of raised (>10 mm Hg) left and right atrial pressures with prominent x and y descents
*   **Square root sign** — dip and plateau pattern of ventricular pressure; most diastolic filling occurs in early diastole (due to raised venous pressure), then halts abruptly in mid-diastole
*   Left ventricular systolic function is usually normal but may be impaired in severe cases
*   Pulmonary artery systolic pressure <50 mm Hg
*   Right ventricular end-diastolic to systolic pressure ratio **>1:3**
*   These findings can be masked by hypovolaemia but revealed with rapid fluid transfusion

#### Differentiation of Constrictive Pericarditis (CP) from Restrictive Cardiomyopathy (RCM)

**Features occurring in both RCM and CP:**
*   Increased EA ratio on mitral valve inflow pattern
*   Dip and plateau ventricular waveform
*   Prominent x and y descents on atrial waveform

**Patients with RCM are more likely to have:**
*   Pulmonary hypertension (pulmonary artery systolic pressure >50 mm Hg)
*   Reduced left ventricular function (EF <40%)
*   Endomyocardial biopsy evidence of an infiltrative process

**Patients with CP are more likely to have:**
*   Equalisation of left and right ventricular end-diastolic pressure (<5 mm Hg)
*   Kussmaul's sign
*   Right ventricular end-diastolic to systolic pressure ratio >1:3
*   CT or echocardiographic evidence of a thickened pericardium

#### Pericardiectomy

**Aim:** Complete removal of all thickened pericardium and epicardium from the left and right ventricles and diaphragm, whilst preserving both phrenic nerves.

**Technique:** Cardiopulmonary bypass and cardioplegic arrest are usually required for clearance of the posterior pericardium.

**Note:** Although incomplete resection of pericardium overlying the atria risks residual constrictive haemodynamics, removal of densely adherent atrial tissue is not always necessary as the pathophysiology mainly affects ventricular filling.

**Outcomes:**
*   Operative mortality: **10–15%**
*   5-year survival: **70%**

## 4. Cardiac Neoplasms</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Classification

*   **Primary tumours** — 75% benign, 25% malignant
*   **Secondary tumours** — metastatic spread (lymphoma, breast, lung, melanoma, sarcoma) and direct spread (oesophagus, lung, breast, thymus) — **30 times more common** than primary cardiac tumours
*   **Subdiaphragmatic tumours** (kidney, uterus, liver, adrenals) may invade the atrium via the inferior vena cava

#### Primary Benign Cardiac Tumours

| Tumour | Characteristics |
|--------|-----------------|
| **Myxoma** | See detailed section below |
| **Lipoma** | Well-encapsulated tumours throughout the heart (especially right atrium); common in obese, elderly, female patients; surgery indicated for obstruction |
| **Fibroelastoma** | Occur on cardiac valves (especially mitral and aortic) or ventricles; frond-like projections; can embolise; treated by resection |
| **Rhabdomyoma** | Most common benign primary cardiac tumour in children; multicentric, pedunculated tumours in either ventricle; obstructive symptoms, valvular regurgitation, or arrhythmias |
| **Other** | Fibroma, haemangioma, teratoma, neurofibroma, lymphangioma |

![](_page_8_Picture_8.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Fibroelastoma.**

#### Primary Malignant Cardiac Tumours

| Tumour | Characteristics |
|--------|-----------------|
| **Angiosarcoma** | Frequently located in the atria; presents with caval or valvular obstruction; distant metastases common at presentation; poor prognosis — median survival ~6 months |
| **Rhabdomyosarcoma** | Multicentric tumours occurring in all locations throughout the heart; treated by resection + adjuvant chemotherapy/radiotherapy; median survival ~12 months |
| **Other** | Fibrous histiocytoma, fibrosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, liposarcoma |

**Note:** Most sarcomas are incurable, but cardiac transplantation can be considered for those without metastatic spread.

## 5. Cardiac Myxomas</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Epidemiology

*   Incidence: **1:500,000** (5% familial)
*   Most common age: **40–60 years**
*   Female predominance: **70%**
*   Account for 40% of benign cardiac tumours in adults and 15% in children
*   Location:
    *   Left atrium: **75%** (usually originating from interatrial septum)
    *   Right atrium: 20%
    *   Ventricles or attached to valves: 5%

#### Carney's Syndrome

*   Autosomal dominant familial form of atrial myxoma
*   Male predominance
*   Earlier onset than non-familial form (often childhood)
*   Commonly occur in both atria
*   **Multicentric** (65%) with frequent **recurrence** (40%)
*   Associated with:
    *   Adrenocortical nodular masses
    *   Sertoli cell testicular tumours
    *   Spotty mucocutaneous pigmentation

![](_page_10_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Myxoma.** A) Within the cavity of the left atrium attached to the interatrial septum. B) Following resection with attached interatrial septum.

#### Pathology

*   Neoplasms of endocardial origin, derived from pluripotent mesenchymal cells or endocardial nerve cells
*   Macroscopic: ovoid, pedunculated masses, friable, mucoid, gray-white, smooth or granular
*   Histological: acid mucopolysaccharide matrix with polygonal cells, capillary-like structures, and haemorrhagic areas

#### Clinical Features

Patients present with symptoms related to:

*   **Obstruction to blood flow**
*   **Interference with valvular function**
*   **Systemic embolisation**
*   **Constitutional symptoms** — fever, weight loss, malaise, myalgia, anaemia

**Auscultation findings:**
*   Diastolic murmur (mimics mitral or tricuspid stenosis)
*   Early diastolic tumour "plop"
*   Pan-systolic murmur of mitral or tricuspid regurgitation ("wrecking ball" effect)

![](_page_11_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 8. Echocardiographic image demonstrating a left atrial myxoma attached to the interatrial septum.**

#### Surgical Principles for Left Atrial Myxomas

**Approach:**
*   Median sternotomy
*   Cardiopulmonary bypass with bicaval cannulation and snares
*   **Minimal cardiac manipulation** to avoid tumour embolisation

**Incision options:**
*   Standard left atriotomy
*   Transseptal atrial incision
*   Dubost bi-atrial incision

**Resection:**
*   Tumour resected **en bloc** with clear resection margins
*   If interatrial septum involved, defect closed with pericardial patch

**Irrigation:**
*   Irrigate atrial cavity with saline to wash away residual neoplastic cells
*   **Avoid using cell-saver or bypass suction**

**Assessment:**
*   Assess valvular function for "wrecking ball" effect causing regurgitation

**Recurrence rate: <5%** due to:
*   Inadequate resection
*   Tumour seeding at primary operation
*   Multicentric tumours

## 6. Traumatic Aortic Rupture (Aortic Transection)</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Pathophysiology

*   Usually occurs following a **deceleration injury**, causing strain at points of anatomical fixation of the thoracic aorta
*   The descending aorta is held in place by the intercostal arteries and the ligamentum arteriosum
*   **Aortic isthmus** (between left subclavian artery and ligamentum arteriosum) is the site of transection in **80–90%** of cases; remainder at origin of aortic arch branches
*   **80–90%** of patients die at the scene of injury
*   **50% of survivors** die within the next 48 hours
*   The adventitia and surrounding fascia can maintain luminal continuity
*   If other life-threatening injuries require treatment first, systolic BP should be kept **<110 mm Hg**

#### Clinical Features

*   Diagnosis should be considered in all patients with severe blunt thoracic trauma, especially those with:
    *   Upper rib fractures (1st–3rd)
    *   Sternal fractures
    *   Scapula fractures
*   Retrosternal or interscapular pain (stretching/dissection of aortic adventitia)
*   Haemodynamic compromise — tachycardia, hypotension, oliguria
*   Peri-aortic haematoma causing compression of surrounding structures → dysphagia, stridor, dyspnoea, or hoarseness

#### Radiological Findings

**Chest radiograph:**
*   Widened (>8 cm) superior mediastinum (note: most trauma CXRs are AP projection, which artificially enlarges the mediastinal silhouette)
*   Fractures of the superior thoracic cage (1st–3rd ribs, sternum, scapula)
*   Obliteration of the aortic knob
*   Left apical pleural cap
*   Left pleural effusion or haemothorax
*   Deviation of the oesophagus (nasogastric tube) to the right
*   Deviation of the trachea (endotracheal tube) to the right
*   Displacement of the left hilum downwards
*   Widening of the paratracheal and paraspinal stripe

![](_page_13_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_13_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 9. A) Computed tomography and B) angiography images demonstrating transection (arrow) of the proximal descending aorta (just distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery), with extravasation of contrast and an irregular luminal contour.**

**CT scan:**
*   Disruption of the aortic lumen
*   Extravasation of radio-opaque contrast
*   Peri-aortic haematoma

#### Surgical Options

**Positioning:** Left thoracotomy with pelvis slightly rotated to gain access to left femoral vessels if required.

**Dissection:** The aortic isthmus is carefully dissected. Following clamping of the aortic arch (between left subclavian and left common carotid arteries), the left subclavian artery, and the distal descending thoracic aorta, an interposition graft is anastomosed.

**Adjunct techniques:**

| Technique | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|-----------|-------------|------------|---------------|
| **Clamp and sew** | No cardiopulmonary bypass, no systemic heparin | Avoids heparinisation | Increased paraplegia risk if clamp time >30 min |
| **Gott shunt** | Heparin-bonded tubing from ascending aorta/LV apex to descending aorta/femoral artery | Bypasses clamped segment | Requires heparin-bonded shunt |
| **Left heart bypass** | Heparin-bonded tubing from LAA to femoral artery with centrifugal pump (~2 L/min), no oxygenator | No systemic heparin, better organ perfusion | Requires pump |
| **Total cardiopulmonary bypass** | Femoral vein to femoral artery | Better collateral control, improved visualisation | Requires systemic heparin |

**Endovascular stenting** should also be considered.

![](_page_15_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 10. A) Gott shunt bypassing blood from the left atrium to the descending aorta using heparin-bonded tubing. B) Total cardiopulmonary bypass from the femoral vein to the femoral artery. C) Completed descending thoracic aortic interposition graft.**

## 7. Emergency Resuscitative Thoracotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Indications (ATLS® Guidelines)

*   Following **penetrating trauma** (not blunt trauma)
*   Within **10 minutes** of pulseless electrical activity (PEA)

#### Technique

*   Left anterolateral thoracotomy through the **4th intercostal space**

#### Therapeutic Options

*   Evacuation of the pericardium
*   Open cardiac massage
*   Clamping of the descending aorta (to stop distal bleeding and increase coronary and cerebral perfusion)

## 9. Penetrating Cardiac Trauma</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Diagnosis

*   Prompt diagnosis of cardiac tamponade is essential
*   Clinical signs:
    *   Haemodynamic compromise
    *   Beck's triad (hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds)
    *   Kussmaul's sign
    *   Pulsus paradoxus
*   Echocardiography

#### Treatment

1.  **Prompt decompression** — paraxiphoid pericardiocentesis
2.  **Operative closure** of the penetrating cardiac wound

#### Surgical Access

*   **Emergency situations:** Left anterolateral thoracotomy
*   **If time permits:** Median sternotomy (better access to pericardium and great vessels)

#### Operative Technique

*   Open the pericardium
*   Evacuate any clots or fluid
*   Perform open cardiac massage
*   Occlude any bleeding point
*   Atrial and ventricular lacerations: **direct suture**, may be buttressed with Teflon® strips
*   Coronary artery lacerations: may need ligation and bypass grafting
*   **Important:** If a posterior cardiac injury is observed, avoid air embolism when lifting the heart
