# Mitral Valve Disease

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Mitral Regurgitation (MR) — Natural History and Pathophysiology
*   **Asymptomatic period** — chronic MR well tolerated if LV function preserved.
*   **Poor prognostic features:** symptoms >1 year, AF, age >60, EF <50%, LVEDV >100 mL/m², LVESV >60 mL/m², LVESD >5 cm, LVEDD >7 cm.
*   **Pathophysiology:** volume overload → eccentric LV hypertrophy → Frank-Starling maintains SV → eventual contractile decline → ↑ LVESV, ↑ filling pressures, pulmonary congestion. EF <60% indicates systolic dysfunction (afterload-reducing effect of MR).

### Carpentier Functional Classification (Figure 1)
| Type | Leaflet Motion | Examples |
|------|---------------|----------|
| I | Normal | Annular dilation, leaflet perforation |
| II | Excessive | Myxomatous degeneration, chordal rupture |
| IIIa | Restricted opening | Rheumatic fever |
| IIIb | Restricted closure | Ischaemic cardiomyopathy |

### Causes of MR
*   **Chronic:** myxomatous degeneration (Type II), rheumatic fever (Type IIIa), ischaemic cardiomyopathy (Type IIIb + Type I).
*   **Acute:** chordal rupture (Type II), endocarditis (Type I), papillary muscle rupture post-MI (Type II).

### Ischaemic MR
*   **Acute:** infarcted/ruptured papillary muscle (Type II).
*   **Chronic:** restricted P2/P3 motion (LV dilation displacing papillary muscles — Type IIIb) + annular dilation (Type I) + reduced annular contraction + reduced closing forces.

### Symptoms of MR
*   Fatigue, weakness (low output), dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND, eventually pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure.

### Signs of MR
*   Displaced volume-loaded apex, apical thrill, S₃, pansystolic murmur radiating to axilla, diastolic flow murmur.

### MR Severity (AHA Guidelines — Table 1)
| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jet area (% LA) | <20% | 20–40% | >40% |
| Vena contracta (cm) | <0.3 | 0.3–0.7 | >0.7 |
| Regurgitant volume (mL) | <30 | 30–60 | >60 |
| Regurgitant fraction (%) | <30 | 30–50 | >50 |
| EROA (cm²) | <0.2 | 0.2–0.4 | >0.4 |

For ischaemic MR: regurgitant volume >30 mL, EROA >0.2 cm² = severe.

### Indications for Mitral Valve Surgery in MR (AHA)
*   **Class I:** severe MR with symptoms (NYHA II–IV) or LV changes (ESD >40 mm, EF <60%).
*   **Class IIa:** severe MR, asymptomatic, good LV function, repair likelihood >90%; severe MR with AF or pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg rest, >60 mmHg exercise).
*   **Class IIb:** severe MR, poor LV function (EF <30%), symptoms (NYHA III–IV) despite maximal medical therapy.

### TOE Indications in Severe MR (AHA)
*   **Class I:** intra-operative (pre-bypass for repair feasibility, post-bypass for repair success).
*   **Class IIa:** pre-operative to guide repair likelihood (>90%).

### Mitral Valve Prolapse (Barlow’s Disease)
*   >2 mm billowing beyond annular plane. Associated with Marfan syndrome.
*   Histology: myxomatous proliferation in zona spongiosa → chordal thinning/elongation.
*   Surgery per AHA guidelines for non-ischaemic MR.

### Mitral Stenosis (MS) — Natural History
*   Symptoms on exertion when MVA <2.5 cm², at rest when <1.5 cm².
*   Progression: MVA reduces 0.1–0.3 cm²/year. Rheumatic fever to signs: 10–20 years; signs to mild symptoms: 10–20 years; mild to decompensation: 5–10 years.
*   10-year survival: 80% (NYHA I/II), 10–15% (NYHA III/IV). Pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg) → mean survival 3 years.

### Pathophysiology of MS
*   Restricted LV inflow → ↑ LA pressure → back pressure to pulmonary circulation → dyspnoea → pulmonary hypertension → RV failure → LV failure.
*   Tachycardia/AF worsen haemodynamics (reduced diastolic filling period).

### Causes of MS
*   Majority: rheumatic fever (Lancefield group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus).
*   Rare: parachute mitral valve, endocardial fibroelastosis, carcinoid.
*   Pathophysiology mimics: cor triatriatum, LA myxoma, pulmonary vein stenosis.

### Symptoms of MS
*   Fatigue, dyspnoea, orthopnoea, PND.
*   LA distension: AF (thromboembolism), Ortner’s syndrome (hoarseness — L recurrent laryngeal nerve), dysphagia, left lung collapse.
*   Pulmonary hypertension/RV failure: peripheral oedema, ascites, haemoptysis.

### Signs of MS
*   Low volume pulse, irregular pulse, tapping non-displaced apex, opening snap, loud S₁, mid-diastolic rumbling murmur (apex).
*   Pulmonary hypertension: mitral facies, central cyanosis, loud P₂, TR (pan-systolic at R sternal edge), PR (Graham Steel — early diastolic on inspiration).

### MS Severity (AHA Guidelines — Table 2)
| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| MVA (cm²) | >1.5 | 1.0–1.5 | <1.0 |
| Mean gradient (mmHg) | <5 | 5–10 | >10 |
| PAP systolic (mmHg) | <30 | 30–50 | >50 |

### MVA Calculation Methods
*   Planimetry (2D echo short axis)
*   Pressure half-time: MVA = 220 / PHT
*   Continuity equation: MVA = (Area<sub>LVOT</sub> × VTI<sub>LVOT</sub>) / VTI<sub>MV</sub>
*   Gorlin equation: MVA = CO / (38 × HR × DFP × √mean gradient)

### Indications for Mitral Valve Surgery in MS (AHA)
*   **Class I:** moderate–severe MS, symptoms (NYHA III–IV), PMBV not suitable.
*   **Class IIa:** severe MS, severe pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg) or symptoms (NYHA I–II), PMBV not suitable.
*   **Class IIb:** moderate–severe MS, recurrent embolic events on anticoagulation.

### Wilkins Mitral Stenosis Score (Figure 4)
*   Parameters: leaflet mobility, leaflet thickening, leaflet calcification, subvalvular thickening.
*   Each scores 0–4 (maximum 16). Wilkins score **>9** → unlikely to be amenable to PMBV.

### Percutaneous Mitral Balloon Valvuloplasty (PMBV)
*   **Indications (AHA Class I):** moderate–severe MS, symptoms (NYHA II–IV) or pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg rest, >60 mmHg exercise).
*   **Indications (AHA Class IIa):** moderate–severe MS, symptoms (NYHA III–IV), non-pliable valve or high surgical risk.
*   **Requirements:** Wilkins score ≤8, no LA thrombus, no moderate–severe MR.
*   **Technique (Figure 5):** femoral vein → RA → trans-septal → LA → across MV → balloon inflation.
*   **Results:** MVA increases from ~1 to ~2 cm².
*   **Complications:** mortality 1%, cardiac perforation 1%, stroke 2%, severe MR 3%.
*   **Best results:** thin, mobile, non-calcified leaflets, young age, no subvalvular fusion/calcification.

### Closed Mitral Valvotomy (Figure 6)
*   Left posterolateral thoracotomy → pericardiotomy anterior to phrenic nerve → finger in LA → Tubbs dilator via LV apex → guided through MV orifice → opened along intercommissural line.

### Surgical Approaches to Mitral Valve (Figure 7)
*   **Standard left atriotomy (Waterson’s groove)**
*   **Bi-atrial trans-septal** — risk to SA nodal artery
*   **Superior roof incision** — risk to LMCA, non-coronary sinus, SVC
*   **Bi-atrial Dubost incision** — good access but difficult closure
*   **Right anterolateral thoracotomy** — minimal access/redo

### Intra-operative Mitral Valve Assessment
*   Inspect each scallop (P1, P2, P3, A1, A2, A3) with nerve hooks.
*   Pre-repair: distend LV with cold saline → identify regurgitation jet.
*   Post-repair: static testing (cold saline) + TOE.

### Structures at Risk During Mitral Valve Surgery (Figure 8)
*   Circumflex coronary artery
*   Coronary sinus
*   Aortic valve (left and non-coronary cusps)
*   AV node and conducting bundles

### Systolic Anterior Motion (SAM) (Figure 9)
*   Leaflet tips displaced anteriorly into LVOT → Venturi effect → MR + LVOT obstruction.
*   More common in HOCM and small elderly women with asymmetric septal hypertrophy.
*   **Prevention:** avoid excess anterior leaflet tissue, excess posterior leaflet height (>1.5 cm) (displaces coaptation line anteriorly).
*   **Exacerbated by:** hypovolaemia, excess inotropes, tachycardia.
*   **Post-repair SAM treatment:** conservative (volume, afterload, rate, inotropes); surgical — remove ring, sliding annuloplasty, incomplete ring (Cosgrove®), larger ring (↑ AP diameter), low profile prosthesis (St Jude®).

### Surgical Treatment Options for Mitral Valve Disease (Figures 11–12)
| Pathology | Options |
|-----------|---------|
| Posterior prolapse | Quadrangular/triangular resection, sliding annuloplasty, artificial chords |
| Anterior prolapse | Triangular resection, artificial chords |
| Bileaflet prolapse | Leaflet resection, artificial chords, Alfieri stitch, replacement |
| Partial papillary rupture | Reimplantation |
| Complete papillary rupture | Replacement |
| Leaflet perforation | Bovine pericardial patch |
| Annular dilation | Complete flexible ring (Physio®) |
| Ischaemic MR | McCarthy-Adams IMR® ring, downsizing annuloplasty, replacement |
| Rheumatic MS | Replacement |

### Sliding Annuloplasty (Figure 13)
*   Indicated after excessive posterior leaflet resection or to reduce SAM risk (posterior leaflet height >1.5 cm or long anterior leaflet).

### Mitral Annuloplasty Rings (Figure 14)
| Ring | Features |
|------|----------|
| Carpentier-McCarthy-Adams IMR® | For ischaemic MR |
| Cosgrove-Edwards® | Incomplete, flexible — for SAM risk |
| Carpentier-Edwards Physio® | Complete, semi-flexible — most frequently used |
| Carpentier Classic® | Rigid, complete — adjustable AP diameter |

### Benefits of Annuloplasty Ring
*   Corrects annular dilation, prevents further dilation, increases leaflet coaptation, reinforces suture lines.

### Advantages of Mitral Valve Repair vs Replacement
*   Greater freedom from mortality, SVD, re-operation, endocarditis, thromboembolism, haemorrhage.
*   Preserves subvalvular apparatus → better LV geometry → less LV dysfunction.

### Therapeutic Options for Ischaemic MR
*   CABG
*   McCarthy-Adams IMR® ring (reduced AP diameter, concavity at P2/P3)
*   Downsizing annuloplasty
*   Mitral valve replacement

### Freedom from Re-intervention (>70 years, 10 years)
| Procedure | Freedom |
|-----------|---------|
| Posterior leaflet repair | 98% |
| Mechanical prosthesis | 98% |
| Anterior leaflet repair | 78% |
| Open commissurotomy | 80% |
| Closed commissurotomy | 70% |
| PMBV | 80% |
| Porcine bioprosthesis | 75% |
| Bovine pericardial | 80% |

### Atrioventricular Disruption Management
1.  Re-establish CPB
2.  Remove prosthetic valve
3.  Bovine pericardial patch across AV groove
4.  Implant smaller, low-profile prosthesis
5.  Avoid lifting heart with prosthesis in situ

## 1. What is the natural history of mitral regurgitation (MR)?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Asymptomatic patients can have a long latent period before the onset of symptoms as chronic MR is well tolerated if left ventricular function is well preserved.
- Deterioration in left ventricular function, however, is masked by the unloading effect of a leaking valve resulting in a **pseudo-normal left ventricular ejection fraction (EF)**.
- **Poor prognostic features of MR include:**
  - **a) Symptoms for >1 year**
  - **b) Atrial fibrillation**
  - **c) Age >60 years**
  - **d) EF <50%**
  - **e) Left ventricular end-diastolic volume >100 mL/m², left ventricular end-systolic volume >60 mL/m²**
  - **f) Left ventricular end-systolic diameter >5 cm, left ventricular end-diastolic diameter >7 cm**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 2. What is the pathophysiology of mitral regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Chronic left ventricular volume overload associated with MR leads to **eccentric hypertrophy** of the left ventricle.
- Stroke volume is initially maintained by the **Frank-Starling mechanism**.
- Eventually left ventricular contractile function declines producing increased left ventricular end-systolic volume, raised left ventricular filling pressures, and raised left atrial and pulmonary venous pressures, finally resulting in pulmonary oedema and congestive heart failure.
- Once the left ventricular ejection fraction falls below approximately **60%** , left ventricular systolic dysfunction is almost certainly present as some of the ejected volume flows into the left atrium (**afterload-reducing effect of MR**).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 3. How is mitral regurgitation classified?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_1_Picture_9.jpeg)

**Figure 1. Carpentier’s functional classification of mitral regurgitation. A) Type I — normal leaflet motion usually producing central regurgitation. B) Type II — excessive leaflet motion above the annular plane usually producing eccentric regurgitation. C) Type III — restricted leaflet motion below the annular plane usually producing eccentric regurgitation.**

- According to **Carpentier’s functional classification** (Figure 1):
  - **a) Type I:** normal leaflet motion, e.g. annular dilation, leaflet perforation
  - **b) Type II:** excess leaflet motion, e.g. myxomatous degeneration, chordal rupture
  - **c) Type III:** restricted leaflet motion:
    - **i) IIIa:** restricted opening, e.g. rheumatic fever
    - **ii) IIIb:** restricted closure, e.g. ischaemic cardiomyopathy

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 4. What are the causes of chronic mitral regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Myxomatous degeneration (Type II)
- Rheumatic fever (Type IIIa)
- Ischaemic cardiomyopathy (mainly Type IIIb and Type I, see below)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 5. What are the causes of acute mitral regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_2_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Papillary muscle rupture following acute myocardial infarction.**

- Chordal rupture (Type II)
- Infective endocarditis (Type I)
- Papillary muscle rupture (following myocardial infarction, Type II) (Figure 2)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 6. What is the aetiology of ischaemic mitral regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Acute ischaemic MR** is usually caused by infarcted, ruptured or non-ruptured papillary muscle (Type II).
- **Chronic ischaemic MR** is caused by a combination of restricted movement of P2 and P3 scallops of the posterior mitral valve leaflet due to left ventricular dilation displacing the papillary muscles (Type IIIb) and functional dilation of the MV annulus (Type I).
- In addition, reduced contraction of the MV annulus and reduced ventricular closing forces on the MV due to poor LV function contribute to ischaemic MR.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 7. What are the symptoms of mitral regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Mitral regurgitation is a progressive disease and hence patients can be asymptomatic for many years.
- **Fatigue and weakness** — related to low cardiac output
- **Dyspnoea, orthopnoea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea**
- Eventually patients may develop pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 8. What are the signs of mitral regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Displaced **volume-loaded apex beat**
- **Apical thrill**
- **Third heart sound (S₃)**
- **Apical pansystolic murmur** — radiating to the axilla (the intensity of the murmur does not necessarily correlate with the severity of MR)
- **Apical diastolic flow murmur** — due to increased flow across the MV in diastole
- **Right ventricular heave** and an **increased pulmonary component of S₂** (if pulmonary hypertension is present)

</div></details>

- According to the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines, there are five main criteria (Table 1).

- **Vena contracta** refers to the narrowest part of the regurgitant jet just after the valve orifice.

</div></details>

## 10. What are the indications for mitral valve surgery in patients with mitral regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

### AHA guidelines

**Class I:**
- Severe MR with **symptoms (NYHA II–IV)** or **left ventricular changes** (end-systolic diameter >40 mm or EF <60%)

**Class IIa:**
- Severe MR in an **asymptomatic patient with good LV function and size**, where the likelihood of repair in the operating centre is **>90%**
- Severe MR with **AF** or **pulmonary hypertension** (>50 mmHg at rest or 60 mmHg with exercise)

**Class IIb:**
- Severe MR, poor left ventricular function (EF <30%) and symptoms (NYHA III–IV) despite maximal anti-failure medical therapy and biventricular pacing

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 11. When should transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) be performed in patients with severe mitral regurgitation?</summary><div class="details-content">

### AHA guidelines

**Class I:**
- **Intra-operative TOE** — pre-bypass to assess the feasibility of repair and post-bypass to assess the success of the repair

**Class IIa:**
- **Pre-operative TOE** to guide whether asymptomatic patients have a >90% chance of repair (see AHA guidelines on MR)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 12. What is mitral valve prolapse due to Barlow’s disease?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Mitral valve prolapse is defined as **>2 mm billowing** of the anterior or posterior mitral valve leaflet beyond the annular plane into the left atrium with or without MR.
- It can be familial or non-familial and may be associated with **Marfan syndrome**.
- Histological analysis reveals marked **myxomatous proliferation** of acid mucopolysaccharides within the zona spongiosa of the mitral valve leaflets, resulting in thinning and elongation of the chordae tendinae.
- Patients with mitral valve prolapse can be asymptomatic or present with severe MR or arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia).
- Surgery is indicated as per AHA guidelines for non-ischaemic MR.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 13. What is the natural history of mitral stenosis (MS)?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Patients usually become symptomatic on exertion when the mitral valve area (MVA) becomes **<2.5 cm²** and symptomatic at rest when the MVA becomes **<1.5 cm²**.
- The natural progression of MS causes the mitral valve area to reduce by **0.1–0.3 cm² per year**.
- The progression from onset of rheumatic fever to onset of signs of MS takes **10–20 years**.
- The progression from signs of MS to mild symptoms of MS takes **10–20 years**.
- The progression from mild symptoms of MS to decompensation (often precipitated by AF) takes **5–10 years**.
- The **10-year survival** is 80% (in patients with NYHA I/II symptoms) and 10–15% (in patients with NYHA III/IV symptoms).
- In patients with pulmonary hypertension (>50 mmHg), the **mean survival is 3 years**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 14. What is the pathophysiology of mitral stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Restricted left ventricular inflow results in increased left atrial pressure; back pressure on the pulmonary circulation causes dyspnoea and eventually pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular failure and subsequent left ventricular failure.
- Pulmonary hypertension occurs secondary to compensatory vasoconstriction and hypertrophy of the pulmonary arterioles.
- The patient’s haemodynamic state is worsened by **tachycardia or atrial fibrillation** which reduces the diastolic filling period.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 15. What are the causes of mitral stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_5_Picture_13.jpeg)

**Figure 3. Rheumatic mitral stenosis.**

- The majority of cases are caused by **rheumatic fever** (Lancefield group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus) (Figure 3).
- Very rare causes include a congenital parachute mitral valve, endocardial fibroelastosis and carcinoid syndrome.
- The pathophysiology of MS can also be caused by cor triatriatum, left atrial myxoma and pulmonary vein stenosis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 16. What are the symptoms of mitral stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Mitral stenosis has a long latent period and therefore can be asymptomatic for many years.
- **Fatigue**
- **Dyspnoea, orthopnoea and paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea**
- **Left atrial distension** resulting in:
  - **a) Atrial fibrillation** and subsequent thrombo-embolic events
  - **b) Left recurrent laryngeal nerve compression** presenting with hoarseness (**Ortner’s syndrome**)
  - **c) Oesophageal compression** producing dysphagia
  - **d) Rarely, left main bronchus compression** causing left lung collapse
- **Pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure** which present as:
  - **a) Peripheral oedema and ascites**
  - **b) Haemoptysis** due to distension and rupture of bronchial veins

</div></details>

## 17. What are the signs of mitral stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Low volume pulse**
- **Irregular pulse**
- **Tapping non-displaced apex beat**
- **Opening snap**
- **Loud S₁ heart sound**
- **Mid-diastolic rumbling murmur** loudest at the apex
- **Pulmonary hypertension:**
  - **a) Mitral facies**
  - **b) Central cyanosis**
  - **c) Loud P₂ heart sound**
  - **d) Tricuspid regurgitation** — pan-systolic murmur at the right sternal edge
  - **e) Pulmonary regurgitation** — Graham Steel early diastolic murmur on inspiration

</div></details>

- According to the American Heart Association (AHA) guidelines, there are three main criteria (Table 2).

## 21. What are the indications for percutaneous mitral balloon valvuloplasty (PMBV) in patients with mitral stenosis?</summary><div class="details-content">

### AHA guidelines

**Class I:**
- Moderate–severe MS with either **symptoms (NYHA II–IV)** or **pulmonary hypertension** (>50 mmHg at rest or 60 mmHg on exercise)

**Class IIa:**
- Moderate–severe MS with **symptoms (NYHA III–IV)** in patients with a non-pliable valve or in patients who are at a high surgical risk of death

- PMBV should be only performed in patients with **favourable valve morphology (Wilkins score of 8 or less)** and **no contraindications** (left atrial thrombus, moderate–severe MR)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 22. How is percutaneous mitral balloon valvuloplasty (PMBV) performed?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_9_Picture_7.jpeg)

**Figure 5. Percutaneous trans-septal mitral balloon valvuloplasty. The femoral vein is accessed using 9–11Fr sheaths. A) The coiled tip guide wire is then advanced into the left ventricle, via the right atrium, interatrial septum, left atrium and mitral valve. B) A valvuloplasty balloon (Jomiva™, Numed, Hopkinton, New York, USA or Inoue-Balloon™, Toray International America Inc., Houston, USA) is advanced into the mitral valve orifice using transoesophageal echocardiography and fluoroscopy guidance. C) The balloon is then progressively inflated with controlled hydraulic pressure, until a satisfactory diameter is reached. D) Following completion, the balloon is deflated and the mitral valve function is assessed.**

- Using local anaesthetic and sedation, a valvuloplasty balloon is passed via the femoral vein into the right atrium, across the intra-atrial septum, into the left atrium, across the mitral valve and inflated (Figure 5).
- Results are best when the leaflets are **thin, mobile, not calcified, in the younger age group** and where the subvalvular chordae have not fused/calcified.
- PMBV typically increases the mitral valve area from **~1 to ~2 cm²**.
- **Complications:** mortality 1%, cardiac perforation 1%, stroke 2%, severe MR 3%.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 23. How is a closed mitral valvotomy performed?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_10_Picture_6.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Closed mitral valvotomy.**

- Following a left posterolateral thoracotomy, the pericardium is opened anterior to the phrenic nerve. An index finger is then inserted into the left atrium and a Tubbs dilator is passed via the apex through the left ventricle, using purse strings to reduce blood loss and air embolism. The dilator is then guided manually through the mitral valve orifice and opened along the natural inter-commissural line. The resulting dilation is assessed manually and by transoesophageal echocardiography (Figure 6).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 24. What are the surgical approaches to the mitral valve?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_11_Picture_10.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Surgical approaches to the mitral valve. A) Standard left atriotomy. B) Bi-atrial vertical trans-septal incision. C) Superior roof incision. D) Bi-atrial Dubost incision. SVC = superior vena cava; IVC = inferior vena cava; RA = right atrium; RPVs = right pulmonary veins.**

- **Standard left atriotomy:**
  - **a)** The pericardial reflections around the inferior and superior pulmonary veins are mobilised
  - **b)** Waterson’s interatrial groove is developed thereby reflecting the right atrium back over the left atrium
  - **c)** An incision is then made in the left atrium anterior and medial to the right superior pulmonary vein and continued inferiorly
- **Bi-atrial trans-septal incision** — oblique incision from right atrial free wall through RAA to left atrial roof, joined by second incision through fossa ovalis. The **sino-atrial nodal artery** is at risk.
- **Superior roof incision** — incision across right atrial free wall between SVC and RAA, extended across interatrial septum and left atrial roof behind aortic root. Risks: LMCA, non-coronary sinus, SVC.
- **Bi-atrial (Dubost) incision** — vertical incision between right superior and inferior pulmonary veins extending across left atrium, right atrium and interatrial septum. Good access but difficult closure.
- **Right anterolateral thoracotomy** — used for minimal access or redo surgery.

</div></details>

## 25. How is the mitral valve assessed intra-operatively?</summary><div class="details-content">

- The valve is inspected using **nerve hooks** to measure the degree of motion of each scallop (P1, P2, P3, A1, A2, A3) of both leaflets compared against a reference point (usually P1 or the anterolateral commissure).
- The valve competency can be checked before repair by **distending the left ventricle with cold saline** and identifying the aetiology and location of the regurgitation jet.
- Competence of the valve following repair is assessed by:
  - **a) Static testing** under direct vision by distending the left ventricle with cold saline
  - **b) TOE** with an appropriately filled left ventricle off cardiopulmonary bypass support

</div></details>

## 27. What is systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_13_Picture_9.jpeg)

**Figure 9. Systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve. LVOTd = left ventricular outflow tract in diastole; LVOTs = left ventricular outflow tract in systole.**

- Systolic anterior motion occurs when the tips of the mitral valve leaflets are displaced anteriorly into the left ventricular outflow tract. This results in a **Venturi effect** pulling on the mitral valve leaflets causing MR and left ventricular outflow tract obstruction. In comparison, during diastole the left ventricular outflow diameter is normal (Figure 9).
- SAM is more common in patients with **hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM)** and small elderly women with asymmetric septal hypertrophy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 28. How is systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve prevented?</summary><div class="details-content">

- The risk of SAM can be reduced by avoiding excess anterior mitral valve leaflet tissue or excess height of the posterior mitral valve leaflet (>1.5 cm), as this will cause the line of leaflet coaptation to be displaced anteriorly into the left ventricular outflow tract.
- SAM is exacerbated by **hypovolaemia, excess inotropes and tachycardia**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 29. How is systolic anterior motion (SAM) post-mitral valve repair treated?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_14_Picture_11.jpeg)

**Figure 10. Surgical management of systolic anterior motion (SAM) of the mitral valve includes implanting a larger annuloplasty ring or implanting an annuloplasty band.**

- **Conservative:** ensure adequate volume status, increase afterload, reduce tachycardia, reduce inotropes
- **Surgical (Figure 10):** remove the annuloplasty ring then consider:
  - **a) Sliding annuloplasty** to reduce the height of the posterior mitral valve leaflet
  - **b) Implanting an incomplete mitral annuloplasty ring** (e.g. Cosgrove® or Duran®)
  - **c) Increasing the size**, particularly the anteroposterior (AP) diameter, of the mitral annuloplasty ring
  - **d) Implanting a low profile prosthetic mitral valve** (e.g. St Jude® mechanical prosthesis)

</div></details>

## 34. What are the advantages of repairing the mitral valve rather than replacing it?</summary><div class="details-content">

- As compared with mitral valve replacement, mitral valve repair is associated with **greater freedom from mortality (operative and long-term), structural valve deterioration, re-operation, infective endocarditis, thrombo-embolism and haemorrhage**.
- As more of the sub-valvular apparatus is preserved, mitral valve repair is better at maintaining left ventricular geometry and hence is associated with **less left ventricular dysfunction** compared with mitral valve replacement.

</div></details>

## 35. Describe the therapeutic options for ischaemic mitral regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Coronary artery bypass grafting
- Implantation of a mitral valve annuloplasty ring (**McCarthy-Adams IMR® ring**), which is specifically designed with a reduced anterior-posterior diameter and a concavity in the region of the P2/P3 scallops
- Downsizing annuloplasty of the mitral valve
- Mitral valve replacement

</div></details>

## 37. How is atrioventricular disruption following mitral valve replacement treated?</summary><div class="details-content">

1. Put the patient back on cardiopulmonary bypass
2. Remove the prosthetic valve
3. Implant a bovine pericardial patch across the atrioventricular groove
4. Implant a smaller prosthetic valve with a low profile
5. Avoid lifting the heart with the prosthesis in situ
