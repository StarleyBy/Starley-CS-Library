# Cardiac Catheterisation

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Basic Principles
*   **Coronary angiography** — invasive injection of radio-opaque dye into coronary ostia to delineate coronary anatomy using multiple 2D cine images.
*   **Access routes:** femoral, brachial, radial, ulnar arteries (modified Seldinger technique). 6Fr (2 mm) catheters allow vessel size estimation.
*   **Views described by X-ray detector position:** RAO (right anterior oblique), LAO (left anterior oblique), AP (anteroposterior), with cranial/caudal tilt.

### Standard Angiographic Views
*   **Left coronary system:** RAO 20° caudal 20°, AP caudal 30°, AP cranial 40°, LAO 50° cranial 30°, LAO 50° caudal 30° (‘spider view’).
*   **LAD identification:** longest vessel, descends to apex, septal arteries branch at 90°.
*   **Circumflex identification:** closest to thoracic spine and descending aorta.
*   **Right coronary system:** LAO 30°, RAO 30°.
*   **Left ventricle:** RAO view (anterobasal, anterior, apical, inferior, inferobasal), LAO view (lateral, septal, inferior).

### Indications and Contraindications
*   **Indications:** chronic stable angina, ACS, positive ETT, ischaemic cardiomyopathy, pre-op assessment (>40 years or multiple risk factors), cardiac arrest survivors.
*   **Contraindications:** acute Type A dissection, aortic valve endocarditis with vegetation, decompensated HF, active infection, renal dysfunction (contrast nephropathy), contrast allergy.
*   **Complications:** local vascular (3%), contrast nephropathy (0.5%), emergency surgery (0.1%), CVA (0.05%), MI (0.05%), mortality <0.05%.

### Coronary Artery Assessment
*   **Significant stenosis:** >60% luminal diameter (84% cross-sectional area reduction). Left main stem: >50% luminal diameter.
*   **Area reduction formula:** area ∝ r² → 50% diameter = 75% area, 70% diameter = 91% area, 90% diameter = 99% area.
*   **Fractional flow reserve (FFR):** ratio of distal to proximal coronary pressure. Normal = 1.0, ischaemic <0.80 (>90% accuracy). FAME study showed FFR-guided revascularisation improves outcomes.
*   **TIMI flow:** Grade 0 (no flow), Grade 1 (reduced, incomplete filling), Grade 2 (slow but complete filling), Grade 3 (prompt antegrade flow).
*   **Intravascular ultrasound (IVUS):** transcatheter ultrasound probe — direct imaging of luminal diameter, vessel wall, atheroma, calcification. Useful for equivocal stenosis and endoluminal calcification.
*   **Intracoronary pressure wire:** measures FFR; adenosine used to assess flow limitation.

### Valvular Assessment
*   **Mitral regurgitation (Sellers’ classification):** I (incomplete LA opacification, clears each beat) → IV (complete LA opacification in one beat, becomes denser, reflux into pulmonary veins).
*   **Aortic regurgitation (Sellers’ classification):** 1+ (small jet, no LV opacification) → 4+ (LV opacification greater than aorta).
*   **LV function:** good (EF >50%), moderate (30–50%), poor (<30%). Regional wall motion: normal, hypokinetic, akinetic, dyskinetic, aneurysmal.

### Haemodynamic Calculations
*   **Fick principle:** Flow = O₂ consumption / (arteriovenous O₂ concentration difference). O₂ content = (Hb × 1.34 × O₂ sats) + (0.003 × PaO₂).
*   **Shunt ratio (Qp:Qs):** = (PV O₂ sats – PA O₂ sats) / (Ao sats – RA sats). Normal = 1:1. PV sats assumed 100%.
*   **Gorlin equation (aortic valve area):** AVA = CO / (44.5 × LVET × HR × √mean gradient). Constant 44.5 for aortic/pulmonary, 31 for mitral/tricuspid.
*   **Square root sign (‘dip and plateau’):** abnormal ventricular diastolic filling — rapid early filling followed by sudden halt. Associated with constrictive pericarditis and restrictive cardiomyopathy (NOT tamponade).

## 1. What is coronary angiography?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Coronary angiography is an invasive cardiological investigation where radio-opaque dye is injected through the coronary ostia to delineate coronary artery anatomy using multiple 2D cine images.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 2. How is coronary angiography performed?</summary><div class="details-content">

- The patient is kept fasted for 4 hours prior to the procedure and given a premedication, including a benzodiazepine and an opiate.
- The patient is placed supine on a radiolucent mobile screening table.
- Access to the aortic root is gained via the femoral, brachial, radial or ulnar artery by placing an arterial sheath using a percutaneous **modified Seldinger technique**.
- The coronary ostia are engaged with a variety of pre-formed catheters (such as a Judkins left or Judkins right) and non-ionic radio-opaque dye is injected into the left and right coronary arteries under fluoroscopic guidance.
- As a **6Fr (2 mm)** catheter is often used, it is possible to judge the size of the vessels relative to the catheter.
- As well as the coronary arteries, the left ventricle, mitral valve, aortic valve and ascending aorta can all be assessed during the same procedure.
- The different views obtained of the coronary arteries are described by the position of the X-ray detector:
  - **a) Right anterior oblique (RAO)**
  - **b) Left anterior oblique (LAO)**
  - **c) Anteroposterior (AP)**
- These views will have different degrees of associated cranial and caudal tilt.
- On completion of the procedure, the arterial sheath is removed and haemostasis can be achieved using a collagen plug (AngioSeal®).

</div></details>

## 6. What are the indications for coronary angiography?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Chronic stable angina.
- Acute coronary syndrome — unstable angina, non-ST elevation myocardial infarction, ST elevation myocardial infarction.
- Positive exercise tolerance test (ETT).
- Ischaemic cardiomyopathy.
- Pre-operative assessment in patients undergoing valve or aortic surgery, if aged >40 years old or with multiple risk factors.
- Survivors of cardiac arrest.
- Patients with multiple risk factors for coronary artery disease where the diagnosis is important for occupation, e.g. airline pilot.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 7. What are the relative contra-indications for coronary angiography?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Acute Type A aortic dissection.
- Aortic valve infective endocarditis with a vegetation present.
- Decompensated heart failure or acute pulmonary oedema.
- Active infection.
- Renal dysfunction, due to the risk of contrast nephropathy.
- Allergy to radio-opaque contrast.

</div></details>

## 10. How is mitral regurgitation classified by angiography?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_8_Picture_14.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Sellers’ grade IV mitral regurgitation. LA = left atrium; LV = left ventricle.**

- According to the **Sellers’ classification**:
  - **a) I:** incomplete opacification of the left atrium that clears with each beat;
  - **b) II:** complete opacification of the left atrium that does not clear with each beat;
  - **c) III:** complete opacification of the left atrium with a similar density to the left ventricle;
  - **d) IV:** complete opacification of the left atrium in one beat, that becomes progressively more dense with each beat, with contrast refluxing into the pulmonary veins.

## 11. How is aortic regurgitation classified by angiography?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_9_Picture_8.jpeg)

**Figure 13. Sellers’ angiographic classification of aortic regurgitation.**

- According to the **Sellers’ classification**:
  - **a) 1+:** small regurgitant jet across the aortic valve but without opacification of the left ventricle;
  - **b) 2+:** regurgitant jet across the aortic valve with faint opacification of the left ventricle;
  - **c) 3+:** dense opacification of the left ventricle with no distinct jet across the aortic valve usually visualized;
  - **d) 4+:** opacification of the left ventricle greater than the aorta.

</div></details>

## 12. How is left ventricular function classified by ventriculography?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Global left ventricular function:**
  - **a) Good:** >50% left ventricular ejection fraction;
  - **b) Moderate:** 30–50% left ventricular ejection fraction;
  - **c) Poor:** <30% left ventricular ejection fraction.
- **Regional wall motion abnormalities** based on five areas of the left ventricle on the RAO view (anterobasal, apex, anterior, inferior, inferobasal) and three areas on the LAO view (lateral, septal, inferior):
  - **a) Normal**
  - **b) Hypokinetic**
  - **c) Akinetic**
  - **d) Dyskinetic**
  - **e) Aneurysmal**

</div></details>

## 13. What is a significant stenosis of a coronary artery?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_10_Figure_14.jpeg)

**Figure 14. Coronary artery stenosis as classified by luminal diameter and cross-sectional area.**

- The correlation between luminal stenosis and cross-sectional area (CSA) stenosis is governed by the formula:

$$ \text{Area} = \pi r^2 $$

where r = radius of vessel, π = 3.14. Hence:
  - **a) 50% luminal diameter reduction** = **75% CSA reduction**
  - **b) 70% luminal diameter reduction** = **91% CSA reduction**
  - **c) 90% luminal diameter reduction** = **99% CSA reduction**

- **Significant stenosis** is defined as **>60% luminal diameter** (84% CSA reduction).
- **Left main stem significant stenosis** is defined as **>50% luminal diameter** (75% CSA reduction).
- It is important to assess each lesion in several views as the interobserver error is low for mild (<50%) and severe (>80%) luminal stenoses but the error is significant for stenoses 50–80%.
- More recently, **fractional flow reserve (FFR)** is being increasingly used to determine the haemodynamic significance of a coronary stenosis. FFR is calculated by the ratio of the coronary pressure distal to the lesion compared to the pressure proximal to the lesion. FFR in a normal coronary artery is **1.0**, with an FFR **<0.80** indicating an ischaemic coronary lesion (>90% accuracy). Data from the **FAME study** suggest that FFR-guided revascularisation, in comparison to angiography-guided revascularisation, results in improved freedom from death, myocardial infarction and repeat revascularisation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 14. What is TIMI flow?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **TIMI (thrombolysis in myocardial infarction)** derives its name from a trial of similar name and describes the different grades of coronary flow:
  - **a) Grade 0:** no contrast flow;
  - **b) Grade 1:** reduced flow and incomplete opacification of the distal vessel;
  - **c) Grade 2:** slow distal filling but complete opacification of the distal vessel;
  - **d) Grade 3:** prompt antegrade contrast flow with rapid clearing.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 15. How are systemic-pulmonary shunt ratios calculated?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Using the **Fick principle**:

$$ \text{Flow} = \frac{\text{O}_2 \text{ consumption}}{\text{arteriovenous O}_2 \text{ concentration difference}} $$

$$ \text{Flow} = \frac{\text{O}_2 \text{ consumption}}{(\text{arteriovenous O}_2 \text{ sats difference}) \times \text{Hb} \times 1.34 \times 10} $$

$$ \text{Pulmonary flow (Qp)} = \frac{\text{O}_2 \text{ consumption}}{(\text{PV O}_2 \text{ sats} - \text{PA O}_2 \text{ sats}) \times \text{Hb} \times 1.34 \times 10} $$

$$ \text{Systemic flow (Qs)} = \frac{\text{O}_2 \text{ consumption}}{(\text{Ao sats} - \text{RA sats}) \times \text{Hb} \times 1.34 \times 10} $$

As O₂ consumption and Hb are the same in both equations:

$$ \text{Qp:Qs ratio} = \frac{\text{PV O}_2 \text{ sats} - \text{PA O}_2 \text{ sats}}{\text{Ao sats} - \text{RA sats}} $$

- **PV O₂ sats** usually assumed to be **100%**
- **RA sats** = (3 × SVC sats + 1 × IVC sats) / 4

where PV = pulmonary vein, PA = pulmonary artery, Ao = aorta, RA = right atrium, SVC = superior vena cava, IVC = inferior vena cava, Hb = haemoglobin, sats = saturations.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 16. How is cardiac output calculated at cardiac catheterisation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Using the **direct Fick method**:

$$ \text{Cardiac output} = \frac{\text{O}_2 \text{ consumption}}{\text{AV O}_2 \text{ concentration difference}} $$

$$ \text{O}_2 \text{ content} = (\text{Hb} \times 1.34 \times \text{O}_2 \text{ sats}) + \text{plasma O}_2 \text{ content} $$

- 1 g of Hb when 100% saturated combines with **1.34 mL O₂**
- **Plasma O₂ content** in 100 mL plasma ~**0.3 mL**

$$ \text{CO} = \frac{\text{O}_2 \text{ consumption}}{\{[(\text{Ao sats} - \text{PA sats}) \times \text{Hb} \times 1.34] + 0.3\} \times 10} $$

where CO = cardiac output, AV = arteriovenous, Hb = haemoglobin, Ao = aorta, PA = pulmonary artery.

- Any systemic artery can be used for systemic saturations but the **pulmonary artery must be used for the mixed venous content**.
- Oxygen consumption is measured by the respiratory gas monitor on a ventilator.
- Cardiac output can also be measured using the **indirect Fick method** with dye dilution (similar to the thermodilution method used with pulmonary artery catheters).

</div></details>

## 17. How is the aortic valve area calculated by cardiac catheterisation?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_13_Figure_10.jpeg)

**Figure 15. Gorlin method of calculating aortic valve area.**

- Using the **Gorlin equation**:

$$ \text{AoV area} = \frac{\text{AoV flow}}{44.5 \times \sqrt{\text{mean aortic gradient}}} $$

and as AoV flow = cardiac output / systolic ejection period:

$$ \text{AoV area} = \frac{\text{CO}}{44.5 \times \text{SEP} \times \sqrt{\text{mean aortic gradient}}} $$

and as SEP = left ventricular ejection time × heart rate:

$$ \text{AoV area} = \frac{\text{CO}}{44.5 \times \text{LVET} \times \text{HR} \times \sqrt{\text{mean aortic gradient}}} $$

where AoV = aortic valve, CO = cardiac output, SEP = systolic ejection period, LVET = left ventricular ejection time, HR = heart rate.

- The same formula and constant can be used for the **pulmonary valve**.
- Although this formula can be used with prosthetic valves, the calculations are dependent on ‘forward flow’, which is inaccurate in the presence of regurgitation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 18. How is the mitral valve area calculated by cardiac catheterisation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Using the **Gorlin equation**:

$$ \text{MV area} = \frac{\text{MV flow}}{31 \times \sqrt{\text{mean mitral gradient}}} $$

and as MV flow = cardiac output / diastolic filling period:

$$ \text{MV area} = \frac{\text{CO}}{31 \times \text{DFP} \times \sqrt{\text{mean mitral gradient}}} $$

where MV = mitral valve, CO = cardiac output, DFP = diastolic filling period.

- The same formula and constant can be used for the **tricuspid valve**.

</div></details>

![](_page_14_Picture_12.jpeg)

- It is useful in patients with equivocal stenosis and vessels with marked endoluminal calcification.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 20. What is intracoronary pressure wire monitoring?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Using a fine wire pressure transducer, the intracoronary pressure can be measured, thereby allowing a comparison of the pressure distal to the lesion with the pressure proximal to the lesion. This allows the **fractional flow reserve (FFR)** to be calculated to determine the haemodynamic significance of a lesion. The normal FFR should be approximately **1.0**, whereas an FFR **<0.8** indicates a significant flow limiting lesion.
- Flow limitation can also be assessed by measuring the pressure gradient after administering a vasodilator, e.g. adenosine.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 21. What is the square root sign (‘dip and plateau’ pressure waveform)?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_15_Figure_2.jpeg)

![](_page_15_Figure_3.jpeg)

**Figure 17. Dip and plateau pressure waveform associated with constrictive pericarditis.**

- The **square root sign** is defined as an abnormal ventricular diastolic filling pattern caused by rapid early ventricular filling followed by a sudden halt in ventricular filling.
- This occurs at the end of the first third of diastole onwards as the ventricle is unable to distend any further.
- The square root sign is associated with **constrictive pericarditis** and **restrictive cardiomyopathy** but not with pericardial tamponade.

</div></details>
