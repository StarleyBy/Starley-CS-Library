# Thoracic Aortic Disease

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Normal Anatomy and Classification
*   **Aneurysm definition:** permanent localised dilation ≥150% of normal diameter.
*   **Aortic layers:** intima (endothelial + elastic), media (elastin, collagen, smooth muscle), adventitia (connective tissue).
*   **Aneurysm classification:** true (all 3 layers) vs false/pseudoaneurysm (adventitia only); saccular vs fusiform; atherosclerotic, dissecting, mycotic; ascending, arch, descending.
*   **Crawford classification (thoracoabdominal):** Type I (distal descending to suprarenal), II (distal descending to infrarenal), III (lower descending into abdomen), IV (below diaphragm — AAA), V (mid-descending to above diaphragm).

### Natural History and Risk Factors
*   **Expansion rate:** <5 cm → 0.2 cm/year; >5 cm → 0.8 cm/year (Laplace’s law).
*   **Rupture risk:** 4–5 cm → 0.3%/year; >6 cm → 3.6%/year.
*   **Median rupture size:** ascending 6 cm, descending 7 cm.
*   **Mean time from symptoms to rupture:** 2 years.
*   **5-year survival (untreated):** thoracic 39%, thoracoabdominal 23%.
*   **Pathophysiology:** cystic medial degeneration (loss of smooth muscle, elastin fragmentation) → loss of elasticity and tensile strength.
*   **Predisposing factors:** age, atherosclerosis, bicuspid aortic valve, hypertension, connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos), dissection, trauma, aortitis.
*   **Rupture risk factors:** aortic diameter, pain/symptoms, age, COPD, smoking.

### Marfan Syndrome
*   Autosomal dominant, fibrillin gene mutation (chromosome 15).
*   **Diagnosis (≥2 features):** tall thin (arm span > height), arachnodactyly, high arched palate, scoliosis/pectus, joint hypermobility, spontaneous pneumothorax, ectopia lentis, floppy MV, aortopathy.

### Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome
*   Autosomal dominant. **Type IV** → Type III collagen defect → aortopathy. Less common than Marfan.

### Clinical Features of Aortic Aneurysms
*   Asymptomatic (incidental), pain (anterior chest/back — sudden suggests dissection/rupture), compression (hoarseness — recurrent laryngeal, dysphagia — oesophagus, stridor — trachea), fistula (haematemesis, haemoptysis).

### Indications for Intervention
*   **Size criteria:**
    - Ascending >5.5 cm (>4.5 cm if AVR/CABG; >4.0–5.0 cm in Marfan or ratio of maximal area/height >10)
    - Arch >5.5 cm
    - Descending >5.5 cm
    - Thoracoabdominal >6.0 cm
*   **Other:** symptoms of expansion/compression/fistula, rapid expansion >1 cm/year, recurrent emboli.

### Indications for Aortic Root Replacement
*   Annulo-aortic ectasia, sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (most common non-coronary), root abscess, Type A dissection extending into root, small root (if root-sparing not possible).

### Surgical Principles and Outcomes
*   **Operative mortality:** root/ascending 2–5%, arch 5–10%, thoracoabdominal 10–20%.
*   **Neurological risk:** stroke 5–10%, paraplegia 5–10% (descending).
*   **5-year survival post-surgery:** ascending 82%, arch 70%, descending 65%, thoracoabdominal 65%.

### Aortic Root Replacement Options (Figures 8–9)
*   **Valve-sparing (normal valve):**
    - David (reimplantation) — stabilises annulus
    - Yacoub (remodelling) — not for connective tissue disorders or annulus >30 mm
*   **Valved conduit (diseased valve):**
    - Classic Bentall (inclusion) — rarely used
    - Modified Bentall — excise root, reimplant coronary buttons
    - Cabrol — interposition graft for coronary reimplantation (tension)
    - Homograft — for root abscess
    - Ross — pulmonary autograft (aortic) + pulmonary homograft

### Ascending Aneurysm Surgical Options (Figure 10)
*   Ascending replacement (Dacron® graft)
*   Reduction aortoplasty
*   Supporting aortoplasty (mesh wrap)

### Acute Aortic Syndrome (Figure 11)
*   Includes: aortic dissection, penetrating aortic ulcer, intramural haematoma, leaking aneurysm.
*   Management: ascending → surgery; descending → medical (but stent/surgery if rupture, unremitting pain, malperfusion).

### Intramural Haematoma (Figure 12)
*   Blood within media without intimal tear. Aetiology: vasa vasorum rupture, plaque haemorrhage, progression from penetrating ulcer.
*   **Higher rupture risk** than dissection (closer to adventitia).

### Penetrating Aortic Ulcer (Figure 13)
*   Focal intimal defect at atherosclerotic plaque → progressive erosion → intramural haematoma, dissection, rupture, pseudoaneurysm.
*   **Surgery indicated:** descending ulcers >20 mm diameter + >10 mm depth; ascending ulcers.

### Aortic Dissection — Classification (Figure 14)
*   **Timing:** acute (<14 days), sub-acute (14 days–2 months), chronic (>2 months).
*   **Stanford:** Type A (ascending involved), Type B (ascending not involved).
*   **DeBakey:** I (whole aorta), II (ascending only), IIIa (descending only), IIIb (descending + abdominal).

### Pathophysiology of Dissection
*   Intimal tear → blood tracks into media → propagates spirally → false lumen. Multiple re-entry tears in descending aorta.
*   **Entry tear locations:** ascending (65%, ~2 cm above non-coronary sinus), descending (20%, left anterolateral), arch (10%, opposite innominate).

### Predisposing Factors for Dissection (ABCDE)
*   A: age, atherosclerosis, aneurysm
*   B: bicuspid aortic valve (fibrillin deficiency), blood pressure (hypertension)
*   C: connective tissue disorders (Marfan, Ehlers-Danlos)
*   D: degenerative (cystic medial degeneration)
*   E: trauma, surgery, iatrogenic, pregnancy

### Clinical Features of Type A Dissection
*   Tearing retrosternal chest pain radiating to back/neck
*   Organ malperfusion (myocardial ischaemia, stroke, abdominal pain)
*   Dyspnoea (AR, tamponade, haemothorax)
*   BP differential between arms, AR murmur, absent pulses

### Investigations for Acute Type A Dissection (Figure 15)
| Modality | Sensitivity | Limitations |
|----------|-------------|-------------|
| TOE | 98% | Operator-dependent, blind spot (distal ascending/proximal arch) |
| CT angiography | 95% | Contrast nephropathy |
| MRI | 99% | Not always available (gold standard) |
| Aortography | 80% | Rarely used, may precipitate rupture |

### Monitoring for Type A Dissection Surgery
*   Arterial lines: pre-arch (right radial), post-arch (femoral/left radial)
*   Urinary catheter, CVC, nasopharyngeal temperature, cerebral oximetry (NIRS)

### Right Axillary Artery Cannulation (Figure 16)
*   Patient: supine, upper body 20°, tilted left. 5 cm infraclavicular incision. Mobilise axillary artery (avoid brachial plexus).
*   18Fr cannula directly (Seldinger) or via 8 mm Dacron® graft.
*   **Not suitable** if dissection extends into brachiocephalic artery.

### Surgery for Type A Dissection — Principles (Figures 17–18)
*   Resect entry tear
*   Aortic root: repair sinus segments or replace
*   Aortic valve: resuspend or replace
*   Arch: hemi-arch or total arch replacement
*   Distal anastomosis: obliterate false lumen
*   **Options:** ascending graft ± AVR ± root replacement (Bentall/Cabrol) ± valve-sparing (David/Yacoub) ± arch replacement
*   **Mortality:** without surgery ~90%; with surgery 10–20%; 5-year survival 60%.

### Management of Type B Dissection (Figure 19)
*   **Medical (first-line):** labetalol (α + β blocker) — reduce force of contraction, shear stress, BP.
*   **Rationale for medical management:** medical mortality 10% vs operative 27% (paraplegia 24%); no survival benefit with surgery.

### Indications for Intervention in Acute Type B Dissection (Figures 20–21)
*   Rupture/impending rupture (large mediastinal haematoma, large haemothorax)
*   Extension with unremitting pain
*   Limb/visceral/spinal cord malperfusion (metabolic acidosis, ↑ lactate, oliguria, paraesthesia/paraplegia)

### Endovascular Stent Grafting — Indications
*   Acute/chronic Type B dissection, traumatic aortic injury, descending aneurysms, coarctation, arch aneurysms (with branched grafts/fenestration/debranching).

### Stent Graft Requirements and Complications (Figure 22)
*   **Requirements:** landing zones >1.5 cm normal aorta, femoral arteries >7 mm, relatively straight descending aorta.
*   **Complications:** endoleak/migration 10–15%, local vascular 5–10%, mortality 5–10%, aortic trauma 5%, stroke/paraplegia 5%.

### Endoleak Classification
| Type | Source |
|------|--------|
| I | Leak at junction (inadequate seal) |
| II | Back bleeding vessels in aneurysmal sac |
| III | Defect in graft prosthesis |
| IV | Graft porosity |

## 5. What is the natural history of thoracic aortic aneurysms?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Surgically untreated thoracic aortic aneurysms carry the risk of rupture, dissection and death.
- Expansion of thoracic aneurysms occurs at a faster rate in larger aneurysms (**0.2 cm per year** in aneurysms <5 cm compared to **0.8 cm per year** in aneurysms >5 cm), as per Laplace’s law.
- The risk of rupture increases as the size of the aneurysm increases (**0.3% per year** for aneurysms 4–5 cm compared to **3.6%** for aneurysms >6 cm).
- The median size for rupture is **6 cm** for ascending and **7 cm** for descending thoracic aortic aneurysms (Figure 6).

![](_page_5_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Graph demonstrating the risk of rupture significantly increases when ascending and descending aortic diameters are larger than 6 and 7 cm, respectively.**

- The mean time from onset of symptoms to rupture of the aneurysm is **2 years**.
- The **5-year survival** of surgically untreated (symptomatic and asymptomatic) thoracic aortic aneurysms is **39%** and thoracoabdominal aneurysms, **23%**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 6. What is the pathophysiology of aortic aneurysm formation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- The media of the aorta normally contains elastin, collagen, smooth muscle cells and ground matrix.
- Aneurysms form after loss of smooth muscle cells and fragmentation of elastin fibres (a process known as **cystic medial degeneration**), which results in loss of elasticity and tensile strength of the media.
- The aorta normally expands and recoils with systole and diastole but dilates when elastic recoil is lost.

</div></details>

## 9. What is Marfan syndrome?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_6_Picture_15.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Computed tomography scan demonstrating a classical ‘onion-shaped’ aortic root aneurysm associated with Marfan syndrome.**

- Marfan syndrome is an **autosomal dominant** condition associated with mutation of the **fibrillin gene on chromosome 15**.
- As there are many different genetic mutations, it results in many different phenotypes.
- The diagnosis of Marfan syndrome is based on **two or more** of the following clinical features:
  - **a) Tall thin patient** — arm span greater than height
  - **b) Arachnodactyly** — long tapered fingers
  - **c) High arched palate**
  - **d) Scoliosis, pectus excavatum or protusio acetabulae**
  - **e) Hypermobility of joints with skin laxity**
  - **f) Spontaneous pneumothorax** with apical bullae
  - **g) Eye signs** — ectopia lentis (upward dislocation of lens), myopia, retinal detachment
  - **h) Floppy mitral valve** resulting in mitral regurgitation and chordal rupture
  - **i) Aortopathy** with weakened aortic wall which dilates even in normotensive patients. This is caused by cystic medial necrosis and may result in a thoracic aortic aneurysm or dissection

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 10. What is Ehlers-Danlos syndrome?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is an **autosomal dominant** condition.
- **Type IV** Ehlers-Danlos syndrome results in a structural defect of **Type III collagen** and subsequent aortopathy.
- It is **less common** than Marfan syndrome.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 11. What are the clinical features of patients with thoracic aortic aneurysms?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Asymptomatic** — incidental finding on imaging
- **Pain** — anterior chest pain or back pain (typically intrascapular), with sudden onset of the pain suggesting dissection or impending rupture
- **Compression of nearby structures** — hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve), dysphagia (oesophagus), stridor or dyspnoea (trachea)
- **Fistula** — haematemesis (oesophagus) or haemoptysis (lung, bronchi)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 12. What are the indications for intervention on thoracic aortic aneurysms?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Size:**
  - **a) Ascending aortic aneurysms >5.5 cm**
  - **b) Ascending aortic aneurysms >4.5 cm** for patients undergoing AVR or CABG
  - **c) Ascending aortic aneurysms 4.0–5.0 cm** (or when the ratio of the maximal ascending or aortic root area (πr²) in cm² divided by the patient’s height in metres exceeds 10) for patients with Marfan syndrome or other connective tissue disorders
  - **d) Aortic arch aneurysms >5.5 cm**
  - **e) Descending aortic aneurysms >5.5 cm**
  - **f) Thoraco-abdominal aneurysms >6.0 cm**
- **Symptoms of expansion, compression or fistula formation** — chest pain, back pain, dyspnoea, dysphagia, hoarseness, haematemesis or haemoptysis
- **Rapid rate of expansion** — >1 cm/year
- **Recurrent emboli** (especially cerebral emboli from the aortic arch)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 13. What are the indications for an aortic root replacement?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Annulo-aortic ectasia** — dilation of the aortic root, using similar size criteria for the ascending aorta (see above)
- **Aneurysm of the sinus of Valsalva** (which occurs most frequently in the non-coronary sinus, then the right coronary sinus and least often in the left coronary sinus). In these patients, it may be possible to perform a selective sinus replacement, instead of a complete aortic root replacement
- **Aortic root abscess**
- **Stanford Type A thoracic aortic dissection** extending into the aortic root
- **Small aortic root** if root-sparing techniques are not possible

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 14. What are the principles of surgery when operating on aortic aneurysms?</summary><div class="details-content">

- The aim of surgery is to prevent rupture, dissection, local compression and fistula formation.
- Surgery should be offered when the combined risk of operative mortality and permanent neurological deficit is **less than the combined risk of rupture and dissection**.
- **Operative mortality** is:
  - 2–5% for aortic root or ascending aortic surgery
  - 5–10% for aortic arch surgery
  - 10–20% for thoracoabdominal aortic surgery
- **Risk of neurological injury:** 5–10% stroke; 5–10% paraplegia (for descending aortic replacement)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 15. What is the 5-year survival following surgery for thoracic aortic aneurysms?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Ascending aortic aneurysm — **82%**
- Aortic arch aneurysm — **70%**
- Descending aortic aneurysm — **65%**
- Thoraco-abdominal aortic aneurysm — **65%**
- As part of the long-term follow-up after thoracic aortic surgery, these patients need **β-blockade** and **annual radiological scanning (CT or MRI)** to assess the residual native aorta.

</div></details>

## 16. What are the operative choices for an aortic root replacement?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_10_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_10_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 8. Valve-sparing aortic root surgery. A) David procedure. B) Yacoub procedure. C) Operative image demonstrating the David procedure with re-implantation of the aortic valve commissural pillars inside the Dacron® interposition graft. The free edges of the aortic valve leaflets have also been reinforced with 7/0 Gore-Tex® sutures.**

![](_page_11_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 9. Aortic root surgery. A) Classic Bentall procedure. B) Modified Bentall procedure. C) Cabrol procedure. D) Aortic homograft implantation. E) Ross procedure.**

- If the aortic root is dilated but the aortic valve is structurally normal, a **valve-sparing procedure** should be considered:
  - **a) David reimplantation procedure** — the commissural pillars are reimplanted within a Dacron® interposition tube graft and the coronary buttons are re-anastomosed. The sutures are passed from the left ventricular outflow tract from inside to out of the graft, thereby stabilising the aortic annulus. The Dacron® tube diameter is equal to the average length of the free margins of three leaflets (Figure 8A)
  - **b) Yacoub remodelling procedure** — the ascending aorta and sinuses are replaced with a Dacron® interposition tube graft that is scalloped to incorporate the aortic valve commissural pillars. The coronary buttons are then re-implanted. As the aortic valve annulus is not stabilised, this technique is **not suitable** for patients with connective tissue disorders or where the aortic valve annulus is dilated >30 mm (Figure 8B)
- If the aortic root is dilated and the aortic valve diseased (e.g. stenotic), an **aortic root and valve replacement** is performed:
  - **a) Classic Bentall procedure (inclusion technique)** — a valved conduit is placed within the aneurysmal aortic root and flow to the coronary buttons is provided by holes within the prosthesis. This procedure is rarely used now (Figure 9A)
  - **b) Modified Bentall procedure** — the aneurysmal aortic root is excised and replaced by a valved conduit (such as a Carboseal® mechanical prosthesis or BioValsalva™ bioprosthesis) with reimplantation of the coronary buttons (Figure 9B)
- **Cabrol procedure** — may be used to avoid tension on the coronary reimplantation by using a prosthetic interposition graft (8 mm Dacron® tube), such as when the coronary ostia are laterally displaced by a large aneurysm (Figure 9C)
- **Homograft aortic root replacement** — a cadaveric human aortic root is implanted followed by re-anastomosis of the coronary buttons. Often used in patients with an aortic root abscess following infective endocarditis of the aortic valve (Figure 9D)
- **Ross procedure** — the aortic root is replaced with the patient’s own pulmonary trunk, which itself is replaced with a pulmonary homograft (Figure 9E)

## 18. What is acute aortic syndrome?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_12_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 11. Acute aortic syndrome. A) Intramural haematoma. B) Penetrating aortic ulcer. C) Aortic dissection.**

- It is defined as a group of life-threatening thoracic aortic pathologies that includes **aortic dissection, penetrating aortic ulcer, intramural haematoma and leaking aortic aneurysm**.
- **Management** of patients with acute aortic syndrome should be similar to those with acute thoracic aortic dissection:
  - **a) Ascending aorta** — surgery
  - **b) Descending aorta** — medical management but stenting or surgery should be considered in patients with impending rupture, unremitting pain or organ malperfusion

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 19. What is an intramural haematoma of the aorta?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_13_Picture_13.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Intramural haematoma (arrows) of the ascending aorta demonstrated on A) axial and B) sagittal computed tomography scans.**

- It is defined as blood within the aortic media **without the presence of an intimal tear**.
- **Aetiology:**
  - **a) Rupture of the vasa vasorum** of the media
  - **b) Haemorrhage within an atherosclerotic plaque**
  - **c) Progression from a penetrating aortic ulcer**
- It may resolve spontaneously or progress and increase in size.
- Unlike dissections, intramural haematomas occur **closer to the adventitia** and hence are at a **greater risk of rupture** than dissections.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 20. What are penetrating aortic ulcers?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_14_Picture_8.jpeg)

**Figure 13. Penetrating aortic ulcers (PAU) observed in the A) right coronary sinus of the aortic root and B) lesser curve of the aortic arch.**

- They are defined as **focal intimal defects** occurring at the site of atherosclerotic plaques.
- Progressive intimal erosion eventually results in pulsatile blood entering the media and hence penetrating ulcers may lead to intramural haematomas, dissection, rupture, pseudoaneurysm and aneurysm formation.
- **Surgery is indicated for patients with:**
  - **a) Descending aortic ulcers with a diameter >20 mm and depth >10 mm**
  - **b) Ascending aortic ulcers**

</div></details>

## 21. How are thoracic aortic dissections classified?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_15_Picture_11.jpeg)

**Figure 14. DeBakey and Stanford classifications of thoracic aortic dissection.**

- Aortic dissection is defined as an intimal tear resulting in a split in the aortic wall between the internal and external elastic laminae within the media.
- **Timing:**
  - **a) Acute** — <14 days
  - **b) Sub-acute** — 14 days – 2 months
  - **c) Chronic** — >2 months
- **Location:**
  - **a) Stanford classification:**
    - **i) Type A:** ascending aorta involved
    - **ii) Type B:** ascending aorta not involved
  - **b) DeBakey classification:**
    - **i) Type I:** whole aorta involved
    - **ii) Type II:** only ascending aorta involved
    - **iii) Type IIIa:** only descending aorta involved
    - **iv) Type IIIb:** descending and abdominal aorta involved

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 22. Describe the pathophysiology of a thoracic aortic dissection</summary><div class="details-content">

- The **entry tear** is defined as the point where blood tracks through the intima into the media.
- The dissection is classified by the extent of media stripping from the entry tear to the distal re-entry point.
- The dissection begins with an intimal tear and is propagated by the ingress of blood into the media spiralling through the length of the aorta.
- Multiple **re-entry tears** are often present in the descending aorta.
- It is important to distinguish the entry tear from the extent of the dissection as surgery for Type A thoracic aortic dissection aims to **excise the entry tear** but often leaves residual separated layers of the aortic arch and descending aorta. As the entry tear is closed and the layers are joined at the distal ascending aorta, no further blood can enter the false lumen unless additional entry intimal tears exist within the aorta.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

- **Ascending aorta (65%)** — approximately 2 cm above the non-coronary sinus
- **Descending aorta (20%)** — proximally on the left anterolateral wall
- **Aortic arch (10%)** — opposite the innominate artery on the lesser curve

## 26. What are the different investigations available for the diagnosis of acute Type A aortic dissection?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_17_Figure_8.jpeg)

**Figure 15. Acute Type A aortic dissection as demonstrated by: A) transoesophageal echocardiography; B) computed tomography; C) magnetic resonance imaging; and D) aortography. The dissection flap (arrows) separates the true and the false lumens.**

- **Transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE, 98% sensitive)** — provides clear images and quantifies AR, but:
  - **a)** Results are operator-dependent
  - **b)** Passing the TOE probe may cause anxiety and hypertension
  - **c)** There is a **blind spot** in the distal ascending aorta and proximal aortic arch
- **Computed tomography with contrast enhancement (95% sensitive)** — quick, commonly available, images pleura, head/neck vessels, pericardium; risk of contrast nephropathy
- **Magnetic resonance imaging (99% sensitive)** — gold standard imaging but not always available
- **Aortography (80% sensitive)** — historically gold standard, now rarely used, may precipitate aortic rupture

</div></details>

- **Central venous access**

## 28. How is the right axillary artery cannulated for cardiopulmonary bypass?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_19_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_19_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Figure 16. Axillary artery cannulation.**

- The patient is placed in a supine position with upper body elevated to **20°** and tilted towards the left, so that the skin overlying the infraclavicular fossa is on a horizontal plane.
- A **5 cm infraclavicular incision** is then made beneath the lateral third of the clavicle.
- The pectoralis major fibres are split with pectoralis minor retracted laterally.
- The axillary artery is mobilised from under the axillary vein **avoiding the medial and lateral cords of the brachial plexus**.
- An **18Fr straight arterial cannula** is then placed into the axillary artery, either **directly** (using the Seldinger wire technique) or **indirectly** via an 8 mm woven Dacron® graft sutured with 5-0 polypropylene.
- It is important to **compare right and left radial artery pressures** to ensure adequate cerebral perfusion.
- This technique is **not suitable** if the patient has an aortic dissection that extends into the brachiocephalic artery.

## 29. What are the principles of surgery for Type A aortic dissection?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_20_Picture_17.jpeg)

**Figure 17. Acute Type A aortic dissection. A) Following opening of the pericardium, a bluish discoloration of the ascending aorta is observed, indicating blood in the aortic wall. B) Following aortotomy, fresh haematoma is observed in the false lumen of the ascending aorta.**

![](_page_21_Figure_5.jpeg)

**Figure 18. Decision-making algorithm delineating the surgical options for acute Type A aortic dissection.**

- **Entry tear** — resect and replace the site of the aortic entry tear
- **Aortic root** — to prevent coronary malperfusion and late aortic root complications, surgery can either:
  - **a) Repair the aortic sinus segments** by adhesive reconstruction to obliterate the false lumen; or
  - **b) Replace the aortic root**
- **Aortic valve** — resuspension or replacement of the aortic valve
- **Aortic arch** — hemi-arch or total arch replacement depending on whether the entry tear has extended into the aortic arch
- **Distal anastomotic line** — adhesive reconstruction at the distal anastomosis to obliterate the false lumen and restore flow through the true lumen
- **The different surgical options include:**
  - **a) Ascending aorta interposition graft**
  - **b) Ascending aorta interposition graft and aortic valve replacement**
  - **c) Ascending aorta interposition graft and resuspension of the aortic valve**
  - **d) Aortic root replacement (Bentall or Cabrol)**
  - **e) Valve-sparing aortic root replacement (Yacoub or David)**
  - **f) In addition, hemi-arch or total arch replacement**
- The underlying principle of surgery for these patients is to prevent the life-threatening complications of acute Type A aortic dissection, which include intra-pericardial rupture, tamponade, myocardial ischaemia and aortic regurgitation.
- **Mortality without surgery approximates 90%** , whereas **surgical mortality ranges between 10–20%** . Five-year survival of hospital survivors following surgery is **60%**.

</div></details>

## 30. What is the management of a Stanford Type B thoracic aortic dissection?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_22_Picture_5.jpeg)

**Figure 19. Type B aortic dissection demonstrated on a coronal computed tomography scan.**

- **Medical management with blood pressure control** — labetalol (α and β-blocker) is used to reduce the force of ventricular contraction, shear stress and absolute blood pressure.
- The **rationale for not operating** on all Type B aortic dissections is:
  - **a)** The mortality with medical management is **10%** compared with an operative mortality of **27%** and an operative paraplegia rate of **24%**
  - **b)** The **5-year survival is not improved** with surgery

## 33. What are the requirements and complications of endovascular stent graft deployment in the thoracic aorta?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_24_Picture_13.jpeg)

![](_page_24_Picture_14.jpeg)

**Figure 22. Endovascular stent graft.**

- **Requirements for optimal results:**
  - **a) Proximal and distal landing zones >1.5 cm** of normal aorta
  - **b) Femoral arteries >7 mm diameter** to allow access for the stent graft
  - **c) Relatively straight descending thoracic aorta**
- **Complications:**
  - **a) Stent complication (endoleak, stent migration)** — 10–15%
  - **b) Local vascular complication** — 5–10%
  - **c) Mortality** — 5–10%
  - **d) Aortic trauma (rupture, dissection, fistula)** — 5%
  - **e) Stroke or paraplegia** — 5%

## 34. Describe the classification of endoleaks that may occur following endovascular stent grafting</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Endoleak** is defined as blood flow between the outside of the stent graft and the diseased vessel wall. It is classified according to the source of blood:
  - **a) Type I:** leak at the junction of the aorta and the stent graft due to an inadequate seal
  - **b) Type II:** back bleeding vessels within the aneurysmal sac
  - **c) Type III:** leak through a defect in the stent graft prosthesis (graft failure)
  - **d) Type IV:** leak through the pores of the stent graft fabric (graft porosity)

</div></details>
