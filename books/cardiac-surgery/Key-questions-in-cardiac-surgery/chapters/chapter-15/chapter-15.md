# Infective Endocarditis

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology and Pathogenesis
*   **Native valve endocarditis** — incidence 2–4 cases per 100,000 persons/year.
*   **Prosthetic valve endocarditis** — 0.5–1% per year.
*   **Distribution:** left-sided valves (mitral > aortic); tricuspid 5%; pulmonary rare.
*   **Pathogenesis:** endothelial disruption + valvular lesions (AS, MR) → non-laminar flow → platelet-fibrin thrombus → bacterial colonisation → vegetation → spread/embolisation.

### Clinical Features
*   **Infection:** fever, night sweats, rigors, weight loss, malaise, anaemia, clubbing, splenomegaly (chronic).
*   **Immune complex deposition:**
    - Roth spots — retinal boat-shaped haemorrhage with pale centre
    - Splinter haemorrhages — thin reddish-brown lines in nailbed
    - Osler’s nodes — painful pulp infarcts on fingers/toes
    - Janeway lesions — painless palmar/plantar erythema
    - Vasculitis (brain, skin, kidney), arthralgia
*   **Cardiac:** new or changing murmur, heart block, heart failure, pyopericardium.
*   **Emboli:** cerebral, retinal, splenic, mesenteric, renal, lower limb (fragile in fungal endocarditis).

### Common Organisms

**Native valve:**
*   Streptococcus (45%) — viridans (milleri, oralis, mitis, mutans, salivarius) or bovis
*   Staphylococcus aureus/epidermidis (35%)
*   Enterococcus faecalis (10%)
*   HACEK, anaerobes, Coxiella burnetii (Q fever), Chlamydia, fungi

**Prosthetic valve (<2 months):**
*   Staphylococcus aureus/epidermidis (45–50%)
*   Gram-negative bacilli, fungi

**Prosthetic valve (>2 months):**
*   Streptococcus viridans (45%)
*   Staphylococcus aureus/epidermidis (35%)
*   Enterococcus faecalis (10%)

### HACEK Organisms
Gram-negative fastidious bacilli requiring 10% CO₂:
*   Haemophilus (aphrophilus, not influenzae)
*   Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans
*   Cardiobacterium hominis
*   Eikenella corrodens
*   Kingella kingae

### Antibiotics (Empirical/Organism-Specific)
| Organism | Antibiotic |
|----------|------------|
| Unknown (Streptococcus suspected) | Benzylpenicillin + gentamicin |
| Unknown (Staphylococcus suspected) | Vancomycin + gentamicin |
| Streptococcus viridans | Benzylpenicillin + gentamicin |
| Staphylococcus aureus | Vancomycin + gentamicin |
| Enterococcus | Amoxycillin + gentamicin |
| HACEK (β-lactam resistant) | Ceftriaxone |

### Causes of Culture-Negative Endocarditis
*   Prior antibiotic therapy
*   Slow-growing/fastidious organisms (HACEK, nutritionally variant Streptococci, Brucella, Legionella, Corynebacterium, Listeria, Bartonella)
*   Fungal endocarditis
*   Obligate intracellular parasites (Coxiella burnetii, Chlamydiae)
*   Non-infective (marantic) endocarditis

### Marantic (Non-Infective) Endocarditis
*   Mucinous adenocarcinoma (pancreas, lung, upper GI)
*   Lymphoma, bladder carcinoma
*   Anti-phospholipid syndrome (Hughes’ syndrome)
*   Acute rheumatic fever
*   Libman-Sacks endocarditis (SLE, scleroderma)

### Duke’s Diagnostic Criteria
**Major criteria:**
1. Three positive blood cultures (12h apart) with typical organisms (S. viridans/bovis, S. aureus, Enterococci, HACEK)
2. Evidence of endocardial infection (vegetation, abscess, prosthetic valve dehiscence, new regurgitation)

**Minor criteria:**
1. Predisposing factors (cardiac lesion, IV drug abuse)
2. Fever >38°C
3. Embolic/vascular phenomena (splinter haemorrhages, vasculitis)
4. Immunological phenomena (Osler’s nodes, Roth spots)
5. Serology consistent with endocarditis
6. Blood cultures compatible but not typical
7. Echocardiographic findings (not covered by major criteria)

**Diagnosis:**
*   **Definite (specificity 99%, sensitivity >80%):** 2 major; 1 major + 3 minor; 5 minor; or pathological evidence
*   **Possible:** findings consistent but insufficient
*   **Rejected:** alternative diagnosis, resolution within 4 days of antibiotics, no evidence at surgery/autopsy

### Indications for Surgery — Native Valve Endocarditis (AHA)
**Class I:**
*   Valvular regurgitation/stenosis leading to heart failure
*   AR/MR with raised LVEDP, LAP, or moderate pulmonary hypertension
*   Fungal or highly resistant organisms
*   Local invasion → heart block, annular/aortic abscess, fistula, leaflet perforation

**Class IIa:**
*   Recurrent emboli with persistent vegetation despite antibiotics

**Class IIb:**
*   Mobile vegetation >10 mm

### Indications for Surgery — Prosthetic Valve Endocarditis (AHA)
**Class I:**
*   Heart failure, valve dehiscence, worsening regurgitation/obstruction, local invasion

**Class IIa:**
*   Persistent bacteraemia or recurrent emboli despite antibiotics, or relapsing infection

**Class III (contraindication):**
*   Uncomplicated prosthetic valve endocarditis with sensitive organism

### Surgical Principles
*   Debridement of infected tissues
*   Closure of cardiac defects
*   Valve repair (preferred if <50% affected) vs replacement
*   Aortic homografts for root abscesses
*   IV drug abusers with tricuspid endocarditis: valvectomy without replacement (only if no significant pulmonary hypertension)

### Aortic Peri-Annular Abscess Treatment
*   Bovine pericardial patch
*   Aortic valve homograft

### Outcomes
| | Operative mortality | 5-year survival |
|---|---|---|
| Native valve | 5–10% | 80% |
| Prosthetic valve | 10–20% | 60% |

### Antibiotic Prophylaxis for Dental Procedures
| Patient | Regimen |
|---------|---------|
| Standard | Amoxycillin (3 g oral 1h pre-op or 2 g IV 30 min pre-op) |
| Penicillin allergy | Clindamycin (600 mg oral 1h pre-op) |
| Previous endocarditis | Amoxycillin (2 g IV 30 min pre-op) + gentamicin (120 mg IV 30 min pre-op) |
| Previous endocarditis + penicillin allergy | Vancomycin (1 g IV 1h pre-op) + gentamicin (120 mg IV 30 min pre-op) |

**Note:** NICE guidelines (UK) do not recommend routine prophylaxis for prosthetic valve patients — controversial; many surgeons still advocate.

## 1. What is the risk of infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Native valve endocarditis has an incidence of **2–4 cases per 100,000 persons per year**.
- The risk of prosthetic valve endocarditis is **0.5–1% per year** following valve surgery.
- Infective endocarditis usually affects the left-sided valves (mitral valve more common than the aortic valve) with **5%** of cases involving the tricuspid valve and the pulmonary valve rarely being affected.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 2. What is the pathogenesis of infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Normal cardiac endothelium is resistant to infection.
- Even with transient bacteraemia, normal immune mechanisms including **thrombocidins** (microbicidal proteins released by platelets) prevent endocarditis.
- Risk factors for native valve endocarditis include:
  - **a) Endothelial disruption**
  - **b) Valvular lesions** such as aortic stenosis or mitral regurgitation resulting in non-laminar flow
- These risk factors induce platelet-fibrin thrombus formation with subsequent bacterial colonisation resulting in **vegetation** formation and spread into the surrounding tissues or embolisation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 3. What are the clinical features of infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Clinical features of infection** — fever, night sweats, rigors, weight loss, malaise, anaemia, and clubbing and splenomegaly if chronic
- **Clinical features of immune complex deposition** are:
  - **a) Roth spots** — retinal boat-shaped haemorrhage with a pale centre
  - **b) Splinter haemorrhages** — thin reddish-brown lines in the nailbed
  - **c) Osler’s nodes** — painful pulp infarcts on fingers, toes, palms or soles
  - **d) Janeway lesions** — painless flat palmar or plantar erythema
  - **e) Vasculitis** (brain, skin, kidney) and arthralgia
- **Clinical features of the cardiac lesion** — new or changing murmur, heart block, heart failure, pyopericardium
- **Clinical features of emboli** (cerebral, retinal, splenic, mesenteric, renal or lower limb) — resulting in organ infarct or abscess, especially with fungal endocarditis (due to friable emboli)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 4. What are the commonest organisms in patients with native valve infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Streptococcus (45%)** — viridans (including milleri, oralis, mitis, mutans, salivarius) or bovis
- **Staphylococcus aureus or epidermidis (35%)**
- **Enterococcus faecalis (10%)**
- Diphtheroid bacilli and micro-aerophilic Streptococci
- **HACEK organisms** (see below)
- Anaerobic gram negative bacilli — Fusobacterium, Bacteroides, Streptobacillus moniliformis, Propionibacterium acnes, Listeria monocytogenes, Brucella abortus, Legionella
- **Coxiella burnetii (Q fever)**
- **Chlamydia psittaci or trachomatis**
- **Fungal** — Candida, Aspergillus, Histoplasma

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 5. What are the HACEK organisms?</summary><div class="details-content">

- A group of gram negative fastidious bacilli that require prolonged culture in **10% CO₂**:
  - **a) Haemophilus species** (including aphrophilus but not influenzae)
  - **b) Actinobacillus actinomycetemcomitans**
  - **c) Cardiobacterium hominis**
  - **d) Eikenella corrodens**
  - **e) Kingella kingae**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 6. What are the commonest organisms in patients with early (<2 months) prosthetic valve infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Staphylococcus aureus and epidermidis (45–50%)**
- Gram negative bacilli
- Fungi
- Streptococcus and Enterococcus (**<10%**)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 7. What are the commonest organisms in patients with late (>2 months) prosthetic valve infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Streptococcus viridans (45%)**
- **Staphylococcus aureus and epidermidis (35%)**
- **Enterococcus faecalis (10%)**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 8. Which antibiotics should be used for the common organisms in infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- If the organism is **unknown** — benzylpenicillin and gentamicin (to cover Streptococcus) or vancomycin and gentamicin (if Staphylococcus suspected)
- **Streptococcus viridans** — benzylpenicillin and gentamicin
- **Staphylococcus aureus** — vancomycin and gentamicin
- **Enterococcus** — amoxycillin and gentamicin
- **HACEK** (often beta-lactam resistant) — ceftriaxone

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 9. What are the causes of culture-negative infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Prior antibiotic therapy**
- **Slow-growing or fastidious organisms** (HACEK, nutritionally variant Streptococci, Brucella, Legionella, Corynebacterium, Listeria, Bartonella)
- **Fungal endocarditis**
- **Obligate intracellular parasites** (Coxiella burnetii, Chlamydiae)
- **Non-infective endocarditis** (marantic endocarditis) — see below

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 10. What are the causes of non-infective (marantic) endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Mucinous adenocarcinoma** (pancreas, lung, upper gastrointestinal tract)
- **Other malignant disease** — lymphoma, bladder carcinoma
- **Anti-phospholipid syndrome** (Hughes’ syndrome)
- **Acute rheumatic fever** (Lancefield group A β-haemolytic Streptococcus)
- **Libman-Sacks endocarditis** (systemic lupus erythematosus, scleroderma)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 11. What are the causes of persistent or recurrent fever in patients with suspected infective endocarditis whilst on antibiotic treatment?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Wrong antibiotic**
- **Wrong diagnosis**
- **Spread of infection** — paravalvular or intracardiac abscess, metastatic infection or mycotic aneurysm
- **Secondary infection**, e.g. hospital-acquired chest infection, venous line infection
- **Antibiotic hypersensitivity** (usually penicillin)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 12. What are Duke’s major criteria for infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Three positive blood cultures (12 hours apart)** showing typical organisms — Streptococcus viridans or bovis, Staphylococcus aureus, Enterococci, HACEK
- **Evidence of endocardial infection** — vegetation, abscess, prosthetic valve dehiscence, new regurgitation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 13. What are Duke’s minor criteria for infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Predisposing factors** (e.g. cardiac lesion, intravenous drug abuser)
- **Fever >38°C**
- **Embolic or vascular phenomena** (e.g. splinter haemorrhages, vasculitis)
- **Immunological phenomena** (e.g. Osler’s nodes, Roth spots)
- **Serology consistent with infective endocarditis**
- **Blood cultures compatible with but not typical for endocarditis**
- **Other echocardiographic findings** consistent with infective endocarditis and not covered by major criteria

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 14. What is the significance of Duke’s criteria?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Definite endocarditis (specificity 99%, sensitivity >80%):**
  - **a) Clinical diagnosis** is made by:
    - **i) Two major criteria**
    - **ii) One major and three minor criteria**
    - **iii) Five minor criteria**
    - **iv) Pathological diagnosis** by histological evidence of active infective endocarditis (from surgical specimen or post mortem)
- **Possible endocarditis** — findings consistent with infective endocarditis but do not satisfy the requirements of definite infective endocarditis
- **Rejected diagnosis of endocarditis:**
  - **a) Firm alternative diagnosis**
  - **b) Resolution of manifestations within 4 days of antibiotic treatment**
  - **c) No evidence of infective endocarditis at surgery or post mortem after therapy for >4 days**

</div></details>

## 15. What are the indications for surgery in patients with native valve endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **b) Aortic or mitral regurgitation with raised left ventricular end-diastolic pressure, left atrial pressure or moderate pulmonary hypertension**

- **d) Local invasion resulting in heart block, annular or aortic abscess, fistula or leaflet perforation**

**Class IIa:**
- **Recurrent emboli with persistent vegetation despite antibiotics**

**Class IIb:**

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 17. What are the principles of surgery for infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Debridement of infected tissues**
- **Closure of cardiac defects**
- **Valve repair or replacement** — repair is the preferred option in mitral and tricuspid valve endocarditis, especially if **<50% of the valve is affected**
- **Aortic homografts** are useful in patients with aortic root abscesses
- In intravenous drug abuse patients with tricuspid valve endocarditis, it may be necessary to perform **tricuspid valvectomy without replacement** to reduce the risk of recurrence but only in the **absence of significant pulmonary hypertension**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 18. What are the treatment options for aortic peri-annular abscesses?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Bovine pericardial patch**
- **Aortic valve homograft**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 19. What is the operative mortality and long-term survival of patients undergoing surgery for infective endocarditis?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Operative mortality:**
  - Native valve endocarditis — **5–10%**
  - Prosthetic valve endocarditis — **10–20%**
- **5-year survival:**
  - Native valve endocarditis — **80%**
  - Prosthetic valve endocarditis — **60%**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 20. What are the antibiotic prophylaxis regimes for patients with prosthetic cardiac valves used prior to dental procedures?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Standard** — amoxycillin (3 g oral 1 hour pre-operatively or 2 g IV 30 minutes pre-operatively)
- **Standard (patients with penicillin allergy)** — clindamycin (600 mg oral 1 hour pre-operatively)
- **Patients with previous infective endocarditis** — amoxycillin (2 g IV 30 minutes pre-operatively) and gentamicin (120 mg IV 30 minutes pre-operatively)
- **Patients with previous infective endocarditis and penicillin allergy** — vancomycin (1 g IV 1 hour pre-operatively) and gentamicin (120 mg IV 30 minutes pre-operatively)
- Recently, **NICE (National Institute for Health and Clinical Excellence) guidelines** have suggested that prophylactic antibiotics are **not required** for all patients with prosthetic valves undergoing dental procedures. These guidelines, however, are **very controversial** and many surgeons still advocate the use of antimicrobial prophylaxis in these patients.

</div></details>
