# Myocardial Protection

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Principles of Myocardial Protection
*   **Aortic cross-clamping** deprives coronary arteries of oxygenated blood → myocardial damage must be minimised.
*   **Methods to reduce myocardial O₂ demand:**
    - **Unloading the heart** (cardiopulmonary bypass) — 6 mL O₂/100g/min
    - **Stopping the heart** (cardioplegic diastolic arrest) — 1 mL O₂/100g/min (90% reduction)
    - **Cooling the heart** (hypothermia) — 7% reduction per 1°C
*   **Ischaemia-reperfusion injury:** damage following restoration of blood supply → triggered by oxygen-free radicals, calcium influx, leucocytes, lactate accumulation, ATP depletion. Results in arrhythmias, myocardial stunning, cell death.

### Routes of Cardioplegia Delivery
*   **Antegrade (aortic root):** 60–100 mmHg, 250 mL/min → arrest in 30–60 s. Unpredictable with CAD; contraindicated in severe AR (ventricular distension).
*   **Antegrade (direct coronary ostia):** used during aortic valve/root procedures.
*   **Retrograde (coronary sinus):** 30–50 mmHg, 150 mL/min → arrest in 2–4 min. Alternative: direct right atrial delivery with SVC/IVC cannulation.
*   **Retrograde cannula insertion:** purse-string 4 cm below RAA; guide past Thebesian valve; confirm position by palpation, manometry (20–30 mmHg), aspiration of dark blood.

### Retrograde Cardioplegia
*   **Advantages:** useful in severe AR, significant CAD, coronary ostial stenosis; does not interrupt operation.
*   **Disadvantages:** less predictable distribution; concerns about RV protection when used alone.

### Cardioplegia Solutions
*   **Crystalloid (St. Thomas’ solution):** Na⁺ 110, K⁺ 16, Ca²⁺ 1.2, Mg²⁺ 16, Cl⁻ 160 mmol/L.
*   **Blood cardioplegia (4:1 blood:St. Thomas):** plus procaine, glutamate, aspartate, oxygen-free radical scavengers, adenosine.
*   **Blood advantages:** provides oxygen/nutrients, buffering capacity, oncotic proteins (↓ oedema), even distribution, faster arrest, free radical scavengers (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione, vitamins C/E).
*   **Efficacy:** equal in normal ventricles; blood cardioplegia may benefit unstable angina or LV dysfunction.

### Mechanism of Cardioplegic Arrest
*   **Extracellular solutions (St. Thomas):** ↑ extracellular K⁺ → prevents repolarisation.
*   **Intracellular solutions (Bretschneider):** ↓ extracellular Na⁺ → blocks depolarisation.
*   **Magnesium:** blocks myosin phosphorylase, delays Ca²⁺ flux, inhibits excitation-contraction coupling.

### Terminal Warm Blood Cardioplegia (‘Hot Shot’)
*   Given immediately before removing aortic cross-clamp.
*   **Effects:** washes out anaerobic metabolites (lactic acid); provides substrate resuscitation (oxygen, ATP). Improves post-ischaemic metabolism and contractility.

### Cross-Clamp Fibrillation
*   **Sequence:** CPB with mild hypothermia (32–34°C) → decompress LV → electrical fibrillation → apply cross-clamp during minimal flow → perform distal anastomosis → remove clamp → DC cardioversion → proximal anastomosis → repeat.
*   **Principles:** electrical fibrillation prior to clamping → global ischaemia with fibrillation → anoxic arrest. Protection from unloading, reduced work, ischaemic pre-conditioning. LV decompression prevents distension/subendocardial ischaemia.
*   **Advantages:** simple, inexpensive, shorter clamp/CPB times, facilitates vessel identification.
*   **Disadvantages:** aortic trauma, thromboembolism (especially in arteriopaths).
*   **Efficacy:** similar to cold blood cardioplegia for enzyme release and LV function.

### Ischaemic Pre-Conditioning
*   Repeated brief sub-lethal ischaemia followed by reperfusion protects myocardium during subsequent prolonged ischaemia (preserves ATP, improves contractile recovery).
*   **First window:** immediately after insult, lasts ~12 hours.
*   **Second window:** 24–36 hours after insult.
*   **Mechanism:** adenosine receptors.
*   **Applied in** cross-clamp fibrillation (sequential graft construction) and OPCAB.

### OPCAB Myocardial Protection
*   Intracoronary shunts
*   Ischaemic pre-conditioning
*   Construct LIMA-to-LAD anastomosis first

### Redo Surgery with Patent LIMA
*   LIMA balloon (pre-operative fluoroscopic guidance)
*   Dissect and temporarily occlude LIMA
*   Moderate hypothermic fibrillatory arrest (without occluding LIMA)
*   Cooling (28°C) with continuous retrograde or intermittent antegrade cold blood cardioplegia (without isolating LIMA)

</div>

## 1. What are the principles of myocardial protection during cardiac surgery?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Using cardiopulmonary bypass and cross-clamping of the aorta, the coronary arteries are deprived of oxygenated blood. During this ischaemic time, damage to the myocardium needs to be minimised, hence the concept of myocardial protection.
- Several different methods of myocardial protection can be used to reduce myocardial O₂ demands:
  - **a) Unloading the heart** (cardiopulmonary bypass)
  - **b) Stopping the heart** (cardioplegic diastolic arrest)
  - **c) Cooling the heart** (hypothermia)
- By arresting the heart, O₂ demands are reduced by **90%** (Table 1; Figure 1).

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Table 1: Cardiac metabolic demands</summary><div class="details-content">

| | O₂ consumption |
|---|---|
| Beating loaded heart | 10 mL O₂ per 100 g myocardium per minute |
| Unloaded heart (cardiopulmonary bypass) | 6 mL O₂ per 100 g myocardium per minute |
| Arrested heart (cardioplegia) | 1 mL O₂ per 100 g myocardium per minute |

</div></details>

- Hypothermia further reduces the myocardial oxygen consumption and metabolic rate by a factor of **7% for every 1°C decrease** in temperature.
- Using ‘off-pump’ coronary artery bypass surgery removes the ischaemic insult of aortic cross-clamping but has ischaemic issues of its own (see below).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 2. What are the effects of cooling on the myocardium?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Cooling the heart results in:
  - **a) Decreased metabolic rate**
  - **b) Decreased enzyme function**
  - **c) Decreased membrane stability**
  - **d) Increased calcium sequestration**
  - **e) Increased glucose utilization**
  - **f) Decreased ATP generation**
  - **g) Decreased tissue oxygen uptake**
  - **h) Decreased osmotic homeostasis**
  - **i) Increased hyperviscosity with rouleaux formation**
- The reduction in myocardial metabolism attributable to hypothermia is **much less** compared to that caused by diastolic arrest.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 3. What is ischaemia-reperfusion injury?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Ischaemia-reperfusion injury** represents damage to tissues following restoration of blood supply after a period of ischaemia.
- The reperfusion triggers an inflammatory response inducing tissue damage secondary to **oxygen-free radicals**, **calcium influx**, **leucocytes**, **lactate accumulation** and **high-energy phosphate depletion**.
- Oxygen-free radicals cause tissue damage by inducing interstitial and intracellular oedema, endothelial injury and increased microvascular permeability.
- Ischaemia-reperfusion injury of the heart may result in **cardiac arrhythmias**, **myocardial stunning** and **myocyte cell death**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 4. What are the options for myocardial protection during cardiac surgery?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Cardioplegic arrest
- Aortic cross-clamping with electrically-induced fibrillation
- Hypothermic arrest with fibrillation (where the aorta remains unclamped)
- ‘Off-pump’ coronary artery surgery
- Pre-operative and peri-operative measures to optimize the balance between the myocardial oxygen supply and demand are also important, including:
  - **a) Administering supplemental oxygen**
  - **b) Minimizing tachycardia** (beta blockers) and hypotension
  - **c) When indicated, using an intra-aortic balloon pump**
  - **d) Giving pre-operative nitrates**

</div></details>

## 5. What are the different routes for delivering cardioplegia?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_3_Picture_4.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Antegrade cardioplegia delivered via a cannula placed in the ascending aorta. RA = right atrium.**

- **Antegrade cardioplegia delivered via the aortic root** (standard method) proximal to the aortic cross-clamp at a pressure of **60–100 mmHg** at **250 mL/min** usually results in arrest within **30–60 s**. Delivery may be unpredictable due to coronary artery disease and should **not** be used in the presence of severe aortic regurgitation, to avoid ventricular damage by mechanical dilation (Figure 2).

![](_page_4_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 3. Antegrade cardioplegia delivered directly into the coronary ostia. The aortic root has been excised, aortic valve exposed and coronary ostia mobilized as buttons. PA = pulmonary artery.**

- **Antegrade cardioplegia delivered directly into the coronary ostia**, such as during aortic valve and root procedures (Figure 3).

![](_page_5_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 4. Retrograde cardioplegia. RA = right atrium.**

- **Retrograde cardioplegia delivered via the coronary sinus** at a pressure of **30–50 mmHg** at **150 mL/min** usually results in arrest over 2–4 minutes (Figure 4).
- An **alternative method** of delivering retrograde cardioplegia is independent cannulation of the SVC and IVC, which are then isolated with tapes. Cardioplegia is then delivered directly into the right atrium. This is a useful technique when these cannulae are already in position during mitral surgery. It also ensures that cardioplegia is delivered via both the Thebesian veins and coronary sinus, and avoids the pitfalls of incorrect cannula position within the coronary sinus and/or variations in coronary venous anatomy.
- Cardioplegia is delivered through a **separate roller pump** with a bubble trap and microfilter.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 6. How is a retrograde cardioplegia cannula inserted?</summary><div class="details-content">

- A **1 cm² purse-string** is placed **~4 cm inferior** to the right atrial appendage.
- Following institution of cardiopulmonary bypass, the right atrium is filled with blood (via the bypass machine) to avoid entrapment of air.
- The tip of the retrograde cardioplegia cannula is curved to **120°** and passed over the inferior vena cava cannula.
- The left hand of the operating surgeon is used to guide the retrograde cannula past the **Thebesian valve** into the coronary sinus within the atrioventricular groove.
- The position of the retrograde cannula can be checked by:
  - **a) Palpation** of the cannula within the coronary sinus
  - **b) Manometry** with the coronary sinus pressure **~20–30 mmHg**
  - **c) Aspirating dark venous blood** (as the myocardium has a high oxygen uptake)
- Complications include malposition and rupture or tearing of the coronary sinus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 7. What are the advantages and disadvantages of using retrograde cardioplegia?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Advantages:**
  - **a) Useful in patients with severe aortic regurgitation**, significant coronary arterial occlusive disease or severe coronary ostial stenosis
  - **b) Retrograde cardioplegia can also be given without interrupting the flow of the operation**
- **Disadvantages:**
  - **a) Less predictable distribution** than antegrade cardioplegia
  - **b) Concerns regarding protection of the right ventricle** when using retrograde cardioplegia alone

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 8. What are the different cardioplegia solutions available?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Crystalloid cardioplegia**, usually given cold (4–10°C)
- **Blood cardioplegia**, which can be given hypothermic or normothermic
- Although crystalloid and blood cardioplegia have equal efficacy in patients with normal ventricles, some studies have shown the benefit of blood cardioplegia in patients with **unstable angina** or **left ventricular dysfunction**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 9. How does cardioplegia cause cardiac arrest?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Cardioplegic solutions induce diastolic arrest by altering the resting potential (**–90 mV**) and ionic gradients (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Cl⁻) in the myocyte with two main mechanisms of action:
  - **a) Extracellular solutions** (such as St. Thomas’ solution) — prevent cardiomyocyte repolarisation by increasing the potassium concentration in the extracellular fluid
  - **b) Intracellular solutions** (such as Bretschneider’s solution) — block depolarisation by lowering extracellular sodium concentrations

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 10. What are the main components of cardioplegia solution?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Crystalloid cardioplegia (St. Thomas’ solution)** contains:
  - **a) Sodium** — 110 mmol/L
  - **b) Potassium** — 16 mmol/L
  - **c) Calcium** — 1.2 mmol/L
  - **d) Magnesium** — 16 mmol/L
  - **e) Chloride** — 160 mmol/L
- **Blood cardioplegia** contains blood and St. Thomas’ solution at a ratio of **4:1**, plus procaine (membrane stabilizer), glutamate, aspartate, oxygen-free radical scavengers and adenosine.
- **Magnesium** blocks phosphorylase activity of myosin, delays calcium flux and inhibits excitation-contraction coupling.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 11. What are the advantages of blood cardioplegia over crystalloid cardioplegia?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Blood:**
  - **a) Provides oxygen and nutrients**
  - **b) Provides a buffering capacity**
  - **c) Minimises intracellular oedema** due to its oncotic proteins
  - **d) Distributes cardioplegia more evenly** thereby inducing a faster onset of arrest
  - **e) Scavenges oxygen-free radicals** as it contains superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione and vitamins C and E

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 12. What are the principles of terminal warm blood cardioplegia (‘hot-shot’)?</summary><div class="details-content">

- A **‘hot shot’** represents a dose of warm cardioplegia given **immediately before removing the aortic cross-clamp**.
- Warm cardioplegia:
  - **a) Allows washout of the products of anaerobic metabolism**, such as lactic acid
  - **b) Provides substrate resuscitation of ischaemic myocytes** with oxygen and ATP
- It has been shown to improve myocardial metabolism and contractility once ventricular contractions resume.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 13. What myocardial protection strategies are available for patients undergoing redo cardiac surgery with a patent left internal mammary artery (LIMA)?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **LIMA balloon** (positioned pre-operatively under fluoroscopic guidance), which can be occluded when giving cardioplegia
- **Dissect out the LIMA** and temporarily occlude when giving cardioplegia
- **Moderate hypothermic fibrillatory arrest** (without occluding the LIMA)
- **Cooling (28°C)** with either continuous retrograde cardioplegia or intermittent antegrade cold blood cardioplegia without isolation of the LIMA

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 14. What is the sequence of events when using the cross-clamp fibrillation technique?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Establish cardiopulmonary bypass with mild hypothermia (**32–34°C**)
- Decompress the left ventricle
- Induce ventricular fibrillation using an electric fibrillator
- Apply the aortic cross-clamp during minimal flow on cardiopulmonary bypass
- Perform the distal anastomosis
- Remove the aortic cross-clamp
- DC cardiovert into sinus rhythm (if necessary)
- Perform the proximal anastomosis
- Repeat the sequence with the next distal anastomosis

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 15. What are the principles of cross-clamp fibrillation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- The heart is **electrically fibrillated prior to aortic cross-clamping** thereby inducing global ischaemia with fibrillation followed by anoxic arrest.
- Some degree of myocardial protection is afforded by unloading the heart (cardiopulmonary bypass), reducing myocardial work (fibrillation) and ischaemic pre-conditioning (see below).
- The left ventricle is **decompressed before placing the aortic cross-clamp** in order to prevent myocardial distension, subendocardial ischaemia and myofibrillar disruption.
- **Mild hypothermia (32–34°C)** increases permissible global ischaemia to approximately **15 minutes** and dampens the amplitude of the fibrillation.
- Lower temperatures, however, cannot be used as it is difficult to defibrillate below **30°C**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 16. What are the advantages and disadvantages of cross-clamp fibrillation?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Advantages:** simple, inexpensive, shorter aortic cross-clamp and cardiopulmonary bypass times, and facilitates identification and differentiation of coronary arteries from veins.
- **Disadvantages:** aortic trauma and thrombo-embolism from repeated aortic cross-clamping (especially in arteriopaths).
- Several prospective trials have shown the efficacy of cross-clamp fibrillation is **similar to cold blood cardioplegia** as regards postoperative enzyme release and left ventricular function.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 17. What is ischaemic pre-conditioning?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Repeated brief insults of sub-lethal ischaemia followed by reperfusion** protects the myocardium during subsequent prolonged episodes of ischaemia by preserving myocardial ATP, thereby resulting in improved recovery of contractile function after reperfusion.
- The **‘first window’** of improved recovery immediately follows the ischaemic insult and lasts about **12 hours**, with the **‘second window’** of recovery occurring **24–36 hours** after the ischaemic insult.
- Ischaemic pre-conditioning occurs through **adenosine receptors**.
- The cross-clamp fibrillation technique with sequential graft construction allows gradually increased areas of myocardium to be reperfused, using ischaemic pre-conditioning.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 18. What techniques are available for myocardial protection during ‘off-pump’ coronary artery bypass surgery?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Intracoronary shunts
- Ischaemic pre-conditioning
- Constructing the left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending artery anastomosis as the first graft

</div></details>
