# Electrocardiography

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### ECG Basics
*   **ECG paper speed** — 25 mm/sec (0.04 sec/small square, 0.2 sec/large square). Height: 0.1 mV/small square, 0.5 mV/large square.
*   **Lead placement** — limb leads (I, II, III) bipolar; augmented limb leads (aVR, aVL, aVF) unipolar. Chest leads V1–V6 positioned from right sternal edge (4th ICS) to midaxillary line (5th ICS).
*   **Heart rate calculation** — 60 / RR interval (seconds) or 300 / number of large squares between R waves.
*   **Normal values** — PR interval 0.12–0.2 sec, QRS duration 0.08–0.12 sec, QTc ≤0.42 sec, P wave <2.5 mm tall, <0.11 sec wide.

### Rhythm Assessment
*   **Sinus rhythm** — P wave precedes each QRS, rate 60–100 bpm.
*   **Atrial fibrillation** — absent P waves, irregularly irregular QRS, fibrillatory waves.
*   **Atrial flutter** — sawtooth flutter waves in V1, atrial rate ~300 bpm, ventricular rate 150 bpm (2:1 block).
*   **Ventricular tachycardia** — broad QRS (>0.12 sec), rate >100 bpm, AV dissociation.
*   **Ventricular fibrillation** — chaotic waveform, no cardiac output.

### Axis Determination
*   **Normal axis** –30° to +90° (positive I, positive aVF). **LAD** –30° to –90° (positive I, negative aVF). **RAD** +90° to +180° (negative I, positive aVF).
*   **Causes of LAD:** LBBB, left anterior hemiblock, inferior MI, LVH, WPW.
*   **Causes of RAD:** RBBB, left posterior hemiblock, RVH, pulmonary embolus, lateral MI, ASD secundum.

### Chamber Enlargement
*   **Left atrial enlargement (P mitrale)** — wide P wave >0.11 sec, biphasic in V1 (negative terminal component).
*   **Right atrial enlargement (P pulmonale)** — tall P wave >2.5 mm in II, peaked in V1.
*   **LVH (Sokolow)** — S V1 + R V5/V6 >35 mm. Lateral T-wave inversion (strain).
*   **RVH** — R V1 + S V6 >11 mm, R/S V1 >1, right axis deviation.

### Ischaemia and Infarction
*   **Ischaemia** — ST depression, T-wave flattening/inversion.
*   **Infarction** — ST elevation (hyperacute), pathological Q waves (>0.04 sec or >25% of QRS), T-wave inversion.
*   **Localisation:**
    *   Inferior (RCA): II, III, aVF
    *   Anterior (LAD): V2–V4
    *   Lateral (Cx/LAD): I, aVL, V5–V6
    *   Posterior (Cx): tall R waves, ST depression in V1–V3 (mirror image)

### Pericarditis
*   **Stage I:** global concave ST elevation.
*   **Stage II:** pseudonormalisation.
*   **Stage III:** T-wave inversion.
*   **Stage IV:** normalisation.

### Electrolyte Abnormalities
*   **Hyperkalaemia** — peaked T waves, wide QRS, small/absent P waves → sine wave → VF.
*   **Hypokalaemia** — flattened T waves, prominent U waves, long QT.
*   **Hypercalcaemia** — short QT.
*   **Hypocalcaemia** — long QT.

### Conduction Blocks
*   **1° AV block** — PR >0.2 sec.
*   **2° AV block Mobitz I (Wenckebach)** — progressive PR prolongation until dropped beat.
*   **2° AV block Mobitz II** — fixed PR interval with intermittent non-conducted P waves.
*   **3° AV block (complete)** — complete AV dissociation, escape rhythm.
*   **RBBB** — QRS >0.12 sec, rSR’ in V1, wide S in V6.
*   **LBBB** — QRS >0.12 sec, QS/rS in V1, broad R in V6, left axis deviation.
*   **Left anterior hemiblock** — left axis deviation, qR in I/aVL, rS in II/III/aVF.
*   **Bifascicular block** — RBBB + LAHB or RBBB + LPHB.
*   **Trifascicular block** — bifascicular block + 1° AV block.

### Pre-excitation Syndromes
*   **WPW syndrome** — short PR (<0.12 sec), delta wave, wide QRS. Type A (positive delta V1), Type B (negative delta V1).
*   **LGL syndrome** — short PR (<0.12 sec), no delta wave, normal QRS.

### Long QT Syndrome
*   **QTc >0.46 sec.** Causes: congenital (Romano-Ward), drugs (sotalol, amiodarone), electrolyte disturbance (hypocalcaemia, hypokalaemia), MI. Risk of torsade de pointes.

### Exercise ECG (Bruce Protocol)
*   **Indications:** atypical chest pain, functional assessment, post-MI prognosis, exercise-induced arrhythmias.
*   **Contraindications:** severe AS, unstable angina, uncontrolled hypertension, severe left main stenosis, acute myocarditis, decompensated HF.
*   **Positive test:** ST depression >2 mm (downsloping, persisting >5 min), ST elevation, chest pain with ST changes, failure of BP to rise, ventricular arrhythmias.

## 1. What is an electrocardiogram (ECG)?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_0_Figure_5.jpeg)

**Figure 1. Electrocardiogram.**

- An electrocardiogram is a graphical representation of the heart’s electrical activity measuring the electrical potential between different parts of the body with a positive deflection representing the electrical vector moving towards the positive electrode.
- The typical ECG paper speed is **25 mm/sec** but can be increased to 50 mm/sec in order to assess the morphology of the complexes more clearly.
- The width of the small squares represents **0.04 sec** and large squares **0.2 sec** (at 25 mm/sec).
- The height of the small squares represents **0.1 mV** and large squares **0.5 mV**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 2. Where are the ECG electrodes placed?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_1_Picture_6.jpeg)

**Figure 2. Positioning of the ECG electrodes.**

- **RA** (right arm), **LA** (left arm), **RL** (right leg), and **LL** (left leg) electrodes are placed on the appropriate limb.
- **V1** is placed in the 4th intercostal space at the right sternal edge.
- **V2** is placed in the 4th intercostal space at the left sternal edge.
- **V3** is placed between V2 and V4.
- **V4** is placed in the 5th intercostal space in the midclavicular line.
- **V5** is placed between V4 and V6.
- **V6** is placed in the 5th intercostal space in the midaxillary line.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 3. What are the limb leads and augmented limb leads?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Limb leads:**
  - **a) I:** negative electrode on the right arm and positive electrode on the left arm;
  - **b) II:** negative electrode on the right arm and positive electrode on the left leg;
  - **c) III:** negative electrode on the left arm and positive electrode on the left leg.
- **Augmented limb leads:**
  - **a) aVR** (augmented vector right): positive electrode on the right arm with a negative electrode combination of the left arm and left leg;
  - **b) aVL** (augmented vector left): positive electrode on the left arm with a negative electrode combination of the right arm and left leg;
  - **c) aVF** (augmented vector foot): positive electrode on the left leg with a negative electrode combination of the right arm and left arm.
- The augmented limb leads are **unipolar** whereas the limb leads are **bipolar**.
- The RL acts as the ground lead.

## 4. What are the important components when assessing an ECG?</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Rate
- **a) Normal heart rate** is 60–100 beats per minute (bpm) with <10% variation (Figure 4);
- **b) Sinus bradycardia** is a heart rate of <60 bpm (Figure 5);
- **c) Sinus tachycardia** is a heart rate of >100 bpm (Figure 6);
- **d) Sinus arrhythmia** is a heart rate variation of >10% (Figure 7).

![](_page_4_Figure_8.jpeg)

**Figure 4. Normal sinus rhythm.**

![](_page_4_Figure_10.jpeg)

**Figure 5. Sinus bradycardia.**

![](_page_4_Figure_12.jpeg)

**Figure 6. Sinus tachycardia.**

![](_page_4_Figure_14.jpeg)

**Figure 7. Sinus arrhythmia.**

#### Rhythm
- **a) Normal sinus rhythm** — the P wave precedes each QRS complex;
- **b) Supraventricular ectopics**;
- **c) Supraventricular tachycardia:**
  - **i) Atrial fibrillation** — absence of P waves, replaced by irregular, chaotic fibrillatory (F) waves with an irregular ventricular response (often >100 bpm) (Figure 8);
  - **ii) Atrial flutter** — flutter waves (most prominent in V1) with an atrial rate typically 300 bpm and a ventricular response of 150 bpm (2:1 block), although the block may be variable (Figure 9);
  - **iii) Atrioventricular nodal (junctional) tachycardia** (Figure 10);
- **d) Ventricular ectopics**, including bigeminy and trigeminy (Figure 11);
- **e) Ventricular tachycardia** — broad QRS complexes (>0.12 sec), a ventricular rate of >100 bpm and atrioventricular discordance (Figure 12);
- **f) Ventricular fibrillation** — coarse or fine fibrillatory waves (Figure 13).

**Figure 8. Atrial fibrillation demonstrated by irregularly irregular QRS complexes and the absence of P waves.**

![](_page_6_Figure_2.jpeg)

![](_page_6_Figure_3.jpeg)

**Figure 9. Atrial flutter with: A) 4:1 block; and B) variable block.**

![](_page_6_Figure_6.jpeg)

**Figure 10. Junctional tachycardia.**

![](_page_6_Figure_8.jpeg)

**Figure 11. Ventricular ectopics every third beat (trigeminy).**

![](_page_7_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 12. Ventricular tachycardia demonstrated by a broad complex tachycardia associated with loss of cardiac output on the arterial pressure trace.**

![](_page_7_Figure_4.jpeg)

**Figure 13. Ventricular fibrillation demonstrated by coarse fibrillatory waves associated with loss of cardiac output on the arterial pressure trace.**

#### Axis
- **a) Normal QRS axis** –30° to +90°;
- **b) Left axis deviation** –30° to –90°;
- **c) Right axis deviation** +90° to +180°;
- **d) Extreme axis deviation** (‘No man’s land’) –90° to –180°.

#### P-wave morphology
- **a) Normal P wave** height <2.5 mm and width <0.11 sec in lead II;
- **b) Tall P wave (P pulmonale)** — right atrial hypertrophy;
- **c) Wide P wave (P mitrale)** — left atrial hypertrophy.

#### PR interval
- **a) Normal duration** 0.12–0.2 sec (3–5 small squares);
- **b) Short PR interval** — Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome, Lown-Ganong-Levine syndrome;
- **c) Prolonged PR interval** — heart block.

#### QRS duration and morphology
- **a) Normal QRS duration** 0.08–0.12 sec with normal Q wave <0.04 sec and <25% of total QRS complex;
- **b) Wide QRS complexes** — right or left bundle branch block;
- **c) Tall QRS complexes** — left or right ventricular hypertrophy;
- **d) Pathological Q waves** — myocardial infarction.

#### ST segment morphology
- **a) Normally isoelectric ST segment**;
- **b) ST elevation** — myocardial infarction, pericarditis, left bundle branch block;
- **c) ST depression** — myocardial ischaemia, digoxin toxicity, left ventricular hypertrophy.

#### T-wave morphology
- **a) Normal T wave**;
- **b) Tall T wave** — hyperkalaemia, hyperacute myocardial infarction, left bundle branch block;
- **c) Small T wave** — hypokalaemia;
- **d) Flattened or inverted T wave** — myocardial ischaemia, digoxin toxicity, left ventricular hypertrophy.

#### U wave
- **a) Normally absent**;
- **b) U wave present** — hypokalaemia.

#### QT interval
- **a) Corrected QT interval (QTc)** = QT interval / √RR interval;
- **b) Normal QTc** ≤0.42 sec;
- **c) Long QTc** — myocardial infarction, hypocalcaemia, sotalol, amiodarone, Romano-Ward syndrome.

</div></details>

## 5. How is the heart rate assessed on an ECG?</summary><div class="details-content">

- With each small square representing 0.04 sec, the RR interval can be measured to obtain the time interval between each cardiac cycle. The heart rate can be calculated using the formula (Figure 14):

$$ \text{Heart rate (bpm)} = \frac{60}{\text{RR interval (seconds)}} $$

![](_page_9_Figure_9.jpeg)

**Figure 14. Calculating the heart rate using the RR interval.** *RR interval = 0.36 sec → HR = 60 / 0.36 = 167 bpm.*

- An alternative is to count the number of large squares between each R wave and then use the formula (Figure 15):

$$ \text{Heart rate (bpm)} = \frac{300}{\text{number of large squares}} $$

![](_page_9_Figure_14.jpeg)

**Figure 15. Calculating the heart rate using the number of large squares method.**

</div></details>

## 10. What are the ECG findings of left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH)?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_14_Figure_4.jpeg)

**Figure 19. Left ventricular hypertrophy demonstrated by large S waves in V1, large R waves in V6 and a strain pattern in V5–6.**

- **Sokolow criteria:** S in V1 + R in V5 or V6 (whichever is taller) >35 mm.
- Other criteria used to diagnose left ventricular hypertrophy include:
  - **a)** S in V1 or R in V5 or R in V6 >25 mm;
  - **b)** R in I + S in III >25 mm;
  - **c)** R in I or R in aVL >14 mm.
- Lateral (I, aVL, V5 and V6) T-wave inversion may also be present as part of a left ventricular ‘strain’ pattern.
- It is important to appreciate the different factors that affect electrocardiographic voltage: left ventricular cavity size and muscle mass, the presence of pericardial fluid, lung volume in front of the heart and chest wall thickness.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 11. What are the ECG findings of right ventricular hypertrophy (RVH)?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_15_Figure_3.jpeg)

**Figure 20. Right ventricular hypertrophy demonstrated by a large R wave in V1 and large S wave in V6.**

- The different criteria used to diagnose right ventricular hypertrophy include:
  - **a)** R in V1 + S in V6 >11 mm;
  - **b)** R in V1 or S in V6 >7 mm;
  - **c)** R/S ratio in V1 >1 or R/S ratio in V6 <1.
- Anterior T-wave inversion may also be present as part of RVH.
- As neonates have RV predominance, their ECG shows a dominant R in V1 and S in V6.
- The axis moves towards the left with an adult configuration by the age of 15 with S in V1 and R in V6.

</div></details>

## 16. How is broad complex supraventricular tachycardia differentiated from ventricular tachycardia on the ECG?</summary><div class="details-content">

- The arrhythmia is more likely to be **ventricular** in origin if there is:
  - **a)** a QRS complex duration >0.14 s;
  - **b)** concordance throughout the chest leads;
  - **c)** QRS morphology changing with time;
  - **d)** left or right axis deviation;
  - **e)** atrioventricular dissociation;
  - **f)** presence of fusion or capture beats;
  - **g)** a regular ventricular rhythm (as opposed to atrial fibrillation with aberrant conduction).

</div></details>

## 17. What is torsade de pointes?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_21_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 29. Torsade de pointes demonstrated by ventricular tachycardia with changing QRS axes on all the leads.**

- **Torsade de pointes** is a form of ventricular tachycardia characterised by broad QRS complexes with multiple morphologies (changing QRS axis) and varying RR intervals.
- Common causes include heart block, hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia, drugs (e.g. sotalol, amiodarone, erythromycin), and long QT syndrome.

## 23. What are the ECG features of heart (atrioventricular) block?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_24_Figure_13.jpeg)

**Figure 33. 1° heart block demonstrated by a prolonged PR interval.**

- **First degree heart block** — PR interval >0.2 sec (Figure 33).

![](_page_24_Figure_17.jpeg)

**Figure 34. 2° heart block — Mobitz Type I demonstrated by progressive lengthening of the PR interval followed by a non-conducted P wave.**

- **Second degree heart block:**
  - **a) Mobitz Type I (Wenckebach phenomenon):** progressive lengthening of the PR interval until a P wave is not conducted, followed by a QRS complex with a shortened PR interval (Figure 34);
  - **b) Mobitz Type II:** fixed PR interval with failure of conduction of the atrial impulse, e.g. 2:1 block, 3:1 block, etc (Figure 35).

![](_page_25_Figure_3.jpeg)

**Figure 35. 2° heart block — Mobitz Type II demonstrated by a 2:1 block.**

![](_page_25_Figure_6.jpeg)

**Figure 36. 3° heart block demonstrated by complete atrio-ventricular dissociation.**

- **Third degree (complete) heart block** — P waves are not conducted through the AV node resulting in complete dissociation with QRS complexes. The ventricles are depolarised by a ventricular escape rhythm (Figure 36).
- Pacemakers should be considered for Mobitz Type II second degree heart block and third degree complete heart block.

- QRS <120 ms (incomplete bundle branch block).
- S in lead I and Q in lead III.

</div></details>

## 28. What is bifascicular block?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_28_Figure_7.jpeg)

**Figure 40. Bifascicular block demonstrated by left anterior hemiblock (left axis deviation), right bundle branch block (QRS duration >120 ms and predominant R wave in V1) and a normal PR interval.**

- When two of the main three fascicles are blocked:
  - **a)** RBBB and LAHB;
  - **b)** RBBB and LPHB;
  - **c)** LAHB and LPHB (i.e. LBBB).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 29. What is trifascicular block?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_29_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 41. Trifascicular block demonstrated by left anterior hemiblock (left axis deviation), right bundle branch block (QRS duration >120 ms and predominant R wave in V1) and 1° heart block (PR interval >200 ms).**

- Traditionally, trifascicular block includes:
  - **a)** RBBB;
  - **b)** LAHB or LPHB;
  - **c)** First degree heart block.
- Technically however, the three fascicles are the right bundle branch, the left anterior branch and the left posterior branch. Hence, trifascicular block by definition actually represents right bundle branch block, left anterior hemiblock and left posterior hemiblock, i.e. complete AV block.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 30. What is Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_30_Figure_2.jpeg)

![](_page_30_Figure_3.jpeg)

**Figure 42. Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome demonstrated by delta waves (arrows) and a short PR interval. A) Type A WPW with a predominant R wave in V1 and B) Type B WPW with a predominant S wave in V1.**

- WPW syndrome causes ventricular pre-excitation due to an accessory atrioventricular pathway (**bundle of Kent**).
- The ECG findings of WPW syndrome include:
  - **a)** short PR interval <120 ms;
  - **b)** delta wave — slurred upstroke from the P wave to the QRS complex (delta wave in V1 is positive in Type A WPW syndrome and negative in Type B WPW syndrome);
  - **c)** widened QRS complex (pre-excitation via the bundle of Kent followed by normal excitation via the AV node, thus resulting in a broad QRS complex);
  - **d)** ST and T-wave changes.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 31. What is Lown-Ganong-Levine (LGL) syndrome?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Ventricular pre-excitation caused by an accessory atrioventricular pathway (**James fibres**).
- The ECG findings of LGL syndrome include:
  - **a)** a short PR interval <120 ms;
  - **b)** the absence of delta waves;
  - **c)** a normal QRS complex width;
  - **d)** ST and T-wave changes.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 32. What is long QT syndrome?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_31_Figure_2.jpeg)

**Figure 43. Long QT syndrome demonstrated by a prolonged QTc (>0.46 sec).**

- Long QT syndrome is defined as a corrected QT interval (QTc) **>0.46 sec** (normal ~0.42 sec).
- It represents prolongation of ventricular repolarisation and is associated with syncope, ventricular arrhythmias (including torsades de pointes) and death.
- **Primary causes** include genetic mutations of myocyte ion channels (e.g. Romano-Ward syndrome — autosomal dominant inherited form).
- **Secondary causes** include myocardial infarction, electrolyte disturbances (hypocalcaemia), drugs (sotalol, amiodarone), and hypothyroidism.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 33. What is electric alternans?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Electric alternans** is a QRS axis or amplitude that alternates between beats.
- It may be caused by a pericardial effusion or tamponade where the heart is moving within fluid.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 34. What are the indications for an exercise ECG?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Patients with an atypical chest pain history to determine the aetiology.
- Evaluation of cardiac function and exercise capacity.
- Prognosis following myocardial infarction.
- Detection of exercise-induced arrhythmias.
- Detection of symptoms in equivocal patients with aortic stenosis.
- Exercise ECG testing has a sensitivity of **80%**, specificity of **75%** and an associated mortality of **0.01%**.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 35. What are the contra-indications for an exercise ECG?</summary><div class="details-content">

- Severe aortic stenosis.
- Acute myocarditis or pericarditis.
- Unstable angina.
- Uncontrolled hypertension.
- Severe left main stem stenosis or left main equivalent.
- Heart failure.
- Certain arrhythmias — fast atrial fibrillation, heart block, ventricular arrhythmias.
- Orthopaedic or neurological impairment.
- Acute myocardial infarction — but it is possible to perform an exercise ECG prior to discharge.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 36. What is the Bruce protocol?</summary><div class="details-content">

- The Bruce protocol is a diagnostic exercise ECG test with seven stages on the treadmill (each lasting 3 minutes) with the speed and incline progressively increased (Table 2).

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Table 2: The Bruce protocol</summary><div class="details-content">

| Stage | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Speed (mph) | 1.7 | 2.5 | 3.4 | 4.2 | 5.0 | 5.5 | 6.0 |
| Incline (%) | 10 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 18 | 20 | 22 |

</div></details>

- The **modified Bruce protocol** starts with a 0% incline at 1.7 mph for stage I, 5% incline at 1.7 mph for stage 2, then as per the Bruce protocol.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 37. What are the endpoints for an exercise ECG?</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Attainment of the target heart rate** — commonly this is 85% of the predicted maximum for the patient’s age:
  - **a)** Males predicted maximum = 220 – age;
  - **b)** Females predicted maximum = 210 – age.
- **Symptoms** — angina pectoris, undue dyspnoea, faintness, fatigue.
- **Cardiac arrhythmias** — heart block, atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia.
- **Severe hypertension**.
- **Positive test** (see below).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## 38. What is a positive exercise ECG test?</summary><div class="details-content">

![](_page_33_Figure_11.jpeg)

**Figure 44. Exercise ECG demonstrating myocardial ischaemia with ST depression in leads II, III and aVF (inferior) and leads V5–6.**

- ST depression >2 mm in several leads (down-sloping and persisting >5 minutes into the recovery period).
- ST elevation.
- Chest pain (in association with ST changes predicts an 85% sensitivity of coronary artery disease).
- Increased R-wave voltage (normally R-wave voltage decreases during exercise).
- Inverted U waves.
- Failure of systolic blood pressure to rise with exercise.
- Ventricular arrhythmias (including >10 ventricular ectopics per minute).
- New mitral regurgitation auscultated immediately after completion of the exercise test.

</div></details>
