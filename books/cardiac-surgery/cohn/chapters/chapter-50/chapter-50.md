# Endovascular Therapy for the Treatment of Thoracic Aortic Disease

##### Patients with thoracic aortic disease pose many challenges. Frequently, these patients are older and present with multiple comorbidities. Additionally, the posterior location of the descending thoracic aorta requires a large thoracotomy incision, with its own inherent morbidity.

##### The modern surgical treatment of thoracic aortic disease began in the 1950s, when successful treatment utilizing segmental resection and graft replacement was first reported by Swan, Lam, DeBakey, and Etheridge. Soon thereafter, DeBakey and Cooley reported the first successful repair of an ascending aortic aneurysm using cardiopulmonary bypass. Cardiopulmonary bypass and commercially available tube grafts were the mainstays of our surgical armamentarium for the next 30 years. Improved diagnostic capabilities, surgical techniques, and perioperative care have resulted in improved outcomes, even as the risk profile has worsened. In an effort to limit the morbidity of these operations, endovascular techniques emerged as an attractive alternative. Originally devised for high-risk patients, and following on developments directed toward aneurysms of the abdominal aorta, thoracic endovascular stent-graft technology has rapidly evolved. Although originally intended for repair of atherosclerotic thoracic aortic aneurysms, thoracic stent-graft applications have been expanded to the treatment of multiple pathologies, including acute and chronic aortic dissections, penetrating atherosclerotic aortic ulcers, and thoracic aortic trauma. Results have steadily improved, and long-term durability has been encouraging; however, the necessity for long-term follow-up remains, with obvious financial implications.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Historical Development
*   **Pioneering Era:** Endovascular stent-graft technology was first introduced by Parodi for abdominal aortic aneurysms. At Stanford, a collaborative effort produced the first homemade thoracic stent graft using Gianturco Z stents covered with woven Dacron, implanted in 1992.
*   **First-Generation Results:** In a high-risk trial of 103 patients (60% deemed unfit for open repair), complete aneurysm thrombosis was achieved in 83%, with 30-day mortality of 9%. Actuarial survival was 82% at 1 year, 49% at 5 years, and 27% at 8 years.
*   **Commercial Devices:** In 2005–2006, commercially produced stent grafts became available (Gore Excluder TAG, Medtronic Talent, Cook Zenith), featuring smaller delivery systems, nitinol exoskeletons, and improved flexibility.

### Endoleak Classification
*   **Type I:** Endoleak at proximal (A) or distal (B) attachment sites—failure of hemostatic seal.
*   **Type II:** Communication between a branch vessel (e.g., intercostal arteries) and the excluded aneurysm sac.
*   **Type III:** Endoleak at graft-to-graft junctions.
*   **Type IV:** Increase in aneurysm sac size without identifiable endoleak ("endotension").

### Early Results (TAG Trial)
*   **Outcomes:** Compared to open repair, stent-graft patients had significantly lower operative blood loss, renal failure, paraplegia, and mortality. ICU stay, hospital stay, and time to return to normal activity were reduced by approximately 50%.
*   **Mortality:** Aneurysm-related mortality at 2 years was 3% (stent-graft) vs. 10% (open repair); all-cause mortality was similar between groups.

### Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm
*   **Natural History:** Thoracic aortic aneurysms have a 5-year rupture risk of 30%. The Yale Group reported mean rupture/dissection rates of 2%/year for aneurysms <5 cm, 3% for 5.0–5.9 cm, and 6.9% for ≥6 cm.
*   **Open Repair Outcomes:** Mortality 5–10%, paraplegia 3–8% from experienced centers.
*   **Endovascular Considerations:** Coverage of the left subclavian artery is frequently employed; preoperative transposition is reserved for select cases. Spinal drains are recommended for high-risk patients.

### Thoracic Aortic Dissection
*   **Complicated Acute Type B (rupture, malperfusion, rapid expansion):** Endovascular repair covers the primary entry tear, redirecting flow into the true lumen and eliminating dynamic malperfusion. Uncovered stents can address static malperfusion in branch vessels.
*   **Uncomplicated Acute Type B:** Increasing enthusiasm for prophylactic stent grafting to promote aortic remodeling and prevent late aneurysmal degeneration.
*   **Chronic Dissection:** More challenging due to multiple fenestrations and extension into the abdomen. Complete false lumen exclusion remains difficult.

### Penetrating Atherosclerotic Ulcers (PAU) and Intramural Hematomas (IMH)
*   **Relationship:** IMH is often associated with or precipitated by PAUs. Covering the PAU with a stent graft may limit progression of IMH and allow healing.
*   **Outcomes:** In a Stanford series of 26 patients (average 51 months follow-up), primary success was 92%, with actuarial survival of 85%, 76%, and 70% at 1, 3, and 5 years. Perioperative mortality was 12%. Increasing aortic diameter and female gender were predictors of treatment failure.

### Thoracic Aortic Trauma
*   **SVS Classification:** Class I (intimal laceration only), Class II (intimal laceration + mild IMH), Class III (pseudoaneurysm), Class IV (rupture/impending rupture).
*   **Management:** Classes I and II—observation with repeat imaging. Classes III and IV—stent-graft coverage is procedure of choice when anatomically feasible. Delayed repair may be appropriate for stable patients to allow recovery from polytrauma.
*   **Paraplegia Risk:** Endografts longer than 15 cm may be associated with increased risk.

### Future Directions
*   Unibranch grafts for proximal repairs into the aortic arch; multibranch and fenestrated grafts for thoracoabdominal aneurysms.
*   Lifelong surveillance is mandatory, as even completely thrombosed sacs can elongate with late Type I endoleaks.

## HISTORY</summary><div class="details-content">

Endovascular stent-graft technology, initially targeting the abdominal aortic aneurysm, was introduced by Parodi. Balloon-expandable stents, sewn inside the ends of a vascular tube graft, were placed within the aneurysmal aorta, excluding the aneurysm sac. Simultaneously, at Stanford University Medical Center, a collaborative effort between interventional radiologists and cardiovascular surgeons proved highly synergistic and resulted in a homemade thoracic stent graft.

![](_page_1_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 50-1** First-generation stent graft assembled from articulated Z stents and covered with a woven Dacron tube graft.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Stanford Homemade Grafts</summary><div class="details-content">

Self-expanding Gianturco Z stents (Cook Company, Bloomington, IN) were fastened together and covered with a woven Dacron graft (Meadox-Boston Scientific, Natick, MA; Fig. 50-1), and then compressed into a 28-French introducer sheath. The first homemade thoracic graft was implanted in the descending thoracic aorta in 1992 after Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained.

Subsequently, a high-risk trial was approved for patients with thoracic aortic aneurysms who were deemed nonsurgical candidates. Thirteen such patients were treated utilizing stent grafts, customized-designed for each patient. Placement of these stents was successful in all patients, with complete thrombosis of the aneurysm sac reported in 12 of the 13 patients. At 1 year, there were no deaths, paraplegia, strokes, or distal embolization.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### High-Risk Trial (n = 103)</summary><div class="details-content">

Feasibility having been established, IRB approval was obtained for a subsequent trial of 103 patients, **60% of whom were deemed unfit for conventional open surgical repair**. Using the same "homemade" first-generation stent graft, complete aneurysm thrombosis was achieved in **83% of patients**. Thirty-day mortality was **9%** and was significantly associated with a history of cerebral vascular accidents and myocardial infarctions.

Major perioperative morbidities included:
- Paraplegia in 3 patients
- Cerebrovascular accidents in 7 patients
- Respiratory insufficiency in 12 patients

Actuarial survival was 81% at 1 year and 73% at 2 years. Given the high-risk nature of this patient population, these first-generation results were deemed satisfactory. It was, however, recognized that mortality and morbidity occurred frequently and that long-term follow-up was necessary to establish the long-term efficacy.

Subsequently, in 2004, mid-term results were reported for these initial 103 patients treated with first-generation stent grafts. Overall actuarial survival was dismal: **82%, 49%, and 27% at 1, 5, and 8 years, respectively**. However, for potentially operable candidates, survival was 93% and 78% at 1 and 5 years, respectively, as contrasted to 74% and 31% survival at 1 and 5 years, respectively, in those patients deemed inoperable. Late rupture in 11 patients reinforced the mandate for continued lifelong follow-up.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Extension to Type B Aortic Dissection</summary><div class="details-content">

This technology was extended to the treatment of complicated acute type B aortic dissections, as reported by Dake and associates in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1999. Again, utilizing these first-generation homemade devices, stent grafts were placed across the primary entry tear, successfully excluding false lumen perfusion. Distal malperfusion was corrected, and false lumen thrombosis within the chest occurred in **79% of patients**. Early mortality was **16%**, which largely reflected very late referral. Favorable clinical results persisted out to a mean follow-up of 13 months.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## TECHNICAL DEVELOPMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

Homemade stent-graft devices persisted for another 10 years, as commercial development was slow to evolve.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Device Specifications</summary><div class="details-content">

Homemade devices were made in 10, 15, and 20 cm lengths, with diameters from 23 to 36 mm. **Two-centimeter proximal and distal landing zones** were recommended, and stent grafts were oversized to approximately **10 to 15%**. These grafts were then loaded into 28-French delivery sheaths, which were advanced into the descending thoracic aorta, usually through a femoral artery cutdown.

In addition to small iliofemoral vessels, additional anatomic constraints of these early first-generation stent grafts, which precluded either delivery or secure fixation, included:
- Acute angulation of the distal arch
- Severe sigmoid-like tortuosity through the diaphragmatic crura
- Excessive mural thrombus

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Endoleak Classification</summary><div class="details-content">

The advent of this new stent-graft technology required a new terminology for **endoleaks**, which allowed the aneurysmal sac to remain pressurized.

| Type | Description |
|------|-------------|
| **Type I** | Endoleak at proximal (A) or distal (B) attachment sites—failure to achieve a hemostatic seal at these implant sites |
| **Type II** | Communication between a branch vessel and the excluded aneurysm sac; commonly seen with back-bleeding intercostal vessels |
| **Type III** | Endoleak at graft-to-graft junctions |
| **Type IV** | Increase in aneurysm sac size in the absence of an identifiable endoleak ("endotension") |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Commercial Devices</summary><div class="details-content">

In 2005 and 2006, commercially produced stent grafts became available:
- **Gore Excluder TAG system** (W.L. Gore, Sunnyvale, CA) (Fig. 50-2)
- **Medtronic Talent Graft** (Medtronic, Sunrise, FL)
- **Cook Zenith** (Cook Company, Bloomington, IN)

![](_page_1_Picture_8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 50-2** Second-generation commercially manufactured thoracic aortic stent graft. The thoracic Excluder TAG system by W.L. Gore contains a thin-walled PTFE graft covered by a nitinol exoskeleton.

These second- and third-generation endoprostheses addressed many of the deficiencies of the early homemade devices:
- Delivery systems have become increasingly **smaller in diameter** and **more flexible**
- Exoskeleton provides **high column strength** and **ductility**, and is compression- and kink-resistant
- **Nitinol** is used predominantly, covered by either Dacron or polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) graft material

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## EARLY RESULTS</summary><div class="details-content">

In January 2005, the phase II multicenter trial of the Gore Excluder TAG thoracic endoprosthesis was reported. This multicenter, prospective, nonrandomized trial was conducted at 17 sites and compared the results of stent-graft repair of descending thoracic aortic aneurysms in **140 patients**, with the results of open repair in **94 patients**. Strict inclusion and exclusion criteria were defined in an attempt to ensure comparability of both groups. Follow-up computed tomography (CT) scans were obtained at 1, 6, 12, and 24 months.

![](_page_2_Picture_3.jpeg)

**Outcomes:**

| Outcome | Stent-Graft Group | Open Repair Group |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Operative blood loss | Significantly less | — |
| Renal failure | Significantly less | — |
| Paraplegia | Significantly less | — |
| Mortality | Significantly less | — |
| Stroke rates | Approximately equal | Approximately equal |

- ICU stay, total hospital stay, and time to return to normal activity were approximately **50% shorter** for the stent-graft group
- Aneurysm-related mortality at 2 years: **3% (stent-graft) vs. 10% (open repair)**
- All-cause mortality at 2 years: **similar between groups**

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## THORACIC AORTIC ANEURYSM</summary><div class="details-content">

Approximately **50% of all thoracic aortic aneurysms** are located in the descending aorta; these aneurysms commonly arise at the level of the left subclavian artery and are often atherosclerotic in nature.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Natural History and Rupture Risk</summary><div class="details-content">

The size–rupture correlation has been demonstrated through the natural history of these aneurysms, as reported by Clouse and associates using the Olmstead County database, in which thoracic aortic aneurysms have an overall **5-year rupture risk of 30%**.

**Risk factors for rupture (Mount Sinai group):**
- Increasing age
- Presence of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
- Maximal thoracic and abdominal aneurysm diameter
- Presence of pain

**Rupture/dissection rates (Yale Aortic Diseases Group):**
- Median diameter at rupture/dissection: **7.2 cm**
- Mean rate: **2%/year** for smaller aneurysms
- **3%/year** for aneurysms 5.0–5.9 cm
- **6.9%/year** for aneurysms ≥ 6.0 cm

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Open Repair Outcomes</summary><div class="details-content">

Open surgical graft replacement has been the traditional mainstay of treatment for these patients:
- Mortality rates: **5–10%** from experienced centers
- Paraplegia rates: **3–8%** (increasing risk associated with increased extent of resection, emergency operation, and renal dysfunction)
- Protective factors: distal circulatory support and cerebral spinal fluid drainage

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Endovascular Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

In an ever-increasingly elderly population with multiple comorbidities, endovascular repair has become increasingly attractive, especially for patients with favorable anatomy.

**Technological advances:**
- Smaller and more flexible delivery systems assure access in most patients
- Coverage of the left subclavian artery to create a satisfactory proximal landing zone is frequently employed with few complications
- Preoperative left subclavian-to-carotid transposition is reserved for:
  - Extensive thoracic coverage
  - Diminutive right vertebral artery
  - Impaired perfusion of both left subclavian and hypogastric arteries
  - Patients with previously repaired abdominal aortic aneurysms

**Spinal cord protection:**
- Patients considered at high risk for paraplegia should receive a spinal drain preoperatively
- Hypotension must be assiduously avoided

**Access:** Increasingly, percutaneous access is sufficient, even in obese patients.

**Ruptured aneurysms:** Endograft technology can be very efficient for the management of ruptured thoracic aortic aneurysms.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## THORACIC AORTIC DISSECTION</summary><div class="details-content">

Perhaps the greatest impact of this endovascular technology has been on the treatment of thoracic aortic dissections.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Complicated Acute Type B Dissection</summary><div class="details-content">

**Definition:** Acute type B dissections with:
- Rupture or impending rupture
- Rapid expansion
- Malperfusion syndromes

Previously, open repair was associated with high mortalities. Endovascular repair, because of its ability to cover the primary entry tear, thus redirecting flow into the true lumen and eliminating "dynamic malperfusion," also allows treatment of "static malperfusion" during the same interventional setting.

Uncovered stents placed in branch vessel orifices occluded by an intimal flap are remarkably effective for these malperfused organ systems.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Uncomplicated Acute Type B Dissection (Prophylactic Approach)</summary><div class="details-content">

There is increasing enthusiasm for **prophylactic placement of stent grafts** to allow aortic remodeling during the acute and subacute phases. Conventional medical therapy successes may still fail in the late follow-up due to aneurysmal expansion of the false lumen. Early stent-graft intervention, if achieved with low morbidity and mortality, could allow positive aortic remodeling in the stented aorta, avoiding these very problematic late aneurysmal complications.

Given the likely development of disease-specific stents—ideally, a covered segment in the proximal portions and uncovered stents for the more distal aorta—prophylactic stent grafting in the subacute stage could allow positive aortic remodeling for the entire thoracic aorta (Fig. 50-4). Genetic risk factors which place patients at increased risk for late aneurysmal changes could be identified, allowing targeting of those patients who would most benefit from such a prophylactic intervention.

(B) Angiogram of the thoracic aorta demonstrating a type B dissection involving the descending thoracic aorta. (C) CT scan of the abdomen, and (D) angiogram of the descending thoracic aorta after stent-graft implantation into the true lumen in the proximal descending thoracic aorta.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Chronic Aortic Dissection</summary><div class="details-content">

Chronic aortic dissections are more problematic. Frequently, these patients are much older, with very large aneurysmal components, and multiple communications between true and false lumens. Open surgical repair has been associated with significant morbidity and mortality.

**Challenges:**
- Dissection frequently extends into the abdomen
- Multiple fenestrations between true and false lumens make exclusion of false lumen filling difficult
- Complete exclusion of a patent false lumen distally can be very problematic, even with snorkel and chimney grafts and occluder devices

Recent publication by Hughes and the Duke aortic group has documented good results with low morbidity by stent grafting from the left subclavian artery to the celiac axis. Roselli and the Cleveland Clinic group recently reported surgical and endovascular experience in this very challenging group of patients, highlighting the competing risks of death and intervention.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## PENETRATING ATHEROSCLEROTIC ULCERS AND INTRAMURAL HEMATOMAS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Pathophysiology and Relationship</summary><div class="details-content">

Intramural hematoma (IMH) of the aorta is attracting growing interest as a variant of aortic dissection. The exact pathophysiology is not well understood. Although by definition, pure IMH likely occurs from hemorrhage into the media from the vasa vasorum, many maintain that an intimal disruption is present in all cases.

Certainly, in the absence of any intimal disruption, there would be no indication for stent-graft repair. IMH, however, is often associated with or even precipitated by **penetrating atherosclerotic ulcers (PAUs)** of the descending thoracic aorta. Therefore, covering the PAU with a stent graft may limit the progression of the IMH and allow healing to occur.

Unfortunately, even with successful stent-graft implantation using both first- and second-generation grafts, retrograde aortic dissection, new ulcer formation, and endoleaks have been noted in a significant percentage of patients, emphasizing the diffuse and severe nature of this disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Outcomes of Stent-Graft Repair</summary><div class="details-content">

The Stanford group has reported their mid-term results treating PAU of the descending thoracic aorta, with an average of **51 months of follow-up** (Fig. 50-5). Using both first- and second-generation commercial devices, **26 patients** were treated, **14 of whom were deemed nonoperative candidates**.

![](_page_4_Picture_7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 50-5** Three-dimensional CT scan of a giant penetrating ulcer involving the descending thoracic aorta that is perfectly suited to treatment with a thoracic stent graft.

**Results:**
- Primary success rate: **92%**
- Actuarial survival: **85%, 76%, and 70%** at 1, 3, and 5 years, respectively
- Perioperative mortality: **12%**

**Predictors of treatment failure:**
- Increasing aortic diameter
- Female gender

These risk factors reflect the importance of careful patient selection based on anatomical criteria and clinical factors. In addition, **long-term follow-up with serial CT angiography** is necessary to detect late complications.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## THORACIC AORTIC TRAUMA</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic injuries secondary to nonpenetrating trauma are frequently lethal lesions, with **80 to 90% of patients dying in the hour after the accident**. Urgent surgical graft replacement of the aorta has been the conventional treatment, but these patients frequently have other major injuries, including closed head injuries, pulmonary contusions, myocardial contusions, and other solid organ injuries, which may limit options for surgical repair.

Thoracic endografts would appear to be an invaluable addition to our surgical armamentarium. Initially, however, all available stent grafts were too large to be utilized inside the smaller aortas of these younger patients. Fortunately, smaller stent grafts, as well as tapered stent grafts, have become available, greatly facilitating repair of these traumatic injuries.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### SVS Classification of Blunt Aortic Injury</summary><div class="details-content">

| Class | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Class I** | Intimal laceration only, no other radiographic changes |
| **Class II** | Intimal laceration and mild intramural hemorrhage without significant compromise of vessel lumen or expansion of adventitial diameter |
| **Class III** | Intramural hemorrhage sufficient to cause increase in aortic diameter and compression of intimal lumen (classical pseudoaneurysm) |
| **Class IV** | Rupture or impending rupture |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Management by Class</summary><div class="details-content">

| Class | Recommended Management |
|-------|------------------------|
| **Class I and II** | No acute treatment; repeat imaging in 24–48 hours |
| **Class III and IV** | Stent-graft coverage is procedure of choice when anatomically feasible |

**Technical considerations:**
- Limit stent-graft length to that necessary to cover the injured area
- Endografts longer than **15 cm** may be associated with increased risk of paraplegia

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Timing of Intervention</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Delayed repair:** May be elected in a stable patient to allow recovery from other injuries (for Class II injuries noted to progress and for Class III injuries)
- **Early intervention:** Warranted with Class III injury and necessity for permissive hypertension in treatment of closed head injury

![](_page_5_Picture_2.jpeg)

![](_page_5_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 50-6** (A) Thoracic angiogram demonstrating a contained rupture of the descending thoracic aorta in a trauma victim. (B) Thoracic angiogram revealing repair of the aortic rupture with a thoracic stent graft.

In patients for whom permissive hypertension is desirable (i.e., patients with closed head injuries), early stent-graft placement may be indicated. Otherwise, for stable Class III or minimally progressive Class II injuries, delayed stent-graft placement may be most effective, allowing interval recovery from polytrauma.

There is little data demonstrating the durability of these stent grafts past 5 years, but it is expected that interval surveillance will allow safe and timely reintervention if necessary.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## FUTURE DIRECTIONS</summary><div class="details-content">

Despite the many advances in the field of endovascular surgery, stent grafting of the thoracic aorta remains in a developmental phase. Newer stent-graft technologies had been slow to become available in the United States, especially compared to China and Japan.

**Emerging technologies:**
- **Unibranch grafts:** May allow more proximal repairs into the aortic arch
- **Multibranch and fenestrated grafts:** May allow complex repair of thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms
- **Disease-specific endografts:** Likely to become available, further enabling endovascular repair

**Surveillance imperative:**
It is important that these patients be followed longitudinally. Early success is no long-term guarantee, as even totally thrombosed aortic aneurysm sacs elongate with late appearance of Type I endoleaks. Serial imaging may allow timely reintervention, but obviously does incur added expense.

Diseases of the thoracic aorta pose a significant challenge to the surgeon because of complexity of the disease and the characteristics of an ever-aging patient population. Current literature supports increasing application of endovascular technology in well-suited patients, but further follow-up and additional trials may be necessary to further refine the exact role of endovascular repair for the future.

</div></details>
