# Percutaneous Catheter-based Mitral Valve Repair

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Introduction and Rationale
*   **Background:** Mitral valve repair (MVR) is the standard of care for severe mitral regurgitation (MR). While transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has advanced rapidly, percutaneous correction of MR has progressed more slowly due to complex valve anatomy, diverse etiologies, and challenges in delivery and anchoring.
*   **Unmet Need:** Percutaneous techniques are being developed primarily for high-risk patients who are poor candidates for conventional open-heart surgery.

### Pathophysiology of MR
*   **Primary MR (Degenerative):** Intrinsic leaflet disease (Carpentier Type II), ranging from fibroelastic deficiency to Barlow's disease. Annular dilatation is often a secondary phenomenon.
*   **Secondary MR (Functional):** Anatomically normal leaflets with regurgitation due to ventricular dilation (Carpentier Type IIIB). Ventricular dilation leads to papillary muscle tethering, preventing leaflet coaptation. Surgical repair focuses on undersized annuloplasty to correct the septal-lateral annular diameter.

### Current Transcatheter Approaches
*   **Approved Devices:** Only three devices have CE Mark approval: MitraClip (edge-to-edge), NeoChord DS1000 (artificial chordae), and Carillon Mitral Contour System (coronary sinus annuloplasty). Only MitraClip has FDA approval, for primary MR in high-risk patients.
*   **MitraClip (Edge-to-Edge):** Mimics the Alfieri stitch, creating a double-orifice valve. Performed via transfemoral venous access and transseptal puncture. The EVEREST II trial showed it is safer (fewer transfusions) but less effective at reducing MR than surgery. Large European registries show high procedural success (~95%) in high-risk patients with both primary and secondary MR.
*   **NeoChord DS1000 (Artificial Chords):** Implants PTFE neochordae via a left minithoracotomy and transapical access. Early results show a steep learning curve, with improved outcomes after initial experience.
*   **Coronary Sinus Annuloplasty (Carillon):** A device placed in the coronary sinus to plicate the posterior annulus. Efficacy is limited by anatomical variability and proximity of the circumflex artery.

### Emerging Technologies and Future Directions
*   **Direct Annular Remodeling:** Devices like Mitralign (transventricular suture plication) and Valtech Cardioband (transseptal adjustable band) aim to directly reduce the septal-lateral annular diameter.
*   **Transcatheter Mitral Valve Replacement (TMVR):** An intriguing concept to eliminate MR recurrence, especially in secondary MR. Challenges include anchoring in a non-calcified annulus and avoiding LV outflow tract obstruction. Valve-in-valve (VinV) and valve-in-ring (VinR) procedures are established, and dedicated TMVR devices (CardiAQ, Tiara, Tendyne, Fortis) are in early feasibility trials.
*   **Guidelines:** 2012 ESC/EACTS guidelines give a Class IIb recommendation for MitraClip in symptomatic patients with severe primary or secondary MR who are inoperable or at high risk. 2014 ACC/AHA guidelines restrict it to prohibitive-risk primary MR.

## PATHOPHYSIOLOGY OF MITRAL REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Mitral valve repair (MVR) remains the standard of care for patients with severe valve incompetence. MVR is usually accomplished by either an open surgical (sternotomy) approach or by minimally invasive (right lateral minithoracotomy) access techniques. Recently there has been an intense interest in developing percutaneous catheter-based techniques, especially for high-risk patients.

Although the success of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has been rapid, progress toward percutaneous correction of mitral regurgitation (MR) has been much more modest. The success of catheter-based treatment of aortic stenosis can be attributed to a number of factors: the singular pathophysiology of the diseased valve, the aortic valve's anatomical location that allows for precise valve stent implantations at the annular level, and the successful development of delivery systems using conventional imaging techniques. The field of percutaneous MVR, however, has not progressed so rapidly for a host of reasons including the complex pathophysiology of MR with a diversity of etiologies, as well as challenges in imaging and delivery and secure anchoring of the valve without impingement on surrounding cardiac structures. These obstacles have led to slower than anticipated clinical adoption of catheter-based approaches for the treatment of MR. To understand the potential for successful therapy, it is first instructive to examine the pathophysiology of the various mechanisms of the disease.

The mitral valve (MV) is a complex structure composed of two leaflets, a fibrous annulus with varying degrees of continuity and integrity, and a subvalvular apparatus consisting of chordae tendineae and papillary muscles attached to the wall of the left ventricle (LV). The etiology of MR can be categorized into two ways: primary also called degenerative or organic, and secondary or functional MR.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Primary Mitral Regurgitation (Degenerative)</summary><div class="details-content">

In primary MR (Carpentier Type II), the intrinsic disease is a result of leaflet degeneration, ranging from fibroelastic deficiency to an excess of connective tissue called Barlow's disease, in patients with MV prolapse. Although MR of intrinsic disease occurs initially as isolated leaflet disease, secondary annular dilatation occurs in the majority of patients by the time that they present for treatment.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Secondary Mitral Regurgitation (Functional)</summary><div class="details-content">

The largest proportion of patients who present with MR, however, are those with secondary MR (Carpentier Type IIIB), in which the valve is anatomically normal but has been stretched by tethering and ventricular dilatation. Secondary MR is not a primary valvular pathology but is a result of a cascade of events initiated by this ventricular dilation. Ventricular dilation leads to apical and lateral distraction of the papillary muscles, which results in tethering of the mitral leaflets. This tethering causes central regurgitation secondary to failure of coaptation of the anatomically normal leaflets during systole. The causes and prognosis of secondary MR are inherently different from intrinsic disease.

Although annular dilatation also occurs in this disease, it is a secondary phenomenon. The principle utilized during surgical repair of secondary MR is based upon overcorrection of the annular dilatation component by performing an undersized annuloplasty to restore leaflet coaptation. To date, it is still unknown whether correcting secondary MR has any impact on the underlying pathology or affects longterm survival.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## CURRENT TRANSCATHETER APPROACHES TO THE TREATMENT OF MITRAL REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

There is a wide spectrum of devices and approaches to manage MR from a percutaneous or transcatheter approach (Table 41-1). Most catheter-based repair techniques are based on techniques that have been proven to be effective in open surgical MVR. Examples include the edge-to-edge technique, placement of artificial chords, and annular remodeling. However, the challenges in adapting these techniques to catheter-based treatment are significant and center mainly on device delivery and imaging. In contrast to open surgical therapy which employs a combination of techniques, transcatheter approaches have not yet successfully combined these different techniques, and, thus, complex pathologies are hard to correct.

Although many different strategies and devices have been developed, only three have received European (CE Mark) approval to date. These are the MitraClip (Abbott Vascular, Irvine, CA), NeoChord DS 1000 (NeoChord, Inc., Louis Park, MN), and Carillon Mitral Contour System (Cardiac Dimensions, Inc., Kirkland, WA). MitraClip has also received US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for commercial sale in the United States, but only for patients with primary MR who are at high risk for surgical repair. Other devices are in various stages of development.

Some devices such as the MitraClip have been employed to treat both primary and secondary MR. Most, however, have been designed to treat only one etiology or the other, with the majority devoted to secondary MR because of the larger clinical need and the existence of excellent surgical repair techniques for primary disease. We will first review the procedures and devices for the management of primary mitral disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## PRIMARY MITRAL REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Percutaneous Edge-to-Edge Repair (MitraClip)</summary><div class="details-content">

The MitraClip system is the only device with extensive clinical application, now with over 19,000 implantations worldwide. The MitraClip mimics the surgical edge-to-edge repair technique of Alfieri (Fig. 41-1). The device received CE approval in 2008, and FDA approval in 2013. However, the FDA approved the MitraClip only for primary MR in high surgical risk patients and not for patients with secondary MR.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Device and Procedure</summary><div class="details-content">

The MitraClip system is designed to allow off-pump endovascular reconstruction of the regurgitant MV. It includes a clip device (MitraClip) and a steerable guiding catheter that enables placement of the clip on the free edges of the MV leaflets. The clip ensures permanent leaflet approximation and creates a double orifice MV (Fig. 41-2). The procedure is performed percutaneously via the femoral vein with transseptal puncture in a cardiac catheterization laboratory or in a hybrid suite with echocardiographic guidance under general anesthesia. The addition of three-dimensional and X-plane echocardiography has significantly facilitated the procedure, as the improved visualization allows for more accurate MitraClip positioning (Fig. 41-3).

Without any strict recommendations from the manufacturer on type or duration of postoperative antiplatelet therapy, several anticoagulation regimens have been proposed. In the Everest I and II trials, aspirin 325 mg daily for 6 months to 1 year was implemented with 75 mg of clopidogrel daily for 1 month. In Europe 100 mg of aspirin daily for 3 months and 75 mg of clopidogrel daily without a loading dose for 4 weeks are more commonly used.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Clinical Outcomes and Limitations</summary><div class="details-content">

The primary benefit associated with the use of the MitraClip is reduction of MR with elimination of the needs for open chest surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass, and cardiac arrest. The potential risks include those associated with cardiac catheterization and transseptal puncture. The major concern regarding this technique is uncertainty regarding the efficacy of a procedure that results in a less complete correction of MR than is usually accomplished surgically. This concern is based on the knowledge that surgical edge-to-edge repair without annuloplasty is associated with a higher rate of recurrent severe regurgitation if the residual MR is greater than 1+ postoperatively. Whether partial reduction of MR is sufficient to translate into ventricular reverse remodeling and, more importantly, any clinical benefit remains to be determined. Additional concerns have been raised regarding the potential impairment of the ability to perform a subsequent surgical valve repair should this become necessary.

There is a recent trend to implant multiple clips during a single procedure to achieve better coaptation and, thus, improved outcomes. Of note, there have been very few reports of mitral stenosis necessitating intervention following MitraClip repair. Even after multiple clip implantations in the same patient, mitral stenosis has rarely occurred.

**Clinical Trials:**
- **Everest I:** Initial feasibility trial (n=55) demonstrated safety and efficacy.
- **Everest II:** Pivotal randomized controlled trial (n=279) comparing MitraClip to open surgery in low-risk patients. The device met safety noninferiority criteria (mainly driven by reduced transfusions) and clinical success noninferiority at 12 months (72.4% vs. 87.8%). Acute procedural success (MR ≤2+) was 77%, and 21% required subsequent surgery. Conclusion: MitraClip is safer but less effective than surgery.
- **High-Risk Registries (EVEREST II High-Risk, REALISM):** In 351 high-risk patients (mean STS mortality 11.3%), MR was reduced to ≤2+ in 86% at discharge. Freedom from death was 77.2% at 12 months, and freedom from MV surgery was 97.8%.

**European Registries:**
- **TRAMI Registry (n=1064):** 95% device success, no procedural deaths in a high-risk cohort (median STS 10).
- **ACCESS-EU (n=567):** 91% MR reduction to ≤2+, no procedural deaths, improvement in NYHA class and 6-minute walk at 1 year.
- **European Sentinel Pilot Registry (n=628):** High procedural success (95.4%) and low in-hospital mortality (2.9%).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Guidelines and Future Directions</summary><div class="details-content">

The current guidelines are largely influenced by the encouraging results of these clinical trials.
- **2012 ESC/EACTS:** Class IIb (LOE C) recommendation to consider MitraClip in symptomatic patients with severe primary or secondary MR who are judged inoperable or at high surgical risk by a "heart team."
- **2014 ACCF/AHA:** Class IIb (LOE b) recommendation only for severe primary MR in symptomatic patients at prohibitive risk for MV surgery.

The future role of MitraClip remains unclear. Three large ongoing randomized trials (COAPT, RESHAPE-HF, and Mitra-Fr) comparing MitraClip plus guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) versus GDMT alone in secondary MR are expected to report results by 2017. Even in optimal primary MR patients, freedom from recurrent MR is only 60% after 9 years without annuloplasty, which may be acceptable for high-risk patients but not for those suitable for surgery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Leaflet Repair Using Artificial Chords (NeoChord DS1000)</summary><div class="details-content">

The NeoChord DS1000 device allows off-pump implantation of artificial neochordae to repair prolapsing or flail mitral leaflets. The access for this procedure is a left minithoracotomy through which a purse-string is placed at the apex of the beating heart. A delivery device that contains an infrared sensor to detect tissue supports optimal grasping of the free edge of the prolapsing leaflet. Via indirect visualization with 3D echo, a suture is passed through the grasped free edge of the leaflet. The two ends of this suture are then brought through the apex of the heart and tied on the epicardial surface (Fig. 41-4). Proper chordal length is determined by adjustment under direct transesophageal echocardiographic guidance.

Current experience with the NeoChord DS1000 is limited to slightly more than 100 patients. The Transapical Artificial Chordae Tendinae feasibility trial showed acute procedural success in 26 out of 30 patients (86.7%), deemed as overall reduction of MR from ≥3+ to ≤2+. Four patients (13.3%) had to be converted to conventional MV repair. At 30-day follow-up, only 17 patients maintained MR grade ≤2+, and four patients with recurrent MR had surgical MV repair. Results improved with experience: durable reduction in MR to ≤2+ at 30 days was achieved in 5 (33.3%) of the first 15 patients and 12 (85.7%) of the last 14 patients. Larger studies are needed to prove efficacy and durability.

## SECONDARY MITRAL REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Mitral Annular Remodeling Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

Although there is dilatation of the entire mitral annulus in patients with secondary MR, the greatest degree of dilation is in the posterior annulus and the greatest increase in dimension is in the septal-lateral (or anterior-posterior) diameter. Therefore, secondary MR is most commonly surgically managed by reduction in the septal lateral diameter of the dilated mitral annulus by an undersized, complete, and rigid annuloplasty ring. A key element of this surgical repair includes anchoring a complete ring to the central fibrous skeleton of the heart at the fibrous trigones. Correction of the septal lateral diameter by as little as 5 to 8 mm has been demonstrated to reconstitute leaflet coaptation and improve MR. Transcatheter approaches to secondary MR are based on this concept of mitral annulus remodeling.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Coronary Sinus Annuloplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

Its proximity to the posterior mitral annulus makes the coronary sinus an attractive access point for placement of devices to remodel the mitral annulus (Fig. 41-5). However, variability in the anatomic relationship of the coronary sinus to the mitral annulus has made it a challenge to obtain consistent decreases in annular dimensions. The distance between the coronary sinus and the mitral annulus is greatest at the posterolateral commissure. Additionally, the left circumflex artery courses between the coronary sinus and the mitral annulus in up to 80% of patients (Fig. 41-6).

The **Carillon Mitral Contour System** is a fixed-length device placed via internal jugular access, with deployable anchors to plicate the coronary sinus. In the AMADEUS and TITAN trials, successful implantation was achieved in only 62-67% of patients, with a notable rate of failure due to interaction with the circumflex artery. While MR reduction and functional class improved in successful implants, the results remain controversial.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Direct Mitral Annular Remodeling</summary><div class="details-content">

Several devices are designed to remodel the annulus by direct plication.

**Mitralign System:** Places suture-based pledgets via a retrograde transventricular approach to plicate the posterior annulus (P1-P2 and P2-P3) (Fig. 41-7). In a 61-patient EU study, the device was implanted in 73.8%, with improvement in MR and symptoms at 30 days. CE mark approval is pending.

**Valtech Cardioband:** Places a tensioning band with anchoring screws into the mitral annulus via a transseptal atrial approach. The band is then adjusted under echo guidance to restore leaflet coaptation. In a multicenter study (n=29), the implant rate was 100%, with 93% acute MR reduction and sustained results (91% MR ≤2+) at 6 months. CE mark approval is pending.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Transcatheter Mitral Valve Replacement (TMVR)</summary><div class="details-content">

Perhaps the most intriguing concept currently for the percutaneous treatment of secondary MR is transcatheter MV replacement (TMVR). One of the disadvantages of surgical annuloplasty for secondary MR is the recurrence of regurgitation in approximately one third of patients by one year, as ongoing ventricular dilation leads to further tethering. MV replacement could theoretically prevent recurrence even with subsequent LV dilation.

**Challenges:** Development has been delayed by complex MV anatomy. Unlike the calcified aortic annulus in AS, the mitral annulus in MR is not calcified, providing no good anchoring zone. However, a surgical mitral prosthesis or complete annuloplasty ring can serve as an ideal anchor for a valve-in-valve (VinV) or valve-in-ring (VinR) procedure.

**Dedicated TMVR Devices (Early Feasibility):**
- **CardiAQ:** Self-expanding, stent-based, bovine pericardial valve. Self-anchors by grabbing native leaflets. Initial transfemoral/transseptal implant in 2012.
- **Tiara:** Self-expanding, nitinol-based, bovine pericardial valve. D-shaped to prevent LV outflow obstruction. Implanted transapically.
- **Tendyne:** Self-expanding, nitinol-based, porcine pericardial valve with a ventricular tethering system anchored to the LV apex. Fully retrievable and repositionable.
- **Fortis:** Cloth-covered nitinol stent with bovine pericardial leaflets. Implanted transapically. Fixation with paddles capturing native leaflets.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## CONCLUSION</summary><div class="details-content">

There are a host of unique and intriguing concepts for transcatheter treatment of MR. Although progress in the transcatheter treatment of aortic stenosis was rapid and promising, progress with MR has been much slower due to the complex MV anatomy, the variable MR pathologies, the necessity for complex delivery systems, the limited early success in clinical trials, and the existence of an effective, established therapy (open surgery). In addition, proper trial design has proven to be challenging, with the demonstration of clinical benefit remaining particularly difficult. However, the clinical unmet need is sizable, and with a host of unique concepts under early investigation, significant progress can be expected in the near future. The MV will be one of the most interesting targets for therapeutic cardiac device innovations in the coming years.

</div></details>
