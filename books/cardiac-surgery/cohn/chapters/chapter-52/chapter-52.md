# Pulmonary Embolism and Pulmonary Thromboendarterectomy

##### Pulmonary embolism (PE) results in at least 630,000 symptomatic episodes in the United States yearly, making it about half as common as acute myocardial infarction, and three times as common as cerebrovascular accidents. Acute PE is the third most common cause of death (after heart disease and cancer). Estimates of PE are probably low because approximately 75% of autopsy-proved PE are not detected clinically and in 70 to 80% of the patients in whom the primary cause of death was PE, premortem diagnosis was completely unsuspected. Of all hospitalized patients who develop PE, 12 to 21% die in the hospital, and another 24 to 39% die within 12 months. Thus, approximately 36 to 60% of patients who survive the initial episode live beyond 12 months, and may present later in life with a wide variety of symptoms.

##### In addition, approximately 2.5 million Americans develop deep vein thrombosis (DVT) each year, and more than 90% of clinically detected pulmonary emboli are associated with lower extremity DVT. However, in two-thirds of patients with DVT and PE, the DVT is asymptomatic.

##### For the most part, DVT and acute PE are managed medically. Cardiac surgeons rarely become involved in management of acute PE, unless it is in a hospitalized patient who survives a massive embolus that causes life-threatening acute right heart failure with low cardiac output, with a large clot burden. On the other hand, the mainstay of treatment for patients with chronic pulmonary thromboembolic disease is the surgical removal of the disease by means of pulmonary thromboendarterectomy. Medical management for this condition is only palliative, and surgery by means of transplantation is an inappropriate use of resources with outcomes inferior to thromboendarterectomy.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Epidemiology
*   **Incidence:** PE occurs in >630,000 symptomatic cases/year in the US (third leading cause of death). DVT affects ~2.5 million Americans annually.
*   **Mortality:** 12–21% of hospitalized PE patients die in hospital; 24–39% die within 12 months. 75% of autopsy-proved PE undiagnosed antemortem.

### Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)
*   **Virchow's Triad:** Venous stasis, vein wall injury, hypercoagulopathy.
*   **Risk Factors:** Previous thromboembolism, age >45, immobilization >7 days, major surgery, cancer, multiple trauma.
*   **Diagnosis:** Duplex scanning (sensitivity 89–100%), venography (gold standard), MRI.
*   **Prophylaxis:** Compression stockings, intermittent pneumatic compression, low-dose heparin, low-molecular-weight heparin.

### Acute Pulmonary Embolism
*   **Classification:** Minor (<30% occlusion, stable), major (30–50% occlusion, mild hypotension), massive (>50% occlusion, hemodynamic instability).
*   **Diagnosis:** V/Q scan (high sensitivity), CT angiography, TTE/TEE (right ventricular dysfunction).
*   **Management:** Heparin, thrombolytics (higher bleeding risk), catheter embolectomy, emergency surgical embolectomy (40–92% mortality), ECLS for unstable patients.

### Chronic Thromboembolic Pulmonary Hypertension (CTEPH)
*   **Incidence:** Estimated 2500–25,000 new cases/year in US; often no history of acute PE.
*   **Pathology:** Organized thrombus with bands, webs, or complete occlusion; classification Type I (central clot) to Type IV (inoperable small-vessel disease).
*   **Diagnosis:** V/Q scan (essential), pulmonary angiography (gold standard). PVR often >800 dynes/sec/cm<sup>-5</sup>.

### Pulmonary Thromboendarterectomy (PTE)
*   **Indications:** NYHA class III–IV, PVR >300 dynes/sec/cm<sup>-5</sup>, surgically accessible thrombus.
*   **Technique:** Median sternotomy, cardiopulmonary bypass, deep hypothermia (20°C), circulatory arrest (≤20 min per side). Bilateral endarterectomy with dissection to subsegmental "tails."
*   **Outcomes:** Mortality <5% in recent series (>3000 patients since 1990), <2% in last 5 years. 95% of patients in NYHA class I–II at 1 year. Survival 75% at ≥6 years.
*   **Complications:** Reperfusion injury (10%), persistent pulmonary hypertension (Type IV disease), delirium (if circulatory arrest >55 min).

## DEEP VEIN THROMBOSIS</summary><div class="details-content">

Deep vein thrombosis primarily affects the veins of the lower extremity or pelvis. It is most common in hospitalized patients but may occur in ambulatory patients outside the hospital. The process may involve superficial as well as deep veins, but superficial venous thrombosis does not generally propagate beyond the saphenofemoral junction and therefore very rarely causes PE. Venous thrombosis of the upper extremity is almost always associated with trauma, indwelling catheters, or other pathologic states and is an uncommon cause of PE, but can be fatal. Pulmonary emboli that do not originate from the deep venous system of the legs, pelvis, or arms are thought to come from a diseased right atrium or ventricle or retroperitoneal and hepatic systems.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Pathogenesis</summary><div class="details-content">

In 1856, Rudolf Virchow made the association between DVT and PE and suggested that the causes of DVT were related to **venous stasis, vein wall injury, and hypercoagulopathy**. This triad of etiologic factors remains relevant today and is supported by an ever-growing body of evidence.

**Venous Stasis**
Immobilization is by far the most important cause of venous stasis in hospitalized patients. Injections of contrast material in foot veins require up to 1 hour to clear from the venous valves in the soleus muscle of immobilized patients. Venous stasis may also be produced by mechanical obstruction of proximal veins, by low cardiac output, by venous dilatation, and by increased blood viscosity. Some pelvic tumors, bulky inguinal adenopathy, the gravid uterus, previous caval or iliac venous disease, and elevated central venous pressures from cardiac causes also enhance venous stasis.

**Vein Wall Injury**
The role of vein wall injury is less clear because DVT often begins in the absence of mechanical trauma. Recent work shows that subtle vein wall injuries may occur during operation in veins remote from the operative field. In animals, endothelial cell tears have been found at junctions of small veins with larger veins at remote sites during hip replacement (Fig. 52-1).

![](_page_1_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 52-1** Scanning electron photomicrograph of a canine jugular vein after total hip replacement with significant operative venous dilatation. An endothelial cell tear (t) is visible near a valve cusp (V). *(Reproduced with permission from Cometra AJ, Stewart GJ, White JV: Combined dihydroergotamine and heparin prophylaxis of postoperative deep vein thrombosis: proposed mechanism of action, Am J Surg 1985 Oct 8;150(4A):39-44.)*

**Hypercoagulopathy**

Three uncommon familial deficiencies associated with venous thrombosis are seen in antithrombin, protein C, and protein S:
- **Antithrombin:** A natural plasma protease that inhibits thrombin after it is formed, and to a lesser extent before it is formed. Antithrombin is also the cofactor that is accelerated 1000-fold by heparin.
- **Protein C:** A potent inhibitor of factor V and platelet-bound factor VII; requires protein S as a cofactor for anticoagulant activity.
- **Protein S:** A vitamin-K-dependent zymogen activated by thrombin and accelerated by thrombomodulin produced by endothelial cells.

A much more common coagulation deficiency, resulting from a mutation of factor V (factor V Leiden) that prevents its degradation by protein C, has been described and is present in approximately **6 to 7%** of study populations of Swedes and North American males. Both the homozygous and heterozygous mutants are strongly associated with venous thrombosis and PE but are not associated with manifestations of arterial thrombosis.

The presence of the **lupus anticoagulant**, which is an acquired IgG or IgM antibody against prothrombinase, increases the likelihood of venous thrombosis by poorly understood mechanisms. The disease may be associated with lupus-like syndromes, immunosuppression, or intake of specific drugs, such as procainamide.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Risk Factors for Deep Vein Thrombosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Table 52-1 presents a list of major risk factors for the development of DVT or PE. Previous thromboembolism, older age, immobilization for more than 1 week, orthopedic surgery of the hip or knee, recent surgery, multiple trauma, and cancer are strong risk factors. In patients with a history of venous thromboembolism the risk of developing a new episode during hospitalization is nearly **eight times** that of someone without a history. Up to 10% of patients with a first episode of DVT or PE and up to 20% of those with a recurrent event develop a new episode of venous thromboembolism within 6 months.

The incidence of DVT and PE increases exponentially with age (Fig. 52-2). Males are at greater risk than females. Prolonged bed rest or immobility from any cause are major risk factors. Although usually other risk factors are present, the incidence of autopsy-proved venous thrombosis rises from 15 to 80% in patients at bed rest for more than 1 week.

The incidence of venous thromboembolism increases threefold in patients who have operations for cancer. Of particular interest to cardiac surgeons and cardiologists is the observation that clinically silent DVT develops during hospitalization in nearly **50% of patients after myocardial revascularization**.

**FIGURE 52-2** Annual incidence of venous thromboembolism in the United States stratified for age. Males have a significantly higher incidence rate of venous thromboembolism than females. Both curves fit an exponential function.

A follow-up study found that the incidence of PE in hospital after coronary arterial bypass operations was **3.2%** and hospital mortality in patients with PE was **18.7%**. Interestingly, valvular surgery was not associated with the development of PE. In a retrospective study of 5694 patients who had open heart surgery, Gillinov and colleagues found the risk of PE proved by V/Q scan (20 patients), angiography (four patients), or autopsy (eight patients) was **0.56%** within 60 days. The mortality was **34%** in patients with PE.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Approximately **two-thirds of patients with DVT do not have clinical symptoms**; therefore, the diagnosis depends on a high degree of clinical suspicion and a variety of objective diagnostic tests.

| Modality | Description | Sensitivity/Specificity |
|----------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Venography** | Most reliable test for detecting thrombus in calf veins | Gold standard |
| **Duplex scanning** | Ultrasound + color flow Doppler; infers clot by flow patterns and inability to compress vessel | 89–100% sensitivity (symptomatic patients) |
| **Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)** | Noninvasive; images entire venous system including upper extremity and mediastinum | 70% sensitivity for pelvic veins, nearly 100% specificity |
| **Impedance plethysmography** | Assesses volume changes in leg after vein occlusion | Useful in symptomatic patients; lower sensitivity in asymptomatic patients |
| **Iodine 125-labeled fibrinogen** | Sensitive for calf vein thrombus | Does not detect iliofemoral vein thrombosis |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Prophylaxis</summary><div class="details-content">

The prevalence of DVT, its strong association with PE, and the identification of risk factors in the pathogenesis of the disease provide the basis and rationale for prophylactic measures that are recommended in patients with two or more major risk factors, such as age over 40 years and major surgery.

| Prophylactic Measure | Effectiveness |
|---------------------|---------------|
| Compression stockings | Reduce DVT incidence to ~40% of controls |
| Intermittent pneumatic compression | Effective; more expensive and cumbersome |
| Low-dose subcutaneous heparin | Reduces DVT incidence to ~35% of controls |
| Low-molecular-weight heparin (once daily) | Reduces DVT incidence to ~18% of controls |

The reduction in PE with subcutaneous standard heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin is similar.

**Calf vein DVT** that does not propagate has a low risk of PE, and controversy exists as to whether or not these patients should be anticoagulated. Of patients who have DVT diagnosed in hospital without PE, the probability of clinically diagnosed PE within the next 12 months is **1.7%**. If PE occurs, the probability of recurrent PE is **8.0%**. **Six months of warfarin anticoagulation** is recommended for patients who have DVT with or without PE, as prophylaxis against recurrent disease.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## PULMONARY EMBOLISM</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Pathology and Pathogenesis</summary><div class="details-content">

The only firm attachment of leg thrombus is at the site of origin, usually a venous saccule or venous valve pocket. The degree of organization within the thrombus varies, but recent clots are more likely to migrate than older thrombi that are more firmly attached to the vessel wall.

Detached venous thrombi are carried in the bloodstream through the right heart into the pulmonary circulation. In autopsy series the percentage of emboli that obstruct two or more lobar arteries (major) ranges between 25 and 67% of all emboli; in clinical trials based on angiographic data the percentage of major emboli ranges from 30 to 64%. The majority of pulmonary emboli lodge in the lower lobes, and are slightly more common in the right lung than the left. Soon after reaching the lungs emboli become coated with a layer of platelets and fibrin.

**Humoral mediators** contribute to hemodynamic consequences:
- Serotonin, adenosine diphosphate (ADP), platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF)
- Thromboxane from platelets coating the thrombus
- Platelet-activating factor (PAF) and leukotrienes from neutrophils
- Inhibition of endothelium-derived relaxing factor (EDRF) with enhanced superoxide anion release

The combination of these effects contributes to increased pulmonary vasoconstriction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

The mortality of a large, untreated PE is **18 to 33%**, but can be reduced to about **8%** if diagnosed and treated. **75 to 90%** of patients who die of pulmonary emboli do so within the first few hours of the primary event.

In patients who have sufficient cardiopulmonary reserve and right ventricular strength to survive the initial few hours, autolysis of emboli occurs over the next few days and weeks. On average, approximately **20%** of the clot disappears by 7 days, and complete resolution may occur by 14 days. For many patients, up to 30 days are needed to dissolve small emboli and up to 60 days for massive clots.

As the natural fibrinolytic system dissolves the embolic mass, the available cross-sectional area of the pulmonary arterial tree progressively increases, and pulmonary vascular resistance and right ventricular afterload decrease. In the vast majority of patients, pulmonary emboli continue to resolve and thus an immediate interventional therapy, particularly surgical embolectomy, is not necessary for survival except in a minority of patients.

In an unknown but small percentage of patients with acute PE the clot will not lyse, and chronic thromboembolic obstruction of the pulmonary vasculature develops. Symptomatic patients usually have more than **40%** of their pulmonary vasculature obstructed by organized and fresh thrombi.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute PE usually presents suddenly. Symptoms and signs vary with the extent of blockage, the magnitude of the humoral response, and the pre-embolus reserve of the cardiac and pulmonary systems. In autopsy series of proven emboli only **16 to 38%** of patients were diagnosed during life.

The acute disease is stratified into minor, major (submassive), or massive embolism:

| Classification | Hemodynamics | Occlusion | Key Features |
|----------------|--------------|-----------|--------------|
| **Minor** | Stable | <30% | Sudden anxiety, tachypnea, pleuritic chest pain, PaO₂ 65–80 torr |
| **Major (submassive)** | Mild hypotension, stable cardiac output | 30–50% | Dyspnea, tachycardia, mild hypotension, PaO₂ 50–65 torr, RV dilatation on echo |
| **Massive** | Hemodynamic instability, low cardiac output | >50% | Acute dyspnea, hypotension, cardiac arrest possible, PaO₂ <50 torr, elevated CVP |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinical diagnosis of acute major or massive PE is wrong in **70 to 80%** of patients who subsequently have angiography.

| Modality | Findings |
|----------|----------|
| **Chest x-ray** | Normal or parenchymal infiltrate, atelectasis, pleural effusion; zone of hypovascularity or wedge-shaped density |
| **ECG** | Nonspecific T-wave or ST changes; 26% of massive PE show cor pulmonale, right axis deviation, or RBBB |
| **Echocardiography** | Right ventricular dilatation, poor RV contractility, acute tricuspid regurgitation (Fig. 52-4) |
| **Ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) scan** | High sensitivity; negative scan excludes clinically significant PE (Fig. 52-3) |
| **CT angiography** | Noninvasive; provides specific information regarding flow |
| **Pulmonary angiography** | Gold standard; but not if circulation cannot be stabilized |

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Management of Acute Major Pulmonary Embolism</summary><div class="details-content">

For the purposes of this chapter, major or submassive PE is defined as an acute episode that causes hypoxia and mild hypotension (systolic arterial pressure >90 mm Hg), but does not cause cardiac arrest or sustained low cardiac output and cardiogenic shock.

**Initial management:**
1. Establish adequate ventilation and circulation (intubation if necessary)
2. Pharmacologic agents to stabilize hemodynamics
3. Intravenous heparin: initial bolus 70 U/kg, followed by 18–20 U/kg/h (if no contraindications)

**Thrombolytic therapy** (streptokinase, urokinase, rt-PA):
- Increases rate of lysis of fresh thrombi over heparin alone during treatment
- No statistical difference in mortality or recurrent PE at 5 days
- Trend toward better results with thrombolytics due to more rapid reduction in RV afterload
- Bleeding complications in approximately **20%** of patients
- No data indicate that thrombolysis reduces subsequent development of CTEPH

**Mechanical removal** by catheter device (femoral or jugular vein):
- Successful extraction with meaningful reduction in pulmonary arterial pressure: **61–84%**

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Management of Acute Massive Pulmonary Embolism</summary><div class="details-content">

If the circulation cannot be stabilized at survival levels within several minutes or if cardiac arrest occurs after a massive PE, time becomes of paramount importance:
- **11%** of patients with fatal PE die within the first hour
- **43–80%** within 2 hours
- **85%** within 6 hours

When surgery is not immediately available, in patients who may not be surgical candidates, or in whom an alternate diagnosis seems more likely, **emergency extracorporeal life support (ECLS)** using peripheral cannulation is an attractive alternative. ECLS can be instituted rapidly outside the operating room, compensates for acute cor pulmonale and hypoxia, and sustains the circulation until the clot partially lyses.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Emergency Pulmonary Thromboembolectomy</summary><div class="details-content">

Emergency pulmonary thromboembolectomy is indicated for suitable patients with life-threatening circulatory insufficiency, but should not be done without a definitive diagnosis because a clinical diagnosis of PE is often wrong.

**Technique:**
- Midline sternotomy incision
- Cardiopulmonary bypass initiated
- Main pulmonary artery opened 1–2 cm downstream to the valve, incision extended into proximal left pulmonary artery
- Forceps and suction catheters used to remove clot from left and right pulmonary arteries
- Sterile pediatric bronchoscope can locate and remove thrombi in tertiary and quaternary vessels
- Alternatively, pleural spaces entered and each lung gently compressed to dislodge small clots

**Adjunctive measures:**
- Greenfield recommends placement of inferior vena caval filter before closing chest
- European surgeons generally clip the intrapericardial vena cava (but causes considerable morbidity in >60% of patients)

**Anticoagulation:** 6 months recommended for most patients with PE; IVC filter recommended for contraindications to anticoagulation, recurrent PE, or those requiring pulmonary thromboendarterectomy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Extracorporeal Life Support</summary><div class="details-content">

The wider availability of long-term extracorporeal perfusion (ECLS) using peripheral vessel cannulation to stabilize the circulation offers a compromise position because most massive pulmonary emboli will dissolve in time. ECLS can be implemented outside the operating room within **15 to 30 minutes** by an equipped team of trained personnel.

ECLS should not be needed beyond 1 to 2 days because clot lysis proceeds rapidly. Once pulmonary vascular resistance is adequately reduced, ECLS should be discontinued.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Results</summary><div class="details-content">

Mortality rates for emergency pulmonary thromboembolectomy vary widely between **40 and 92%**.

| Preoperative Status | Mortality |
|---------------------|----------|
| Cardiac arrest requiring ECLS | 45–75% |
| Without cardiac arrest | 8–36% |
| ECLS during cardiac resuscitation | 43–56% |

Recurrent embolism is uncommon, and approximately **80%** of survivors maintain normal pulmonary arterial pressures and exercise tolerance.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## CHRONIC THROMBOEMBOLIC PULMONARY HYPERTENSION</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Incidence</summary><div class="details-content">

The incidence of pulmonary hypertension caused by chronic PE is difficult to determine. Twenty-five years ago, it was estimated that there were more than 500,000 survivors per year of acute symptomatic episodes of acute pulmonary embolization in the United States alone.

One estimate is that chronic thromboembolic disease develops in only **0.5%** of patients with a clinically recognized acute PE. If these figures are correct, approximately **2500 individuals** would progress to chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH) in the United States each year. However, because most patients diagnosed with chronic thromboembolic disease have no antecedent history of acute embolism, the true incidence is probably much higher—on the order of **five to ten times** this number.

Calculations extrapolated from mortality rates and the random incidence of major thrombotic occlusion found at autopsy would support a postulate that more than **100,000 people** in the United States currently have pulmonary hypertension that could be relieved by operation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Pathology and Pathogenesis</summary><div class="details-content">

Although most individuals with chronic pulmonary thromboembolic disease are unaware of a past thromboembolic event and give no history of DVT, the origin of most cases of unresolved pulmonary emboli are from acute embolic episodes. Why some patients have unresolved emboli is not certain.

**Possible factors:**
- Volume of acute embolic material overwhelms lytic mechanisms
- Total occlusion prevents lytic material from reaching embolus
- Repetitive emboli cannot be resolved
- Abnormal lytic mechanisms or hypercoagulable state
- Chronic indwelling central venous catheters and pacemaker leads
- Tumor emboli (stomach, breast, kidney malignancies)
- Right atrial myxoma fragmentation

**After the clot becomes wedged in the pulmonary artery, one of two processes occurs:**
1. **Organization with canalization:** Producing multiple small endothelialized channels separated by fibrous septa (bands and webs)
2. **Complete fibrous organization:** Solid mass of dense fibrous connective tissue totally obstructing the arterial lumen

**Surgical classification of thromboembolic disease:**

| Type | Description | Proportion |
|------|-------------|------------|
| **Type I** | Major vessel clot visible on opening pulmonary arteries | ~15% |
| **Type II** | No major vessel thrombus; thickened intima, webs; plane raised in main/lobar/segmental vessels | ~55% |
| **Type III** | Very distal disease confined to segmental/subsegmental branches; most challenging | ~30% |
| **Type IV** | Intrinsic small-vessel disease (not primary thromboembolic); inoperable | — |

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension is a frequently under-recognized but treatable cause of pulmonary hypertension. There are no signs or symptoms specific for chronic thromboembolism.

**Symptoms:**
- **Exertional dyspnea** (most common; out of proportion to clinical findings)
- Nonspecific chest pains or tightness (~50% of patients)
- Hemoptysis (from abnormally dilated vessels)
- Peripheral edema, early satiety, epigastric fullness (right heart failure)

**Physical signs:**
- Large A-wave in jugular venous pulse (early); V-wave predominant as right heart fails
- Right ventricle palpable near lower left sternal border
- Accentuated P2; narrowly split second heart sound
- Sharp systolic ejection click over pulmonary artery
- Right atrial gallop; tricuspid insufficiency (high-pitched murmur)
- Possible pulmonic regurgitation murmur

**Pulmonary function tests:**
- Minimal changes in lung volume and ventilation
- Diffusing capacity (DLCO) often reduced (may be only abnormality)
- Pulmonary arterial pressures elevated; suprasystemic pressures not uncommon
- Resting cardiac outputs lower than normal
- Most patients hypoxic (PaO₂ 50–83 torr, average 65 torr)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

To ensure accurate diagnosis, a standardized evaluation is recommended for all patients who present with unexplained pulmonary hypertension.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Medical Treatment</summary><div class="details-content">

**Chronic anticoagulation** represents the mainstay of a medical regimen:
- Prevents future embolic episodes
- Limits development of thrombus in low-flow regions

**Inferior vena caval filters** are used routinely to prevent recurrent embolization.

**Right ventricular failure** is treated with diuretics and vasodilators, but the effect is generally transient because the failure is owing to mechanical obstruction and will not resolve until the obstruction is removed. The prognosis is unaffected by medical therapy, which should be regarded as only supportive.

The only other surgical option for these patients is transplantation. However, transplantation is not appropriate for this disease because:
- Mortality and morbidity on waiting list
- Higher risk of the operation
- Survival: ~80% at 1 year for transplantation vs. >95% for pulmonary endarterectomy
- Pulmonary endarterectomy is permanently curative without risk of rejection or immunosuppression

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

The natural history of CTEPH is dismal, and nearly all patients die of progressive right heart failure. Because of the insidious onset, the diagnosis is usually made relatively late in the progression of the disease when dyspnea and/or early symptoms of right heart failure develop and pulmonary hypertension is severe (>40 mm Hg mean).

In Riedel's series of 13 patients:
- Nine died a mean of **28 months** after diagnosis of right heart failure
- Seven of the 13 had recurrent episodes of fresh emboli

The severity of pulmonary hypertension at the time of diagnosis inversely correlates with duration of survival.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## PULMONARY THROMBOENDARTERECTOMY</summary><div class="details-content">

Although there were previous attempts, Allison et al did the first successful pulmonary "thromboendarterectomy" through a sternotomy using surface hypothermia, but only fresh clots were removed. Since then, most of the surgical experience in pulmonary endarterectomy has been reported from the UCSD Medical Center, beginning in 1970, now totaling more than **3500 cases**. The operation described below, using deep hypothermia and circulatory arrest, is the standard procedure.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Indications</summary><div class="details-content">

When the diagnosis of thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension has been firmly established, the decision for operation is based on the severity of symptoms and the general condition of the patient.

**Three major reasons for considering thromboendarterectomy:**
1. **Hemodynamic:** Prevent or ameliorate right ventricular compromise caused by pulmonary hypertension
2. **Alveolo-respiratory:** Improve respiratory function by removal of large ventilated but unperfused dead space
3. **Prophylactic:** Prevent progressive right ventricular dysfunction, retrograde extension of obstruction, and secondary arteriopathic changes in remaining patent vessels

**Typical patient:**
- NYHA class III or IV
- Severely elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) at rest
- No significant comorbid disease unrelated to right heart failure
- Chronic thrombi on angiogram in balance with measured PVR level

Most patients have PVR in the range of **800 dynes/sec/cm<sup>-5</sup>** ; approximately 20% have PVR >1000 dynes/sec/cm<sup>-5</sup> with suprasystemic pulmonary artery pressures. **There is no upper limit** of PVR level, pulmonary artery pressure, or degree of right ventricular dysfunction that excludes patients from operation.

If not previously implanted, an **inferior vena caval filter** is routinely placed several days in advance of the operation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

**Principles**

The endarterectomy must be **bilateral** because this is a bilateral disease in the vast majority of patients. The only reasonable approach is through a **median sternotomy incision**, which:
- Provides bilateral access
- Avoids entry into pleural cavities
- Allows ready institution of cardiopulmonary bypass

**Cardiopulmonary bypass** is essential for cardiovascular stability and to cool the patient to allow circulatory arrest. Periods of circulatory arrest (limited to **20 minutes**, with restoration of flow between each arrest) are necessary to ensure perfect visibility in a bloodless field.

A **true endarterectomy in the plane of the media** must be accomplished. Removal of visible thrombus is largely incidental; in most patients, no free thrombus is present.

**Preparation and Anesthetic Considerations**

- Routine monitoring: ECG, cutaneous oximetry, radial and pulmonary artery pressures
- Femoral artery catheter (in addition to radial line) for accurate measurements during rewarming
- Electroencephalographic recording to ensure absence of cerebral activity before circulatory arrest
- Head enveloped in cooling jacket; cerebral cooling after initiation of bypass
- Temperature measurements: esophagus, tympanic membrane, urinary catheter, rectum, blood
- Up to 500 mL autologous whole blood withdrawn if patient stable

**Surgical Technique**

After median sternotomy, pericardium incised longitudinally. Typically the right heart is enlarged with tense right atrium and variable tricuspid regurgitation.

Anticoagulation: Heparin (400 U/kg IV) to maintain ACT >400 seconds. Full cardiopulmonary bypass with high ascending aortic cannulation and two caval cannulae. Temporary pulmonary artery vent placed in main pulmonary artery 1 cm distal to pulmonary valve.

Surface and blood cooling with 10°C gradient between arterial blood and bladder/rectal temperature. Cooling takes 45–60 minutes. When ventricular fibrillation occurs, left atrial vent placed through right superior pulmonary vein.

**Right-sided dissection (Fig. 52-7):**
- Surgeon stands on patient's left side
- Right pulmonary artery mobilized medially (not laterally) to SVC
- Incision in right pulmonary artery from beneath ascending aorta under SVC into lower lobe branch
- Any loose thrombus removed
- If bronchial circulation not excessive, endarterectomy plane can be found

**FIGURE 52-7** Recommended surgical approach on the right side. This approach, medial to the superior vena cava (SVC), and between the SVC and aorta, provides a direct view into the right pulmonary artery. Note that an approach on the lateral side of the SVC will only provide a restricted view, and should be avoided.

When temperature reaches 20°C, aorta cross-clamped and single dose of cold cardioplegic solution (1 L) administered. Modified cerebellar retractor placed between aorta and SVC. Thiopental (500 mg–1 g) administered until EEG isoelectric. Circulatory arrest initiated, patient exsanguinated.

Microtome knife used to develop endarterectomy plane posteriorly. Ideal layer is pearly white and strips easily. Dissection too deep shows reddish/pinkish adventitia. Endarterectomy performed with eversion technique using specially developed dissection instrument (Jamieson aspirator). Each subsegmental branch followed and freed individually until it ends in a "tail" with no further obstruction.

After right-sided endarterectomy completed, circulation restarted, arteriotomy repaired with continuous 6-0 polypropylene suture.

**Left-sided dissection (Fig. 52-8):**
- Surgeon moves to patient's right side
- Pulmonary vent catheter withdrawn
- Arteriotomy made from pulmonary vent site out laterally beneath pericardial reflection into lower lobe
- Care to avoid entry into left pleural space and injury to phrenic nerve
- Lymphatic vessel at pericardial reflection ("Jamieson's lymphatic") clipped

**FIGURE 52-8** Surgical approach on the left side. The incision in the left pulmonary artery begins in the midpoint of the main pulmonary trunk, at the insertion site of the pulmonary artery vent. This incision provides better visibility than a more distal approach (*dotted line and arrow*). Care must be taken to avoid injury to the phrenic nerve.

Left-sided dissection analogous to right. Circulatory arrest intervals limited to 20 minutes.

After completion of endarterectomy, cardiopulmonary bypass reinstituted, warming commenced. Methylprednisolone (500 mg IV) and mannitol (12.5 g IV) administered. Rewarming takes 90–120 minutes.

Right atrium opened and examined; any intra-atrial communication closed. Tricuspid valve repair not performed unless independent structural damage—right ventricular remodeling restores tricuspid competence within days.

When patient rewarmed, cardiopulmonary bypass discontinued. Dopamine administered at renal doses; other inotropic agents and vasodilators titrated as necessary.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Postoperative Care</summary><div class="details-content">

All patients are mechanically ventilated overnight, with maintained diuresis aiming for preoperative weight within 24 hours.

**Ventilation:** Higher minute ventilation often required for temporary metabolic acidosis. Tidal volumes higher than normal; maximum inspiratory pressure <30 cm H₂O. Extubation on first postoperative day when possible.

**Diuresis:** Patients have considerable positive fluid balance. Early spontaneous aggressive diuresis augmented with diuretics. Hematocrit maintained >30% to increase oxygen-carrying capacity and reduce likelihood of reperfusion phenomenon.

**Arrhythmias:** Atrial arrhythmias occur in approximately 10%—no more common than other nonvalvular heart surgery.

**Transfusion:** Required in minority of patients with careful blood conservation.

**Inferior Vena Caval Filter and Anticoagulation:** Greenfield filter usually inserted preoperatively. Postoperative venous thrombosis prophylaxis with intermittent pneumatic compression; subcutaneous heparin begun evening of surgery. Warfarin started after pacing wires and mediastinal tubes removed; target INR 2.5–3.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

**Persistent Pulmonary Hypertension**

The decrease in PVR usually results in immediate and sustained restoration of pulmonary artery pressures to normal. In a few patients, additional reduction may occur over the next few days. If pulmonary artery pressures do not resolve substantially after complete endarterectomy, this indicates Type IV disease (inoperable small-vessel disease). Attempts at pharmacologic manipulation with nitroprusside, epoprostenol, or inhaled nitric oxide are generally not effective.

**The "Reperfusion Response"**

A specific complication occurring in many patients to some degree is localized pulmonary edema (reperfusion injury). True reperfusion injury adversely impacting clinical course occurs in approximately **10%** of patients.

**Presentation:**
- Occurs within hours of operation
- Profound desaturation
- Edema-like fluid (sometimes bloody) suctioned from endotracheal tube

**Causes:**
- Persistent high pulmonary artery pressures after operation
- Reactive hyperemia after revascularization of segments with long-standing no flow
- High-permeability lung injury in denuded endothelium

**Management:**
- Diuresis, maintenance of hematocrit, early PEEP
- Supportive care; will resolve if hemodynamics and oxygenation maintained
- Hematocrit kept 32–36%
- Ventilatory status may be position sensitive
- FIO₂ kept as low as compatible with O₂ saturation ≥90%
- Inhaled nitric oxide (20–40 ppm) may improve gas exchange
- Extracorporeal support (ECMO, CO₂ removal) occasionally used for 7–10 days
- Steroids discouraged (ineffective, may cause infection)

**Delirium**

Early experience (before 1990) showed substantial postoperative delirium (77% with circulatory arrest >55 minutes). With shorter arrest times, incidence fell to 11%. Postoperative confusion now no more common than with ordinary open heart surgery.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Results</summary><div class="details-content">

More than **3500** pulmonary thromboendarterectomies performed at UCSD Medical Center since 1970. Mean patient age: **54 years** (range 3–85). Slight male predominance. Nearly one-third of cases had at least one additional cardiac procedure (closure of PFO/ASD 26%, CABG 8%).

**Hemodynamic Results**
- Reduction in pulmonary pressures and resistance to normal levels immediate and sustained
- Before operation: >95% in NYHA class III or IV
- At 1 year: 95% in NYHA class I or II
- Right ventricular geometry rapidly reverts toward normal; tricuspid function returns within days

**Operative Morbidity**
- Severe reperfusion injury: 10%
- Re-exploration for hemorrhage: 2.5%
- Sternal wound complications: 1.8%
- Less than half of patients required blood transfusion

**Deaths**
- Overall mortality (30-day/in-hospital): 6% (entire 35-year experience)
- Mortality since 1990 (>3000 patients): <5%
- Mortality in last 5 years: <2%
- Primary causes: operation on patients without thromboembolic disease (50%), irreversible reperfusion pulmonary edema (25%)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Late Follow-up</summary><div class="details-content">

A survey of 420 patients >1 year after operation (308 responses) demonstrated:
- Survival: **75% at ≥6 years**
- NYHA class I or II: 93% (vs. 95% class III/IV preoperatively)
- Return to work: 62% of previously unemployed patients
- Quality of life: slightly lower than normal individuals, significantly higher than pre-endarterectomy
- Oxygen use: only 10%
- Patient satisfaction: 77% "much improved," 20% "improved"

Pulmonary endarterectomy offers substantial improvement in survival, function, and quality of life with minimal later healthcare requirements.
