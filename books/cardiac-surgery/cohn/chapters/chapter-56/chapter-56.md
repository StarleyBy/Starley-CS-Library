# Surgery for Adult Congenital Heart Disease

##### Adults with congenital heart disease now outnumber children with this malady. They constitute a growing population of survivors, with increasingly complex treatment requirements for their heart disease. Over the past two decades, the spectrum of complexity for adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) patients has evolved from late primary repairs of patients with simple lesions—including coarctation, patent ductus arteriosus, septal defects, and tetralogy of Fallot—to nth time reoperations on survivors of complex multistaged palliations. Significant efforts to define the optimal program resources necessary to effectively treat ACHD patients have been made worldwide, yet many congenital heart patients continue to suffer from poor continuity of care as they enter adulthood. This chapter will describe several salient treatment strategies for adult patients undergoing congenital heart surgery.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Philosophy and Systems
*   **Cumulative Trauma Reduction:** Multidisciplinary team approach selecting least traumatic effective therapy first (e.g., device closure before surgical repair for ASD).
*   **Information Systems:** Web-based EMR with intraoperative images improves reoperative planning; personal health records (PHRs) enhance patient education.

### Reoperative Sternotomy
*   **Risk Factors:** Retrosternal conduits (especially calcified/stented), pulmonary hypertension, RV enlargement, prior sternal infections, outflow tract aneurysms.
*   **Technique:** Cautery dissection from inferior sternal edge; oscillating saw; if injury occurs, immediate neck incision for innominate artery cannulation. Carbon dioxide field flooding reduces air embolism risk.

### Common Procedures

**Atrial Septal Defects**
*   Device closure preferred for secundum defects. Sinus venosus, primum, and defects with deficient rims require surgery.
*   Sinus venosus: Bicaval cannulation, lateral atrial incision, pericardial patch to baffle anomalous veins to left atrium.
*   Primum defects: Cleft mitral valve routinely repaired; results excellent even in advanced age.

**Ebstein's Anomaly**
*   Cone technique: Dissect anterior/posterior leaflets, rotate anterior leaflet clockwise, suture to septal leaflet border, creating cone-shaped valve. Results: low mortality, reduced TR, improved functional class.

**Fontan Revision**
*   Convert atriopulmonary connections to extracardiac cavopulmonary conduit. Combined with arrhythmia surgery and atrioventricular valve repair/replacement. Routine placement of dual-chamber antitachycardia pacemaker recommended.

**Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Reconstruction**
*   Indications: Pulmonary insufficiency/stenosis after tetralogy of Fallot, truncus, pulmonary atresia. Percutaneous pulmonary valve replacement (PPVR) increasingly available (32% stent fractures, benign).
*   Conduit options: Homografts, bovine jugular vein (Contegra), bioprosthetic valves. Mechanical valves no longer used in pulmonary position.

**Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Reconstruction**
*   Ross, Ross-Konno, Konno for complex LVOTO. 4% require conduit replacement at 10 years; 19–39% reoperation rate for autograft at 5–10 years. Reoperations challenging; antiadhesion barriers recommended.

**Arrhythmia Surgery**
*   Right-sided Maze: 93% freedom from arrhythmia. Lead extraction in ACHD patients often requires hybrid surgical/electrophysiology approach.

**Hybrid Procedures**
*   Combined surgical and interventional approaches in single operation. Allows central vascular access, stent deployment under direct vision, and emergent cardiopulmonary bypass support.

**Coarctation of the Aorta**
*   Increasingly managed with balloon dilation and stents. Surgical repair via left thoracotomy with interposition graft; 75% normotensive without medication at follow-up.

### Interventional Device Management
*   Stents in pulmonary arteries and aorta require careful planning for reoperation. After 3 months, stents incorporated into vessel wall—incision through stent with division of all links, onlay patch reconstruction.

### Outcomes
*   Pediatric congenital heart surgeons have lower mortality for ACHD surgery (1.87%) vs adult cardiac surgeons (4.84%, p < .0001). Arrhythmia leading cause of death in cyanotic lesions; myocardial infarction now leading cause in noncyanotic ACHD.

## REDUCING CUMULATIVE THERAPEUTIC TRAUMA</summary><div class="details-content">

The optimal venue for ACHD treatment is unknown, and it is fair to say that no two programs are the same. The need for a coordinated and comprehensive programmatic strategy for care has been well described, and should encompass not only the lifetime of a generation of patients with congenital heart defects from fetal to adult life, but also the health of pregnant mothers with congenital heart disease who will give birth to a new generation. Pregnant mothers with fetal diagnoses of congenital heart disease may be asked to undergo invasive intrauterine procedures to palliate their fetal child's heart, thus creating a new group of adult patients dealing with the trauma of congenital heart care.

Our congenital heart program philosophy is to reduce the cumulative trauma of care for each patient with congenital heart disease over their lifetime. To achieve this, we discuss each adult patient referred to our program for surgery or intervention in a combined conference with participation from adult and pediatric interventional cardiologists and surgeons, dedicated cardiac intensivists, cardiac anesthesiologists, cardiac imaging specialists, and the cardiac nursing, pharmacy and social work teams. Treatment options are selected so that the least traumatic form of effective therapy is chosen as the initial approach. Failure to achieve a good result leads to an escalation to the next least traumatic option. As an example, a patient with a secundum atrial septal defect (ASD) would be put forward for device closure, and if this failed, the patient would then be given the option of a minimally invasive surgical repair.

## INFORMATION SYSTEMS</summary><div class="details-content">

Adult congenital heart disease patients with complex lesions often have dense medical histories, stored in multimedia formats, which expose the weaknesses of current paper and electronic medical record (EMR) systems. Increasing utilization of EMRs by medical teams, and personal health records (PHRs) by patients and families, has been demonstrated to improve outcomes in some patients with chronic disease. However, pediatric heart patients and their families often have not been educated about their disease, and what to expect as they transition to adulthood. The PHR could be used to enhance patients' understanding, and provide roadmaps for follow-up treatments and diagnostic studies. These information systems could also be linked to national and international registries designed to measure clinical outcomes and performance for centers treating adult congenital heart patients.

The emergence of Web-based hospital EMR systems allows our cardiac team to track patients over their lifetime, and retrieve critical patient information on demand. We have also enabled our patients to access specific data fields from the EMR. We capture intraoperative endoscopic images of each congenital heart lesion, before and after repair, and store these images in our EMR. We also capture and store a daily picture of each hospitalized patient. These images are reviewed in morbidity and mortality conferences, to correlate with other clinical information and imaging techniques. The operative images are reviewed prior to reoperations, to regain familiarity with the patient's anatomy. ## COMMON ADULT CONGENITAL HEART DISEASE PROCEDURES</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Reoperative Sternotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

Congenital heart procedures in adults are frequently reoperations, however, standardized protocols for reoperations in patients with acquired heart disease may not match the challenges presented by ACHD patients. Risk factors for traumatic repeat sternotomy in ACHD patients differ from those identified in patients with acquired heart disease, which often focus on dealing with patent coronary artery bypass grafts. Femoral cannulation is often difficult in ACHD patients who have undergone multiple interventional catheterizations during their growth years, resulting in stenotic or occluded femoral vessels. Consequently, we rarely use elective or emergent peripheral cannulation.

**Key risk factors for ACHD reoperations:**
- Retrosternal ventricular to pulmonary artery conduits (especially calcified, stented, or embedded in sternum)
- Fusion to sternum suggested by conduit not moving with cardiac cycle on lateral angiograms
- Pulmonary hypertension
- Right ventricular enlargement
- Prior sternal infections
- Aneurysms of right or left ventricular outflow tracts
- Pectus excavatum
- Aortopulmonary shunts

Patients with multiple risk factors may be placed on bypass to decompress the right ventricle prior to sternal division.

**Technique:**
- Excise previous sternotomy scars and remove sternal wires
- Remove xiphoid process
- Cautery dissection at inferior sternal edge
- Oscillating saw divides anterior sternal table
- Rakes elevate sternal halves to expose retrosternal scar
- Release short segments of scar, then gradually divide sternum with saw

**Injury Management:**
- If cardiac or great vessel penetration occurs without pre-exposed cannulation vessels:
  - Release sternal retractors
  - Extend neck incision up and right to expose innominate artery and internal jugular/high innominate vein
  - Cannulate through pursestrings, initiate bypass
- If suprasternal access not possible, dissect inferior vena cava just above diaphragm ("simplified aortic cannulation")
- Complete sternotomy with patient in Trendelenburg position

**Adhesion Prevention:**
- Continuous carbon dioxide infusion to field reduces air embolism risk
- Cover heart with saline-soaked gauze throughout operation
- Antiadhesion materials (expanded PTFE membrane, bioresorbable films, extracellular matrix sheets) placed behind sternum prior to closure

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Atrial Septal Defects</summary><div class="details-content">

The advent of transcatheter device closure for ASDs has significantly changed the average complexity level of ACHD surgical case volumes. Patent foramen ovale and most secundum defects are effectively closed with devices, and are rarely referred for surgical closure in programs with effective interventional catheterization teams. Sinus venosus and primum defects, transitional and complete atrioventricular canal, common atrium, and secundum defects with deficient inferior and superior rims are referred for surgery and we repair these on cardiopulmonary bypass with bicaval cannulation and ischemic arrest.

**Incisional Approaches:**
Multiple incisional approaches have been described to improve cosmetic result and reduce operative trauma, including partial upper and lower sternotomy, transxiphoid, anterior thoracotomy, and submammary incisions. Despite the visible scar, median sternotomy may be the least traumatic incision for ACHD patients, allowing:
- Avoidance of vascular trauma from peripheral cannulation
- Avoidance of intercostal muscle, vessel, and nerve damage
- Mitigation of post-thoracotomy pain syndrome
- Direct aortic control for safe cannulation, decannulation, deairing, and cardioplegia
- Direct and rapid access to entire mediastinum for unanticipated anatomic variations

**Venous Cannulation:**
- Bicaval via right atrial appendage into superior vena cava, and down inferior vena caval junction

**Secundum Defects:**
- Repairs best performed with patch materials (glutaraldehyde-treated pericardium preferred)
- Smooth surface on left atrial side
- Running fine polypropylene sutures for tension-free closure (Fig. 56-2A)

**Sinus Venosus Defects:**
- Place right-angled cannula in innominate vein for exposure of partial anomalous pulmonary veins entering SVC
- Atrial incision made laterally to avoid sinus node area, extended superiorly above highest anomalous vein

**Primum ASDs:**
- Cleft mitral valve routinely repaired with fine running polypropylene sutures approximating line of contact between leaflet segments
- Even patients with competent valves are repaired (late onset of cleft regurgitation known to occur)
- Patients at risk for mitral stenosis (particularly those with single papillary muscle in LV) may have clefts left open to avoid valvar stenosis
- Results excellent even in patients at advanced ages

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Ebstein's Anomaly</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgery for Ebstein's anomaly can be performed in older patients at low risk and with good late outcome. The operation is comprised of tricuspid valve repair or replacement, and concomitant procedures such as ASD closure, arrhythmia surgery, and coronary artery bypass grafting. Repair techniques for these patients continue to evolve.

**Cone Technique:**
- Requires presence of untethered and well-developed anterior tricuspid valve leaflet
- Dissect anterior and posterior tricuspid valve leaflets from their right ventricular attachment
- Rotate free edge of anterior leaflet clockwise and suture to septal leaflet border
- Produces cone-shaped valve, fixed distally at RV apex and proximally at tricuspid valve annulus
- Incorporate septal leaflet into cone wall whenever possible
- Partially close ASD

**Results:**
- Low mortality
- Significantly less tricuspid regurgitation
- Improvement in functional class

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Fontan Revision</summary><div class="details-content">

In the modern era, the primary cause of death for adult patients with cyanotic lesions is arrhythmia, followed by heart failure. Fontan patients may present with arrhythmia and complications related to systemic ventricular failure, protein-losing enteropathy (PLE), systemic venous pathway obstruction, and semilunar and atrioventricular valve dysfunction. Initial evaluations must focus on ensuring a completely unobstructed vascular pathway to the lungs.

**Complications by Surgical Type:**
- Intracardiac baffles and atriopulmonary connections: extreme right atrial enlargement, stagnant flow, right pulmonary vein compression, arrhythmia

**Fontan Conversion:**
- Takedown of previously created venous connection
- Creation of extracardiac cavopulmonary connection with conduit
- Extracardiac Fontan excludes systemic veins from heart—any catheterization procedures requiring atrial level intervention (particularly electrophysiology) must be planned before conversion
- Frequently combine with arrhythmia surgery and treatment of atrioventricular valve dysfunction
- Valve repairs often complex; replacements often required for good hemodynamic results

**Technique:**
- Bicaval cannulation, heart warm and beating
- Inferior vena cava transected at cavo-atrial junction under clamp; cardiac end oversewn with running 4-0 polypropylene
- Ring-reinforced expanded PTFE graft (19–23 mm diameter), leave enough length to avoid right pulmonary vein compression
- Superior anastomosis to SVC–right pulmonary artery junction with running 6-0 polypropylene
- Hybrid stenting procedures for stenoses in retroaortic pulmonary arteries

**Pacemaker Management:**
- Unpredictable anatomy of conduction tissue in ACHD results in frequent need for pacemaker insertion
- Based on experience with 120 Fontan conversions (1994–2008), Tsao et al recommend routine placement of dual-chamber antitachycardia pacemaker with bipolar steroid-eluting leads in patients undergoing Fontan revision

**Outcomes:**
- Results depend on underlying anatomy, right ventricular function, and pulmonary vascular resistance

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

Right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) reconstruction after previous repair of tetralogy of Fallot, double outlet right ventricle, pulmonary atresia, truncus arteriosus, and arterial switch procedures are increasingly common, as patients who underwent successful neonatal and infant repairs are returning with pulmonary insufficiency and/or stenosis, resulting in right ventricular dysfunction, exercise intolerance, arrhythmias, and sudden death.

**Percutaneous Pulmonary Valve Replacement (PPVR):**
- First performed by Bonhoeffer and colleagues in 2000
- Clinical trials demonstrate safety and efficacy; NYHA functional class improvement evident at 1 month
- Growing experience with transcatheter pulmonary valve insertion into RVOT homografts and conduits:
  - Homograft infection rare
  - Stent fractures common (32%) but benign
  - Median maximum instantaneous gradient across RVOT: 23 mm Hg
- Surgical standby required; patients with rupture can undergo emergent bypass and surgical repair with good results

**Surgical Approach (for patients not amenable to PPVR):**
- Median sternotomy, bicaval cannulation, moderate hypothermia
- May perform without ischemic arrest if bubble test with Valsalva negative; however, we prefer aortic cross-clamp
- Control branch pulmonary arteries with tourniquets
- Place RV vent in atrium for clear operative field
- Use previous RV incision or patch as safe reentry point; extend incision to inspect RV and resect obstructing muscle bundles

**Conduit Options:**
| Conduit Type | Advantages | Considerations |
|--------------|------------|----------------|
| Pulmonary/aortic homografts | Length for spanning gaps; suitable for future PPVR | Cut to appropriate length; avoid tension or kinking |
| Bovine jugular vein (Contegra) | Beveled proximal graft eliminates need for hood patch; 10-year results show less failure than homografts | Sizing and implantation similar to homografts |
| Bioprosthetic valves | Ring provides stable landing zone for future PPVR | Position to avoid sternal compression and left main coronary compression |
| Mechanical valves | — | No longer used in pulmonary position (precludes future PPVR) |

- Distal pulmonary artery anastomosis with running 6-0 monofilament polypropylene
- Trim proximal homograft muscle cuff to match RV incision
- Proximal anastomosis with larger running suture; avoid left main and anterior descending coronary arteries
- Use intraoperative fluorescent imaging to map coronary arteries if epicardial scarring obscures visualization
- Complete anterior outflow tract with native or bovine pericardial patch (Fig. 56-3, 56-4, 56-5)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

Isolated areas of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) are less common in ACHD patients than in those with acquired left-sided lesions. ACHD patients with LVOTO must be evaluated for upstream and downstream stenoses, as multiple levels of obstruction are common.

**Shone's Syndrome:**
- Patients often undergo numerous surgical and interventional procedures over their lifetimes
- Interventions are at best palliative

**Ross Operation:**
- Often used in pediatric patients with complex LVOTO to treat combined valvar and supravalvar lesions, avoid anticoagulation, and maintain growth potential
- May require reoperations as adults to replace or repair failing neo aortic valves, and stenotic or regurgitant pulmonary valves
- Follow-up: 4% require conduit replacement for RV dysfunction at 10 years; conduit size <14 mm independent predictor of allograft dysfunction
- Freedom from reoperation for pulmonary autograft regurgitation: 87–96%
- Aneurysmal dilation of neo aortic root (root technique) seen in up to 11% at 7 years
- Good outcomes after reoperation if diagnosed early before neo aortic valve becomes insufficient

**Ross-Konno and Konno Procedures:**
- Konno aortico ventriculoplasty treats complex LVOTO with supravalvar, valvar, and subvalvar obstruction
- Ross-Konno (transplanted pulmonary autograft with ventricular septal patch or infundibular extension) increases in size as patients grow
- Review of 53 patients (1980–2004, average age 19):
  - Risk factors for overall mortality: NYHA class (hazard ratio 2.22, p = .04)
  - Cumulative probability of aortic valve reoperation: 19% at 5 years, 39% at 10 years
  - Pulmonary regurgitation in 6 patients; PVR in 3 (6%)

**Reoperative Considerations:**
- Reoperations after Ross, Ross-Konno, and Konno particularly challenging
- Especially if RVOT reconstructed with large patch covering anterior aortic wall—exposure of aortic root necessitates reentry into right ventricle
- Antiadhesion barriers recommended

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Arrhythmia Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

Maintaining a functional conduction system is a crucial aspect of reducing the cumulative lifetime trauma for a congenital heart patient. Despite efforts to prevent conduction tissue injury, ACHD patients are at risk for a wide array of conduction delays, as well as atrial and ventricular tachyarrhythmias.

**Pacemaker/Defibrillator Management:**
- Lead insertion, extractions, and generator changes frequently complicated in ACHD patients (unusual anatomic pathways, stenotic/occluded veins)
- Epicardial lead placements often necessary in single ventricle patients with limited or no venous access to endocardium
- Indications for lead removal: pocket infection, malfunctioning leads, skin erosion, endocarditis/septicemia, vena cava thrombosis, painful leads
- Lead extraction technology evolving; can be safely managed as hybrid effort with surgeon and electrophysiologist
- Lead removal catheters using blades or laser energy to excise embedded leads
- Surgical support necessary for cardiac perforation
- Outcomes generally good even in octogenarians; sepsis strongest predictor of death after device removal

**Arrhythmia Ablation:**
- Supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias major cause of morbidity and mortality in ACHD
- Right-sided Maze procedure for atrial fibrillation/flutter undergoing open-heart repairs: 93% freedom from arrhythmia, improvement in functional class
- Minimally invasive approaches (Mini-Maze) increasingly popular in acquired heart disease but may be difficult in ACHD due to unusual anatomy and reoperative scarring
- Cox-Maze for SVT often complex; alternative using intraoperative monopolar irrigated radiofrequency ablation (IRA) described with good results
- Prophylactic arrhythmia surgery for high-risk groups evolving

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Hybrid Procedures</summary><div class="details-content">

We define hybrid procedures as those using combined surgical and interventional personnel and technology during a single operation. Hybrid procedures can be performed in the catheterization laboratory, the operating room, or ideally in a hybrid procedure suite. We select the venue based on which technology is most critical for the success of a given procedure.

The advent of percutaneous cardiac valve replacement and endovascular stenting reinforce the need to embrace a unified team approach for adult congenital heart patients. In those requiring interventional, surgical, and electrophysiological procedures, we attempt to integrate the procedures into a single hybrid operation, with the surgical team providing the least traumatic form of vascular access.

**Advantages:**
- Surgeons can provide central vascular and direct cardiac access for sheath placement in patients with stenotic or occluded peripheral vessels
- Central access for placing large devices in small patients
- Routine deployment of aortic and pulmonary artery stents in OR using direct vision, video-assisted cardioscopy, and angiography (Fig. 56-6)
- Elective or emergent cardiopulmonary bypass support available on demand (Fig. 56-7)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Coarctation of the Aorta</summary><div class="details-content">

Primary and recurrent coarctation of the aorta in adult patients is increasingly managed percutaneously with balloon dilation and stents. We provide surgical backup for these procedures in the event of device migration, dissection, or rupture, and occasionally to establish vascular access.

**Surgical Approach:**
- When transcatheter therapy not feasible
- Left thoracotomy in patients with left aortic arch
- Perfusion team on standby for left atrial appendage to descending aortic cardiopulmonary bypass

**Techniques:**
| Technique | Indication |
|-----------|------------|
| Coarctation resection with end-to-end anastomosis | Limited aortic mobility |
| Onlay patch enlargement (tissue or prosthetic) | — |
| Synthetic tube interposition | Preferred for tension-free repairs in adults |

**Postoperative Management:**
- Subcutaneous local anesthetic infusion catheters and liposomal bupivacaine for pain control
- Anticipate labile blood pressure responses in patients with long-standing hypertension
- Postoperative hypertension common; responds to sodium nitroprusside

**Outcomes:**
- Mortality rare
- At follow-up, 75% of patients normotensive without medication

## INTERVENTIONAL DEVICE MANAGEMENT</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac surgeons will increasingly encounter ACHD patients with intravascular devices in the aorta and pulmonary arteries, atrial and ventricular septal occlusion devices, and transcatheter stented valves. Vascular stents are being effectively used in neonates and infants, with the understanding that patients will inevitably outgrow the maximum size these stents can achieve. We have a growing experience with reoperations on patients who have outgrown stents in the aortic arch and the branch pulmonary arteries, and these are far more common than reoperations involving septal occlusion devices.

**Early Reoperation (<3 months):**
- May be possible to completely remove stent
- Chip away cells using scissors
- Collapse stent inward and peel away from vessel wall

**Late Reoperation (>3 months):**
- Vascular ingrowth incorporates stent into vessel wall, making removal difficult and traumatic
- Operative planning requires optimal location of longitudinal incision to open stent
- Stent spread open like "hot dog bun" to create space for onlay patch
- Must cut every link to distal ends to avoid leaving persistent complete metal ring (Fig. 56-8)

![](_page_7_Figure_11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 56-8** An operative image of reoperation on a stented pulmonary artery, requiring an incision through the front wall of the stent and through the most distal cells.

**Technical Considerations:**
- Stents often end close to branch points; distal extension of stent incision must avoid spiral tears into smaller branch vessels (may cause branch occlusion)
- Onlay patches: native or bovine pericardium, PTFE grafts, pulmonary or aortic homografts, or extracellular matrix grafts
- If final vessel reconstruction does not achieve full adult-sized lumen or is compressed by adjacent structures, patched split stent is safe landing zone for subsequent adult-sized stent
- Stents can be placed immediately in OR (hybrid procedure) or later after patched vessel heals (reduces suture line rupture and bleeding risk)

**Atrial Septal Stents:**
- Occasionally used to achieve unrestrictive flow across atrial septum when restriction develops after previous surgical septectomy
- At late reoperation, stents firmly encased in septal tissue, may extend into pulmonary vein orifices
- Removal: scissor dissection, trim exposed metal, peel out embedded sections, enucleate ingrown scar tissue
- Inspect atrium for full-thickness tears; repair with polypropylene suture

## IMPROVED OUTCOMES USING CONGENITAL HEART SURGEONS TO TREAT ADULT CONGENITAL HEART DISEASE</summary><div class="details-content">

Management guidelines for adults with congenital heart disease have been developed by the American Heart Association and the American College of Cardiology.

**Volume and Outcomes:**
- Review of 72 US centers performing ACHD surgery: 2800 operations/year, range 0–230 (median 28) per program
- Median two surgeons per program; each surgeon averages 20 cases/year

**Surgeon Specialty and Mortality:**
| Surgeon Type | Mortality |
|--------------|-----------|
| Pediatric congenital heart surgeons | 1.87% |
| Adult cardiac surgeons | 4.84% (p < .0001) |

**Causes of Death:**
- Arrhythmia remains primary contributing cause for patients with cyanotic lesions
- Myocardial infarction now leading contributing cause for adults with noncyanotic congenital heart disease (consistent with late survival and increasing impact of acquired heart disease)

With increasing experience and a team approach synthesizing surgery, intervention, electrophysiology, anesthesia, nursing and intensive care, adults with congenital heart disease should experience improved outcomes with less cumulative lifetime therapeutic trauma.
