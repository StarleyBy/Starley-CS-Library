# Ross Procedure

##### The Ross procedure replaces the diseased aortic valve with a viable pulmonary autograft and uses an appropriate conduit (e.g., a cryopreserved pulmonary homograft) to reconstruct the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). As initially described, the autograft was placed as a scalloped subcoronary implant. The complexity of the operation and concerns regarding autograft insufficiency have limited widespread adoption of the procedure. The subsequent use of the full root technique, in addition to the increasing availability of homografts, has increased interest in the operation. More recent concerns regarding autograft dilation and neoaortic insufficiency have led to further refinements.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Overview
* **Ross procedure:** Replaces diseased aortic valve with viable pulmonary autograft. RVOT reconstructed with pulmonary homograft.
* **Evolution:** Initially scalloped subcoronary implant (complex, concerns about insufficiency) → full root technique (increased adoption). Recent concerns about autograft dilation → further refinements.

### Surgical Anatomy
* **Critical elements:** Proper enucleation of pulmonary root, undistorted implantation into LVOT.
* **Adult modification:** Pulmonary autograft placed within appropriately sized Dacron conduit (prevents root dilation and neoaortic insufficiency, stabilizes sinotubular junction).
* **Anatomy:** Thorough understanding of anatomic relationships between pulmonary and aortic valves essential.

### Preoperative Considerations
* **Ideal candidates:** Infants, children, adolescents (growth potential, favorable hemodynamics, no anticoagulation). Also young adults wishing to avoid anticoagulation or with endocarditis requiring valve replacement.
* **Bicuspid aortic valve:** Excellent results in adults.
* **Age extension:** Low rate of RVOT stenosis in older patients → may extend popularity to sixth decade.
* **Patient counseling:** Inform about possibility of autograft failure.
* **Avoid if:** Significant geometric discrepancy between pulmonary and aortic annuli (preoperative detection minimizes complication). If moderate discrepancy exists, surgeon should know mismatch-minimizing techniques.
* **Exclusion criteria:** Abnormal pulmonary valve, complex connective tissue disease, immune complex-mediated disease with valvular sequelae.

### Operative Steps
* **Exposure:** Median sternotomy. Bicaval cannulation (facilitates exposure, avoids venous air entrapment after autograft enucleation).
* **Cardiopulmonary bypass:** Cool to 32°C (89.6°F). Vent through right superior pulmonary vein.
* **Aortic resection:** Divide aorta at sinotubular junction, inspect aortic valve. Harvest generous coronary buttons, excise aortic valve and root.
* **Pulmonary transection:** Transect pulmonary artery below branch pulmonary artery.
* **Autograft harvest:** Visual inspect pulmonary valve leaflets. Incise RVOT across infundibulum ~4 mm below pulmonary valve leaflets (use right-angled clamp through pulmonary valve to identify site). Excise pulmonary root with 3-4 mm rim of myocardium.
* **Avoid injury:** Identify aberrant coronary arteries crossing RVOT. Dissect along septal myocardium, avoid first septal perforator and LAD. Continue along LAD posteriorly, avoid left main coronary artery.
* **Autograft preparation:** Excise excessive myocardium from explanted pulmonary root (prevents LVOT obstruction). Pass Hegar dilator through pulmonary valve to select Dacron tube (choose tube graft 2 mm larger than autograft to avoid distortion/narrowing).
* **Inclusion technique (Dacron conduit):** Secure autograft within tube graft using running 4-0 polypropylene through myocardium just below valve leaflet. Cut graft at top of commissures, suture distal autograft to Dacron graft with 4-0 polypropylene. Saline test confirms leaflet competency.
* **LVOT implantation:** Suture tubularized autograft to LVOT with running 3-0 polypropylene.
* **Coronary reimplantation:** Excise portion of Dacron conduit and corresponding internal autograft sinus at proper location. Left and right coronary button anastomoses with 5-0 polypropylene (buttons provide structural support to right and left sinuses by stabilizing them to tube graft).
* **Noncoronary sinus reinforcement:** Suture piece of homograft inside noncoronary sinus to corresponding wall of Dacron conduit (addresses unsupported noncoronary sinus).
* **RVOT reconstruction:** Appropriately sized pulmonary homograft. Distal anastomosis below bifurcation, proximal suture line with 4-0 polypropylene.
* **Distal suture:** Complete distal suture of tubularized autograft.

### Postoperative Care
* **Hemostasis:** Ensure excellent hemostasis before leaving OR. Any bleeding (especially LVOT suture line or coronary buttons) should be repaired on CPB with cardioplegic arrest.
* **Avoid blind sutures** at proximal suture line (may injure autograft leaflets).
* **TEE:** Confirm good valve function and absence of LVOT gradient.
* **Blood pressure:** Avoid hypertension in ICU. Myocardial function generally good, inotropic support usually not necessary.

### Pearls and Pitfalls
* **Meticulous technique** imperative to avoid bleeding.
* **Autograft enucleation:** Definite tissue plane between pulmonary root and surrounding structures. Most easily identified by initiating enucleation on right (aortic) side of autograft.
* **Proper alignment** of autograft in LVOT mandatory for successful outcome.

## Step 1. Surgical Anatomy</summary><div class="details-content">

Relevant surgical anatomy centers on proper enucleation of the pulmonary root and undistorted implantation into the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT). In adults, we currently place the pulmonary autograft within an appropriately sized Dacron conduit to prevent pulmonary autograft root dilation and subsequent neoaortic insufficiency. This technique also stabilizes the sinotubular junction.

A thorough understanding of the anatomic relationships between the pulmonary and aortic valves is critical ([Fig. 17.1](#fig-17-1)).

## Step 2. Preoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The growth potential of the autograft, favorable hemodynamics, and avoidance of anticoagulation have made Ross procedure the operation of choice for infants, children, and adolescents with aortic valve disease requiring aortic valve replacement. It should also be considered for young adults who wish to avoid anticoagulation or who have endocarditis requiring valve replacement.

We have had excellent results using the Ross procedure in adults with bicuspid aortic valves requiring replacement. Recent evidence has suggested a low rate of RVOT stenosis in older patients, which may extend the popularity of the operation for patients up to the sixth decade.

It is important to inform patients about the possibility of autograft failure. Avoiding the Ross operation when a significant geometric discrepancy between the pulmonary and aortic annuli is detected preoperatively should minimize this complication. If a moderate-sized discrepancy exists between the aortic and pulmonary roots, a number of techniques to minimize mismatch have been developed; the surgeon should be familiar with them before performing the procedure.

Patients with an abnormal pulmonary valve, a complex connective tissue disease, or an immune complex–mediated disease with known valvular sequelae should be excluded.

## Step 3. Operative Steps</summary><div class="details-content">

A standard median sternotomy is performed. The pericardium is incised, and pericardial stay sutures are placed. Bicaval cannulation is used, which facilitates exposure and avoids venous air entrapment following autograft enucleation. Antegrade and retrograde cardioplegia cannulae are placed, except when aortic sufficiency is present, in which case handheld cannulae may be used. The patient is placed on cardiopulmonary bypass and cooled to 32°C (89.6°F). A vent is placed through the right superior pulmonary vein ([Fig. 17.2](#fig-17-2)).

![](_page_5_Picture_2.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-2"></span>**Figure 17.2** Placement of vent through right superior pulmonary vein.

The aorta is divided at the sinotubular junction, and the aortic valve is inspected. If no repair option is available, generous coronary buttons are harvested, and the aortic valve and root are excised. The pulmonary artery is transected below the branch pulmonary artery ([Fig. 17.3](#fig-17-3)).

![](_page_5_Picture_4.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-3"></span>**Figure 17.3** Transection of pulmonary artery below branch pulmonary artery.

After visual inspection of the pulmonary valve leaflets, the pulmonary root with the valve leaflets is excised from the RVOT. The incision is initiated in the RVOT across the infundibulum, approximately 4 mm below the pulmonary valve leaflets. A right-angled clamp can be placed through the pulmonary valve to identify the proper site to begin the ventriculotomy. The pulmonary root should be excised with a 3- to 4-mm rim of myocardium ([Figs. 17.4](#fig-17-4) and [17.5](#fig-17-5)).

![](_page_5_Picture_6.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-4"></span>**Figure 17.4** Incision in RVOT across infundibulum.

![](_page_5_Picture_8.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-5"></span>**Figure 17.5** Excision of pulmonary root with myocardial rim.

Aberrant coronary arteries coursing across the RVOT should be identified. The dissection extends along the septal myocardium, avoiding the first septal perforator and left anterior descending artery. The dissection continues along the course of the left anterior descending artery posteriorly, avoiding injury to the left main coronary artery ([Figs. 17.6](#fig-17-6) and [17.7](#fig-17-7)).

![](_page_7_Picture_2.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-6"></span>**Figure 17.6** Dissection along septal myocardium.

![](_page_7_Picture_4.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-7"></span>**Figure 17.7** Dissection along LAD posteriorly.

After the autograft is harvested, excessive myocardium is excised from the explanted pulmonary root to avoid LVOT obstruction after implantation ([Fig. 17.8](#fig-17-8)).

![](_page_7_Picture_6.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-8"></span>**Figure 17.8** Excision of excessive myocardium from explanted pulmonary root.

A Hegar dilator is gently passed through the pulmonary valve to select an appropriately sized Dacron tube. We usually pick a tube graft 2 mm larger than the measured size of the autograft to avoid distortion and narrowing. The autograft is secured within the tube graft using running 4-0 polypropylene sutures passed through the myocardium, just below the valve leaflet ([Fig. 17.9](#fig-17-9)).

![](_page_7_Picture_8.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-9"></span>**Figure 17.9** Securing autograft within Dacron tube graft.

After the pulmonary root is secured, the graft is cut at the top of the commissures, and the distal autograft is sutured to the Dacron graft using 4-0 polypropylene ([Fig. 17.10](#fig-17-10)). Once the autograft is completely implanted within the graft, a saline test can confirm leaflet competency ([Fig. 17.11](#fig-17-11)).

![](_page_7_Picture_10.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-10"></span>**Figure 17.10** Distal autograft sutured to Dacron graft.

![](_page_7_Picture_12.jpeg)

<span id="fig-17-11"></span>**Figure 17.11** Saline test confirming leaflet competency.

The tubularized autograft is sutured to the LVOT using running 3-0 polypropylene ([Figs. 17.12](#fig-17-12) and [17.13](#fig-17-13)). After the proximal anastomosis is complete, the coronary buttons are reimplanted. A portion of the Dacron conduit and the corresponding internal autograft sinus are excised after determining the proper location for coronary reimplantation ([Fig. 17.14](#fig-17-14)). The left and right coronary button anastomoses are performed with 5-0 polypropylene sutures. The coronary buttons create structural support to the right and left sinuses of the autograft by stabilizing them to the tube graft. Because of concerns regarding the unsupported noncoronary sinus, we suture a piece of homograft inside the noncoronary sinus to the corresponding wall of the Dacron conduit ([Fig. 17.15](#fig-17-15)).

## Step 4. Postoperative Care</summary><div class="details-content">

Good postoperative care mandates ensuring excellent hemostasis before leaving the operating room. Any bleeding, especially from the LVOT suture line or coronary buttons, should be repaired, if necessary, on cardiopulmonary bypass using cardioplegic arrest.

Placement of blind sutures at the proximal suture line should be avoided because autograft leaflets may be injured.

Transesophageal echocardiography should confirm good valve function and lack of an LVOT gradient.

Avoidance of hypertension should be emphasized in the intensive care unit. Generally, myocardial function is good, and inotropic support is not necessary.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Step 5. Pearls and Pitfalls</summary><div class="details-content">

- Meticulous technique is imperative to avoid bleeding.
- When enucleating the autograft, a definite tissue plane can be identified between the pulmonary root and surrounding structures. This is most easily identified by initiating the enucleation on the right (aortic) side of the autograft.
- Proper alignment of the autograft in the LVOT is mandatory for a successful outcome.
