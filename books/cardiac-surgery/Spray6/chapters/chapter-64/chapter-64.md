# Cardiac transplantation for congenital heart disease

##### This chapter describes technical strategies for cardiac transplantation in recipients with complex congenital heart disease, including abnormalities of systemic and pulmonary venous return, atrial situs, great artery relationships, pulmonary arteries, ascending aorta and aortic arch, ventricular orientation, and anatomy altered by previous operations (Fontan, atrial switch, arterial switch, double switch, etc.). Organ procurement techniques, recipient reconstruction, and hemostasis are discussed.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Recipient Categories (Complex Congenital Heart Disease)
* **Abnormalities of systemic or pulmonary venous return:** Isolated left SVC, bilateral SVC, isolated left IVC, abnormal hepatic veins, extracardiac anomalous pulmonary venous connections.
* **Atrial situs abnormalities:** Situs inversus totalis, isomeric atrial appendages.
* **Great artery abnormalities:** Transposition, malposition, previous Lecompte maneuver.
* **Pulmonary artery abnormalities:** Hypoplasia, discontinuity, post‑surgical/interventional changes.
* **Aortic arch abnormalities:** Hypoplasia, coarctation, interruption, hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS).
* **Ventricular orientation abnormalities:** Dextrocardia, dextroversion, mesocardia, mesoversion.
* **Post‑operative alterations:** Previous cavopulmonary anastomoses (Glenn), Fontan, atrial switch (Mustard/Senning), arterial switch, double switch.

### Organ Procurement for Complex Recipients
* **Planning and communication** between procuring and implanting teams critical. Femoral lines preferred in donor.
* **Left SVC recipients:** Procure entire innominate vein with parts of left/right internal jugular veins.
* **Pulmonary arteries:** May procure from hilum to hilum (impacts lung procurement).
* **Aortic arch:** Procure entire arch; divide innominate vein (if not needed), ligamentum, brachiocephalic vessels before cross‑clamping ([Fig. 64.1](#fig-64-1)).

### Surgical Procedure – Recipient
* **Re‑entry sternotomy** common (dilated hearts, previous operations). Preoperative MRI/CT for planning. Establish peripheral CPB access before skin incision.
* **Dissection** meticulous; plan for arterial/venous cannulation sites.

### Abnormal Systemic/Pulmonary Venous Return
* **Isolated left SVC:** Anastomose donor innominate vein to left SVC ([Fig. 64.2](#fig-64-2)).
* **Bilateral SVC:** Use donor internal jugular and innominate vein ([Fig. 64.3](#fig-64-3)).
* **Isolated left IVC:** Leave generous recipient right atrial cuff attached to IVC; mobilise toward midline.
* **Abnormal hepatic veins:** Use common atrial patch containing IVC and hepatic veins.
* **Extracardiac anomalous pulmonary venous return:** Fillet recipient pulmonary venous confluence; modify donor left atriotomy to match; anastomose.

### Atrial Situs Abnormalities (Situs Inversus)
* **Key principle:** Position all anastomoses toward midline. Mobilise malpositioned aorta to donor aorta.
* **Donor PA to undersurface of recipient branch PA** (oversew recipient main PA, open branch PA undersurface longitudinally).
* **Left SVC and left IVC** reconstructed as in isolated cases ([Fig. 64.4](#fig-64-4)).
* **Pericardial release** (avoid phrenic nerves) to accommodate normal donor levocardia.

### Great Artery Abnormalities
* Similar to situs inversus: mobilise malpositioned aorta; donor PA to undersurface of recipient branch PA.
* **Take down Lecompte maneuver:** Divide pulmonary and aortic trunks; relocate PA to normal position; use donor PA for reconstruction.

### Pulmonary Artery Abnormalities
* **Options:** Donor PA patch arterioplasty on intrapericardial PA; or replace entire intrapericardial PA with donor branch PAs (procure hilum to hilum) ([Fig. 64.5a](#fig-64-5a)).
* **If branch PAs not available** (lung procurement), use onlay patch arterioplasty ([Fig. 64.5b](#fig-64-5b)).

### Aortic Arch Abnormalities (including HLHS)
* **Neonates with HLHS:** CPB via pulmonary artery cannulation, systemic perfusion through duct. Tourniquet on duct or branch PAs to control run‑off.
* **Arch reconstruction:** Short circulatory arrest or antegrade cerebral perfusion (carotid cannula or temporary shunt to innominate). Generous incision from ascending aorta across arch into descending aorta (beyond first intercostal). Reconstruct undersurface with donor aortic arch tongue ([Fig. 64.6](#fig-64-6)).
* **Less severe hypoplasia/coarctation/interruption:** Modifications of above technique.

### Ventricular Orientation Abnormalities
* Unilateral or bilateral pericardial release to create space for cardiac apex. Identify and avoid phrenic nerves.

### Previous Operations (Fontan, Glenn, Atrial/Arterial Switch, etc.)
* **Highly challenging.** Combine multiple strategies. Preoperative imaging essential.
* **Example – failed Fontan after HLHS palliation:** Resect most intrapericardial structures; prepare cuffs for SVC, IVC, pulmonary venous confluence, branch PAs, reconstructed aorta ([Fig. 64.7](#fig-64-7)).
* **Anastomotic sequence:** left atrium → IVC → right and left branch PAs → aorta → SVC.
* CPB with aortic and bicaval cannulation (circulatory arrest optional).

### Hemostasis
* **Post‑bypass coagulopathy common.** Aggressive blood product resuscitation. Antifibrinolytics (aminocaproic acid) and recombinant factor VII. “Multi‑surgeon” approach helpful.

### Conclusion
* Successful transplantation for congenital heart disease requires comprehensive knowledge of these technical strategies, communication, planning, and teamwork.

## BACKGROUND</summary><div class="details-content">

Excellent results can be expected after cardiac transplantation for children and adults with congenital heart disease. In order to achieve these excellent results in recipients with complex cardiac anatomy, the surgical team must have a variety of specific technical strategies available in their armamentarium. This chapter will review the technical strategies that may be necessary for successful cardiac transplantation in those recipients.

Recipients with complex cardiac anatomy can be categorized into the following groups:

- **1.** Abnormalities of systemic or pulmonary venous return
- **2.** Abnormalities of the sidedness of the atrial chambers, or, in other words, the atrial “situs”
- **3.** Abnormalities of the relationship of the great arteries (including transposition of the great arteries, malposition of the great arteries, and previous Lecompte maneuver)
- **4.** Abnormalities of the main or branch pulmonary arteries (including hypoplasia, discontinuity, and anatomy altered by previous surgical and transcatheter interventions)
- **5.** Abnormalities of the ascending aorta and aortic arch (including hypoplasia and interruption)
- **6.** Abnormalities of the orientation of the ventricular mass (including dextrocardia, dextroversion, mesocardia, mesoversion)
- **7.** Abnormalities secondary to previous operations that have altered the pathways of systemic venous return, pulmonary venous return, and/or pulmonary arterial anatomy (including previous superior cavopulmonary anastomosis(es), previous Fontan operations, previous atrial switch operations, previous arterial switch operations, and previous double‑switch operations).

A variety of technical strategies designed to facilitate cardiac transplantation in these groups of patients are reviewed below.

## TECHNICAL STRATEGIES</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Organ procurement for the recipient with complex cardiac anatomy</summary><div class="details-content">

Planning and communication between procuring surgeon, implanting surgeon, anesthesia, and the other organ‑procuring teams is crucial. The location of the arterial line and central line in the organ donor is important. Femoral lines may be the best choice.

For recipients with a left superior vena cava (SVC), procurement includes the entire innominate vein, with parts of the left and right internal jugular veins, as necessary. The pulmonary arteries may be procured from hilum to hilum. This strategy will impact potential lung procurement.

The entire aortic arch may be procured. This dissection is enhanced by division of the donor innominate vein (if it is not needed for subsequent reconstruction), ligamentum arteriosum, as well as the brachiocephalic vessels, all prior to cross‑clamping ([Fig. 64.1](#fig-64-1)).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical procedure – recipient</summary><div class="details-content">

As always, careful induction of anesthesia is carried out with specifics related to the individual patient characteristics. Commonly these are reoperative patients with dilated hearts making re‑entry sternotomy quite challenging. Preoperative imaging with MRI or CT scan is useful and a specific plan for re‑entry is established. Prior to the initial skin incision, it is essential to ascertain what sites for arterial and venous cannulation are available and what specific method of peripheral institution of bypass is to be used, should the need arise. Thoughtful planning will optimize institution of emergent or semi‑elective cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) during re‑entry. At times, this is a critical and life‑saving process. The dissection of the recipient heart and necessary structures to be reconstructed should be carried out in a well‑planned and meticulous manner.

Frequent communication between the harvesting and implanting team is needed to keep the process coordinated. Unexpected issues with the donor or recipient need to be discussed. The timing of organ arrival should be coordinated so that donor heart cold ischemic time as well as recipient operative and CPB times are minimized.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Abnormalities of systemic or pulmonary venous return</summary><div class="details-content">

For recipients with an isolated left SVC, the donor innominate vein is anastomosed to the left SVC ([Fig. 64.2](#fig-64-2)). Similarly, for patients with bilateral SVC anastomosis, the donor internal jugular and innominate vein is used ([Fig. 64.3](#fig-64-3)). The neoaorta connection is kept short to minimize tension within the venous structures. For isolated left inferior vena cava (IVC), a generous portion of recipient right atrium is left attached to the recipient IVC. These recipient structures are mobilized toward the midline, and the extra right atrial tissue can be used to create a tension‑free connection to the donor heart. Abnormal hepatic venous drainage can be reconstructed by utilizing a common patch of atrium containing the IVC and the isolated hepatic veins using this extra tissue to create a tension‑free connection, similar to isolated left IVC.

<span id="fig-64-2"></span>![](_page_64_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 64.2** Anastomosis of donor innominate vein to recipient left SVC.

<span id="fig-64-3"></span>![](_page_64_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 64.3** Bilateral SVC reconstruction using donor internal jugular and innominate vein.

For extracardiac anomalous pulmonary venous connections, the recipient pulmonary venous confluence is filleted open. The donor left atriotomy is modified to match the recipient pulmonary venous opening. Utilizing the original pulmonary venous openings or creating new ones as needed, the recipient pulmonary venous confluence is anastomosed to the created donor left atriotomy.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Abnormalities of the sided‑ness of the atrial chambers or atrial “situs” including “situs inversus totalis”</summary><div class="details-content">

As stated by Montalvo and Bailey, the “key in situs inversus is to position all anastomoses toward the midline.” Generally, a malpositioned aorta can be mobilized and anastomosed to the normally related donor aorta. The donor pulmonary artery can be anastomosed to the undersurface of the recipient left or right pulmonary artery as needed. If this technique is used, the main pulmonary artery of the recipient is oversewn and the undersurface of the branch pulmonary arteries of the recipient is opened longitudinally to receive the donor main pulmonary artery. The left SVC and left IVC are reconstructed as they are when found in isolated cases ([Fig. 64.4](#fig-64-4)). Generous opening of the pericardium, avoiding the phrenic nerve, is often helpful to accommodate the normal donor levocardic orientation.

<span id="fig-64-4"></span>![](_page_64_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 64.4** Reconstruction for situs inversus: anastomoses toward midline.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Abnormalities of the relationship of the great arteries (including transposition of the great arteries, malposition of the great arteries, and previous Lecompte maneuver)</summary><div class="details-content">

With abnormal relationships of the great arteries, reconstruction is similar to situs inversus. Generally, a malpositioned aorta can be mobilized and anastomosed to the normally related donor aorta. The donor pulmonary artery can be anastomosed to the undersurface of the recipient left or right pulmonary artery as needed.

Taking down a Lecompte maneuver can be accomplished by dividing the pulmonary and aortic trunks and relocating the pulmonary artery back to its normal position. Donor pulmonary artery can be used to facilitate the reconstruction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Abnormalities of the main or branch pulmonary arteries (including hypoplasia, discontinuity, and anatomy altered by previous surgical and transcatheter interventions)</summary><div class="details-content">

The technique of recipient pulmonary arterial reconstruction can vary from using the donor pulmonary artery to perform patch arterioplasty on a segment of the intrapericardial portion of the pulmonary artery to replacing the entire recipient intrapericardial pulmonary artery with donor branch pulmonary arteries. When the entire intrapericardial pulmonary arterial branches need replacement or reconstruction, it is usually best accomplished by procuring the donor pulmonary branches from hilum to hilum ([Fig. 64.5a](#fig-64-5a)). This requires careful planning and discussions with all involved so that competition for the intrapericardial pulmonary artery by a lung procurement team will not be an issue. When simultaneous lung procurement results in the non‑availability of branch pulmonary arteries for the cardiac transplant team, an alternative method such as onlay patching of the branch pulmonary arteries can be utilized ([Fig. 64.5b](#fig-64-5b)).

### Abnormalities of the ascending aorta and aortic arch (including hypoplastic left heart syndrome, hypoplasia, and interruption)</summary><div class="details-content">

In neonates with hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS), a diminutive ascending aorta requires establishing CPB with pulmonary arterial cannulation and systemic perfusion through duct. Pulmonary arterial run‑off is controlled with a tourniquet around the duct or the branch pulmonary arteries.

Arch reconstruction can be performed with a short period of circulatory arrest or with continuous antegrade cerebral perfusion (either via a cannula advanced into a carotid artery or via a temporary shunt sewn to the innominate artery). A generous incision in the recipient ascending aorta extends across the transverse arch and down into the descending aorta. This incision extends distally to beyond the recipient’s first intercostal artery takeoff, to minimize ductal constriction and subsequent coarctation. The undersurface of the aortic arch is reconstructed with a generous tongue of donor aorta arch ([Fig. 64.6](#fig-64-6)). Patients with less severe forms of aortic hypoplasia, coarctation and interruption are reconstructed with modifications of the above technique as needed.

<span id="fig-64-6"></span>![](_page_64_Picture_6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 64.6** Arch reconstruction with donor aortic arch tongue.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Abnormalities of the orientation of the ventricular mass (including dextrocardia, dextroversion, mesocardia, mesoversion)</summary><div class="details-content">

Unilateral or bilateral pericardial release will create space for the cardiac mass and cardiac apex. Great care must be taken to identify and avoid the phrenic nerve(s).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Abnormalities secondary to previous operations that have altered the pathways of systemic venous return, pulmonary venous return, and/or pulmonary arterial anatomy (including previous superior cavopulmonary anastomosis(es), previous Fontan operations, previous atrial switch operations, previous arterial switch operations, and previous double‑switch operations)</summary><div class="details-content">

These cases are very challenging in several domains. Many of the technical strategies mentioned above may be necessary in various combinations. An accurate understanding of the preoperative anatomy is very important. A creative and complex reconstruction will be planned specific to the recipient preoperative anatomy. This plan will determine the specific procurement needs regarding extra tissue to be harvested to yield a successful complex implantation. A typical example is the failed Fontan patient following staged palliation for hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS). Usually, most of the intrapericardial structures are resected and cuffs of tissue are prepared near the pericardial reflection for the SVC, IVC, pulmonary venous confluence, pulmonary arteries just proximal to their branch points, and the previously reconstructed aorta ([Fig. 64.7](#fig-64-7)). The implantation would be on CPB with aortic arterial and bicaval venous cannulation, although circulatory arrest can be utilized if needed or preferred. The sequence of anastomosis would be as follows:

- **1.** left atrium
- **2.** IVC
- **3.** right and left branch pulmonary arteries
- **4.** aorta
- **5.** SVC.

**FIGURE 64.7** Prepared recipient cuffs for failed Fontan (SVC, IVC, pulmonary venous confluence, branch PAs, aorta).

## HEMOSTASIS</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative hemostasis can be very challenging. The age‑old adage of “dry in – dry out” is useful but commonly post‑bypass coagulopathy and hemorrhage are very problematic. Serious coagulopathies are frequently encountered and aggressive blood product resuscitation is essential. Liberal use of adjuncts such as antifibrinolytics (aminocaproic acid) and recombinant factor seven is encouraged. Often these are very lengthy and difficult procedures performed in the middle of the night. A team or “multi‑surgeon” approach can be very helpful.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SUMMARY</summary><div class="details-content">

A variety of technical strategies are available to facilitate cardiac transplantation for the recipient with congenital heart disease. A comprehensive working knowledge of these techniques is necessary in order to achieve optimal outcomes. Communication, planning, and excellent teamwork are critical in treating these challenging patients.
