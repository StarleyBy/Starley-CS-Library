# Congenital mitral valve repair

##### This chapter describes the surgical management of congenital mitral valve malformations, including Carpentier's pathophysiologic classification, indications for repair, operative techniques for various types of mitral stenosis and regurgitation (commissurotomy, papillary muscle splitting, supravalvar ring resection, cleft closure, elongated chordae repair, parachute valve, hammock valve, mitral arcade, leaflet extension, etc.), intraoperative assessment, postoperative care, and outcomes.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Pathophysiology and Classification
* **Congenital mitral valve (MV) malformations:** Wide spectrum; repair preferred over replacement in children to avoid prosthetic complications.
* **Carpentier classification (1976):** Groups lesions by function – regurgitation (prevalent) vs stenosis (prevalent). Regurgitation subdivided by leaflet motion (Type I normal, Type II prolapse, Type III restricted). Stenosis subdivided by papillary muscle (PM) anatomy (Type A normal PMs, Type B abnormal PMs) ([Table 67.1](#table-67-1)).
* **Mitruka–Lamberti classification (2000):** Topographic – supravalvar, valvar, subvalvar, mixed ([Table 67.2](#table-67-2)). Limitation: many lesions fall into “mixed” category.

### Indications for Repair
* **Symptoms:** Dyspnoea, failure to thrive, cardiogenic shock. Stenotic valves present earlier than incompetent valves.
* **Timing:** Defer surgery if possible (atrioventricular valve development continues in first months). Interim medical therapy.
* **Goal:** “Physiologic repair” rather than anatomic perfection; improve function, defer valve replacement to older age.

### Operative Principles
* **Approach:** Transseptal preferred; everting pledgeted sutures at annulus for exposure ([Fig. 67.1](#fig-67-1)). Hypothermic circulatory arrest occasionally for complex malformations.
* **Intraoperative imaging:** Transesophageal or epicardial 2D/3D echocardiography essential for planning and post‑repair assessment.

### Mitral Stenosis – Type A (Normal Papillary Muscles)

**Commissure fusion/short chordae**
* **Pathology:** PMs implanted directly onto commissure with absent/short chordae; fused, thickened commissures; restricted leaflet motion ([Fig. 67.2](#fig-67-2)).
* **Repair:** Commissurotomy ([Fig. 67.3](#fig-67-3)), splitting of PMs ([Fig. 67.4](#fig-67-4)), PM fenestration in older children ([Fig. 67.5](#fig-67-5)).

**Supravalvar ring**
* **Pathology:** Fibrous circumferential ring above MV annulus ([Fig. 67.6](#fig-67-6)). Often associated with subvalvar malformations.
* **Repair:** Complete ring resection; address associated subvalvar lesions ([Figs. 67.7a,b](#fig-67-7a)).

**Excess valvar tissue**
* **Pathology:** Interchordal tissue impinging on chordae ([Fig. 67.8](#fig-67-8)).
* **Repair:** Excision of excess tissue ([Fig. 67.9](#fig-67-9)); may combine with PM splitting.

### Mitral Stenosis – Type B (Abnormal Papillary Muscles)

**Parachute mitral valve**
* **Pathology:** All chordae attach to single PM ([Fig. 67.10](#fig-67-10)). Often with supravalvar ring.
* **Repair:** Split the PM bearing chordae down to ventricular wall ([Fig. 67.11](#fig-67-11)); avoid chordal detachment.

**Hammock valve / mitral arcade**
* **Pathology:** Absent first‑degree chordae; short second‑degree chordae attach leaflets directly to PMs. Hammock: two large cylinder‑like PMs ([Fig. 67.12](#fig-67-12)). Arcade: fused PMs creating “muscle arcade” ([Fig. 67.13](#fig-67-13)).
* **Repair:** Split both PMs (hammock, [Fig. 67.14](#fig-67-14)) or longitudinally split muscle arcade (arcade, [Fig. 67.15](#fig-67-15)); free leaflets anchored by short chordae.

### Mitral Regurgitation – Type I (Normal Leaflet Motion)

**Annular dilatation**
* **Pathology:** Rare isolated; usually secondary to other lesions ([Fig. 67.16](#fig-67-16)).
* **Repair:** Posterior annular plication (Kay technique, [Fig. 67.17](#fig-67-17)). **Avoid prosthetic rings** in children (limit growth, scar tissue).

**Cleft leaflet**
* **Pathology:** Normally formed MV but vertical cleft in anterior leaflet ([Fig. 67.18](#fig-67-18)). Not to be confused with AV canal cleft.
* **Repair:** Interrupted fine sutures (single or figure‑eight) to close cleft ([Fig. 67.19](#fig-67-19)); often combined with annular plication.

**Leaflet defect**
* **Pathology:** Rare; usually posterior MV leaflet ([Figs. 67.20, 67.21](#fig-67-20)).
* **Repair:** Direct suture or patch closure ([Figs. 67.22, 67.23](#fig-67-22)); may combine with hemi‑annular posterior ring plication.

### Mitral Regurgitation – Type II (Leaflet Prolapse)

**Elongated chordae**
* **Pathology:** Congenital elongation of chordae ([Fig. 67.24](#fig-67-24)); differentiate from acquired myxoid degeneration or ALCAPA.
* **Repair:** Create groove in PM, bury excess chordal length with suture ([Figs. 67.25a,b](#fig-67-25a)). Alternative: longitudinal suture anchored to PM ([Figs. 67.26a,b](#fig-67-26a)). Artificial chordae also used ([Fig. 67.27](#fig-67-27)).

### Mitral Regurgitation – Type III (Restricted Leaflet Motion)

**Papillary muscle hypoplasia**
* **Pathology:** Small PMs cause poor leaflet coaptation ([Fig. 67.28](#fig-67-28)).
* **Repair:** Posterior leaflet extension with biological tissue ([Figs. 67.29, 67.30](#fig-67-29)), incise PMs down to ventricular wall, posterior annulus plication if dilated.

**Parachute MV with incompetence**
* **Pathology:** Associated with stenosis; regurgitation due to anterior leaflet hypoplasia near commissure (“cleft commissure”).
* **Repair:** Extend anterior leaflet with biological tissue ([Figs. 67.31a–c](#fig-67-31a)); may add posterior annuloplasty.

**Hammock valve / mitral arcade (regurgitant form)**
* Splitting of PMs or muscle arcade improves leaflet excursion.

### Intraoperative Testing and Postoperative Care
* **Hydrodynamic test:** Inject cold saline into LV; small leaks accepted if coaptation satisfactory (perfect competence difficult in small children).
* **Post‑bypass echocardiography (TEE/epicardial):** Assess leaflet motion, coaptation, residual regurgitation (PISA method), stenosis (Doppler).
* **Left atrial line** inserted; repeat echo before extubation. Pulmonary artery line if preoperative pulmonary hypertension.

### Outcome
* MV repair effective and reliable; results improved in recent years.
* Minimal repair often sufficient for very dysplastic valves in early infancy; may perform adequately for years.
* Parachute MV has worse outcomes and higher early mortality.
* Perioperative mortality increased with associated major anomalies (LVOT obstruction, VSD, impaired LV function). Durability of repair independent of associated anomalies.

## HISTORY AND CLASSIFICATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical management of mitral valve (MV) dysplasia remains a therapeutic challenge due to the wide spectrum of different valve malformations, and the high incidence of associated cardiac anomalies in a context of a relatively rare congenital heart disease (CHD).

Congenital MV repair, rather than replacement, has been advocated for many years, mostly because of the undesired deleterious effects of prosthetic valve replacement, particularly in small children.

That said, there is still no optimal classification to fulfil surgical criteria. Using a pathophysiologic classification, Alain Carpentier, in 1976 ([Table 67.1](#table-67-1)), divided the MV anomalies into two functional groups: *regurgitation* (or prevalent regurgitation) and *stenosis* (or prevalent stenosis), although it is uncommon that a dysplastic MV shows isolated signs of stenosis or incompetence alone. Stenotic MVs were further divided into two types according to the anatomy of the papillary muscles (PMs) ([Table 67.1](#table-67-1)). Regurgitant MVs were further divided into three types, according to the leaflets’ motion (normal, restricted, or prolapsed).

More recently, Mitruka and Lamberti ([Table 67.2](#table-67-2)) proposed a simpler, albeit less detailed, anatomic classification dividing the whole spectrum of malformations topically into supravalvar, valvar, subvalvar, and mixed lesions. A limitation of such a classification, however, is that many MV lesions fall into the “mixed lesions” category and therefore remain without any specific topic classification.

Despite the availability of more sophisticated preoperative diagnostic tools today than were around at the time of Carpentier’s classification, which was based on surgical observation, the preoperative and intraoperative MV classification can still sometimes be complicated, especially when more than one MV anomaly is present.

With the aid of Carpentier’s classification, the most common congenital MV malformations are discussed here, together with the related surgical treatment.

## INDICATIONS FOR CONGENITAL MITRAL VALVE REPAIR</summary><div class="details-content">

Indications for MV repair are usually dictated by symptoms. Neonates and young infants can present with severe symptoms. Surgical indication can also be dictated by the association with other congenital cardiac anomalies. In symptomatic neonates and young infants, when possible, interim medical therapy can be preferred to an early surgical repair; it must be remembered that atrioventricular valve development continues into the first few months of life. MV surgery should be deferred as long as the patient’s condition permits clinical management, to enhance the chances for a more successful and satisfactory repair later.

The most common symptoms in small children are dyspnea and failure to thrive. Some patients can be admitted on an emergency basis in cardiogenic shock, requiring mechanical ventilation and inotropic support. Bigger children can be admitted for valve repair while still asymptomatic. Stenotic (or prevalent stenotic) valves frequently show onset of early symptoms when compared to incompetent (or prevalent incompetent) valves. Realistically, congenital MV repair, when performed on severely malformed MVs in infancy, remains a palliative temporary treatment, the main aim of surgery being to obtain an improvement of the MV function, which would ameliorate the clinical status and allow deferral of eventual MV replacement to an older age. MV reconstruction needs to be carefully planned and tailored for each patient with the aim of respecting the original conformation of the valve to achieve a “physiologic repair” rather than an “anatomic one.”

## OPERATION</summary><div class="details-content">

As a general rule, each operation must be tailored to the peculiar anatomy of each type of congenital MV dysplasia. Regardless of the anatomic and physiologic severity of the MV malformation(s), reconstructive surgery at any age should always be attempted with the aim of avoiding the high incidence of early and late complications, as well as the high mortality and reoperation rate which are related to prosthetic valve replacement, in the pediatric age group.

Intraoperative transesophageal or epicardial 2D and 3D echocardiography is essential for planning the best surgical strategy and for checking the performance of the MV immediately after repair.

The surgical approach is similar to that described in [Chapter 16](#page--1-0), “Mitral valve repair,” for adult patients with acquired MV disease. A transseptal approach is always preferred, especially in small children. Four everting pledgeted stitches can be placed at the annulus site ([Fig. 67.1](#fig-67-1)) to enhance surgical exposure. A hypothermic circulatory arrest is occasionally employed, particularly when dealing with complex malformations, in order to gain a completely bloodless field and to make a thorough MV inspection before planning the detailed surgical strategy.

### MITRAL VALVE STENOSIS</summary><div class="details-content">

#### Type A (normal papillary muscles)</summary><div class="details-content">

**COMMISSURE FUSION/SHORT CHORDAE**

This is a common complex MV malformation. As described by Carpentier et al., one or two PMs are directly implanted onto the commissure without any (or with very small) chordae tendinae. The commissures are fused and thickened; the motion of the leaflets is restricted ([Fig. 67.2](#fig-67-2)). The functional MV orifice is often very stenotic. The anatomical MV annulus can be normal (type 3 Mitruka–Lamberti).

<span id="fig-67-2"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.2** Commissure fusion and short chordae with restricted leaflet motion.

This condition is treated by commissurotomy ([Fig. 67.3](#fig-67-3)) and splitting of the papillary muscles ([Fig. 67.4](#fig-67-4)). PM fenestration ([Fig. 67.5](#fig-67-5)) with the aim of enhancing the leaflets’ motion is also possible in older children.

<span id="fig-67-3"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.3** Commissurotomy.

<span id="fig-67-4"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.4** Splitting of papillary muscles.

<span id="fig-67-5"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_5.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.5** Papillary muscle fenestration.

**SUPRAVALVAR RING**

A supravalvar ring is seldom found as an isolated form and very often it reflects a complex malformation of the whole subvalvar apparatus. It consists of a fibrous circumferential ring of different size which lies just above the MV annulus (and often fused with it), creating an obstruction at supravalvar level (type A, Mitruka–Lamberti classification) ([Fig. 67.6](#fig-67-6)). When small, it can be an occasional intraoperative finding.

<span id="fig-67-6"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_6.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.6** Supravalvar ring.

Surgery consists of a complete resection of the ring, often including other maneuvers in the subvalvular area to treat associated lesions ([Figs. 67.7a and 67.7b](#fig-67-7a)).

<span id="fig-67-7a"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_7a.jpeg)

<span id="fig-67-7b"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_7b.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.7b** Completed resection.

**EXCESS VALVAR TISSUE**

In some cases excess interchordal tissue is found to be impinging on the proper chordae motion ([Fig. 67.8](#fig-67-8)), often in association with other MV anomalies. Treatment consists of resection of the excess tissue with the aim of freeing the chordae operatus ([Fig. 67.9](#fig-67-9)). A splitting of one or both PMs can be performed in association with the resection (see [Fig. 67.4](#fig-67-4)).

#### Type B (abnormal papillary muscles)</summary><div class="details-content">

**PARACHUTE MITRAL VALVE**

Parachute mitral valve is a rather common malformation. All the chordae tendinae are attached to a single PM ([Fig. 67.10](#fig-67-10)). When two PMs are present, only one is receiving chordal attachment. This lesion can often be found in association with other MV malformations, most commonly a supravalvar ring.

<span id="fig-67-10"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_10.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.10** Parachute mitral valve – all chordae to single papillary muscle.

Surgery consists of a splitting of the PM‑bearing chordae, down to the ventricular wall ([Fig. 67.11](#fig-67-11)). Care must be taken not to disrupt the chordal apparatus or weaken the PM with consequent possible chordal detachment.

<span id="fig-67-11"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.11** Splitting of the papillary muscle in parachute valve.

**HAMMOCK VALVE/MITRAL ARCADE**

These complex lesions are similar in that both are characterized by the presence of abnormal PMs in conjunction with the absence of first‑degree chordae. Short second‑degree chordae tendinae directly attach the MV leaflets to the PMs.

In the Hammock valve, two large cylinder‑like PMs are present, impinging on the inlet to the ventricle ([Fig. 67.12](#fig-67-12)). In the mitral arcade, there is a fusion of both PMs creating a “muscle arcade” where small chordae attach ([Fig. 67.13](#fig-67-13)). Other MV malformations are often found in association i.e. commissures fusion and supravalvar ring.

<span id="fig-67-12"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.12** Hammock valve – two large cylinder‑like papillary muscles.

<span id="fig-67-13"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.13** Mitral arcade – fused papillary muscles creating muscle arcade.

Surgical repair is similar in the two malformations, consisting of a splitting of both PMs in Hammock valve ([Fig. 67.14](#fig-67-14)) or of a longitudinal splitting of the “muscle arcade” in mitral arcade ([Fig. 67.15](#fig-67-15)). In both procedures the aim is to free the leaflets which are anchored to the PMs by the short chordae.

### MITRAL VALVE REGURGITATION</summary><div class="details-content">

Carpentier classifies these lesions according to the leaflet motion: normal, prolapsed, or restricted.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Type I (normal leaflet motion)</summary><div class="details-content">

**ANNULAR DILATATION**

A pure annular dilatation is seldom found as an isolated form of MV deformity. It is often the result of other associated malformations causing MV regurgitation and ventricular dilatation ([Fig. 67.16](#fig-67-16)).

<span id="fig-67-16"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_16.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.16** Annular dilatation.

Surgery consists of a posterior annular plication, as described by Kay in 1977 ([Fig. 67.17](#fig-67-17)). No prosthetic rings, including the open one, should be used in children, due to the scar tissue caused by the sutures and the ring itself, which can limit proper annulus growth and leaflet motion.

<span id="fig-67-17"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_17.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.17** Posterior annular plication (Kay technique).

**CLEFT LEAFLET**

Cleft leaflet should not be confused with the so‑called “cleft” which is found in association with an atrioventricular canal defect. It usually involves the anterior MV leaflet. The MV apparatus is usually normal with the exception of a vertical cleft, which separates the anterior leaflet into two hemileaflets ([Fig. 67.18](#fig-67-18)). Abnormal small chordae can be attached to the free margins of the cleft. The edges of the cleft can often be thickened, as a result of the turbulence created by the regurgitation at that point.

<span id="fig-67-18"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_18.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.18** Cleft anterior leaflet.

Surgery consists of total closure of the cleft with interrupted fine stitches (single or figure‑eight) with particular care taken not to distort the MV anterior leaflet and reduce the coaptation with the posterior one ([Fig. 67.19](#fig-67-19)). An annular plication is often performed in association with this.

<span id="fig-67-19"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_19.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.19** Cleft closure with interrupted sutures.

**LEAFLET DEFECT**

This is quite a rare malformation which most commonly involves the posterior MV leaflet ([Figs. 67.20 and 67.21](#fig-67-20)). Repair is usually undertaken by a direct suture or patch closure of the gap ([Figs. 67.22 and 67.23](#fig-67-22)), often associated with a hemi‑annular posterior ring plication.

#### Type II (leaflet prolapse)</summary><div class="details-content">

**ELONGATED CHORDAE**

Elongated chordae can be found in any of the PMs and also in association with other MV malformations ([Fig. 67.24](#fig-67-24)). Congenital forms need to be differentiated from other acquired forms of myxoid degeneration of the whole valve apparatus or forms found in association with anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the main pulmonary artery where the PMs are infarcted and elongated.

<span id="fig-67-24"></span>![](_page_67_Picture_24.jpeg)

**FIGURE 67.24** Elongated chordae.

Elongated chordae are treated as described by Carpentier, by creating a groove in the PM, next to the elongated chorda, and by encircling the chordae with a suture, imbricating them into the groove ([Figs. 67.25a and 67.25b](#fig-67-25a)). Tying of the suture buries the excessive length of the chordae into the groove, which is subsequently closed. This maneuver may be difficult in small children, and therefore a fine, longitudinal suture can be passed over each chorda and anchored onto the PM ([Figs. 67.26a and 67.26b](#fig-67-26a)).

#### Type III (restricted leaflet motion)</summary><div class="details-content">

This is a very rare condition with normal PMs. It is often confused with forms with associated valve stenosis (i.e. commissure fusion/short chordae).

**PAPILLARY MUSCLE HYPOPLASIA**

Chordae tendinae are attached to small PMs ([Fig. 67.28](#fig-67-28)). The mechanism of valve regurgitation is due to a lack of leaflet coaptation. Surgical treatment often includes a posterior leaflet extension (biological tissue is usually employed) in order to favor better leaflet coaptation ([Figs. 67.29 and 67.30](#fig-67-29)). The incision of the small PMs down to the ventricular wall can also be performed. When annular dilatation is present in association with PM hypoplasia, an annulus remodeling can also be performed by a posterior plication (or hemiplication).

**PARACHUTE MITRAL VALVE (incompetent form)**

An incompetent parachute MV can be found in association with signs of MV stenosis (see “Mitral valve stenosis – parachute mitral valve”). The regurgitation is usually due to the hypoplasia of the anterior leaflet near the commissure, usually the anterior one, a malformation also called “cleft commissure” (Carpentier classification). The anterior leaflet can be extended with biological autologous or heterologous tissue ([Figs. 67.31a–c](#fig-67-31a)). An annuloplasty (or partial annuloplasty) of the posterior leaflet can also be performed in association.

**FIGURES 67.31a–c** Anterior leaflet extension with biological tissue.

**HAMMOCK VALVE/MITRAL ARCADE (regurgitant form)**

Both malformations can also present with signs of regurgitation due to the restricted leaflet motion. A splitting of the PMs or the muscle arcade usually produces a better leaflet excursion.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Testing the results of the repair</summary><div class="details-content">

As for acquired lesions, the result of MV reconstruction is tested intraoperatively by injecting cold saline solution into the left ventricular cavity. During this maneuver, care must be taken not to distort the MV annulus with stay stitches or retractors on the annulus during the injection. This test is important for assessing a proper closure of the MV leaflets after repair of incompetent MVs but also after repair of stenotic MV lesions, where reconstructing maneuvers might have caused MV damage.

Small MV leakages are usually accepted in the presence of a satisfactory leaflets coaptation. In small children, in contrast to adult patients, a perfect MV competence at the hydrodynamic test is often difficult to achieve.

At the end of the cardiopulmonary bypass, MV performance is tested again by means of transesophageal or epicardial echocardiography interrogation. Four‑ and two‑chamber and long‑axis views are used for assessing MV anatomy and function, particularly leaflet movements and coaptation and also the anatomy and function of the whole subvalvar apparatus (chordae tendinae and PMs).

With the aid of color Doppler modality flow, after careful adjustment of the color settings, the presence and direction of regurgitation can be detected and the effective regurgitation orifice area measured with the proximal isovelocity surface area (PISA) method. With the use of spectral Doppler, the presence of residual stenosis or regurgitation is tested further, with the measurement of the velocity of the filling pattern of the left ventricle. By rotating the probe at “commissural view” at 90 degrees and 120 degrees, the same interrogation is repeated, as previously described.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## POSTOPERATIVE CARE</summary><div class="details-content">

A left atrial line is often inserted either through the intra‑atrial groove or through the left atrial appendix. Valve performance is also checked again by 2D echocardiography in the intensive care unit before removing the left atrial line and weaning the patient off the ventilator. In the presence of preoperative pulmonary artery hypertension, a pulmonary artery line is often inserted through the main pulmonary artery or through the infundibulum.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## OUTCOME</summary><div class="details-content">

Recent series have shown that MV repair in infants and children is an effective and reliable treatment with acceptable mortality and success rates, which have improved considerably in the last few years. MV reconstruction needs to be planned in detail, for each malformation, on the basis of information received preoperatively by 2D and 3D echocardiography. Such information is extremely important for the surgeon, for planning in advance the ideal surgical strategy and at the time of repair. In very dysplastic valves, it must be borne in mind that minimal reconstructing maneuvers are often sufficient to improve MV performance. It has been found that very dysplastic valves which were treated “minimally” in early infancy can still perform adequately after several years.

MV repair must always be attempted as a first line of treatment, especially in infants, despite the frequent severity of MVs dysplasia, to avoid the drawbacks of the currently available prosthesis. It has also been found that anatomical severity of MV malformations influences the long‑term freedom from MV replacement. Parachute MVs tend to have worse outcomes and are associated with a high rate of early mortality.

There is an increased risk for perioperative mortality when major cardiac anomalies, such as left outflow tract obstruction, ventricular septal defect, or impaired preoperative ventricular function, are present in association with MV malformations. Nevertheless, the durability of valve repair appears to be independent of the presence of associated cardiac anomalies.
