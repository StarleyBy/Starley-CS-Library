### **SECTION I: TAPVC IN BIVENTRICULAR MORPHOLOGY**

### **Definition and Classification**

Total (totally) anomalous pulmonary venous connection (TAPVC) is a cardiac malformation in which there is no direct connection between any pulmonary vein and the left atrium.

In TAPVC, all the pulmonary veins connect to the right atrium or one of its tributaries. Although not part of the malformation, a patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect is present in essentially all persons with TAPVC and is necessary for survival after birth. TAPVCs are classified according to their drainage into four types: supracardiac (type 1), cardiac (type 2), infracardiac (type 3), and mixed (type 4) (Fig. 30.1).

This chapter concerns TAPVC in hearts with concordant atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial connections without other major cardiac anomalies except patent ductus arteriosus. TAPVC can occur in hearts with a wide range of other cardiac anomalies, ranging from ventricular septal defect to tetralogy of Fallot to functional single ventricle (FSV). TAPVC in hearts with atrial isomerism is considered in Chapter 53.

### **Historical Note**

TAPVC was apparently first described by Wilson in 1798.1 He described an unusual formation of the human heart, comprising a trunk of two large veins that came from the lungs and joined the superior caval vein. In 1951, Muller, while at the University of California Medical Center in Los Angeles, reported the first successful surgical approach.2 His correction was partial, achieved by anastomosing the common pulmonary vein to the left atrial appendage using a closed technique. In 1956, Lewis, Varco, and colleagues3 at the University of Minnesota reported successful open repair of this malformation, using moderate hypothermia induced by surface cooling and temporary occlusion of venous inflow to the heart. The same year, Burroughs and Kirklin reported successful repair of TAPVC using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).4 Their report also described a successful operation several years earlier using the atrial well technique of Gross and colleagues.5 Subsequently, it became apparent that mortality in infants following repair of TAPVC using CPB was strikingly higher than in older patients, but attempts to improve results by staged operation or palliative measures were generally unsuccessful.6-10 Success was reported from time to time, however, even for critically ill infants with infracardiac connection.11 Eventually, improvement in intraoperative techniques substantially improved results in infants. In 1967, Dillard and colleagues achieved good results using hypothermic circulatory arrest without CPB,12 and in 1971, Malm, Gersony, and colleagues reported success in a small group of young infants using standard normothermic CPB.13 Hypothermic circulatory arrest and limited CPB were used in 1969 with strikingly improved results.14,15 However, refinements in intraoperative techniques developed over the last 3 decades now allow excellent outcomes using continuous CPB. The latest addition to the armamentarium in the treatment and palliation of TAPVC is balloon dilation or stent placement in an obstructed vertical vein. This has been described as both a therapeutic strategy or for urgent preoperative stabilization of critically ill infants.16-19

### **Morphology**

### **Pulmonary Venous Anatomy**

TAPVC is supracardiac (type 1) in about 45% of cases, cardiac (type 2) in about 25%, infracardiac (type 3) in about 25%, and mixed (type 4) in about 5% to 10% (see Fig. 30.1).20-24 The connection in *supracardiac TAPVC* is usually to a left vertical vein draining into the left brachiocephalic vein, less often to the superior vena cava, usually at its junction with the right atrium, and rarely to the azygos vein. In *cardiac TAPVC,* the connection is usually to the coronary sinus and less often to the right atrium directly. Connection to the supradiaphragmatic inferior vena cava also has been reported.25 The most common sites of connection in patients with *infracardiac (infradiaphragmatic) TAPVC* are the portal vein (65% of cases, according to Duff and colleagues26) and ductus venosus; less common are the gastric vein, right or left hepatic vein, and inferior vena cava. Uncommonly, the pulmonary venous drainage may be through two connections.27 Also, part of the pulmonary venous drainage may be to one site and part to another in what is termed *mixed TAPVC*. At least 15 different morphologic mixed variants have been identified. In the most common form, the left upper lobe of the lung drains to a left vertical vein, and the remainder of both lungs drains to the coronary sinus. In the next most common form, the right lung drains to the coronary sinus, and the left lung drains to a vertical vein.22 Chowdhury and colleagues have categorized this wide assortment of mixed TAPVC into three general groups: the 212 pattern, the 311 pattern, and the bizarre pattern.28

No matter what the final connection or termination may be, individual right and left pulmonary veins usually converge to form a *common pulmonary vein*, which in turn connects to the systemic venous system in one of the ways noted earlier. It is usually posterior to the pericardium. Its long axis is usually oriented transversely, with the pulmonary veins of the left lung converging to form its left extremity and those from the right lung to form its right extremity. When the drainage is infracardiac, the right and left pulmonary veins slope downward to converge into a vertical common pulmonary vein, with the entire arrangement having a Y, T, or tree shape.29-31 Rarely, there are two vertical veins that are not confluent until below the diaphragm.32 Two vertical veins have also been identified in supracardiac TAPVC.33 A common pulmonary vein may be absent in some cases with cardiac or mixed connections. Its apparent absence in some patients may be an illusion attributable to a defect in the anterior wall of the common pulmonary vein. That defect is the orifice connecting it to the coronary sinus or right atrium.

Pulmonary venous obstruction (PVO) is a severe associated condition usually resulting from a stenosis involving the vein connecting the common pulmonary vein to the systemic venous system (e.g., an obstructed course of the vertical vein). Severe obstruction may be caused by the so-called vascular vice, in which the left vertical vein passes posterior rather than anterior to the left pulmonary artery and is compressed between it and the left main bronchus.34A localized stenosis may also occur at the junction of the left vertical vein with either the left brachiocephalic vein or the common pulmonary vein, or at the junction of a connecting vein that joins the superior vena cava.

### **Cardiac Chamber and Septal Anatomy**

For survival after birth, communication between systemic and pulmonary circulations must exist. Nearly always, an atrial septal defect or patent foramen ovale is present. However, in the review of Delisle and colleagues, one of 93 autopsy cases was an 11-yearold with an intact atrial septum and multiple ventricular septal defects, and Hastreiter and colleagues reported a 6-week-old patient with TAPVC to the ductus venosus, a patent ductus, and a closed foramen ovale. 24,41 Even though atrial communication in TAPVC is usually of adequate size and not obstructive, 6,39 restriction to systemic blood flow at the atrial level has been suggested to play a role in the early development of congestive heart failure in some patients.<sup>42</sup> The right atrium is enlarged and thick walled in patients with TAPVC, and the left atrium is abnormally small.<sup>20,43</sup> Cineangiographic studies by Mathew and colleagues have shown left atrial volume to be 53% of predicted normal, while left atrial appendage size is preserved.<sup>44</sup> The diminutive left atrium is attributed to the failure of incorporation and contribution of the embryonic pulmonary vein component during embryonic development. 45 In addition, in patients with TAPVC to the right atrium, the posterior attachment of the atrial septum is shifted to the left, so the septum lies nearer to the sagittal than the usual coronal plane. Anatomic studies have shown that the left ventricle is usually normal in size. 20,46 Haworth and Reid's quantitative study showed that inflow measurements of the left ventricle were normal in eight of nine infants dying with TAPVC.<sup>47</sup> In one infant, however, left ventricular (LV) inflow measurements were abnormally small, and weight of the free LV wall plus the septum was less than that of a normal fetus at full term. In all nine infants, LV free-wall thickness was normal. In a quantitative autopsy study of infants with TAPVC, Bove and colleagues found LV mass to be normal as well.<sup>48</sup> However, they found the LV cavity was small because of leftward displacement of the septum secondary to right ventricular (RV) pressure-volume overload. Correspondingly, Nakazawa and colleagues reported that angiographically determined LV end-diastolic volume (LVEDV) was 79% less than normal (P = .009) in a group of infants with TAPVC and severe pulmonary hypertension.<sup>49</sup> Hammon and colleagues also reported small LVEDV in infants with TAPVC.50

The right ventricle varies in size, depending on the magnitude of pulmonary blood flow, presence or absence of pulmonary venous stenosis, and the point at which anomalous pulmonary veins connect. ### **Pulmonary Vasculature**

Because most infants with TAPVC have marked pulmonary hypertension, structural changes are usually found in the lungs of even the youngest infants dying with the malformation.<sup>52</sup> Haworth and Reid demonstrated increased pulmonary arterial muscularity in all infants dying with TAPVC, including an 8-day-old neonate, as shown by increased arterial wall thickness and extension of muscle into smaller and more peripheral arteries than normal.<sup>47</sup> Vein wall thickness was increased in all but the youngest child.

In addition, when obstruction is severe, high venous pressure can lead to secondary pulmonary lymphangiectasia, which can compromise the postoperative clinical course.<sup>53</sup>

#### **Associated Conditions**

Except for atrial communication, most infants presenting with severe symptoms from TAPVC have either no associated condition or a small or large patent ductus arteriosus. Patent ductus arteriosus is present in nearly all infants coming to operation in the first few weeks of life with PVO and, overall, in about 15% of cases. Ventricular septal defects occasionally occur. However,

more than one third of cases coming to autopsy, few of which are infants, have other major associated cardiac anomalies.24 These include tetralogy of Fallot, double-outlet right ventricle, interrupted aortic arch,54 and other lesions.55 The combination of TAPVC with other major cardiac anomalies is especially likely to occur when there is atrial isomerism (see Chapter 53).20,24,56 Other associations have been identified. Esophageal varices can occur in obstructed TAPVC, and these are likely caused by obstructed veins.57-62

### **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

### **Presentation**

Patients with TAPVC present as seriously and often critically ill neonates, especially when a component of obstruction is present.63 The diagnosis can be missed when obstruction is absent, because of lack of florid signs and symptoms. TAPVC must be suspected in any neonate who has unexplained tachypnea, the cardinal sign of this anomaly. During the first 2 weeks of life, there are other causes of tachypnea that may be impossible to distinguish clinically from TAPVC, particularly a diffuse pneumonic process and retention of fetal lung fluid.64 Meconium aspiration and myocarditis may also confound the diagnosis. Respiratory distress syndrome should not be difficult to differentiate, because of its classic radiologic features, prematurity, and intercostal and sternal indrawing. Cyanosis is usually unimpressive in TAPVC unless there is marked PVO or a widely open ductus arteriosus that permits right-to-left shunting. Both LV and RV functions are depressed compared with normal (*P* , .001 in both instances) in infants presenting when seriously ill with obstructed TAPVC and marked pulmonary hypertension.50,49 Severe metabolic acidosis develops soon after birth when PVO is severe, rapidly leading to myocardial necrosis. Some neonates are so critically ill that they require intubation immediately upon hospital admission and before evaluation is begun.

### **Examination**

In neonates and infants, the heart is not particularly overactive on examination. There may be an unimpressive precordial systolic murmur and gallop sound (the latter often proves to be a tricuspid flow murmur). The second heart sound is usually single or narrowly split. In older children, the signs are those of a large atrial septal defect unless there is increased pulmonary vascular resistance.

### **Chest Radiography**

On chest radiography, heart size is usually near normal if there is PVO, but it may be large when there is increased pulmonary blood flow. The latter is associated with plethora (Fig. 30.3A), but the more common PVO is evident as a diffuse haziness or, in its severe forms, a "ground glass" appearance. This sign is reduced when the pulmonary circuit can decompress via a patent ductus arteriosus. Older infants with TAPVC to the left brachiocephalic vein have a characteristic "figure-of-eight" or "snowman" configuration on the chest radiograph (Fig. 30.3B).64

### **Echocardiography**

Modern echocardiography should be able to demonstrate normal pulmonary venous connection of all pulmonary veins to the left atrium in most children, including neonates. In those children where no pulmonary vein connection to the left atrium can be demonstrated, additional focused echocardiography is indicated, with TAPVC clearly in the differential diagnosis list, until proven otherwise.

Two-dimensional (2D) echocardiography is remarkably accurate in assessing the morphology of TAPVC (Fig. 30.4).65 Along with Doppler color flow interrogation, it is almost always diagnostic.66-68 Echocardiographic features include criteria for RV diastolic overload and an echo-free space posterior to the left atrium.69 However, a second drainage site might be overlooked.66 Other echocardiographic clues that should direct toward a TAPVC diagnosis are exclusive right-to-left shunting at atrial level, a small left atrium, and a dilated caval vein. Echocardiography is commonly accepted as a definitive diagnostic procedure in neonates with important PVO, because contrast medium is not required.70-72 Cardiac catheterization delays operation and exacerbates myocardial failure and pulmonary edema.

Prenatal echocardiography: prenatal diagnosis of TAPVC affects the postnatal outcome, particularly in obstructive forms, because planned delivery and perinatal management are mandatory. TAPVC is, however, under- and misdiagnosed in utero, with prenatal detection rates of less than 2%.73 Fetal 2D and 3D ultrasonography may improve the prenatal diagnosis of TAPVC.

Paladini and colleagues published a systematic review describing the sonographic features and associated anomalies of TAPVC in the prenatal setting. They concluded that leading sonographic signs are ventricular disproportion (right ventricle . left ventricle), increased area behind the left atrium, and the finding of a vertical vein. Prenatal diagnosis was established by using color or power Doppler in 85% of cases. Obstructed venous return can be expected in roughly one-third of cases of TAPVC, and outcome is favorable in less than half of cases.74

### **Cardiac Catheterization and Cineangiography**

Angiograms obtained by pulmonary artery or pulmonary vein injections define the malformation, identify the site of drainage, and often localize the site of PVO. When the connection is to a left vertical vein, the common pulmonary vein and vertical vein can usually be demonstrated (Fig. 30.5A). When the anomalous connection is to the coronary sinus, it appears as an ovoid opacification over the left side of the spine within the right atrial contour.75 When it is infracardiac, the descending vein can usually be demonstrated, although its precise infradiaphragmatic connection may not be seen (Fig. 30.5B).

Umbilical vein catheterization permits direct injection of contrast medium into the anomalously connecting infradiaphragmatic vein and an accurate diagnosis of its connections.31 Presence of PVO is established by demonstrating a gradient between left atrial and pulmonary artery wedge pressures. In the current era, the role of diagnostic cardiac catheterization and angiography is very limited. Noninvasive diagnostics modalities provide sufficient anatomic and pathophysiologic information in almost all cases. Even though cardiac catheterization can provide useful information, this invasive and time-consuming option should not be used in seriously ill neonates and should not result in treatment delay.

### **Magnetic Resonance Imaging and Computed Tomography**

Because of diagnostic limitations of echocardiography in complex cases and morbidity associated with cardiac catheterization in gravely ill patients, both magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) have become increasingly important in diagnosing TAPVC (Table 30.1). Both modalities should be used selectively, primarily in patients in whom echocardiography is not definitive. When compared with both catheterization and echocardiography, numerous studies have demonstrated the accuracy of MRI and CT in diagnosing TAPVC.76-82 Several demonstrate improved accuracy of diagnosis using both helical CT angiography, with and without 3D reconstruction,79 and gadolinium-enhanced 3D cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) angiography.77,78

Even though CT provides superior spatial resolution and is fast in assessing 3D dimensional anatomy, MR provides, in addition to anatomic data, valuable functional data, volumetry, and flow analysis that may be used to calculate degree of right heart enlargement and shunt fraction to determine the functional importance of lesions.83,84

# **Physiology of Common Mixing Chamber**

In TAPVC, the right atrium is theoretically a common mixing chamber.85 This situation is reflected in the frequent finding of close similarity of oxygen content and saturations from the right atrium, left atrium, pulmonary artery, and systemic artery.86 There is considerable deviation from this pattern, however, because of streaming of systemic venous return in the right atrium, directing inferior vena caval blood through the foramen ovale to the mitral valve, and superior vena caval blood through the tricuspid valve. Thus, in infracardiac TAPVC, systemic arterial saturation is typically higher than pulmonary artery saturation. In supracardiac TAPVC in the presence of patent ductus arteriousus (PDA), reversed differential cyanosis can be observed, with upper extremity oxygen saturation being lower than in the lower body.87

# **Natural History**

TAPVC is relatively uncommon, accounting for only about 1.5% to 3% of cases of congenital heart disease.20 Infants born with TAPVC have a generally unfavorable prognosis, with only about 20% surviving the first year of life.43,88 Only about 50% survive beyond 3 months, with death occurring during the first few weeks or months of life in most neonates in whom tachypnea, cyanosis, and clinical evidence of low cardiac output develop. Such infants usually have PVO, long pulmonary venous pathways, and a small patent foramen ovale.43 Survival past the critical first few weeks and months does not portend a favorable prognosis, because only about half the patients surviving to age 3 months survive to 1 year. Infants who survive the first few weeks of life usually have cardiomegaly and a large pulmonary blood flow, with mild cyanosis. Most have some degree of pulmonary artery hypertension.39 Their clinical syndrome includes tachypnea, recurrent episodes of severe pulmonary congestion, failure to thrive, fluid retention, and hepatomegaly.

Those with TAPVC who survive the first year of life without surgical treatment usually have a large atrial septal defect. Characteristically, they exhibit important physical underdevelopment similar to that of patients with other kinds of large left-to-right shunts, mild cyanosis, and mild exercise intolerance (see "Survival" under Natural History in Chapter 29). Like patients with isolated large atrial septal defects, they tend to have a stable hemodynamic state for 10 to 20 years, with little change in pulmonary vascular resistance and thus little change in pulmonary artery pressure, blood flow, and arterial oxygen levels.39 In the second decade of life, pulmonary vascular disease develops in some patients, and there is increasing cyanosis as pulmonary blood flow diminishes (Eisenmenger complex).89

To quantify the natural history, Hazelrig and colleagues analyzed data from 183 autopsied cases of surgically untreated TAPVC reported in the literature.90 Median survival was 2 months, with the shortest survival being 1 day and the longest 49 years; 90% of deaths occurred in the first year of life. Obstruction of the pulmonary venous pathway importantly reduced median survival (*P* , .0001) (Fig. 30.7) from 2.5 months in the nonobstructed group to 3 weeks in the obstructed group. Patients with supracardiac and cardiac connections had a similar history, with median survival of 2.5 and 3 months, respectively, whereas those with infracardiac connections had a worse prognosis, with median survival of 3 weeks (Fig. 30.8). Only three patients had mixed connections; two died at 5 months and one at 3.3 months. Presence of an atrial septal defect (rather than a patent foramen ovale) was associated with increased survival, particularly when the connection was not infracardiac (see Fig. 30.8).

# **Technique of Operation**

Repair of TAPVC is via median sternotomy. The CPB technique can vary depending on surgeon preference, ranging from continuous CPB with either moderate or deep hypothermia to limited CPB with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. Cardiac arrest using cardioplegia is essential for repair (see Chapters 2 and 3 for a detailed discussion of these techniques).

The ductus arteriosus must be dissected and closed routinely in infants, even if not visualized in preoperative studies.91,92 This is usually accomplished just after CPB is established. After adequate cooling the pulmonary venous confluence is opened and anastomosed to an opening in the left atrium. Excessive pulmonary blood flow due to collaterals may limit visualization in the operative field. Using drop suckers, or by reducing CPB flow, visualization can be improved. Circulatory arrest may be necessary to accomplish an accurate repair. At some point in the operation, the foramen ovale or atrial septal defect must be closed. This is usually done after correcting the anomalous pulmonary veins. Ligation of the vertical vein is typically done (see special situations and controversies).

Regardless of the type of TAPVC or type of technical repair, anastomosis of the common pulmonary vein to the left atrium is performed with a continuous suture technique using fine polypropylene or polydioxanone suture. The sutureless technique for repair of TAPVC is covered in "Special Situations and Controversies: Sutureless Repair of TAPVC" later in this section.

# **TAPVC to Left Brachiocephalic Vein (Supracardiac Type)**

After establishing CPB, ligation of the ductus arteriosus and cooling the patient, the common pulmonary vein, lying behind the pericardium, is identified after lifting up the apex of the heart for a moment to visualize the retrocardiac portion of the pericardium. The right pulmonary artery, running parallel and just cephalad to the common pulmonary vein, is also identified to avoid confusing it with the common pulmonary vein. The vertical vein connecting the common pulmonary vein to the left brachiocephalic vein can sometimes be seen inside the pericardium, but in most cases the pericardium on the left must be retracted toward the patient's right and the persistent left vertical vein identified beneath the mediastinal pleura. The vein is isolated after carefully identifying and avoiding injury to the left phrenic nerve.

Cardioplegic cardiac arrest is established after cross clamping the aorta. The common pulmonary vein can be exposed in several ways. One method approaches the common pulmonary vein from the right side of the heart. The posterior pericardial reflection is opened (Fig. 30.9A), and the common pulmonary vein is opened (Fig. 30.9B-C).93 The posterior left atrial wall is opened, the incision is extended toward the left atrial appendage, and an adequate sized anastomosis is then made between the common pulmonary vein and left atrium (Fig. 30.9D-F). The continuous suture line must not be pulled up so tightly as to purse-string the anastomosis and narrow it. It is helpful to place several interrupted sutures at the completion of the anastomosis to prevent purse-stringing (see Fig. 30.9F). The right atrium is opened and the foramen ovale or atrial septal defect is closed. The remainder of CPB and reestablishment of myocardial perfusion are completed (see Chapters 2 and 3).

A second method of repairing the TAPVC is by exposing the common pulmonary vein from the left side of the heart by lifting the heart out of the pericardial sac by retracting the cardiac apex anteriorly and rightward. The incision in the common pulmonary vein is made under direct vision. The back of the left atrium is also exposed by this maneuver and is incised. The anastomosis is then made in a fashion similar to that described in the preceding text.

A third method of repairing TAPVC to the left brachiocephalic vein is via a right atrial approach.94 This method has the advantage of allowing the anastomosis of the common pulmonary vein to the left atrium to be performed in precise anatomic relationships, because there is no retraction or displacement of critical structures to gain exposure. The right atrium is incised parallel to the atrioventricular groove (Fig. 30.10A), exposing the atrial septum. The septum primum is excised to enlarge access to the left atrium. The posterior wall of the left atrium is incised transversely (Fig. 30.10B) into the free pericardial space behind the atrium. The common pulmonary vein is identified lying beneath the pericardium directly behind the incision in the left atrium. An incision is made in the common pulmonary vein, which extends from the bifurcation on the left side to the bifurcation on the right side (see Fig. 30.10C). A large anastomosis is constructed between the common pulmonary vein and left atrium (Fig. 30.10D). This anastomosis has little chance for distortion because it is performed without displacing anatomically adjacent structures. Repair is completed by closing the atrial septal defect with a pericardial patch (Fig. 30.10E).

### **TAPVC to Superior Vena Cava (Supracardiac Type)**

A common pulmonary vein is usually present in the rare anomaly of TAPVC to the superior vena cava, providing free communication between right and left pulmonary veins. After the presence of the common pulmonary vein is confirmed by direct inspection (see "TAPVC to Left Brachiocephalic Vein" earlier), the operation proceeds in the same manner as described for patients with TAPVC to the left brachiocephalic vein, using the right atrial approach. The connection into the lower part of the superior vena cava is identified, and palpation is performed with a right-angled clamp to confirm the anatomic details. The sinus is disconnected from the superior vena cava by cutting across the connection. The resultant opening in the common pulmonary vein is extended in both directions and the anastomosis to the left atrium is made. The site of connection into the superior vena cava is then easily closed from within the right atrium, using a pericardial patch. The right atrium is then closed, rewarming begun, and the remainder of the operation completed as described for patients with connection to the left brachiocephalic vein.

### **TAPVC to Right Atrium (Cardiac Type)**

Initial stages of the operation are as described for other types of TAPVC using the right atrial approach. The right atrium is opened obliquely. The anomalous connection into the right atrium is explored with an instrument to verify the presence of a confluent pulmonary vein. The foramen ovale is then enlarged, and working through it, an incision is made in the posterior left atrial wall. The common pulmonary vein is visualized through this incision, and its anterior wall is incised. This opening is enlarged and anastomosed to the left atrial incision, still working from within the atria. The enlarged foramen ovale is closed by direct suture. The original connection of the common pulmonary vein to the right atrium is closed with a relatively small pericardial patch; it must be remembered that the pulmonary venous pathway from the right lung is beneath the patch.

Alternatively, as described for the repair of TAPVC to the superior vena cava, the common pulmonary vein can be detached from the right atrium, the opening is enlarged and used for anastomosis to the left atrium, and the resulting defect in the posterior atrial wall closed. The remainder of the operation is completed as usual.

### **TAPVC to Infradiaphragmatic Vein (Infracardiac Type)**

In TAPVC to an infradiaphragmatic vein, the distal (inferior) portion of the common pulmonary vein is vertical, and proximally (superiorly) it forms a Y or T connection with the left and right pulmonary veins. Therefore, after initial stages of the operation have been performed as described for other types of TAPVC, a decision is made about the approach, which may be similar to that for other types, through the opened right atrium, or through the back of the left atrium directly after tilting the apex of the heart up and to the right. Good results have been obtained by all approaches. The approach using retraction of the cardiac mass out of the pericardial sac is described. The heart is freed from its posterior attachments and the back of the left atrium exposed (Fig. 30.13A). The common pulmonary vein and left atrium are incised (Fig. 30.13B). The common pulmonary vein is anastomosed to the back of the left atrium (Fig. 30.13C), is ligated (Fig. 30.13D), and may be divided to allow the anastomosis to conform better.96,97

# **Miscellaneous Types of TAPVC**

Some rare types of TAPVC occur, such as connection to the azygos vein or inferior vena cava, or dual connection from the common pulmonary vein.25,33,37,38 These connections can usually be sorted out using MRI or CT. In the rare event that the connection cannot readily be found or dissected out at operation, the common pulmonary vein is opened and the connection(s) identified from within it. After the connection is closed, the usual anastomosis is made between the common pulmonary vein and left atrium.

### **Mixed TAPVC**

Patients with mixed TAPVC present a diagnostic as well as surgical challenge. MRI or CT is almost always used to supplement echocardiographic imaging and to provide an accurate characterization of the morphologic details.77-82 Chowdhury and colleagues have recently categorized the spectrum of mixed TAPVC into five major groups.22,28 Each group is characterized by a set of similar general morphologic patterns, but many variations occur from case to case.

Management of each patient must be individualized based on analysis of the mixture presented. A variety of techniques must be used, including many of those already described for TAPVC to the left brachiocephalic vein, right atrium, superior vena cava, and structures below the diaphragm. Combining these techniques with others, such as construction of a right atrial intracardiac baffle for isolated veins to the superior vena cava (similar to repair of partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection) or direct anastomosis of isolated left upper pulmonary vein to the left atrial appendage, will usually allow complete repair.98-102 In some small babies in whom an extensive operation would be required for complete one-stage repair, subtotal repair can be successful, leaving unrepaired, for example, anomalous connection of the left upper lobe to the left brachiocephalic vein.

# **Special Features of Postoperative Care**

Overall management of patients after correction of TAPVC is accomplished in the usual way (see Chapter 4). The relationship between left and right atrial pressures is of particular importance. Left atrial pressure may be considerably higher than right when pulmonary artery pressure decreases immediately after repair, particularly when a small and noncompliant left atrium acts more like a conduit than a reservoir.103 The left atrial pressure pulse after operation is then characterized by a steep *y* descent (Fig. 30.14). A small or borderline-sized left ventricle contributes to this situation.

In infants, particularly neonates, who present for operation in critical condition with PVO, pulmonary artery pressure and resistance may remain high early postoperatively, which in part may reflect the damaging effects of CPB (see Chapter 2), preoperative lung injury, and postoperative acidemia. Because of this, continuous monitoring of pulmonary artery pressure for at least 48 hours after repair is essential.104 When pulmonary artery systolic pressure remains elevated to two thirds or more of systemic arterial systolic pressure, right atrial and RV end-diastolic pressures are usually higher than left atrial pressure, and RV stroke volume is likely to be reduced. When this situation is present, acidemia must be vigorously treated; in addition, Paco2 should be kept low (25-30 mmHg) by hyperventilation.40

Fentanyl should be continued for at least 36 hours postoperatively, and all features of the regimen to minimize occurrence of paroxysmal pulmonary hypertension employed. Use of inhaled nitric oxide may be particularly helpful in this setting and has essentially replaced other pulmonary vasodilatory drugs that have important side effects such as systemic hypotension. ### **Results**

Results of operation for TAPVC can be excellent, but three types of complication still occur and can be fatal: (1) consequences of severe preoperative cardiopulmonary instability in neonates with obstruction, (2) a strong tendency to early postoperative paroxysms of pulmonary hypertension (described earlier), and (3) delayed development and progression of pulmonary vein stenosis (PVS).

*Early (Hospital) Death.* Hospital mortality following surgery for TAPVC has been low for patients aged 1 year or more since the early days of cardiac surgery.7 For neonates and infants undergoing operation before 1970, however, mortality was high.26 Since the early 1970s, hospital mortality has, however, significantly improved, dropping from 85% to around 5%, but with considerable variability in single-institution reports, ranging from 2% to 20%.36,97,106-116 Data from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons database, which includes combined outcomes of 32 institutions, indicate that among 226 cases of TAPVC repair between 2002 and 2004, early mortality was 12%; 9.9% for infants within the normal weight range but 29% for infants weighing less than 2.5 kg.

*Time-Related Survival.* Long-term survival is determined primarily by early mortality. Late deaths occur, but at a much lower rate than early deaths.115–118 Karamlou and colleagues reported that most deaths occurring outside the perioperative period occurred within a few months of surgery, with essentially no late events. In the study by Ando and colleagues, survival was unchanged at 90% from 2.3 months to 10 years of follow-up.106 Kirshbom and colleagues reported similar findings.109 Other studies indicate a slight drop in survival at mid to late follow-up. The study by Hancock Friesen and colleagues shows 1-month survival of 90% and 3-year survival of 87%.117 In 2010, a large retrospective, international, collaborative, population-based study involving all 19 pediatric cardiac centers in the United Kingdom, Ireland, and Sweden reported similar 3-year survival of 85.2% for surgically treated patients.119 When looking at results according to type of TAPVC, mortality of patient undergoing surgery for supracardiac, cardiac, infracardiac, or mixed type, was 11%, 11%, 16%, and 22%, respectively. Chowdhury and colleagues demonstrated late mortality of 4.3% in patients with mixed TAPVC, with follow-up of 42 months (median).22

### **Modes of Death**

The most common modes of hospital death after repair are cardiac failure and hypertensive pulmonary artery crises.120,121 Postoperative pulmonary hypertensive crisis can occur in neonates and infants who go into surgery with severe cardiopulmonary instability, even though an adequate vein repair has been achieved, or in patients with postoperative PVO. In the modern era, most early and late deaths are associated with some combination of PVO, complex mixed TAPVC morphology, and low birth weight.22,106,109,113,117,121

### **Incremental Risk Factors for Death**

Risk factors for early death have been identified.118 These include earlier year of operation, single-ventricle intracardiac morphology, mixed TAPVC morphology, infradiaphragmatic TAPVC morphology, PVO, poor preoperative physiologic state, lower age at surgery, and lower weight at operation.22,28,50,91,97,99,100,109,116,122-124

Importantly, the many studies cited here are not uniform in identifying all these risk factors. This variability reflects variations in analysis from study to study and also acknowledges that many or most of these risk factors are mutable. For example, Bove and colleagues found severe acidosis on admission to be the most important risk factor for hospital death after repair of infracardiac TAPVC.91 Other studies indicate that preoperative PVO is not a risk factor unless accompanied by single-ventricle intracardiac morphology.109 Studies by Bando and colleagues137 and Hyde and colleagues126 argue that preoperative PVO and infradiaphragmatic connection have been neutralized as risk factors for death. Risk factors for death in mixed TAPVC have been defined (Table 30.2).22

Risk factors for late death are difficult to define because late deaths occur infrequently. Most deaths outside the perioperative period involve recurrent or persistent PVO. Possibly, preoperatively small pulmonary veins predispose patients to develop the lethal postoperative complication of PVS.128 Therefore, this finding may also be a risk factor for death after repair, as may small size of the coronary sinus in cardiac TAPVC.129

#### **Functional Status**

McBride and colleagues have shown that compared with healthy children, peak oxygen consumption (88% ± 16% of predicted) and ventilatory anaerobic threshold (91% ± 21% of predicted) were mildly reduced in a group of 27 patients with a mean age of 11 years. Chronotropic impairment was observed in seven patients (32%).<sup>127</sup> Neurodevelopmental outcome has been evaluated. Kirshbom and colleagues assessed 30 patients, mean age 11 years, who had undergone TAPVC repair in the neonatal period or infancy.<sup>132</sup> All patients underwent repair using hypothermic circulatory arrest. Microcephaly was present in 28%, and abnormal neuromuscular examination in 27%. Performance IQ, motor skills, and attention were below normal, while full and verbal IQ and memory were normal. # **Indication and Timing of Operation**

Investigation must be undertaken promptly in any neonate or infant, no matter how young, who develops signs and symptoms suggestive of TAPVC. Procrastination leads to death in babies with obstructive TAPVC (and in some without it) or to hospital admission in a semi-moribund state, which importantly increases operative mortality.

Operation should be undertaken as an emergency immediately after diagnosis by 2D echocardiography in neonates and infants who enter the hospital critically ill. Preoperative preparation and stabilization should be brief; however, use of temporizing catheter-based interventions allow for optimization in some hemodynamically unstable neonates. In cases of severe circulatory collapse, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) may be required as a to bridge to intervention. In stable patients, typically non-neonates without obstruction, the operation can be scheduled electively, typically within the first 4 to 6 weeks of life. In infants in whom the diagnosis is made between 6 and 12 months of age, operation should be undertaken promptly because risk of operation is low, and even infants who appear to be doing well are at risk of dying before age 1 year.88,150

# **Special Situations and Controversies**

### **Sutureless Repair of TAPVC**

The sutureless repair technique was first described by Lacour-Gayet138 and Najm152 in the late 1990s for treatment of PVS. The principles of the sutureless technique are to avoid any direct suture on the pulmonary veins, which may incite the development of subsequent PVS. Use of the sutureless technique overcomes some of the geometric complexity of the pulmonary venous confluence and maximizes the size of the anastomosis by suturing the left atrium to the pericardial reflection surrounding the opened pulmonary venous confluence (Fig. 30.17).

The original application of the sutureless technique described incisions through or resection of pulmonary venous stenotic tissue and anastomosis between the left atrium and the pericardium away from the edges of the opened pulmonary veins.138 A multicenter analysis by Kalfa and colleagues analyzed 73 patients who underwent 103 operations for postsurgical PVS (76% had TAPVC). Following reoperation, .50% of developed restenosis, nearly all underwent reintervention, and the overall mortality was nearly 30%.139 Among surgical procedures, use of sutureless repair (rather than direct suture techniques) was an independent predictor against restenosis (HR 0.27; *P* 5 .006) and mortality tended to be lower (20% vs. 33%, *P* 5 .22).

Because of its ease of application, this technique has also been increasingly applied for primary repair of TAPVC, with several

Similarly, another meta-analysis of 27 studies showed a lower incidence of early postoperative PVO (4.6 vs. 13.5%; *P* , .05) and reoperation (3.4.vs 12.4%; *P* , .05) with the sutureless method.159 Others have also reported more favorable freedom from PVO with the sutureless technique.120,160,161 Another analysis matched patients among 207 consecutive repairs of TAPVC (sutureless, *n* 5 81; conventional, *n* 5 98). After adjusting for other factors, conventional technique was a risk factor for mortality (HR 4.1, *P* , .05) and PVO (HR 5.6, *P* , .05).162

A single center analysis from Toronto reported similar outcomes with both sutureless and conventional techniques, but with higher risk patients in the sutureless group.163 A single center study by Liufu and colleagues reported on 63 patients undergoing conventional (*n* 5 27) or sutureless (*n* 5 36) repair between 2009 and 2019.164 Overall survival was 90% at 5 years, and sutureless technique was associated with a lower early mortality and greater freedom from PVO.

Currently, the lack of control comparison of cohorts limits consensus about whether this should become the technique of choice for primary TAPVC repair.

### **Sutureless Repair Technique for Pulmonary Vein Stenosis (PVS)**

Typically, aortobicaval cannulation with full flow CPB and cardioplegic arrest is sufficient to allow visualization of the left atrium and pulmonary veins. After opening the left atrium, the pulmonary veins are visualized and inspected for ostial stenosis. In severe cases, these may be pinholes and difficult to identify. The stenotic pulmonary vein is incised and continued until normal pulmonary vein tissue is identified (if possible). Axial imaging is important to help delineate the size of distal veins and extent of incision required. For left-sided PVS, the left atrium is exposed by retracting the heart cephalad and to the right. Incision in the left atrium at the atrial appendage is then extended to the entrance of the left pulmonary veins. For right-sided PVS, the left atrium is opened transversely with counter incisions in the affected pulmonary veins and resection of any obstructive tissue. The left atrium is then sutured to the surrounding pericardium around the incised pulmonary veins creating an unobstructed return of pulmonary blood flow. Care should be taken to create a hemostatic suture line while suturing to the posterior pericardium while also avoiding inadvertent injury to the phrenic nerve from the atriopericardial suture line.165

### **Delayed Operation**

In critically ill neonates and young infants whose obstruction is primarily at the atrial level (an unusual situation), balloon atrial septostomy (with a blade if necessary) with delay of operation for 1 to 2 days is a reasonable approach.42 Preoperative preparation of the critically ill neonate by infusion of prostaglandin E1 combined with low-dose dopamine for a few hours has been advised by Serraf and colleagues.104 However, such treatment may shunt blood away from the lungs and increase cyanosis in neonates with severe PVO and pulmonary venous hypertension. The most promising palliative strategy, however, is stenting of an obstructed vertical vein. Several case reports described successful preoperative stabilization of critically ill infants.16-18

### **Late Presentation of TAPVC**

Rarely, individuals survive into childhood or early adult life with TAPVC and are first seen for surgical consideration at that time. Operation is advisable only if severe pulmonary vascular disease has not developed; the criteria for operability are the same as for patients with atrial and ventricular septal defects (see "Indications for Operation" in Chapters 29 and 33). In TAPVC, degree of cyanosis is not helpful because of the common mixing chamber, nor is height of mean pulmonary artery pressure relative to systemic pressure, because the shunt is not at systemic level.

When pulmonary vascular disease is suspected clinically, measurement of pulmonary vascular resistance is required at preoperative cardiac catheterization. Using the Fick principle, the patient's response to 100% oxygen and inhaled nitric oxide is determined. If pulmonary vascular resistance is less than 8 U · m2 using these maneuvers, operation is undertaken. If resistance is higher than 8 U · m2 , chronic pulmonary vasodilatory therapy, both pre- and postoperatively, can be considered in an attempt to increase operability.

### **Operative Exposure**

Tucker and colleagues described exposing the structures for anastomosis of the common pulmonary vein to the left atrium through the transverse sinus between the aorta and superior vena cava.166 This approach can occasionally be helpful if the common pulmonary vein is positioned superiorly.

### **Surgical Enlargement of Left Atrium**

Trusler and colleagues found that in dogs, a decrease in atrial volume of more than 50% resulted in an important reduction in cardiac output.167 Brighton and colleagues, using a mechanical model, obtained substantial improvement in cardiac output by adding a flexible atrium to the inlet of their artificial heart.168 In studying the effect of atrial compliance on cardiac performance with a mathematical electrical analog, Suga found that cardiac performance was markedly improved by increasing atrial compliance while maintaining constant ventricular contractility.169 Analysis suggested that increased atrial compliance facilitated the reservoir function of the atrium and maintenance of a relatively high mean atrial pressure during ventricular filling. These facts, combined with the preoperatively observed small left atrium in patients with TAPVC, have made its enlargement by shifting the atrial septum to the right attractive.94,170,171

A recent study by Mishra and colleagues compared patients with patch enlargement during atrial septal defect closure with the nonenlarged group.172 They found significantly better early surgical outcome in terms of lower inotropic score and duration, reduced duration of ventilation, and reduced ICU stay, in the patients that had undergone left atrium (LA) enlargement. In addition, in patients with suprasystemic or high pulmonary artery (PA) pressures, leaving a patent foramen ovale (PFO) also improved the postoperative outcomes.

Katz and colleagues, on the other hand, found similar survival with and without left atrial enlargement.142 This may be because surgical efforts to improve left atrial size are not effective, or because adequate left atrial enlargement may result from incorporating the common pulmonary vein into the left atrium.44,51 Also, lack of left atrial reservoir function may rarely be critical early or late postoperatively if other aspects of the situation favor survival. Nonetheless, the repair must be done in a manner that *does not decrease* size of the left atrium.

### **Ligation of the Vertical Vein**

Leaving the vertical vein open might allow decompression of a small noncompliant left atrium, by providing pop-off to the systemic venous system. This might avert hemodynamic instability during postoperative pulmonary hypertensive crises.101,173 In most cases, and during most circumstances this left-to-right shunt is expected to have little hemodynamic consequence, and many veins will close spontaneously.173-175 However, important left-to-right shunting has been described.176-178 Catheter closure using Amplatzer devices has been shown to be feasible by multiple case reports.179-181 Chowdhury and colleagues proposed a protocol for selective ligation of the vertical vein by test occlusion of the vertical vein. If pulmonary artery pressure increased to systemic or suprasystemic levels, the vertical vein was not ligated.174 Alternatively, a small PFO may be left at the time of closing the atrial septum, thus allowing safe ligation of the vertical vein and providing catheter access to the left atrium should subsequent postrepair PVS develop.

# **SECTION II: TAPVC IN FUNCTIONAL SINGLE VENTRICLE**

Even though surgical outcome of patients with FSV has improved in recent decades, outcome in patients with concomitant TAPVC has remained poor.182 In patients with right atrial isomerism and FSV, obstructed TAPVC has been the most important independent predictor of mortality, with an OR 7.8 *P* 5 .0005.183

Primary diagnoses in functional single-ventricle patients with TAPVC include hypoplastic left heart syndrome, unbalanced atrioventricular septal defect, tricuspid atresia, and double inlet left ventricle, among others. The most common subtypes of TAPVC in patients with FSV are supracardiac 54%, cardiac 25%, infracardiac 15%, and mixed in 5%.182 PVO is associated in approximately 50% to 60% of patients.182,184,185

# **Timing of Repair**

In patients who present with PVO, clinical presentation often necessitates early TAPVC repair, meaning before bidirectional Glenn (BDG) anastomosis. Mortality of patients in the neonatal or early infantile period who underwent repair of TAPVC before BDG has been reported as high as 60%. For those patients who survive the initial TAPVC repair and are able to undergo BDG, additional mortality for the second stage operation remains high (.80%). In the study of Hoashi and colleagues, only 8% of the total number of initial patients with FSV and TAPVC were able to undergo Fontan completion.184

On the other hand, those who underwent combined BDG and TAPVC repair mortality was lower at 39%. Of these patients, .60% were able to proceed to Fontan completion. A likely explanation for these findings is that combined BDG and TAPVC repair can be scheduled at an older age (infantile period) compared with those requiring early neonatal repair.

Palliative vertical vein stent placement provides an alternative strategy which makes it possible to postpone TAPVC beyond the neonatal period. Recent studies suggest that, for neonates with obstructive TAPVC and FSV, delayed TAPVC repair using primary vertical vein stenting appeared to improve survival compared with the conventional strategy.185,186 TAPVC correction at a later age after physical growth might prevent anatomic and surgical technical problems seen in neonatal correction, including distortion and possibly excess intimal proliferation.184

### **Results**

Several studies have reported single center outcomes.183,184,187-191 Overall 5-year survival rate of patients with FSV and TAPVC is 31% to 55%. Reported risk factors that influence survival are coexistence of pulmonary atresia with major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs), TAPVC repair prior to BDG, younger age at the time of repair, infracardiac type TAPVC,188 and mixed type TAPVC.191 Approximately one-third to one-half of all patients with FSV and TAPVC achieve Fontan completion. Of the initial survivors, this percentage is approximately 80%.189,191 Mortality of patients undergoing Fontan completion has been reported from 0% to 18%.189,191

Common late complications include protein-losing enteropathy, need for atrioventricular valve (AVV) replacement, and arrhythmias. Arrhythmias include junctional tachycardia caused by the presence of twin atrioventricular nodes.189 Another important late complication is recurrent PVS. Freedom from PVS at 5 years after TAPVC repair was 53.5% to 78%.184,189,191 Even though PVS did not seem to influence survival in some studies, it was a risk factor for achieving Fontan in others.184

TAPVC in patients with FSV (the majority being right isomerism) remains an extremely challenging patient population with poor results. Delayed TAPVC repair using primary vertical vein stenting and improved surgical technique to prevent recurrent PVO might be a path to improve outcomes in this complex subset of patients.

### **SECTION III: PULMONARY VEIN STENOSIS**

### **Definition**

Pulmonary vein stenosis (PVS) results in narrowing or obstruction in one or more of the pulmonary veins.192 There are two broad categories of PVS: (1) primary (congenital) PVS involves stenosis in normally connected pulmonary veins and (2) secondary (acquired): post TAPVC repair PVS (PR-PVS) typically occurs in 10% to 15% of children after repair of TAPVC (see Section I). PVS following radiofrequency ablation of atrial fibrillation is seen in the adult population.

# **Morphology**

PVS is characterized by neointimal lesions in the pulmonary veins that are composed of myofibroblast-like cells and increased extracellular matrix that have evidence of tyrosine kinase receptor activation in lesional cells.193

### **Clinical Presentation**

PVS is a rare disease with heterogenous presentation. Age at presentation and disease severity are dependent on the number of pulmonary veins involved, the severity of obstruction, and the presence of associated cardiac defects or genetic syndromes. As PVS evolves, patients present with symptoms and signs of cardiorespiratory failure with progressive pulmonary hypertension. Severe PVS can manifest with tachypnea, recurrent pulmonary infections, and hemoptysis. Cyanosis may be identified in the presence of intra- or extracardiac right-to-left shunts with progressive pulmonary hypertension. Patients may succumb at any time to sudden death because of suprasystemic pulmonary hypertension crises.

To date, no genetic cause has been identified. Genetic syndromes can be present in approximately 30% of children with a diagnosis of PVS, with trisomy 21, CHARGE, and VACTERL being common.194,195 Concomitant congenital heart disease can also be present, ranging from simple malformations such as atrial or ventricular septal defects to major congenital anomalies such as FSV with heterotaxy syndromes. Primary PVS associated with prematurity may represent a distinct entity and can present with associated lung-related comorbidities such as bronchopulmonary dysplasia and pulmonary hypertension.

# **Diagnostic Criteria**

### **Echocardiography**

Echocardiography is an important noninvasive modality for diagnosis and surveillance of PVS. As primary PVS can occur in the setting of congenital heart diseases, careful interrogation of the pulmonary veins should always be part of any preoperative or postoperative echocardiogram for these patients. Doppler interrogation of the pulmonary veins, pulmonary arteries, and right heart pressures should be documented on anyone with a suspicion of PVS. Echocardiography is limited to the venoatrial junction and so cannot assess adequately PVS in intraparenchymal pulmonary veins.

### **Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and Computed Tomographic Angiography (CTA)**

Use of cardiac MRI and CTA are important surveillance adjuncts that should be utilized to confirm the diagnosis of PVS and to monitor disease progression. Cardiac MRI and CTA visualize the intraparenchymal pulmonary veins and provide additional information with tomographic and 3D views. Cardiac MRI can also provide flow information to various pulmonary veins and lung segments. Availability, local expertise and patient factors influence choice of modality. In particular, cardiac MRI requires longer acquisition times and sedation while CTA has higher radiation exposure, but advancement of protocols is helping to minimize these factors. A study looking at the utility of cardiac MRI in PVS has shown that unilateral PVS is associated with diastolic reverse flow in the ipsilateral branch pulmonary arteries and diastolic forward flow in the contralateral branch pulmonary arteries, whereas bilateral PVS is associated with diastolic forward flow in most or all of the branch pulmonary arteries.196,197

# **Diagnostic Catheterization and Angiography**

Catheterization and angiography are important components of PVS evaluation when there is a need for further delineation of the anatomy and hemodynamics or if anticipating interventional procedures. One can readily measure pulmonary pressures, and selective injections into the branch pulmonary arteries can confirm and characterize PVS.

# **Lung Perfusion Scans**

Lung perfusion scans have been used to follow progression of PVS, as it quantifies blood flow to various lung segments. Lung scans correlate with angiographic findings and can be used as a noninvasive surveillance modality at centers who routinely use them.198

# **Natural History**

Involvement of multiple veins and early age of diagnosis are associated with a guarded prognosis, as children develop secondary pulmonary hypertension and right heart failure. A recent multicenter study of ex-premature infants demonstrated 55% survival at 2 years.199

# **Multidisciplinary Team Approach to Therapy**

Clinical decision for timing of intervention and surgical intervention is best done in a multidisciplinary team approach. Integration of diagnostic modalities and individual patient anatomy can help teams to decide when surgical or catheter-based interventions are appropriate. Typically, the presence of symptoms, elevated right sided pressures, or involvement of multiple pulmonary veins are important trigger points for intervention.200 Mainstays of treatment are surgical and catheter-based interventions, with experimental medications being used in select patients. In cases with severe PVS, lung transplantation or palliative care should be explored in the context of joint family decision making.201

# **Technique of Operation**

Surgical reconstruction options include the sutureless technique of repair (see detailed description in Section I, "Special Situations and Controversies") and reconstruction with direct suturing to the pulmonary veins. Some centers have adopted incorporation of the anatomic repair strategy whereby surgical revision may involve realigning the pulmonary veins to improve geometry and remove possible nidus for developing obstruction.202 Patching of the anterior aspect of the vein may be required to create an unobstructed pathway back to the left atrium. To increase left atrial size, the atrial septum may need to be realigned. Long-term results of this technique are not yet reported.

### **Results**

Multiple studies have documented a 2-year survival of 60% for those with progressive multivessel disease.203,204 Risk factors for poor prognosis include congenital PVS, earlier age of diagnosis, higher number of veins affected, single ventricle physiology, and bilateral lesions. Use of the sutureless technique was associated with better late outcomes in some centers.205,206 Surgery and catheter based interventions must be tailored to the individual patient anatomy, and use of multidisciplinary teams is a critical component of improved outcomes in children with PVS. Surgical, catheter-based, and experimental therapies can be effective in some children; however, survival among children requiring intervention for multivessel involvement remains at approximately 60%.207,208 Regardless of the type of restenosis, freedom from reintervention after postrepair PVS remains low.209 Surveillance for recurrence and use of multimodal approach is warranted.

# **Special Situations and Controversies**

### **Catheter-Based Interventions**

Catheter-based interventions, used as sole therapy, are associated with high rates of restenosis,210 but these methods play an important role in multimodality management of PVS. Balloon angioplasty and cutting balloon angioplasty are associated with hemodynamic and angiographic evidence of relief of PVS; however, freedom from reintervention at 1 year is low.211 Stent implantation has better freedom from reintervention (62% at 6 months and 42% at 1 year), and importantly, stents with a luminal diameter of .7 mm are associated with a longer time to reintervention.211 Drug-eluting stents (DES) that have antiproliferative drugs including paclitaxel, sirolimus, and everolimus have been used with improved patency of pulmonary veins, but a small recent report demonstrated no difference in patient survival.212 A limitation of DES is that typical diameters are less than 5 mm, which may lead to mismatch between the size of the stented pulmonary vein and somatic growth. Intentional stent fracture shows some promise in addressing small stent size. Other techniques such as drug-eluting balloons and use of bioabsorbable stents have insufficient data available in pediatric patients with PVS.

### **Hybrid Approach**

Surgery and catheter-based interventions need not be mutually exclusive. Drug-eluting balloons have also been used postanastomosis due to the antimitogenic properties. Intraoperative stenting can be done in vessels that are not amenable to surgical repair. Identification of the pinhole orifice or long diffusely hypoplastic segment can allow direct visualization and placement of a drug eluting stent that can be used to subsequently rehabilitate the vessel in the catheterization laboratory. In severe cases of PVS, temporizing angioplasty may reduce RV pressures and allow for optimization of the patient preoperatively. Likewise, in patients at high risk for restenosis, a small atrial communication can be left to facilitate catheter-based procedures.

### **Experimental Therapies**

Therapies designed to slow PVS progression have been evaluated in various single armed observational trials and retrospective reviews. These typically target myofibroblast hypercellularity seen in end-stage human PVS samples using chemotherapeutic agents. Methotrexate and vinblastine (NCT00215046) were used in advanced primary PVS; however, 1-year survival was 38% with a high incidence of important drug side effects.213 A separate study using imatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor, and bevacizumab, a chemotherapy agent, reported a 72-week survival of 77%, an improvement over the results of methotrexate and vinblastine.214 However, an earlier retrospective study compared the early study cohort (imatinib/bevacizumab) to a contemporary surgical/interventional cohort who did not receive study drugs and demonstrated no difference in overall mortality between the two groups.215 A separate study in a porcine model showed that losartan administration reduced neointimal formation but the results of the study in pediatric patients is still pending.216 Sirolimus, an mTOR inhibitor, has been utilized in PVS patients, with some centers reporting positive impact on vein patency and survival.217,218 To date, there is no larger study with a control cohort published.
