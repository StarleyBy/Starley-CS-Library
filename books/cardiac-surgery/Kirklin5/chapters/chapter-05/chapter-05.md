It allows excellent exposure and access but also immediate control of all cardiac and vascular structures for most of the surgical procedures in adult and pediatric cardiac surgery. Due to the low perioperative mortality following primary surgery and the improved long-term survival of both pediatric patients following repair of congenital malformations and adult patients following coronary artery bypass grafting and valve and aortic surgery, the number of patients who require two or more cardiac operations is increasing. ### General Principles and Indications for a Redo Cardiac Surgery Procedure

Strategies on how to plan a reoperative cardiac surgery have been established to minimize all technical risks due to redo-sternotomy (injuries to cardiac and vascular structures, life-threatening arrhythmias) and therefore to optimize outcomes. <sup>14-17</sup> The increasing importance of multidisciplinary boards <sup>18</sup> to discuss all potential therapeutic options and define the most suitable solution for each patient has been emphasized in recent guidelines. <sup>19-22</sup> Depending on the situation, such boards may include cardiovascular radiologists, cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, cardiovascular anesthesiologists, hematologists with a special interest in transfusion

medicine, intensive care specialists, and perfusionists. During the board discussion, the personalized approach must balance the risks and benefits of every procedure for each individual patient. Since the advent and dissemination of catheter-based procedures (percutaneous coronary intervention, transcatheter aortic valve implantation, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair of the mitral valve, and other less invasive technologies), every option should be analyzed, taking into account the overall risk profile of every patient (age, comorbidities, risk factors, frailty, life expectancy); the general intraoperative risks; the expected technical challenges; the incidence of perioperative complications; the benefit of a surgical versus transcatheter procedure; and the potential for the most expedient and complete rehabilitation. Finally, the wish of the patients has found broader recognition in the decision of the multidisciplinary teams in the most recent guidelines.

#### **Considerations About Prophylactic/ Protective Techniques at Initial Surgery to Facilitate a Reoperative Procedure**

There are a few technical particularities that can be applied during the initial surgery that may considerably reduce the risks of a subsequent redo-sternotomy. Among them, closure of the pericardium whenever possible is the simplest, particularly in the presence of a somewhat prominent ascending aorta.23,24 However, in some cases, closure of the pericardium over the cardiac cavities is not possible because this would compress the ventricles during the immediate postoperative phase and therefore significantly reduce cardiac performance.

In some situations (coronary artery bypass grafting, congenital surgery, left ventricular assist device [LVAD] implantation), it might be wise to use a thin synthetic pericardial membrane, especially when a redo-operation might be expected in the future.25,26 The brachiocephalic vein should not be denudated from the surrounding fat (thymus) tissue; this probably helps minimize the risk of a subsequent injury in the case of a redo-sternotomy.

Another important detail in coronary artery bypass surgery is the positioning of the bypass grafts. Creation of a small lateral pericardial window to pass the left internal thoracic artery from the left pleural cavity to the pericardial space is useful as this creates a small groove between the left lung and the pericardium, and the internal thoracic artery falls down into this space and is not pushed into the middle of the mediastinum by the expanding left lung. A similar strategy is recommended for the positioning of the right internal thoracic artery when it is used to revascularize the left coronary system; to pass it through the transverse sinus posteriorly to the aorta and the main pulmonary artery may avoid injury at the time of a redo-surgery. Finally, whenever possible, radial artery and saphenous vein grafts should not cross the midline (over the right ventricle) nor lie directly behind the sternum. As a general strategy, a complete arterial revascularization will be followed by fewer redo-coronary artery bypass grafting procedures because of the better long-term patency of arterial grafts.27-32

### **Value of Imaging**

Conventional chest x-ray with a lateral projection might help identify the close relationship between the anterior wall of the right ventricle and the aorta with the posterior surface of the sternum, but it is never as precise as a native or contrast computed tomography (CT). A CT scan of the chest with intravenous contrast application allows proper evaluation of the proximity of the brachiocephalic vein, the great vessels, and the right ventricle to the sternum, and in case of a prior coronary artery bypass grafting procedure, it will demonstrate the position of the bypass conduits in relation to the sternum and the great vessels.33-36 This information has major practical consequences as it will help in deciding in which cases preventive insertion of catheter wires for transcutaneous cannulation or even prior cannulation (with or without prophylactic institution of cardiopulmonary bypass) would be reasonable before sawing the sternum. #### **Technique**

Anesthesia induction and maintenance are similar to those performed for conventional primary surgery using a standard endotracheal tube. (A double-lumen tube is useful when the approach for reoperative surgery is performed via a right lateral thoracotomy.) The standard monitoring for open heart procedures (i.e., transesophageal echocardiography, electrocardiography, and nearinfrared spectroscopy, including arterial and central venous lines) is used.37 Carbon dioxide (Co2) with a flow of 2 liters per minute is continuously insufflated into the operating field throughout the procedure. No special surgical equipment that differs from that for conventional surgery is necessary, excepting a series of traumatic clamps (Figs 5.3 and 5.4) to tightly close the skin in case of major bleeding. This is a very effective method to avoid exsanguination before cardiopulmonary bypass can be instituted and blood retransfused via the circuit.

#### **Preliminary Safety Measures**

Before disinfection and draping, disposable gel pads are placed in the midaxillary line on each side for external cardioversion if necessary. This is important to safely manage situations where the patient develops ventricular tachycardia or ventricular fibrillation before the heart is dissected and an internal defibrillator could be used. It is strongly recommended to have one groin region (usually the right one) disinfected and free for potential emergency cannulation. In addition, the perfusionist should be available in the operating theater, the cardiopulmonary bypass circuit should be filled before sternotomy, and the lines of the circuit should be available on the operating table.

#### **Reopening the Sternum**

As for a primary sternotomy, the skin incision might extend from 2 to 3 cm below the suprasternal notch to a point situated at the distal end of the xyphoid process (Fig. 5.5). Usually the skin is very mobile, and patients enjoy a limited skin scar, especially in the cranial part. The prior scar is excised when it is hypertrophic or when a keloid has developed, and the subcutaneous tissue is gently mobilized to avoid tension when the wound is closed at the end of the operation. Hemostasis of the subcutaneous tissue is performed meticulously, particularly when the patient is operated under antiplatelet drugs or oral anticoagulation. There might be a small transversal vein in the region of the suprasternal notch that should be coagulated with the electro-knife or ligated if possible. When bleeding is important, packing with a compress is useful, and definitive hemostasis will be performed once the sternum has been opened.

#### **Injury to the Brachiocephalic Vein**

Injury of the brachiocephalic vein must be suspected when major venous bleeding occurs during sawing at the upper end of the sternotomy. In case of massive bleeding that can't be controlled because the sternum cannot yet be spread, packing and rapid peripheral institution of cardiopulmonary bypass may be necessary to retrieve blood into the circuit. If bleeding appears after sternum spreading, small injuries can be treated with simple sutures, keeping in mind that the wall of the vein is very thin. Therefore, 5.0 or better 6.0 polypropylene suture is the ideal monofilament material to be used. To allow a tension-free stitch, the sternum spreader should be somehow turned back. #### **Injury to the Right Ventricle**

The risk of a significant injury of the right ventricle may be particularly high when the pericardium has been left open on its whole length at primary surgery and/or in case of right ventricular hypertrophy/dilatation. As for the brachiocephalic vein, the injury may happen either during sternum sawing or after insertion of the sternal retractor. Opening both pleural cavities may facilitate spreading the sternum retractor and reducing the tension of adhesions between the sternum and anterior part of the ventricle.

Otherwise, opening of the retractor should be done very gently since a too-strong stretching of the anterior surface of the right ventricle may lead to tears that may be difficult to repair. When a tear in the right ventricle has occurred and can't be immediately repaired because of the lack of space between both sternal sides, digital control or packing with large compresses may be attempted. If this is not possible or not efficient enough, temporary closure of the sternotomy with several traumatic clamps may control the hemorrhage (see earlier) and give enough time to institute cardiopulmonary bypass via peripheral cannulation. Once cardiopulmonary bypass has been instituted, the heart can be unloaded, and repair of tears in the right ventricle is much easier. U-stitches with autologous pericardial reinforcement or with Teflon-pledgeted sutures are the most common technique to close right ventricular injuries. Fortunately, there are no major coronary arteries crossing the anterior wall of the right ventricle, but any branch and/or bypass graft should be avoided, of course.

#### **Injury to the Ascending Aorta and of the Main Pulmonary Artery**

Injury of the ascending aorta is another catastrophic event that may occur, particularly in case of aortic aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm following previous aortic surgery. Injury of the main pulmonary artery is the less-frequent fatal injury that may occur during reoperative surgery; prior repair of the right ventricular outflow tract using a prosthetic or a xenograft conduit or a homograft in a heterotopic position with the pericardium left open is a typical high-risk situation for such an injury. Both conditions will be discussed later in this chapter.

#### **Injury to the Lung**

Injury to the lung parenchyma is relatively uncommon, but this problem may happen in patients with chronic obstructive lung disease and emphysema and in those in whom both pleural cavities have been opened at initial surgery and the pleura have been sutured together to help cover the great vessels and eventually bypass grafts. Generally speaking, ventilation can be completely stopped during sawing, but when this step takes more time, reducing the tidal volume is more appropriate.

Depending on the size of the injury, suturing the lung parenchyma with monofilament sutures may be necessary in case of major air leaks, although use of fibrin glue may be sufficient for small injuries. Control of such injuries should be done at the end of the procedure before sternal closure, using water to look for residual air leaks.

#### **Redo for Coronary Artery Bypass Surgery**

Reoperation because of coronary artery disease following prior surgical myocardial revascularization has become less frequent in more recent years. There are multiple reasons for this observation: the increased use of multiple arterial grafts up to complete arterial revascularization at initial surgery, the better secondary prevention of cardiovascular risk factors with aggressive medicament treatment of hypercholesterolemia and arterial hypertension, and increased awareness and better compliance of patients to life-long antiplatelet treatment.38,39 Finally, the broader use of percutaneous coronary interventions in the long term following coronary artery bypass grafting to treat progression of the disease in the native coronary arteries and in the bypass conduits, together with better stent materials and combined antiplatelets treatment, have also contributed to a decrease in the necessity for reoperative coronary surgery.20,21

Nevertheless, redo-coronary bypass grafting might still be indicated in younger patients because of progression of coronary artery disease in the native vessels (especially in the left main stem), incomplete revascularization at the initial surgery, stenosed vein grafts, in situations where the diseased vessels are not easily amenable to percutaneous intervention, and in those patients who may potentially have a survival benefit with the placement of an arterial conduit to the left anterior descending branch.

There are many challenges that might occur during redocoronary artery bypass grafting.7,40-42 As for any reoperative cardiac surgery, opening of the sternum represents the first challenge, followed by safe establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass without injury to patient nor embolization from patent but stenosed grafts, and finally the proper identification of the target coronary arteries and the availability of bypass graft material (the second internal thoracic or radial artery, or residual vein graft material). Therefore, preoperative assessment is exactly the same as for primary coronary surgery. Preoperative coronary angiography is mandatory since it will demonstrate the patency of previous grafts, and CT scan may show precisely the trajectory of these grafts, especially when they are close to the posterior surface of the sternum and/or when they cross the midline (for instance, a right internal thoracic artery to the left descending branch or to a marginal branch) (Figs. 5.10 and 5.11).

A similar strategy may be applied for the right internal thoracic artery when it is used to revascularize the left coronary system at initial surgery; the artery is preferentially passed through the transverse sinus behind the aorta and the main pulmonary artery to be anastomosed with any branch of the circumflex artery. In case the risk of injury of life-threatening structures is judged to be high, preliminary percutaneous introduction of wires into the external iliac artery and vein or even percutaneous cannulation should be performed.

#### **Dealing with Patent Internal Thoracic Artery (-ies)**

In case one or both internal thoracic arteries have been used as bypass conduits at the initial procedure, two different strategies may be applied in the setting of reoperative surgery. If the conduits can be easily localized and prepared (this is usually the internal thoracic artery that lies in the groove between the lung and the pericardium), they can be encircled and prepared for subsequent occlusion with an atraumatic bulldog clamp. This maneuver is easier when the internal thoracic artery had been prepared in a skeletonized fashion at the initial operation. If preparation of the internal thoracic artery turns out to be hazardous, coronary perfusion may be maintained during surgery thus eliminating the potential for complete myocardial ischemia. In case the ascending aorta is not opened during redo (isolated bypass or mitral valve repair or replacement), an aortic vent should be installed. When the aortic root is opened (for instance, aortic valve replacement), the backflow through of the internal thoracic artery through the coronary ostia may be blocked using a very thin Fogarty balloon catheter.

Myocardial protection in the setting of previous coronary artery bypass grafting requires careful attention. A single-shot, low-volume (100 mL) crystalloid solution (CardioplexolTM, Bichsel, Interlaken, Switzerland) is rather easy to apply, stops the heart immediately, and adequately protects the myocardium for at least 45 to 60 minutes.44 This solution is similar to the Del Nido cardioplegia with the exception that it is based on a very small volume. It can be repeated in case of prolonged myocardial ischemia and has the additional advantage that it does not lead to any hemodilution. As an alternative, antegrade or retrograde blood cardioplegia can be used.

#### **Reoperative Aortic Valve Replacement (After Previous Valve Replacement and/or Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting)**

Reoperative aortic valve replacement was a frequent procedure following coronary artery bypass grafting and/or prior aortic valve replacement until recently but has now been largely replaced by transcatheter aortic valve replacement, which is considered the primary option for a majority of patients aged more than 70 years in the reoperative setting. The main indications for a conventional surgical aortic valve replacement remain endocarditis and situations where a transcatheter valve replacement may have the potential for coronary obstruction. This phenomenon is called *sinus sequestration*, where coronary obstruction may occur to the behavior of displaced prosthetic valve leaflets. Another potentially dangerous situation is observed in patients with a low and narrow sinutubular junction, because this creates a "tube graft" within the ascending aorta, which sequesters the sinuses of Valsalva and obstructs blood flow to the coronary arteries that arise therein. This seems to develop particularly when the surgical valve has externally mounted leaflets, which are not constrained by the commissural suture posts and are more easily displaced toward the coronaries.45

#### **Venting of the Left Ventricle as Management of Severe Aortic Regurgitation During Sternotomy**

Among several challenging conditions, a regurgitant aortic valve (either the native one or a prior implanted and degenerated tissue valve) requires particular attention because, in case of ventricular fibrillation during preparation of the heart and the aorta and before aortic clamping is possible, uncontrolled left ventricular dilatation may occur with the potential for overdistension and irreversible myocardial damage.

Whenever possible, the left ventricular vent should be introduced as usual through the right superior pulmonary vein. If this is not possible, the vent may be placed into the left ventricle through the apex of the left ventricle (either through sternotomy or exceptionally through a separate small left anterior thoracotomy).

#### **Reoperative Aortic Surgery in Presence of Large Aortic Aneurysms or Pseudoaneurysms Adherent to or Penetrating into the Sternum: Hypothermia, Circulatory Arrest, and Cerebral Protection**

Reoperative aortic surgery is increasingly performed nowadays, mainly due to the improved immediate and long-term survival of patients following repair of acute aortic dissection and aortic aneurysm. Reasons for reoperation may include aortic valve dysfunction and/or aortic root dilatation following replacement of the ascending aorta, infective endocarditis and graft infection, aneurysm of the downstream aorta (distal ascending or aortic arch, particularly when the interposition graft was short at the initial operation), and finally pseudoaneurysm at the level of previous anastomoses. Other reasons for reoperative surgery may of course be coronary artery disease and new onset of mitral and tricuspid valve diseases.

CT-angiography is a prerequisite examination for risk assessment and proper operative planning, including sternum reentry, management of large aortic aneurysm and pseudoaneurysms close to the sternum, site of cannulation, eventual institution of perfusion before sternotomy, as well as the degree of hypothermia and type of cerebral protection.48-50

#### **Endovascular Clamping**

In cases of difficult access to the aorta or in those with aortic aneurysm or pseudoaneurysm close to or penetrating into the sternum with a higher risk of entering the aorta during resternotomy, peripheral cannulation via the right subclavian or more rarely the femoral artery to achieve hypothermic circulatory arrest before sternotomy is mandatory. This will allow us to expose and control a clampable segment of the ascending aorta in a bloodless field. However, in patients with significant aortic valve regurgitation, obligatory closed chest hypothermic circulatory arrest means a risk of uncontrolled left ventricular dilatation as soon as ventricular fibrillation occurs and until the period of circulatory arrest can start.

In this situation, a strategy based on aortic endoclamping has been proposed, which allows a safe approach to both retrosternal aortic aneurysm and severe aortic regurgitation.55,56 In these cases, femoro-femoral cardiopulmonary bypass, transfemoral aortic endoclamping, and percutaneous retrograde cardioplegia administration is performed before sternotomy in patients with normal descending and abdominal aorta.

In those with a calcified aortic arch and/or descending aorta or with significant thrombotic deposits, femoro-subclavian cardiopulmonary bypass with transaxillary aortic endoclamping and percutaneous retrograde cardioplegia administration before sternotomy is recommended.

In all cases, patients are draped with the inguinal and subclavicular regions exposed. Both radial arteries are cannulated for arterial pressure monitoring. Transesophageal echocardiography is used to control guiding and facilitate positioning of the intracardiac percutaneous catheters and to scan descending aorta for the presence of atheromatous disease. Venous and arterial cannulae are inserted using the Seldinger technique. The Y-branch of the arterial cannula is used to introduce the endoclamp into the ascending aorta (Fig. 5.18). Inflation of the endoclamp is performed under control of transesophageal echocardiography. An Endo-Plege catheter (Heartport, Redwood City, CA) is placed via the right internal jugular vein for retrograde cardioplegia delivery. This approach requires sufficient training and experience with standard port-access procedures; the optimal placement of the endo-retrograde cardioplegia catheter may be especially challenging. This makes the technique useful for elective but not emergency cases. While the aorta is occluded with the endoclamp, monitoring of adequate bilateral carotid blood flow is essential to ensure that the balloon is not obstructing one of the supra-aortic branches.

In the most recent period, control of percutaneously introduced catheters (antegrade or retrograde cardioplegia as well as aortic endoclamp) can be performed using fluoroscopy in a hybrid room. This may significantly accelerate the procedure.

#### **Reoperative Surgery for Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Pathologies**

Repair of the right ventricular outflow tract represents a rather common procedure for several congenital malformations such as pulmonary valve stenosis, Fallot tetralogy, transposition of the great arteries with pulmonary stenosis, truncus arteriosus, and others. Because of outgrowth of smaller patients when an implant has been used earlier in life, and structural degeneration of prior implanted xenograft and homograft conduits and/or valves, reoperations are quite frequent in this location. Other patients received a simple transannular patch and may experience severe pulmonary regurgitation.

A technically relevant particularity of these pathologies is the fact that many of them are associated with a hypertrophied right ventricle and frequently with pulmonary stenosis or regurgitation. The simplest technique to address these problems is the replacement of the previous implant with a new one. However, in some of these patients, the previous conduit is located very close to the posterior surface of the sternum, particularly in those patients in whom the pericardium was left open because of the heterotopic position of the conduit or because of hemodynamic instability at the end of the prior procedure.

In the majority of cases, central cannulation might be performed but for the condition described earlier, peripheral cannulation might be a good option in adolescents and adult patients because starting cardiopulmonary bypass before sternal reentry may be useful to unload the heart in case of massive right ventricular dilatation and very close relationship between the right ventricle and/or the conduit with the posterior surface of the sternum. One special issue may be massive calcification of the conduit that makes using sutures to control the hemorrhage impossible.

In the ideal case, the procedure can be performed on a beating heart; this reduces the need for mediastinal dissection and for circular preparation of the aorta, which might be challenging in case of strong adhesions and/or calcified homograft or xenograft conduit. In such case, the conduit is open above the estimated site of the valve and the distal anastomosis is inspected from inside. This allows minimal dissection of the main pulmonary artery from the aorta, just enough to permit a circular anastomosis proximal to the pulmonary bifurcation. Whenever possible, the new implant is sutured within an orthotopic position to avoid an anterior shift of the conduit at the end of the procedure. A thin PTFE membrane is liberally inserted to close the pericardium and create a new dissection plane between the conduit and the sternum that will be useful for further surgeries.

#### **Reoperative Approach for Heart Transplantation in Case of Prior Left Ventricular Assist Device**

Due to the scarcity of donor organs, an increasing proportion of patients awaiting heart transplantation must be bridged using a VAD because of therapy-refractory heart failure.57 This supplementary surgical step in the management of patients with advanced heart failure represents a significant additional risk when cardiac transplantation is performed. This is the reason why some "protective" actions should be taken at the time of LVAD implantation. Among them, positioning of the outflow graft and the driveline should be away from the midline behind the sternum and, in addition, the most complete isolation of the different components of the device, including the arterial outflow graft to the ascending aorta and the driveline, should be performed using a 0.6-mm Gore-Tex membrane.26 The last generations of LVAD should be more or less packed completely within such a membrane to avoid adhesions of the pump with the left lung, especially when the pleura has to be opened to gain space for the housing part of the pump. This helps avoid air leaks through the lung parenchyma at the time of device explantation.

Also in this situation, the usual risks of sternal reentry should be assessed using a CT-angiography prior to transplantation. Whenever possible, central cannulation should be attempted because it will allow the best possible flow. However, sometimes the remaining free portion of the ascending aorta following the endto-side anastomosis with the outflow graft is scarce, and cannulation has to be performed at the level of the proximal arch or, in case of emergency, through the outflow graft itself. In patients with a prolonged preoperative assistance with an LVAD, peripheral cannulation might be more challenging because of the progressive narrowing of the iliac and femoral vessels secondary to vascular regression as a consequence of nonpulsatile blood flow, as observed occasionally.

As for any sternum reentry, careful dissection starting below the xyphoid process is recommended because this gains a nice surface of the posterior surface of the sternum free of adhesions before starting sawing. The major danger of injuring the driveline while the patient is still on assistance is located at the lower end of the sternum. Khoshbin and Schuler and colleagues have recommended wrapping the driveline almost once around the pump itself to divert its path away from the posterior surface of the sternum.26 It is then tunneled from the left costophrenic angle to the desired position in the abdominal wall.

Another important recommendation is to anastomose the outflow graft on the right side of the ascending aorta (and not at the anterior level) and to avoid redundant length that may displace the graft anteriorly against the sternum.

At heart transplantation, the pump itself is prepared free from the pericardium at the apex of the heart using a combination of sharp and blunt dissection. This is similar to explantation of a defibrillator or a pacemaker device from its fibrous pocket. Particular attention should be given to the phrenic nerve that may easily be damaged in case of sharp dissection or overstretching the lateral pericardium. Once the pump has been mobilized, the native heart can be explanted as usual.

#### **Sternotomy Following Less-Invasive Approach Through Right Thoracotomy**

This situation is probably the simplest one as the anterior pericardium has been left intact between the sternum and the cardiac and vascular structures at initial surgery (mitral and/or tricuspid valve). However, sternotomy using the oscillating saw is still recommended in case the pericardium has been left open at the end of the initial procedure and may have retracted. This precaution is also recommended in case of prior aortic valve replacement through a right lateral thoracotomy in the second intercostal space because, in such cases, the pericardium is usually opened in a more anterior fashion to optimize exposure of the ascending aorta.

Usually, adhesions are limited to the right-sided heart structures (right atrium, inferior and superior vena cava, and the atrial incisions). Arterial cannulation is almost always possible in the cranial part of the ascending aorta or alternatively through the subclavian or femoral artery. Venous cannulation may be performed percutaneously or though surgical exposure at the level of the right external iliac vein.

#### **Right-Sided Thoracotomy for Reoperative Mitral Valve Procedures Following Cardiac Procedures (Mitral and/or Aortic Valve, Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting) Through Sternotomy**

Right lateral thoracotomy may be a very attractive approach in cases of reoperative mitral and/or tricuspid valve surgery in a patient following prior mitral/tricuspid valve surgery, aortic valve surgery, or coronary artery bypass grafting.58,59

Peripheral arterial and venous cannulation allows for minimal preparation within the pericardium and only the left (and/ or the right) atrium must be dissected and exposed. This access usually gives nice exposure of the mitral valve anulus, particularly in the subaortic region following previous aortic valve replacement. The procedure is performed on the beating heart without aortic cross-clamping and without cardioplegic arrest. The technique differs from that of a conventional mitral valve procedure and particular attention is directed to de-airing of the heart.

Surgery is performed on normothermic partial cardiopulmonary bypass. Snaring of the superior and inferior vena cava is applied only in case of additional tricuspid valve surgery and when the mitral valve is accessed through the interatrial septum. Venting of the left ventricle is another very important step to prevent any ejection of blood from the left ventricle into the ascending aorta. Nevertheless, an aortic root vent should be placed on the right lateral side of the ascending aorta whenever possible. Left atrial retractors are inserted to maximize exposure of the mitral valve; pulling too strongly on the retractor should be avoided because it may compress the right coronary artery and/or cause significant aortic valve regurgitation. De-airing of the heart at the end of the procedure is a very important step that must be controlled by continuous transoesophageal echocardiography.

#### **Comment and Brief Review of the Most Recent Literature**

One of the most comprehensive analysis on redo-cardiac surgery has been published recently by the group at the Cleveland Clinic.1,2 The authors summarized the main challenges of reoperative cardiac surgery (up to seventh-time sternotomy) as follows: more complex pathology and patient comorbidities on the one side, and risks of inadvertent injury during reentry and mediastinal dissection.

At the same time, they highlighted the advantages of what is today defined as a multidisciplinary team-based approach that integrates all available information of high-resolution imaging, the methods for rescue in case of major injury associated with sternal re-entry and dissection of the cardiac and vascular structures, as well as a balanced appreciation of the most recent percutaneous advances and endovascular adjuncts to avoid or facilitate cardiac reoperations.

The group recommends CT scan as a routine preoperative imaging before reoperative cardiac surgery. If aortic disease is suspected and the renal function is normal, imaging is performed with contrast; otherwise, a native CT scan without contrast is obtained. The information gained through this imaging allows optimal preparation, including prophylactic introduction of wires for quick cannulation or even surgical cutdown to easily access the subclavian artery and/or the femoral vessels. In addition, the authors highlighted the role of collaborative communication among team members in the operating room, regular briefings and debriefings, as well as structured discussion on challenging situations and complications.

With regards to the timing of cannulation, the Cleveland group of authors differentiated between what was called an early (prophylactic cardiopulmonary bypass institution before sternotomy) group versus a late cardiopulmonary bypass (following sternal reentry). A simplified decision algorithm for the optimal strategy in patients requiring a redo-sternotomy but not at high risk for early institution of cardiopulmonary bypass has been proposed by the Cleveland group and is reproduced in Fig. 5.19.

Compared with the late group, prevalence of aortic valve disease and aortic dissection was higher in the early group, although mitral valve and coronary artery disease were lower. The early group had a higher prevalence of bypass grafts and aortic aneurysms/pseudoaneurysms adherent to the sternum. Institution of early cardiopulmonary bypass with active cooling and circulatory arrest was found to be particularly important when aortic injury is highly anticipated based on preoperative imaging. However, avoiding early heparinization and performing more dissection off-pump may be advantageous, particularly in cases requiring extensive and time-consuming dissection, as long as the patient is hemodynamically stable and tolerates cardiac manipulation.

A further important finding of this experience was the fact that intraoperative adverse events, including reentry injuries, were associated with worse outcomes, including mortality, morbidity, and higher hospital costs, but there was no association between outcomes and injured structure or timing of when the injury occurred.4
