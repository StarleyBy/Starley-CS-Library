# Congenital Anomalies of the Coronary Arteries

##### Congenital anomalies of the coronary arteries represent a varied group of lesions seen in 1% to 5% of the population. Approximately 20% have the potential to cause coronary ischemia and its sequelae.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Classification and Overview
* **Prevalence:** Congenital coronary anomalies occur in 1-5% of the population; ~20% have ischemic potential.
* **Major Categories (requiring intervention):** Anomalous coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ACAPA), anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA), and large coronary arteriovenous fistulae.

### Anomalous Coronary Artery from the Pulmonary Artery (ACAPA)
* **Definition:** Anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA) — left main coronary (or LAD/circumflex) connects to pulmonary trunk rather than aorta. Right coronary connects normally to aorta. Collaterals from RCA fill LCA in retrograde fashion, creating coronary steal into pulmonary trunk.
* **Pathophysiology:** Left ventricular myocardial ischemia due to retrograde flow. Left ventricle is dilated, hypertrophied, with diffuse fibrosis. ~65% of untreated infants die in first year.
* **Clinical Presentation (Infants):** Poor feeding (cardinal symptom — takes 2-3 oz then stops with breathlessness, sweating, arching back — presumed angina), heart failure by 2-3 months, circumoral pallor. ECG shows anterolateral infarction (Q waves, ST elevation in lateral leads). Echo shows dilated, poorly contracting LV (EF typically <20%), flow reversal in LCA, dilated RCA.
* **Clinical Presentation (Adults, delayed diagnosis):** Effort angina, fatigue, dyspnea, continuous murmur at upper left sternal edge (retrograde flow into pulmonary trunk). Mitral regurgitation may dominate.
* **Surgical Repair (Coronary Translocation — Preferred):** Median sternotomy, CPB with mild-moderate hypothermia. Snare branch PAs to prevent perfusion steal. Transect pulmonary trunk, excise coronary button with surrounding PA wall, mobilize LCA, create aortotomy in left posterolateral aorta, anastomose coronary button to aorta (7-0 absorbable suture). Patch PA defect, reconstruct PA end-to-end.
* **Takeuchi Repair (Tunnel Operation):** Create aortopulmonary window and tunnel within PA using anterior PA wall flap to connect aorta to remote coronary ostium. Less relevant now but useful when translocation impossible. Complications include tunnel obstruction, PA stenosis, aortic regurgitation.
* **Ligation of LCA (Obsolete):** Abandoned due to clear benefits of dual-coronary circulation.
* **Results:** Early mortality 0-14% in recent series (STS 2007-2016: 2.8%; ECHSA 1999-2019: 6%). Risk factors: weight <5 kg (OR 7.0), preoperative shock (OR 4.6), postoperative ECMO (OR 11.8). Reimplantation vs. other techniques protective for late death/transplant (OR 0.04). LV function normalizes over weeks to months (hibernating myocardium recovery). Mitral regurgitation improves variably; concomitant mitral repair in selected cases (severe regurgitation, structural abnormalities).

### Anomalous Aortic Origin of a Coronary Artery (AAOCA)
* **Definition:** Coronary artery connects to aorta at location other than expected sinus. Most commonly anomalous left coronary from right sinus or anomalous right coronary from left sinus.
* **High-Risk Anatomy:** Interarterial course (between aorta and pulmonary trunk) + intramural segment (within aortic wall) + ostial stenosis/slit-like ostium + intercoronary pillar compression.
* **Pathophysiology (Unclear):** Likely due to ostial abnormalities (slit-like/oval ostium with ellipticity index 2.4-2.5 vs. normal 1.1), intramural segment compression (worsens with systole and exercise), intercoronary pillar compression. Intramural segment shares common media with aorta (elastic fibers rather than smooth muscle).
* **Clinical Presentation:** Majority asymptomatic, diagnosed incidentally. ~50% symptomatic: exertional chest pain, exertional syncope, palpitations, dyspnea. Sudden cardiac arrest as first presentation (mostly during/after exercise). 85% of sudden deaths occur in anomalous left coronary (though anomalous right is 6× more prevalent).
* **Diagnosis:** Echocardiography first, but CTA is mainstay (high spatial resolution, ECG-gated, no sedation needed in infants with "feed and swaddle"). Assess: ostial location, intramural length, interarterial segment, ostial morphology, coronary dominance, intercoronary pillar, transseptal course, myocardial bridges. Functional testing: dobutamine-stress CMR (preferred), stress echocardiography, SPECT, PET-CT. Exercise stress testing has poor correlation.
* **Surgical Techniques:**
  - **Unroofing (Preferred for long intramural segment above commissure):** Incise intramural segment along its length, excise common wall between aorta and coronary, tacking sutures. Moves ostium to correct sinus, relieves stenosis, moves coronary away from intercoronary pillar. Requires intramural segment ≥4-5 mm.
  - **Neo-ostium Creation (Partial unroofing):** For long intramural segment that travels below commissure. Unroof into correct sinus while leaving proximal segment behind commissure intact.
  - **Coronary Reimplantation:** For no intramural segment or short intramural segment (<4-5 mm). Transect coronary at aortic connection, close proximal stump, create new ostium in correct sinus, anastomose.
  - **Transinfundibular Unroofing (For transseptal course):** Open RVOT, unroof intramyocardial coronary segment, patch RVOT with pericardium.
  - **CABG (Reserved for additional atherosclerotic disease):** Not recommended as sole treatment due to competitive flow and graft thrombosis risk.
* **Results:** Excellent early outcomes (mortality near 0). CHSS registry (395 patients): major adverse events 9% (death 0.8%, new moderate/severe AR 1.8%, coronary reintervention 3.2%, postoperative ischemia 3.5%). Manipulation of aortic valve commissure increases AR risk.
* **Indications (Shared decision-making):** Ischemic symptoms or positive functional testing → surgical intervention (Level I). Asymptomatic anomalous left coronary with interarterial course → offer surgery (AATS consensus). Factors: age, anatomy (intramural length, ostial morphology), symptoms, functional testing, athletic identity, anxiety. Exercise restriction may have adverse long-term effects on all-cause mortality and psychosocial impact.

### Coronary Arteriovenous Fistula
* **Definition:** Direct communication between coronary artery and cardiac chamber, coronary sinus, SVC, pulmonary trunk, or pulmonary veins.
* **Morphology:** Originates from RCA in 50-60%, LAD more common for fistulas to pulmonary trunk. Proximal coronary artery is dilated (degree proportional to shunt size). Fistulous opening usually 2-5 mm, may be multiple (16%) or angiomatous (10%). Most (>90%) drain into right heart chambers or great vessels (RV 40%, RA 25%, pulmonary trunk 15-20%, coronary sinus 7%).
* **Clinical Presentation:** Most asymptomatic, diagnosed by continuous murmur. Symptoms: effort dyspnea, fatigue (from left-to-right shunt). Angina in 7-39% (coronary steal). Heart failure in 10-15% (more common in older patients, coronary sinus fistulae 50%). Infective endocarditis in 4-5%.
* **Diagnosis:** Continuous murmur (crescendo-decrescendo, loudest in diastole). Location varies with drainage site. ECG normal in ~50%, otherwise RV or LV overload. CTA excellent for morphology; echocardiography detects enlarged coronaries.
* **Natural History:** Small fistulae may remain small; moderate fistulae slowly increase in size. Heart failure onset peaks in 5th-6th decades. Spontaneous closure rare. Fistula-related death: 1% in patients <20 years, 14% in older patients.
* **Surgical Technique:** Median sternotomy, CPB when artery dilated/tortuous, fistula inaccessible, or aneurysm present. Open receiving chamber, close fistula from within with purse-string suture. For fistula to LV or coronary continues beyond fistula, open coronary and close from within. Excise aneurysms if present; may need CABG if aneurysm involves most of feeding coronary.
* **Transcatheter Closure (Now initial therapy of choice for suitable anatomy):** Coils, vascular occluders (nitinol), covered stents. Complications: vessel dissection/rupture, device embolization, residual fistula, myocardial infarction (thrombus in aneurysmal coronary from stagnant flow).
* **Results:** Hospital mortality near zero (except giant aneurysms). Late complications: coronary thrombosis (34% in pediatric series over 11 years — risk factors: large fistulae, distal type, older age), myocardial infarction (10-11% long-term). Antiplatelet therapy or warfarin recommended for patients with dilated residual coronaries for >1 year.

## Introduction</summary><div class="details-content">

Congenital anomalies of the coronary arteries represent a varied group of lesions. Taken together, they are seen in 1% to 5% of the population, depending on the method of detection. Many anomalies are incidental findings with little or no consequences, but approximately 20% have the potential to cause coronary ischemia and its sequelae.

Box 38.1 presents a useful classification of all congenital coronary artery anomalies adapted from different sources. Some, such as an eccentric location of a coronary artery ostium, multiple ostia, and duplication of coronary arteries, have limited physiologic relevance but may be important if other cardiac procedures are required. Other anomalies such as myocardial bridges, ectasia or aneurysm, and small fistulae may or may not require treatment, depending on whether they have physiologic consequences.

Anomalous coronary artery from the pulmonary artery, anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery, and large coronary arteriovenous fistulae tend to require intervention. This chapter focuses on these anomalies.

## SECTION I: ANOMALOUS CORONARY ARTERY FROM THE PULMONARY ARTERY</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Definition</summary><div class="details-content">

Anomalous left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA) is a congenital cardiovascular malformation in which the left main coronary artery (or uncommonly the left anterior descending or circumflex) connects anomalously with the proximal pulmonary trunk or with one of its branches.

The branching pattern of the anomalous left coronary artery remains normal, and the right coronary artery connects normally to the aorta and has a normal branching pattern. Collaterals from the right coronary artery feed the left coronary artery, in which flow is reversed so the left coronary artery drains into the pulmonary trunk. Very rarely, both coronary arteries connect to the pulmonary trunk by a single trunk.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

In 1885, Brooks in Dublin described, apparently for the first time, anomalous connection of a coronary artery to the pulmonary trunk, and in 1908, Abbott described anomalous connection of left coronary artery to the pulmonary trunk. Bland, White, and Garland in 1933 described the clinical syndrome associated with the anomaly, based on their experience with a 3-month-old infant who died from it. The pathophysiology, as suggested by Brooks in his original paper, is inadequate left ventricular myocardial blood flow—despite good collaterals between right and left coronary arteries—because of retrograde flow from left coronary artery to pulmonary trunk. Edwards supported this hypothesis, as did Case and colleagues in 1958. The latter also reported the postmortem observation that radiopaque dye injected into the ascending aorta passed out through the normal right coronary artery and, by collaterals, filled the left coronary artery in retrograde fashion.

Sabiston and colleagues verified retrograde flow at the first successful operation for the anomaly in 1959 by measuring a striking increase in left coronary artery pressure when its anomalous connection to the pulmonary trunk was occluded. Actual demonstration of left-to-right shunt into the pulmonary trunk was by Agustsson and colleagues in 1962 and by Rudolph and colleagues in 1963.

Earliest surgical attempts to ameliorate the condition were indirect. The first attempt was apparently by W.J. Potts, who created an aortopulmonary (AP) fistula to increase saturation in the pulmonary trunk. Kittle and colleagues banded the pulmonary trunk, and Paul and Robbins used pericardial poudrage. These procedures are obsolete.

Successful ligation of the anomalous left coronary artery connection by Sabiston and colleagues in 1959 was followed by a similar report from Rowe and Young in 1960. As early as 1953, Mustard reported attempts to anastomose the turned-down left common carotid artery to the anomalous left coronary artery that he detached from the pulmonary trunk together with a button of pulmonary trunk wall. Apley and colleagues attempted a similar procedure using the left subclavian artery in 1957. Meyer and colleagues first used this latter procedure successfully to create a two-artery coronary system in 1968, and others, including Pinsky and colleagues, reported such a repair.

In 1966, Cooley and colleagues reported use of coronary artery bypass vein grafting from the aorta to the left main or proximal left anterior descending artery after closing the left coronary ostium from within the pulmonary trunk. The next procedure to evolve was translocation of the anomalous coronary artery from the pulmonary trunk to the ascending aorta. Such a procedure was performed unsuccessfully in 1972 using hypothermic circulatory arrest. This was first performed successfully for the rare condition of abnormal connection of the right coronary artery to the pulmonary trunk (where the artery lies anteriorly and is more readily translocated) by Tingelstad and colleagues in 1971 and for the left coronary artery by Neches and colleagues in 1974. The latter also described successful interposition of a free left subclavian artery segment between the left coronary artery and the back of the ascending aorta.

In 1979, use of a tunnel within the pulmonary trunk to connect the ostium of the anomalous coronary artery to the aorta via an aortopulmonary window was introduced. It was created either of pericardium, as described by Hamilton and colleagues, or of pulmonary trunk wall, as described by Takeuchi and colleagues. Arciniegas and colleagues modified this concept by placing a free subclavian artery graft inside the pulmonary trunk. Reconstructive techniques have been devised to permit implanting coronary arteries that are remote from the aorta. Use of temporary ventricular assistance in infants has been an important adjunct to postoperative management. The aneurysmal left ventricular wall was excised unsuccessfully in 1960. This procedure, combined with ligation of the left coronary artery, was subsequently performed successfully by Turina and colleagues in 1973 and Flemma and colleagues in 1975.

### Morphogenesis and Morphology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Morphogenesis</summary><div class="details-content">

Important advances have been made in our understanding of the embryologic origin of the coronary arteries over the last decade. Initially, while the embryonic interventricular communication is still open, the ventricular walls are composed of a trabecular meshwork that allows nourishment of the cardiomyocytes by direct perfusion from the ventricular cavities. As the compact component of the ventricular wall grows, coalescing mural and epicardial vessels are developed. Separately, endocardial sprouts originate from the walls of the aortic root adjacent to the pulmonary trunk and distal to the outflow cushions that will develop the arterial valve cusps and thus high in the aorta. As the aortic root separates from the pulmonary trunk, ongoing growth of the aortic root develops the aortic sinuses and incorporates the orifices of the coronary arteries within the newly formed sinuses, thus moving those origins proximally to below the sinutubular junction. The endocardial sprouts from the aorta then connect with the network of epicardial and mural vessels, thus establishing antegrade coronary blood flow. That is, the proximal coronary arteries grow *from* the peritruncal area *into* the aorta with formation of single orifices for left and right coronary arteries. It is for this reason that since the second edition of this book, Dr. John Kirklin and subsequent editors have abandoned the former term *anomalous origin* or *arising from* in favor of *anomalous connection*. The origin of the coronary arteries from the correct sinuses of Valsalva within the aorta is thus partly dependent on the normal separation of the arterial roots and formation of the sinuses.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Morphology</summary><div class="details-content">

The anomalous left main coronary artery connects most often to the sinus of Valsalva immediately above the posterior cusp of the pulmonary trunk (from the posterior sinus facing the left coronary sinus) and rarely from that above the lateral cusp (nonfacing sinus). The left main coronary artery is of variable length but usually divides into anterior descending and circumflex branches within 5 or 6 mm of its origin. Collateral communications between right and left coronary arteries are always present but vary in extent. Uncommonly, only the circumflex branch connects anomalously to the pulmonary trunk and rarely only the left anterior descending branch.

Very rarely, the left main coronary artery or only the circumflex artery connects to the right pulmonary artery near its origin rather than to the pulmonary trunk. Even more unusual is the connection of both left and right coronary arteries to the pulmonary trunk (see "Anomalous Coronary Artery from the Pulmonary Artery in Adults" under Special Situations and Controversies later in this section).

The left ventricle is always hypertrophied and usually greatly dilated, with dilation often involving primarily the left ventricular apex. Diffuse left ventricular fibrosis is virtually always present, and patients dying in infancy usually have evidence of recent and old anterolateral myocardial infarction. Fibrosis is most marked in the subendocardial layer. Focal calcification may be present in fibrotic areas. Secondary subendocardial fibroelastosis of variable degree is usually present. However, a considerable amount of left ventricular dysfunction, in infants at least, must be ischemic in origin because of dramatic improvement in left ventricular function that can result from an operation that creates a two-artery coronary system. Improved left ventricular function is not immediate but occurs over weeks to months. Chronic ischemia accompanying this lesion results in devitalization (or adaptive response) of the myocardium at a cellular and biochemical level. Devitalized myocardium has been termed *hibernating* myocardium. Devitalized muscle slowly recovers over the time described once adequate blood flow and oxygen delivery are established.

Several pathologic features may result in mitral valve regurgitation. There may be extensive fibrosis and sometimes calcification in the papillary muscles, leading to dysfunction. Endocardial fibroelastosis may involve the mitral apparatus, with fusion and shortening of chordae tendineae. Also, papillary muscles may be abnormally positioned, leading to mitral regurgitation. Extensive left ventricular fibrosis can produce left ventricular and mitral anular dilation and mitral regurgitation. Reversible left ventricular ischemia must, to some degree, contribute to the mitral valve regurgitation in infants because, in most cases, it decreases to an important extent after surgical treatment and creation of a two-artery coronary system.

### Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Clinical Findings</summary><div class="details-content">

Symptoms may be recognized within a week or so of birth. When there are no other anomalies, these are seldom severe enough to warrant referral before age 2 months. Presumably, high postnatal pulmonary artery pressure limits runoff into the pulmonary trunk, so there is less coronary steal, and myocardial dysfunction is gradual in onset rather than sudden. Circumoral pallor and blueness are often present. The cardinal symptom is poor feeding. The baby takes the first 2 to 3 ounces well but then stops; there is breathlessness and sweating, and the baby may draw up the knees, arch the back, and uncommonly, cry or scream. The presumed cause is angina. As a result of the feeding problem, weight gain is poor. Few infants with these symptoms improve spontaneously. Usually, by age 2 to 3 months, there is overt heart failure with persistent tachypnea and tachycardia. The infant, by then, is seriously ill and occasionally moribund.

Clinical signs are difficult to distinguish from those of cardiomyopathy. There may be a nonspecific systolic murmur at the base, or a more definite apical pansystolic murmur caused by mitral regurgitation, and an apical gallop rhythm. A continuous murmur is not audible in infants. A precordial lift is common in association with marked and frequently gross cardiomegaly. Hepatomegaly is also present, and rales are heard throughout the lungs.

In some patients, a rich collateral circulation from the right coronary artery may provide adequate circulation to the left coronary artery system. These patients may have a delayed presentation even beyond the age 20 years. Some adults remain asymptomatic or complain only of fatigue, dyspnea, or palpitations. About half have effort angina. There usually is a nonspecific systolic murmur, sometimes an apical pansystolic murmur from mitral regurgitation, and occasionally a continuous murmur over the upper left sternal edge due to retrograde flow from the coronary artery into the pulmonary trunk. Occasionally, mitral regurgitation dominates the clinical picture, producing heart failure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Electrocardiogram</summary><div class="details-content">

The electrocardiogram (ECG) is frequently helpful in diagnosis because it usually shows anterolateral infarction with Q waves and ST-segment elevation in lateral chest leads. Because 20% to 45% of patients do not show abnormal Q waves, the diagnosis should be strongly suspected in cases of abnormal R-wave progression. The resting ECG is virtually always abnormal in patients presenting late with ST-T segment changes or evidence of old anterolateral infarction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Chest Radiography</summary><div class="details-content">

The chest radiograph almost universally shows cardiomegaly. In addition, interstitial pulmonary edema may be evident in infants with heart failure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Echocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography in infants shows a dilated, poorly contracting left ventricle (with ejection fraction typically <20%) and reveals the functional status and morphology of the mitral valve. In addition, 2D and pulsed Doppler echocardiography may detect an abnormally large right coronary artery and anomalous connection of the left coronary artery to the pulmonary trunk, with retrograde flow in it (Fig. 38.1). The most important markers visualized on echocardiography include, in order of decreasing sensitivity, flow reversal in the left main coronary artery, collateral coronary flow, dilation of the right coronary artery, abnormal Doppler signal in the pulmonary artery, mitral regurgitation, left ventricular systolic dysfunction, left ventricular fibroelastosis, and visualization of an anomalous coronary connection. Visualization on echocardiography of the left coronary artery arising from the aorta is not a reliable sign to rule out an anomalous pulmonary connection of the left coronary artery, and further imaging should be pursued if there is a suspicion for it otherwise. In many cases, echocardiography alone may be diagnostic enough to proceed with surgical correction, especially in patients who present in extremis and require emergency surgical repair.

#### Computerized Tomographic Angiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Advances in computerized tomographic angiography (CTA) imaging and ECG gating have made CTA an essential adjuvant in the diagnosis of coronary artery anomalies (Fig. 38.2). CTA identifies the exact origin and course of the anomalous coronary, the branching of the vessel, the presence of collaterals, and the morphology of the right coronary artery, which is usually dilated.

#### Cardiac Magnetic Resonance</summary><div class="details-content">

The role that cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) plays in the diagnosis of these anomalies is limited, especially due to its lower spatial resolution compared to CTA and the need for sedation in infants. In some patients with late presentation and stable clinical course, CMR may provide valuable information regarding myocardial function and viability, left ventricular dimensions and dynamics, regional wall motion abnormalities, and mitral regurgitation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Cardiac Catheterization</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac catheterization and coronary angiography were previously used to provide definitive diagnosis but are now seldom necessary. Their use is limited to cases in which other noninvasive modalities are inconclusive or to help define other associated anomalies not assessed by other means. An aortogram demonstrates connection of the single right coronary artery to the aorta and retrograde filling of the left coronary artery through collaterals, which produces a varying degree of opacification of the pulmonary trunk (Fig. 38.3). A left ventriculogram can be used to assess left ventricular function and degree of mitral regurgitation.

#### Other Tests</summary><div class="details-content">

In infants, myocardial enzymes may be elevated. In patients presenting late, exercise ECG usually shows an abnormal ischemic response. Myocardial viability studies such as stress thallium myocardial imaging, stress echocardiography, stress CMR, and positron emission tomography scans may be used to evaluate myocardial viability, but their use is limited because they are unlikely to change patient management.

### Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

Anomalous connection of left coronary artery to pulmonary trunk is rare, occurring in 0.26% of patients with congenital heart disease undergoing cardiac catheterization. About 65% of infants born with it die during the first year from intractable left ventricular failure (Fig. 38.4). However, they uncommonly do so in the first 2 months of life. Explanation for this symptom-free interval is unclear because extensive left ventricular scarring, particularly of the subendocardium, and evidence of old and recent infarction are usually present by then. It likely results from a combination of initially elevated pulmonary artery pressure, which limits the runoff, and gradual accumulation of myocyte dysfunction and loss.

If death does not occur during the first year, the hazard lessens considerably, and the chronic phase of natural history is reached. Survival to this stage may be related to rich interarterial collaterals, possibly associated with a slightly restrictive opening between the left coronary artery and pulmonary trunk. Supporting this is a continuous murmur heard in about 5% of patients. Many such patients are healthy and a few have normal ECGs. Survival beyond the first year may also be related to marked right coronary dominance, with this vessel supplying not only the diaphragmatic portion of the left ventricle but also much of the septum and lateral wall. Patients with this arrangement may occasionally only have papillary muscle ischemia and fibrosis, and mitral regurgitation may dominate the clinical picture.

Most patients who survive infancy continue to be at risk of death from chronic heart failure secondary to ischemic left ventricular cardiomyopathy. Those who survive until the fourth decade are at less risk of death from heart failure (see Fig. 38.4), and those few patients who live to the fifth and sixth decades occasionally die suddenly, as do older patients with long-standing ischemic heart disease. In adult patients, myocardial ischemia and fibrosis are prominent, and occasionally, extensive myocardial calcification develops. However, left ventricular ejection fraction is only moderately depressed or normal in most of these patients.

### Technique of Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Coronary Translocation</summary><div class="details-content">

The aim of surgical repair is to construct a two-artery coronary system. Direct translocation of the anomalous coronary artery into the aortic root is almost always possible with appropriate mobilization. Smith and colleagues found the distance between the midpoint of the empty left aortic sinus and the posterior aspect of the anomalously connected coronary artery to vary between 2 and 18 mm. The longer distances probably preclude direct implant and may require alternative lengthening procedures.

The operation is best done with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and mild to moderate hypothermia to allow for potentially decreasing bypass flows intermittently due to significant coronary return. After sternotomy, the pericardium is opened *without touching the heart* because even the slightest trauma can induce ventricular fibrillation. Cannulation is achieved through direct aortic cannulation and either single venous or bicaval cannulation. Immediately before commencing CPB, tourniquets are placed around the left and right pulmonary arteries. The tourniquets are tightened as CPB is initiated to prevent perfusion steal into the pulmonary bed from collateral flow through the anomalous left coronary artery. If present, the patent ductus arteriosus is ligated.

After application of an aortic clamp, cardioplegia is administered directly into the aortic root. Myocardial protection during aortic clamping is particularly important for two reasons: the existing compromised state of the myocardium and the potential for inadequate delivery of cardioplegic solution to the left ventricle. It is important that both pulmonary arteries are appropriately snared to prevent runoff of cardioplegia into the pulmonary bed. A left-sided vent is placed through a purse string in the right upper pulmonary vein or through the atrial septum if the right atrium is opened.

After administering antegrade aortic cardioplegia, the branch pulmonary artery tourniquets are removed. A transverse incision is made in the pulmonary trunk just distal to the commissure of the pulmonary valve. Additional cardioplegia is administered directly into the ostium of the anomalous left coronary artery.

Direct translocation of the anomalous coronary artery is easier when it arises from the posterior right-sided aspect of the pulmonary trunk (Fig. 38.5A). The pulmonary trunk is completely transected several millimeters above the level of the anomalous coronary (Fig. 38.5B). The incision is arranged so that a sizable button of pulmonary trunk wall around the coronary ostium is excised (Fig. 38.5B). The left coronary artery is mobilized with care not to injure the vessel or its branches. An opening is made in the adjacent left posterolateral portion of the aorta (Fig. 38.5C). This opening can be performed either as an oval-shaped opening or as a medially based trapdoor to keep an appropriate orientation of the coronary. It is imperative not to injure the aortic valve while creating the opening. Some surgeons create an anterior aortotomy to visualize the aortic valve before creating the coronary translocation opening. The button around the coronary ostium is anastomosed to the aorta with 7-0 monofilament absorbable sutures, the coronary artery explant site is patched, and the pulmonary trunk is reconstructed by end-to-end anastomosis (see Fig. 38.5C).

The remainder of the operation is completed in the usual fashion. Weaning the patient from CPB may require patience, intravenous nitroglycerin, judicious inotropic support, and monitoring of left and right atrial pressures to avoid distention of the heart.

#### Tunnel Operation (Takeuchi Repair)</summary><div class="details-content">

The Takeuchi repair has been used for cases where the distance between the anomalous left coronary artery and the aorta is too long. As surgeons have gained more experience with coronary artery translocation as part of the arterial switch operation (see "Arterial Switch Operation" in Chapter 44), it has become clear that coronary translocation is possible in virtually all instances. This realization, in addition to the potential complications associated with the Takeuchi repair, has made this technique less relevant, but it remains a useful tool in the surgical armamentarium.

A button of aortic wall about 5 to 6 mm in diameter is excised at a point at which the left wall of the aorta is in contact with the right side of the pulmonary trunk, taking special care to avoid injury to the aortic valve cusps (Fig. 38.7A). Directly opposite this, a button is excised from the right wall of the pulmonary trunk (Fig. 38.7B [also see *A*]). These openings are sewn together with continuous 7-0 polypropylene to create an aortopulmonary window.

Using a flap of anterior pulmonary trunk wall hinged on the right, the anterior wall of the tunnel is created, completing the tunnel conveying blood from the aortopulmonary window across the back of the pulmonary trunk to the anomalously connecting left coronary artery (Fig. 38.7C [also see *B*]). The large defect in the anterior wall of the pulmonary trunk is reconstructed with a patch of pericardium, pulmonary trunk allograft, or PTFE (Fig. 38.7D). Occasionally, this operation narrows the immediately supravalvar portion of the pulmonary trunk sufficiently to require a transannular patch.

#### Ligation of Left Coronary Artery</summary><div class="details-content">

Treatment by simple ligation of the left coronary artery has been largely abandoned. This is due to the clear benefits of establishing a dual-coronary circulation and the long-term complications associated with ligation.

### Special Features of Postoperative Care</summary><div class="details-content">

Infants tend to present with marginal cardiac output and severely depressed ventricular function. As such, hemodynamics may be marginal postoperatively. A left atrial pressure monitoring catheter should be used to guide postoperative resuscitation and inotropic management. Fluid status should be carefully managed, as unnecessary preload will lead to ventricular distention and worsening hemodynamics.

In critically ill small infants, low cardiac output can be anticipated during the first few postoperative days, and appropriate measures apply (see "Cardiovascular Subsystem" in Section I of Chapter 4). These measures may include use of temporary left ventricular assistance (see "Temporary Ventricular Assistance" in Section I of Chapter 4) or extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support (see "Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation Support" in Section II in Chapter 20).

Antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation has not been used routinely for this patient population but may be considered in select cases, especially on those presenting late with important collateral flow and coronary dilation, in an attempt to prevent coronary thrombosis.

</div></details>

### Results</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Survival</summary><div class="details-content">

**Early (Hospital) Death.** Early mortality after surgical repair has improved dramatically over the last several decades. Mortality in various subsets of patients from series reported from 1975 to 1980 ranged from 0% to 75%. Perioperative mortality in more recent series ranges between 0% and 14%, with several studies showing no early deaths (Table 38.1). Improvements in survival are likely related to a combination of improved postoperative support, the use of surgical techniques favoring a dual-coronary circulation, and the ability to provide postoperative temporary mechanical support.

**Time-Related Survival.** Several single-institutional studies report on the long-term survival of patients after surgical repair. Lange and colleagues report 20-year survival of 86%±64% among 78 patients who underwent surgical repair between 1977 and 2015 with a median follow-up of 21 years. The 10-year survival for those undergoing repair after 1995 was 97%±63%. Another study of 42 patients and a median follow-up of 14 years (range 4 months to 31 years) reported no late deaths, with 20-year survival of 98%.

A few other clinical studies have been conducted using larger multi-institutional databases. Fudulu and colleagues conducted an extensive study using a national database in the United Kingdom encompassing all procedures for repair of anomalous connection of coronary arteries to pulmonary trunk between 2000 and 2013. Of the 120 patients that underwent repair during that time, 102 had long-term follow-up data. Ten-year survival was 95.1% (95% confidence interval 88.7% to 97.9%), with all deaths occurring during the first year. Another study by Thomas and colleagues linked records of children undergoing repair in the Pediatric Cardiac Care Consortium between 1982 and 2003 with the National Death Index and the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network to examine outcomes through 2019. Of the 155 patients with identifiers who survived to discharge, 3 experienced a cardiovascular death, and 3 other modes of death during a median follow-up of 23 years (interquartile range 19 to 28 years). The 30-year transplant-free survival among those discharged alive was 95.5% (95% confidence interval 92.3% to 98.8%).

#### Incremental Risk Factors for Premature Death After Repair</summary><div class="details-content">

There is an early, rapidly declining hazard phase for death after repair (perioperative mortality) followed by a low constant phase.

Preoperative clinical status is associated with an increased risk of early death after repair. In a large study of 703 patients who underwent repair of anomalous connection of coronary arteries to pulmonary trunk in the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Congenital Heart Surgery Database, the odds of mortality were higher in patients <5 kg in weight, if preoperative shock was present, and if extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support was required (Table 38.2). Similarly, among 907 patients repaired in the European Congenital Heart Surgeons Association Database, hospital mortality was associated with lower weight, smaller body surface area, longer length of CPB, longer postoperative ventilation (intubation) time, and use of postoperative mechanical support.

According to the study by Thomas and colleagues, and contrary to the findings regarding risk factors for early death, repair in infancy appears to portend a lower risk for long-term (postdischarge) death or transplant after repair (Table 38.3). Another major factor associated with late mortality in that study was use of surgical techniques other than coronary reimplant. This finding was also reported by Radman and colleagues in which a composite outcome of reintervention, transplantation, and death was associated with use of the Takeuchi procedure or other atypical repairs (hazard ratio 8, 95% confidence interval 2.1 to 30) and presence of moderate or worse mitral regurgitation on discharge echocardiogram (hazard ratio 3.4, 95% confidence interval 1.2 to 9.1).

#### Functional Status</summary><div class="details-content">

Functional status is generally good late-postoperatively. Of 21 patients assessed by Cochrane and colleagues, 18 were in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class I and 3 in class II at midterm follow-up. Similarly, Ojala and colleagues reported on 29 patients who underwent coronary reimplant and found 23 patients in NYHA class I and 6 in class II at a median follow-up of 11 years.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Left Ventricular Function and Dimensions</summary><div class="details-content">

Despite important ventricular dysfunction at presentation, left ventricular function and dimensions improve dramatically after surgical repair, but in many patients, the process can be slow, with some patients requiring inotropic support for several months. Cabrera and colleagues reported an improvement in left ventricular ejection fraction in 34 patients from 21%±6% preoperatively to 41%±6% at hospital discharge, and 60%±7% at last follow-up with a median follow-up of 6 years. Naimo and colleagues analyzed serial postoperative echocardiograms for 32 patients after repair and showed that left ventricular function was normal at a median of 9.5 months (range 2.3 to 16 months). Similar findings were reported by Radman and colleagues who found that most of 98 patients with available data at 3 years after discharge had normal left ventricular function. Weigand and colleagues showed normalization of function in all 25 patients that they followed with a median time to normalization of 91 days (range 5 days to 1.2 years).

In addition to preoperative ventricular function, late presentation plays an important role in degree of improvement in function. In a study involving 105 patients, Zhang and colleagues created a classification and regression tree analysis to identify 3 distinct risk groups for early mortality based on preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction and age at repair. They found that improved left ventricular function depended on the risk group, with patients having a preoperative ejection fraction >41.6% having normalization of ventricular function at 4 days, patients having preoperative ejection fraction ≤41.6% and age ≤127 days having normalization of function in 39 days, and those having preoperative ejection fraction >41.6% and age >127 days having normalization of function in 486 days (Figs. 38.8 and 38.9).

Left ventricular dimensions also improve over time, with left ventricular end-diastolic dimension normalizing after 2 to 3 years. Left ventricular reverse remodeling, defined as a ≥10% decrease in left ventricular dimensions, has been associated with better outcomes. These findings indicate that in many patients, severe left ventricular dysfunction present preoperatively is due to reversible devitalization of the myocardium, or myocardial *hibernation,* as opposed to myocardial *stunning* (see "Myocardial Cell Stunning" under Damage from Global Myocardial Ischemia in Chapter 3).

Normalization of left ventricular function may not represent normal myocardium. Using speckle-tracking echocardiography and CMR, Castaldi and colleagues analyzed 10 asymptomatic patients with normal ventricular function who had undergone surgical repair 8.7±4.7 years prior. Using strain and strain rates, they found that longitudinal and circumferential functions were reduced in the left coronary artery territory compared to the right and to normal controls. In addition, CMR identified 3 patients with left coronary artery stenosis; they had the lowest longitudinal strain values in left coronary territories, mildly depressed circumferential strain, and normal radial strain. Another study using speckle-tracking on 18 patients a median of 17 years after repair showed that despite normal ventricular function, longitudinal strain and diastolic function were impaired in all cardiac chambers, particularly in the left coronary territory. Similar findings were encountered by Di Salvo and colleagues on 30 patients after repair. Despite normal left ventricular dimensions and ejection fraction, diastolic variables were abnormal, and left ventricular longitudinal strain and torsion were impaired compared to normal patients.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Mitral Regurgitation</summary><div class="details-content">

Mitral regurgitation frequently exists at presentation in these patients. In addition to left ventricular dysfunction, mechanisms of regurgitation include anular dilation, anterior or posterior leaflet prolapse, papillary muscle infarction and fibrosis, and mitral clefts.

Whether to perform mitral valve intervention during initial repair in patients with mitral regurgitation remains controversial. It has been long argued that the basis for regurgitation is ischemia and is reversible. Therefore, the mitral valve should not be addressed routinely at initial operation for the anomalous coronary artery. If residual regurgitation is present, it can be addressed once the ventricle has recovered. Others have advocated mitral repair in selected cases, such as when regurgitation is moderate or severe, or there are structural abnormalities in the mitral valve. As such, the use of mitral repair as part of the initial operation is variable, ranging from 0% to 50%, with large multi-institutional studies reporting 8% and 16%.

Improvement in mitral regurgitation over time is not as predictable as improvement in ventricular function (see Fig. 38.9). Regardless of the strategy used, it is clear that need for mitral valve intervention in the future is not ignorable. Patients older than age 1 may be at a higher risk of persistent or recurrent important mitral regurgitation in the future.

In a series by Naimo and colleagues involving 42 patients, concomitant repair was undertaken in 9 patients, 5 with severe regurgitation and 4 with moderate. Freedom from late mitral valve intervention at 5 and 10 years in patients that did not undergo repair was 86%±6% (95% confidence interval 71% to 94%). Of the 11 patients with severe regurgitation, 5 (45%) underwent repair at the time of initial anomalous coronary artery repair, 3 underwent intervention later on, and the remaining 3 had mild regurgitation at last follow-up. Zhang and colleagues analyzed 101 patients who underwent anomalous coronary artery repair in the setting of more than mild mitral regurgitation; 66 (65%) underwent concomitant mitral intervention. In their series, cumulative improvement in mitral regurgitation (to ≤mild regurgitation) was 34% (95% confidence interval 19% to 50%) among those who underwent mitral intervention compared to 59% (95% confidence interval 41% to 73%) among those who did not. In patients who underwent mitral intervention, lower grade of mitral regurgitation on postoperative day 1 was the only independent predictor of long-term mitral regurgitation improvement (hazard ratio 0.08, 95% confidence interval 0.018 to 0.366). In contrast, in patients who did not undergo intervention, only a preoperative lower mitral valve anulus diameter z-score was associated with long-term mitral regurgitation improvement (hazard ratio 0.48, 95% confidence interval 0.232 to 0.993). In the multiinstitutional study by Thomas and colleagues, 18 (8%) patients underwent mitral valve intervention at the time of repair, with no perioperative deaths. In the cohort of patients with moderate or severe mitral regurgitation, undergoing a mitral valve operation was associated with a 28% unadjusted risk reduction in perioperative mortality compared to those that did not undergo intervention (relative survival ratio 1.28, *P* = .033).

Based on these reports, one can conclude that (1) mitral regurgitation is common on presentation, (2) it tends to improve with time but improvement is unpredictable, (3) mitral valve intervention during initial repair appears safe, (4) mitral intervention is generally not justified for patients with less than severe regurgitation, and (5) some patients with severe mitral regurgitation may benefit from initial repair, although which particular patients remains unclear.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

Diagnosis of anomalous connection of left coronary artery to pulmonary trunk is an indication for surgical intervention. In an infant, regardless of clinical status, repair should be performed urgently. The goal of the operation should be to create a dual-coronary artery circulation based on the clear short- and long-term advantages over ligation of the anomalous coronary.

Translocation of the left coronary artery is possible in nearly all pediatric cases and is the procedure of choice. In patients in whom the anatomy is unfavorable, particularly in adult patients, placing an interposition graft, performing a Takeuchi repair, or placing a coronary artery bypass graft with ligation of the proximal coronary artery may be considered. Whether there is a need to address the mitral valve in the setting of severe mitral regurgitation is unclear (see "Mitral Regurgitation" under Results in preceding text).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Special Situations and Controversies</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Anomalous Coronary Artery from the Pulmonary Artery in Adults</summary><div class="details-content">

Diagnosis in adults is unusual and poses additional challenges. Most patients are symptomatic, presenting with chest pain, palpitations, and symptoms of heart failure. The majority have evidence of ischemia on functional testing. In adults, direct implant of the anomalous coronary artery is challenging due to friability of the vessels and loss of elasticity. If tension-free translocation is not possible, placing an interposition graft or performing coronary artery bypass grafting with ligation of the proximal artery may be necessary.

Yau and colleagues performed a comprehensive literature search of all adult cases of anomalous connection of left coronary artery to pulmonary trunk reported in the literature. They found 151 cases with an average age of 41 years, the oldest age 83. Only 14% were asymptomatic; 17% presented with syncope, arrhythmias, or sudden cardiac death, and 66% presented with other symptoms. The majority had functional tests indicative of ischemia. Among the 119 patients who underwent surgical intervention, 79% had a dual-coronary circulation established. The remainder had simple ligation of the anomalous coronary artery. Surgical mortality was reported to be 1% to 4%.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Anomalous Right Coronary Artery, Circumflex Coronary Artery, or Left Anterior Descending Coronary Artery from the Pulmonary Artery</summary><div class="details-content">

An anomaly rarer than anomalous connection of left coronary artery to pulmonary trunk is anomalous connection of the right coronary artery to the pulmonary trunk. The common name for this defect is anomalous right coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ARCAPA).

A systematic review by Guenther and colleagues in 2020 identified 223 cases in the literature. Undoubtedly, this lesion is underdiagnosed because of its relatively benign nature compared with anomalous left coronary artery. Many patients are asymptomatic; the diagnosis is most commonly made during workup for a murmur. It is occasionally associated with symptoms in an older child or adult or sudden death. There is a bimodal age distribution in symptomatic patients with a peak during infancy and another between ages 40 and 60 years. Treatment is similar to anomalous left coronary artery to pulmonary trunk, with the majority of patients undergoing coronary reimplantation to the aorta.

Anomalous connection of circumflex and left anterior descending coronary artery to pulmonary trunk are also less lethal than an anomalous left coronary artery, although there have been cases presenting with sudden cardiac arrest. Both are also rarer than anomalous connection of right coronary artery to the pulmonary trunk. Indications for surgery and technique of operation are similar to those for anomalous connection of the right coronary artery. Operation is generally indicated at the time of diagnosis for all these variations.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Anomalous All Coronary Arteries from the Pulmonary Artery</summary><div class="details-content">

Rarely, all coronary blood flow originates from the pulmonary trunk, either with a single ostium and trunk from which all branches emerge or from two ostia close together, giving rise to left and right coronary systems. In less than half the cases reported, this has been an isolated anomaly. In such cases, symptoms appear within a few days of birth, and death follows within 2 weeks. A systematic review identified 57 cases in the literature with 45% of cases diagnosed at autopsy. Surgical repair was performed in 22 (46%) with use of aortic translocation in 14 and Takeuchi repair in 7. Due to the high risk of mortality with this lesion, surgical repair should be instituted promptly after diagnosis.

</div></details>

## SECTION II: ANOMALOUS AORTIC ORIGIN OF A CORONARY ARTERY</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Definition</summary><div class="details-content">

Anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA) is a congenital cardiovascular malformation in the setting of ventriculoarterial concordant connection in which a coronary artery connects to the aorta at a location other than the expected sinus. It includes anomalies where either (1) the left main coronary artery connects to the aorta in a site other than the left coronary sinus, (2) the right coronary artery connects to a site other than the right coronary sinus, or (3) the circumflex connects to the right coronary sinus or proximal right coronary artery. Connection of the circumflex to the proximal right coronary artery is the most common anomaly, but this is rarely of clinical importance as an isolated entity. The anomalous circumflex artery takes a posterior course around the aortic root in approximation to the aortic valve anulus, and vessel compromise during aortic valve replacement has been reported.

The anomalously connected coronary artery to the aorta frequently passes between the aorta and pulmonary trunk *(interarterial course)* before normally distributing to the myocardium and commonly has a proximal course running within the aortic wall *(intramural course)*. It occasionally has ostial stenosis.

Anomalous aortic origin of a main coronary artery to aorta, particularly one with an interarterial segment, has been associated with risk of sudden cardiac death, and it remains unclear which set of anatomic variations predisposes to ischemia and sudden cardiac death. Whether the course between the great arteries plays a pathophysiologic role in all clinical events remains controversial. The anomalously connected artery does not always pass between the aorta and pulmonary trunk but may have a retroaortic, prepulmonic, or transseptal course. Rarely, the anomalous artery does not connect to the opposite coronary sinus but rather to the posterior ("noncoronary") sinus.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

An anomalous connection of a main coronary artery to aorta was first described in 1952 by Nicod, in a 21-year-old male who suffered sudden cardiac death while performing gymnastics at a military school. The first description of the anomaly in the English literature was by Jokl and colleagues in 1962, who described a 14-year-old high school student who died after participating in a cross-country race. Cheitlin and colleagues in 1974 described a series of 51 cases from the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology. Of these, 33 had anomalous connection of the left coronary artery to the right sinus, and 9 (27%) had sudden unexplained deaths. In 1983, Brandt and colleagues described a patient with anomalous connection of the right coronary artery to the left sinus of Valsalva who presented with myocardial ischemia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Morphology</summary><div class="details-content">

The pathophysiological mechanisms related to ischemia and sudden cardiac death in anomalous connection of a main coronary artery to aorta are largely unclear but likely related to a set of morphologic characteristics of the anomalous coronary. They include presence of ostial abnormalities, angulation of the ostium, compression of an intramural segment (a segment of the anomalous coronary that travels within the wall of the aorta before entering the aortic ostium), compression of the interarterial segment (the segment of the coronary that travels between the great arteries), and coronary spasm.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Connections</summary><div class="details-content">

In the normal heart, the left coronary artery connects to the left "facing" sinus (the leftward posterior sinus facing the pulmonary trunk), and the right coronary artery connects to the right anterior facing sinus (the anterior sinus facing the pulmonary trunk). A separate connection of a conal or accessory branch is a normal variation. The normal ostia are located in the middle of the sinus and below the level of the sinutubular junction.

Mery and colleagues have described a topography nomenclature map to facilitate description of the location of connections of the coronary arteries (Fig. 38.10). This map is consistent with the modified Leiden classification commonly used to describe connections of the coronary arteries in the setting of transposition of the great arteries (see Chapter 44). The topography map assigns a number to each sinus (1 for the right-facing sinus, 2 for the left-facing sinus, and 3 for the nonfacing sinus). It divides each sinus into a, b, or c, depending on proximity to the commissures. Commissural origins are denoted as X, Y, or Z. Height of the coronary ostium is designated using Roman numerals: I for central connection to the sinus, II for connection to the sinus above the level of the free edge of the open cusps but below the sinutubular junction, III for connection to the sinutubular junction, and IV for connection above the sinutubular junction (ascending aorta). Based on this topographic nomenclature, the normal left coronary artery connects to 2b-I or 2b-II, and the normal right coronary artery connects to 1b-I or 1b-II. This system allows for uniform description and communication among surgeons, radiologists, and cardiologists.

There is wide variability of connection and course of the coronary arteries in this anomaly. Most anomalous coronaries connect to the opposite sinus of Valsalva just above the level of the intercoronary commissure of the aortic valve. This corresponds to connection 1c-II or 1c-III for the anomalous left coronary artery and 2a-II or 2a-III for the anomalous right coronary artery. Sometimes, the anomalous artery can originate inferior to the level of the commissure (level I) or higher on the ascending aorta (level IV).

There is also wide variability in the coronary ostia (Fig. 38.11). Although the anomalous coronary artery tends to connect to an ostium separate from (Type 1) or adjacent to (Type 2) the normal opposite coronary artery, it can also originate from a single ostium that bifurcates within the wall (Type 3) or a single coronary artery trunk that bifurcates within the mediastinum (Type 4).

The normal coronary arteries connect to round ostia at nearly orthogonal angles to the aortic wall. The size of the ostia is equal to or larger than the diameter of the corresponding coronary artery. As anomalous coronary arteries connect at tangential angles, their ostia are acutely angulated and abnormal, and at risk for compression. The ostium of the anomalous coronary, therefore, tends to be either oval (with the transverse diameter equal to 50% to 90% of the longitudinal diameter) or slit-like (transverse diameter <50% of the longitudinal diameter).

Using virtual angioscopy from CMR studies in 27 patients, Harris and colleagues found that all anomalous coronaries had ostia with a high ellipticity index (ratio of vertical to horizontal diameter) of 2.4 to 2.5±0.5 compared to normal 1.1±0.1.

#### Course</summary><div class="details-content">

The anomalous coronary artery can take different courses to its normal distribution area – prepulmonic, interarterial, subpulmonic, retroaortic, and retrocardiac. An anomalous coronary artery with an interarterial segment is the lesion mostly associated with increased risk of sudden cardiac death. It tends to have a variable length of intramurality in which the vessel is within the wall of the aorta before exiting into the mediastinum. It is believed that intramurality plays an important role in pathophysiology of sudden cardiac death. Using intravascular ultrasound (IVUS), Angelini and colleagues demonstrated that the proximal intramural course of the anomalous coronary can be importantly compressed, which worsens during systole and even further during simulated exercise. The intramural segment seems to play a more important role in ischemia than the actual location of connection of the coronary artery.

Histologically, the intramural segment of the anomalous coronary shares a common media with the aorta. Its wall is mainly composed of elastic fibers instead of smooth muscle as is the case for epicardial coronary arteries.

A morphological structure that is underappreciated in AAOCA, but that likely plays an important role in pathophysiology of ischemia and sudden cardiac death, is the intercoronary pillar. The intercoronary pillar is a thickening of aortic tissue that travels cranially from the level of the intercoronary commissure up to the sinutubular junction and can be quite thick in some individuals. The anomalous coronary can be subject to important compression as it courses behind this thickening of tissue.

In some instances, an anomalous left coronary artery or left anterior descending artery connecting to the right coronary artery or right sinus of Valsalva will have a transseptal course rather than an interarterial course. In these cases, the anomalous artery dives deep into the ventricular septum and has an intramyocardial course within the posterior aspect of the right ventricular outflow tract below the level of the pulmonary valve prior to exiting epicardially on the other side of the outflow tract. The degree of compression of the intramyocardial segment of transseptal coronary arteries and their clinical significance are variable.

Anomalous coronaries can coexist with the presence of other intramyocardial segments (myocardial bridges), especially involving the left anterior descending artery. Myocardial bridges are commonly found in asymptomatic individuals, and their clinical relevance is variable (see text that follows). Assessing the relative importance of myocardial bridges and other morphological features in these patients is critical to determining optimal management.

</div></details>

#### Coronary Dominance</summary><div class="details-content">

Right or left coronary dominance is determined by which coronary system supplies the posterior descending artery. Coronary dominance seems to be relevant in determining the clinical importance of coronary artery anomalies.

</div></details>

### Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinical presentation of anomalous connection of a main coronary artery to aorta is variable. The majority of patients are asymptomatic and will remain so. A large proportion of patients are diagnosed incidentally as part of routine pre-sport-participation screening, evaluation of a murmur or an abnormal ECG, or as part of an unrelated imaging study. According to different clinical series, approximately half of patients present with symptoms, including chest pain on exertion, dizziness or syncope on exertion, non-specific chest pain, palpitations, or shortness of breath. A few patients have as initial presentation an episode of sudden cardiac arrest, mostly during exercise. Basso and colleagues reported 27 young athletes who experienced sudden cardiac death with the diagnosis of anomalous connection of a major coronary artery to aorta identified only at autopsy. All died during or immediately after exercise, and 15 (55%) had had no prior cardiovascular symptoms.

In a large prospective series of 163 patients, Molossi and colleagues reported an incidental diagnosis in 80 (49%) patients, and 35 (21%) had exertional symptoms, 32 (20%) non-exertional symptoms, 5 (3%) sudden cardiac arrest, 3 (2%) an arrhythmia, and 8 (5%) were asymptomatic but evaluated due to a family history of cardiac disease. In a report from the AAOCA Registry of the Congenital Heart Surgeons Society (CHSS) encompassing 198 patients from 23 centers, 106 (54%) patients were symptomatic, with the most common symptoms being chest pain during exercise (*n* = 48, 24%), chest pain at rest (*n* = 30, 15%), and syncope during exercise (*n* = 16, 8%). Four patients presented with sudden cardiac arrest; two of them died. Symptomatic patients typically present in the second or third decade of life but may sometimes present later in life or, rarely, during the first decade.

The use of ECG is limited in the diagnosis, with most patients having a normal ECG at presentation except those presenting with active ischemia. In a multicenter study of 239 patients by Cipriani and colleagues, 16% presented with an abnormal ECG with Q waves, ST abnormalities, or ventricular ectopic beats.

Diagnostic workup should include a combination of *anatomic imaging* to assess the connection and course of the anomalous artery and *functional testing* to evaluate presence of ischemia. Some diagnostic modalities provide both anatomic and functional assessment.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Anatomic Imaging</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography is the first imaging modality in most patients. It can usually define the origin and course of the anomalous coronary artery, and sometimes reveal whether there are one or two ostia and whether there is an intramural course (Fig. 38.12). However, it is generally not sufficiently accurate to identify more detailed anatomic features such as intramurality and acute ostial angulation. Therefore, further anatomic delineation with cross-sectional imaging, such as CTA or CMR, is recommended.

Due to its high spatial resolution, CTA has become the mainstay imaging modality for diagnosis and characterization of this anomaly (Fig. 38.13). Imaging can usually be performed without sedation or beta-blockade, but ECG gating is important to achieve optimal images. Several studies have shown relatively good correlation between CTA and surgical findings. CTA can reliably identify type of anomalous connection, location of connection of the anomalous coronary, presence of interarterial and intramural segments, and morphology of the ostium. Length of intramurality can be assessed by a combination of variables, including shape or eccentricity of the coronary artery (intramural coronary segments are oval instead of round), presence of mediastinal fat between the coronary artery and aorta, and distance between aortic lumen and lumen of the coronary artery (shorter distances tend to correlate with intramural segments). Length of intramurality tends to be slightly overestimated by CTA compared to surgical measurement. Virtual endoluminal reconstruction views have also been useful in determining the location of connection and the ostial morphology of the anomalous coronary arteries. The morphologic variables that should be assessed and reported on CTA include:

- Type of anomalous coronary
- Ostial location
- Coronary dominance
- Relationship between the different coronary ostia
- Ostial morphology
- Presence and length of intramurality
- Presence and length of an interarterial segment
- Presence of an intramyocardial course (transseptal course and myocardial bridges)
- Course behind a thickened intercoronary pillar

CMR has been used in some series to diagnose and characterize anatomy. However, its lower spatial resolution compared to CTA limits its ability to define clearly all necessary anatomic details. Therefore, CTA as the anatomic imaging modality of choice is recommended. Stress CMR is helpful as a functional imaging modality (see text that follows).

Despite use of cross-sectional imaging for diagnosis of this coronary anomaly, coronary angiography continues to be helpful in certain circumstances. IVUS can assess the degree of compression or stenosis of the ostium and intramural segment, especially while simulating exercise with a combination of saline, atropine, and dobutamine. In addition, measurement of the pressure gradient between the aorta and the distal coronary artery using fractional flow reserve (FFR), with and without provocative testing, assesses the significance of compression of the anomalous coronary. No clear guidelines, however, currently exist regarding specific FFR thresholds. These techniques have been used in children and adolescents, especially those with intramyocardial vessels, to assess importance of that segment. Furthermore, atherosclerotic coronary artery disease has been reported in up to 60% of patients with this anomaly. It is therefore recommended that every adult being considered for surgical intervention undergo cardiac catheterization preoperatively. Coronary angiography should also be considered in patients with intramyocardial segments (i.e., transseptal course or myocardial bridge) and those in which more invasive testing can help with risk stratification.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Functional Testing</summary><div class="details-content">

Exercise stress testing (EST) is widely used to assess ischemia in this anomaly, but its utility is controversial. Basso and colleagues reported on 27 athletes who suffered sudden cardiac death in Italy and 6 of them had had a negative EST within 6 to 18 months before their death. Qasim and colleagues found poor correlation between EST and stress CMR in 155 patients with this anomaly. The addition of cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) increased sensitivity of the study at the expense of specificity. Based on these reports, EST should not be used as the only functional testing modality, especially if negative. Additional functional modalities should be used to identify presence of ischemia.

Stress CMR assesses myocardial perfusion, wall motion, and myocardial viability. True exercise stress CMR may be performed using a treadmill next to the CMR scanner or an in-scanner supine ergometer. These techniques, however, are limited by the need for specialized equipment, the difficulty in using them in children and adolescents, motion artifacts from heavy breathing, inability to reach maximum heart rate, and, for the treadmill CMR, changes in heart rate as the patient is moved from the treadmill to the scanner. Due to these limitations, stress CMR has been mainly performed using pharmacologic agents to simulate exercise. Dobutamine has been found to simulate exercise well because it increases heart rate and myocardial contractility while decreasing systemic vascular resistance and increasing coronary vasodilation. Dobutamine-stress CMR is not only safe in pediatric and adult patients with this coronary anomaly but has been found to correlate with invasive measurements of coronary perfusion in these patients.

Dobutamine-stress echocardiography has been used in these patients and may become a potentially helpful functional imaging modality. However, its appropriate use requires significant expertise.

Functional imaging can also be performed using nuclear perfusion imaging. Stress-rest <sup>99m</sup>Technetium-sestamibi myocardial perfusion single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) has been used to assess ischemia in patients with this coronary anomaly. Recent studies have combined CTA with SPECT to create hybrid images that contain data on coronary anatomy detail and perfusion. Other groups have used positron-emission tomography with computerized tomography (PET-CT) to define the coronary anatomy and presence of ischemia. PET-CT can be performed either using exercise or with pharmacologic agents such as dobutamine and has been found to have superior spatial resolution and less susceptibility to artifact than SPECT. These modalities will likely play an increasing role in defining ischemia in patients with this anomaly in the future.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

The natural history of anomalous connection of a major coronary artery to aorta is largely unclear but is associated with a low but important risk of sudden cardiac death. Extensive series of young athletes have found an incidence of sudden cardiac death ranging from as high as 1:43,770 person-years to as low as 1:160,000 person-years. Of these, this anomaly accounts for approximately 14% of deaths. The combined US and Italian registries of sudden deaths in competitive athletes revealed 27 individuals dying with anomalous coronary arteries; all but one death occurred between ages 10 and 32. The other individual was 9 years old. In two-thirds of the deaths, there were no previous events or symptoms.

The particular type of coronary anomaly likely plays a role in the risk of sudden cardiac death. Although anomalous right coronary arteries are six times more prevalent than anomalous left coronary arteries, 85% of sudden cardiac deaths occur in patients with anomalous left coronary arteries. Most clinical events, including sudden death, are recorded in the second and third decades of life and occur during or immediately following exertion. Interestingly, many patients with sudden death had previously exercised at higher degrees of intensity and duration without having any events.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Technique of Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

Several operations have been used to treat this coronary anomaly. Choice of operation depends on specific morphologic details, emphasizing the importance of accurate preoperative imaging (see "Selection of Surgical Technique" later in this section).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Unroofing</summary><div class="details-content">

Unroofing of the proximal coronary artery is indicated when an intramural course is present and the intramural segment is above the level of the aortic valve commissure (Fig. 38.14). This may be all that is necessary if the intramural segment is long enough that unroofing moves the effective ostium into the appropriate sinus. It accomplishes several things: it relieves any stenosis in the intramural segment, moves the coronary away from the interarterial position, and moves the coronary ostium away from the intercoronary pillar of the aortic valve. Unroofing can be accomplished whether or not there is a single coronary ostium or separate ostia as long as there is an intramural segment. If the intramural segment is absent or short, unroofing will fail to place the coronary ostium in the correct sinus away from potential compression by the intercoronary pillar. In these cases, an alternative surgical strategy should be used.

The procedure is performed through a median sternotomy. It is useful to carefully dissect between the aortic root and pulmonary trunk until the anomalous coronary is identified.

Ascending aorta cannulation and either bicaval or single right atrial cannulation are performed based on surgeon's preference (bicaval cannulation may allow less cluttered visualization of the aortocoronary complex) and the need to perform other procedures such as closure of a patent foramen ovale. After initiation of CPB, the patient is cooled to mild hypothermia, and the anomalous coronary is completely dissected, especially at the site where it connects to the aorta. Antegrade cardioplegia is administered, and the left heart vented through either the right upper pulmonary vein or atrial septum (through a small right atriotomy). An oblique or hockey-stick aortotomy is performed.

The anatomy is inspected with particular emphasis on ostial morphology and presence, length, and direction of the intramural segment. It is helpful to place a fine transmural stitch from outside to inside the aorta at the site where the anomalous coronary artery connects to the aortic wall. This permits defining the length and direction of the intramural segment; the intramural segment is the segment between the coronary ostium and that transmural stitch. The intramural segment is completely incised using fine scissors or a scalpel using the transmural stitch as a guide for the unroofing. The incised tissue can be debrided with care not to destabilize the aortic valve suspension system (the inferior rim of tissue is often left alone). If there is concern about having destabilized the aortic valve, a commissural suspension stitch should be placed. The ostium of the coronary is examined. It should be round, wide-open, away from the intercoronary pillar, and with a relatively direct takeoff to the distal coronary. If unroofing fails to place the ostium away from the intercoronary pillar, an alternative technique is recommended (see "Coronary Reimplantation" in text that follows). If the unroofing incision was extended beyond the ostium, fine sutures should be placed at the corner to avoid bleeding. A series of fine interrupted sutures are then placed between the coronary and aortic intima to exclude any soft tissue from the circulation and to open the ostium of the coronary further.

The aortotomy is closed with running polypropylene suture, the heart is deaired, and the rest of the operation is completed in the usual fashion (see "Completing Cardiopulmonary Bypass" in Chapter 2).

#### Neo-ostium Creation or Partial Unroofing</summary><div class="details-content">

Creation of a neo-ostium is a technique that can be used if a long intramural segment travels below the level of the aortic valve (Fig. 38.15). The procedure entails unroofing the intramural segment within the correct sinus while leaving the proximal segment, which travels behind the aortic valve commissure intact. The procedure requires that the intramural segment is long enough to create an appropriate ostium in the correct sinus.

The procedure is similar to the unroofing operation (see "Unroofing" in previous text). After performing the aortotomy, the anatomy is inspected. A transmural stitch placed from outside to inside the aorta is useful to define length and direction of the intramural segment. If the intramural segment is identified coursing behind the aortic valve or its commissure and the intramural segment within the correct sinus is deemed long enough, one may create a neo-ostium. A coronary probe or a fine right angle instrument is placed through the ostium and into the intramural segment. The intramural segment is partially unroofed into the correct sinus using a fine scalpel. The edges are debrided sharply, and a series of fine polypropylene tacking sutures are placed around the newly created ostium. The coronary should have a straight takeoff, and the ostium should be at least the diameter of the distal coronary. The aortotomy is closed in the usual fashion.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Coronary Reimplantation</summary><div class="details-content">

Coronary reimplantation is a technique that can be used when there is no intramural segment, or the intramural segment is so short that unroofing would not permit the ostium to be in the correct sinus and away from the intercoronary pillar. It is also a useful technique for cases where the intramural segment courses below the level of the aortic valve but is not long enough for creating a neo-ostium. Although the coronary artery may be harvested with an aortic button akin to how it is done in an arterial switch operation, in the majority of occasions, the proximity of the ostium to the aortic valve precludes this. In most cases, therefore, the coronary is transected at its connection to the aorta and reimplanted into the correct sinus (Fig. 38.16).

The procedure uses the principles described for unroofing. If coronary reimplantation is possible, placing a fine marking suture on the ideal location for reimplantation before aortic clamping and carefully dissecting as much coronary artery as possible where it connects to the aorta is helpful. Once an aortotomy is performed, the coronary anatomy is inspected. If it is clear that unroofing is not feasible due to the relationship between the intramural segment and aortic valve, or if after attempted unroofing, the ostium is still closely related to the intercoronary pillar and at risk for compression, coronary reimplantation is undertaken. A fine stay suture is placed on the coronary to maintain appropriate orientation, and the vessel is carefully transected at its connection to the aorta. The proximal stump is closed with running 7-0 or 6-0 polypropylene suture. An opening is performed in the correct sinus at the previously selected site using an aortic punch. An anastomosis is performed between the coronary artery and the aorta with either interrupted 7-0 or 8-0 polypropylene suture or a combination of running suture on the posterior wall and interrupted sutures anteriorly. It is important not to torque the vessel as the anastomosis is being performed. If the natural orientation of the coronary artery hugs the aorta and the aortic valve is not in the way, it is possible to enlarge the ostium in that direction and incise the coronary artery in a similar direction to create a larger anastomosis. In this scenario, it is important not to oversize the aortic opening to allow for an appropriate "cobra-head" anastomosis and avoid flattening the proximal coronary segment.

#### Techniques for Transseptal Coronary Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

The management of a left coronary artery that courses within the interventricular septum (i.e., a transseptal coronary) is more challenging. Two approaches can be used. A "posterior approach" entails transecting the pulmonary trunk and dividing the muscle fibers covering the coronary artery throughout its transseptal course. This approach is useful if the transseptal segment is relatively superficial. It has been supplemented by some surgeons with an anterior pulmonary trunk translocation (see "Other Surgical Techniques" later in this section).

The second technique commonly used is transinfundibular unroofing (Fig. 38.17). The procedure is performed through a median sternotomy with CPB, mild hypothermia, and cardioplegic arrest. The right ventricular outflow tract is opened transversely below the level of the pulmonary valve, and the incision is extended posteriorly, similar to what would be done if one were harvesting an autograft for a Ross procedure. The posterior aspect of the outflow tract is incised above the transseptal coronary, unroofing the entire segment. It is important to divide any fibers between the coronary and the surrounding tissue, thus completely freeing it. A rectangular piece of autologous pericardium is then used to patch the posterior and lateral aspects of the right ventricular outflow and while anastomosing native tissue anteriorly.

#### Other Surgical Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

Patch ostioplasty has been used to relieve ostial stenosis and address the course of the intramural segment by creating corresponding longitudinal incisions on the aorta and the coronary artery, joining them and enlarging the area with a patch. This technique relieves obstruction at the ostial level and bypasses any intramural segment, but the long-term effects of having a patch on the coronary artery are unknown.

Simple ostioplasty (i.e., debriding the ostium) can be used in specific circumstances where there is significant ostial stenosis with no intramural segment, and the coronary is connected to the aorta away from any potential compression by the pillar or valve.

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) has been described using the internal thoracic artery or a saphenous vein graft to the anomalously connected artery. This option is not recommended as a sole treatment unless there is additional important atherosclerotic coronary artery disease for several reasons. First, the long-term efficacy of the graft is uncertain in young patients. Second, the graft will be in competition with essentially normal antegrade flow through the anomalously connected artery, raising concern about graft thrombosis or, at the very least, adequacy of the graft when it may be acutely needed during transient compression of the anomalously connected artery.

Other surgical techniques have been described to treat single coronary trunks with the idea that compression of the interarterial segment of the coronary artery between the aorta and the pulmonary artery plays a role in the pathophysiology of the disease. These techniques include translocating the distal pulmonary trunk leftward into the left pulmonary artery (Fig. 38.18) or translocating the right pulmonary artery branch anterior to the aorta (Fig. 38.19), similar to the Lecompte maneuver in the arterial switch operation for transposition (see Chapter 44). These techniques have been less commonly used recently because it is believed that compression of the anomalous coronary artery by the pulmonary trunk is unlikely to contribute importantly to ischemia.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Selection of Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

Because the exact pathophysiologic mechanism of ischemia in this anomaly is unclear, surgical intervention should be aimed at addressing all potential anatomic sources of coronary flow reduction (i.e., ostial stenosis, intramural segment, compression by the intercoronary pillar, incorrect sinus of connection, and transseptal course).

Fig. 38.20 describes an algorithm to help guide selection of the optimal surgical technique based on the different anatomic features of the anomaly. *Unroofing* is an optimal technique if a long intramural segment travels above the level of the aortic valve as long as it allows the coronary ostium to be placed on the correct sinus away from potential compression by the intercoronary pillar. Generally, an intramural segment shorter than 4 to 5 mm is not long enough to allow unroofing to achieve this goal. Patients with shorter intramural segments or those with no intramural segment would benefit from *coronary reimplantation* instead (Fig. 38.21).

If the intramural segment travels below the level of the aortic valve, simple unroofing would require takedown and resuspension of the aortic valve commissure. This technique is not recommended due to the possibility of developing aortic regurgitation. If the intramural segment is long enough to allow partial unroofing into the correct sinus, creating *a neo-ostium* is an optimal technique. If the intramural segment is not long enough to permit this technique, *coronary reimplantation* should be considered.

In general, CABG should be reserved for patients with additional atherosclerotic coronary artery disease to avoid graft thrombosis from competitive flow. A patient with a transseptal left coronary artery or left anterior descending artery requiring surgical intervention should undergo either a *transinfundibular unroofing* or a *posterior myocardial unroofing*.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Results</summary><div class="details-content">

Early and midterm outcomes are generally excellent regardless of the specific operation, with mortality approaching zero and relief of symptoms in patients who are symptomatic preoperatively. Romp and colleagues reported no mortality and complete relief of symptoms in nine previously symptomatic patients, all of whom underwent unroofing of an intramural artery. Of concern, however, despite the lack of postoperative symptoms in 24 repaired patients, Brothers and colleagues demonstrated signs of ischemia in almost 40% of patients evaluated with stress echocardiography, EST, and stress myocardial perfusion scanning at a mean follow-up of 15 months after surgery. They reported that exercise capacity and quality of life were normal following surgical repair.

In a study reporting results of a standardized workup and management algorithm, Mery and colleagues reported on 44 patients who underwent surgical intervention, unroofing in 35 patients, coronary reimplantation in 7, ostioplasty in 1, and side-to-side anastomosis in 1. There were no perioperative deaths, but one patient who underwent coronary reimplantation required CABG due to postoperative ischemia. One patient who presented with aborted sudden cardiac death and underwent unroofing presented 20 months later with another episode of aborted sudden cardiac death. The patient was found on IVUS and FFR to have persistent compression of the coronary by the intercoronary pillar and underwent coronary reimplantation.

Similarly, Mainwaring and colleagues reported on midterm outcomes of 115 patients who underwent repair with unroofing, coronary reimplantation, and pulmonary trunk translocation. There were no perioperative or midterm deaths at 6 years of follow-up. Complications included postpericardiotomy syndrome in four patients, transient heart block in one, and pleural effusions in four. One required surgical revision for residual narrowing. During follow-up, one patient with preoperative cardiac dysfunction required heart transplant, one had persistent ischemic symptoms after unroofing requiring coronary reimplantation, and two were found postoperatively to have myocardial bridges (one underwent surgical repair).

Jegatheeswaran and colleagues described results of surgical intervention of this anomaly within the multi-institutional registry by the CHSS. A total of 395 patients underwent repair, with the majority (87%) undergoing unroofing procedures. Of these patients, 34 (9%) had a major adverse event, including death (0.8%), new moderate or severe aortic regurgitation (1.8%), coronary reintervention (3.2%), need for extracorporeal membrane oxygenation support (0.8%), postoperative ischemia (3.5%), or new cardiac dysfunction (1.5%). Manipulation of the aortic valve commissure was associated with an increased risk of developing aortic regurgitation.

Padalino and colleagues reported results of a multicenter retrospective study involving 16 centers in Europe. The study included 217 patients, of whom 156 (72%) underwent surgical intervention (unroofing in 56%, coronary reimplantation in 19%, CABG in 15%, and other techniques in 9%). Complications included low cardiac output syndrome in 9 (5.8%), early reoperation in 7 (4.5%), need for postoperative mechanical circulatory support in 6 (3.8%), and early mortality in 2 (1.3%).

As part of a systematic review and meta-analysis, Ponzoni and colleagues reviewed 384 patients who underwent repair from 13 publications, with most undergoing unroofing procedures. Pooled perioperative and long-term mortality was low, with 3 deaths (0.78%). Reoperation occurred in 0% to 8% of patients.

Najm and colleagues recently described 14 patients, ages 12 to 62 years, who underwent transinfundibular repair of a transseptal coronary artery. There were no perioperative deaths, and all patients had a good result on postoperative CTA. Postoperative assessment via cardiac catheterization in 11 patients showed no residual coronary compression with IVUS and FFR, whereas preoperative catheterization had shown important compression.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

Indications for surgical intervention in anomalous connection of a coronary artery to pulmonary trunk remain controversial. Ischemia, whether reversible or not, in the distribution of the anomalously connecting coronary artery is an indication for operation. A syncopal event, angina, or an episode of sudden death with resuscitation is an indication for operation, even if ischemia cannot be demonstrated at evaluation. In other situations, in view of limited long-term data, guidelines have been generally vague as to when to recommend surgical intervention. Formal society guidelines for managing adults with congenital heart disease provide some general guidance regarding surgical intervention. The 2018 American Heart Association (AHA)/American College of Cardiology (ACC) Guidelines for Management of Adults with Congenital Heart Disease recommends surgical intervention (level I) for patients who have "symptoms or diagnostic evidence consistent with coronary ischemia attributable to the anomalous coronary artery." Surgical intervention is reasonable (level IIa) for patients with anomalous left coronary artery connection in the absence of symptoms or ischemia or in the setting of ventricular arrhythmias. Surgical intervention or continued observation may be reasonable (level IIb) for asymptomatic patients without ischemia.

The 2020 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for managing adult congenital heart disease recommend surgical intervention in patients with typical anginal symptoms who present with evidence of stress-induced myocardial ischemia or high-risk anatomy (level I). These guidelines state that surgical intervention should be considered (level IIa) in asymptomatic patients with evidence of myocardial ischemia or with an anomalous left coronary artery with high-risk anatomy. Intervention may be considered (level IIb) for symptomatic patients in the absence of myocardial ischemia or high-risk anatomy or asymptomatic patients with anomalous left coronary artery connection without myocardial ischemia or high-risk anatomy if they present at a young age (<35 years).

In 2017, the American Association of Thoracic Surgery (AATS) published expert consensus guidelines for this anomaly. These guidelines recommend that such patients with ischemic symptoms (i.e., ischemic chest pain or syncope suspected to be due to ventricular arrhythmias or a history of aborted sudden cardiac death) should be exercise-restricted and offered surgical intervention (level I). They also recommend offering surgical intervention to asymptomatic patients with anomalous connection of the left coronary artery to the right sinus of Valsalva and an interarterial course.

The decision as to whether to proceed with surgical intervention or not is complex and is best taken as a shared decision between clinicians and the patient and family. Factors that should influence that decision include age; type of coronary anomaly (e.g., left or right); anatomy (e.g., presence and length of the intramural segment, degree of proximal coronary compression, ostial morphology, and stenosis); presence of symptoms concerning for ischemia (e.g., exertional chest pain or syncope, aborted sudden cardiac death); evidence of ischemia on stress testing; evidence of functional compression on advanced imaging; degree of anxiety about the diagnosis (the diagnosis itself may be incapacitating to some patients and families); and the patient's and family's values and desires (e.g., importance of athletics and participation in competitive sports for the patient). All of these factors should be weighed to reach the optimal decision for a particular patient.

Agrawal and colleagues recommend performing a psychosocial evaluation as part of the initial clinical evaluation of patients with this anomaly to define the athletic identity and anxiety profile of the patient and family. All of these factors can then be used, with the clinical evaluation, to determine the strength of recommendation regarding surgical intervention. Fig. 38.22 shows an example of a shared decision-making tool used to guide the decision regarding surgical intervention, considering age, symptomatology, type of anomaly, anatomic characteristics, and athletic identity/anxiety. Patients with ischemic symptoms or functional testing positive for ischemia should be advised to proceed with surgical repair. For asymptomatic patients without evidence of ischemia, the strength of recommendation should depend on other factors (age, anatomy, athletic identity, and anxiety). It is important to note that this framework and table represent only a tool for discussion with the patient and family, as the final decision should result from true shared decision-making considering the known and unknown risks of each strategy.

The role of exercise restriction as a management strategy for this anomaly is also unclear. Mery and colleagues reported the results of a decision analysis model for its management, comparing observation, exercise restriction, and surgical intervention for right and left coronary anomalies. They found that the adverse long-term effects of exercise restriction on all-cause mortality appeared to be more important than the low risk of sudden cardiac death. In addition, the psychosocial impact of exercise restriction can be substantial, especially for young athletes. As such, it seems prudent to recommend exercise restriction on a very selective basis only for patients considered to have a very high risk of sudden cardiac death (e.g., those with a history of aborted sudden cardiac death) while they are awaiting surgical intervention or if surgical intervention is not being pursued.

### Special Situations and Controversies</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Myocardial Bridges</summary><div class="details-content">

Myocardial bridging can occur in patients with AAOCA and sometimes contribute to ischemia. Mainwaring and colleagues reported persistent ischemia due to myocardial bridging after surgical repair of the coronary anomaly.

An intramyocardial course of a coronary artery has an estimated prevalence of approximately 25%, but bridging commonly refers to intramyocardial coronary arteries that may have inadequate flow reserve or vasospasm related to systolic compression. Indeed, defining the significance of myocardial bridges for a particular patient may be difficult, especially in the setting of a coronary anomaly.

It is therefore recommended that patients be evaluated by CTA for evidence of myocardial bridging in addition to the primary coronary anomaly. If a myocardial bridge is identified, cardiac catheterization with IVUS and FFR may then be used to assess the hemodynamic importance of the myocardial bridge. Important myocardial bridges should be addressed with myocardial unroofing during the repair.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transcatheter Intervention</summary><div class="details-content">

Use of interventional catheter-based techniques, including coronary artery stenting, has been sparingly described for this anomaly. Angelini and colleagues described 42 patients with anomalous connection of the right coronary artery to the left sinus of Valsalva with an intramural course who underwent stent placement. Of these 42 patients, 23 underwent follow-up stress testing by nuclear perfusion scan; 2 had perfusion defects in stented areas. Of the 30 patients with more than 1 year of clinical follow-up, 10 underwent coronary angiography due to clinical indications, and 4 (13% of the 30) had in-stent restenosis.

Due to the scarcity of data and concerns for in-stent restenosis, transcatheter intervention should be reserved for older patients with evidence of ischemia who are not candidates for surgical intervention.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## SECTION III: CORONARY ARTERIOVENOUS FISTULA</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Definition</summary><div class="details-content">

A congenital coronary arteriovenous fistula is a direct communication between a coronary artery and the lumen of any one of the four cardiac chambers, the coronary sinus or its tributary veins, the superior vena cava, the pulmonary trunk, or the pulmonary veins close to the heart. Fistulous coronary connections that occur in congenital pulmonary and aortic atresia (see "Right Ventricular Coronary Artery Fistulae" under Morphology in Chapter 36 and "Other Associated Cardiac Anomalies" under Morphology in Chapter 51) are excluded from this chapter.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

A congenital coronary arteriovenous fistula was first described by Krause in 1865. The first report in the English literature was that of Trevor in 1912, who described autopsy findings in a case with a fistula from the right coronary artery into the right ventricle. The patient died from associated endocarditis. Autopsy reports from Blakeway and from Halpert followed. The first report of surgical correction was in 1947 by Biörck and Crafoord, who discovered a fistulous connection to the pulmonary trunk at thoracotomy in a patient presumed to have a patent ductus arteriosus. It was closed with sutures. Probably the first reported case of a fistula that was correctly diagnosed preoperatively was that of Fell and colleagues in 1958, and the first report of repair using CPB was that of Swan and colleagues in 1959. Currarino and colleagues described use of angiography in diagnosis in 1959. The first description of catheter-based occlusion of a fistula was by Reidy and colleagues in 1983, who described a fistula between the circumflex coronary artery and a bronchial artery that they occluded with a detachable balloon.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Morphology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Coronary Artery Site</summary><div class="details-content">

The right coronary artery or its branches are the origination site for the fistula in 50% to 60% of cases when draining into a cardiac chamber, with the left anterior descending artery being more common for fistulas that terminate in the pulmonary trunk. Fistulas can originate from both coronary artery systems in 5% to 10% of cases. The fistulous artery is almost invariably part of a normally distributed coronary artery with a normal branching pattern. The fistula occurs either in the main artery, which then continues beyond the fistula (side-to-side pattern), or at the termination of the main artery itself or a branch (end-artery pattern). The coronary artery proximal to the fistula is always dilated and elongated and may be serpiginous, and the degree of these changes is roughly proportional to the size of shunt through the fistula. Usually, dilation is uniform throughout, but it may become aneurysmal anywhere along its course. Rarely a giant aneurysm occurs involving the whole artery. This is particularly prone to occur in fistulas from the right coronary artery entering either the posterior wall of the left ventricle or right ventricle (Fig. 38.23). Although such aneurysms enlarge progressively, rupture is rare. Should the artery continue beyond the fistula, it reduces abruptly to a diameter smaller than expected. It is probable that in such cases, a coronary steal phenomenon occurs. There is some suggestion that fistulas may predispose to premature atherosclerosis but the evidence is not yet clear.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Site of Fistulous Connection</summary><div class="details-content">

Fistulous connection between the coronary artery and heart may enter any of the four cardiac chambers, the coronary sinus or its tributary veins, or the great arteries or veins adjacent to the heart (pulmonary trunk, proximal pulmonary veins, proximal superior vena cava, and left superior vena cava). There are, however, certain predilections. More than 90% of fistulas open into right heart chambers or their connecting vessels. True arteriovenous fistulas to the veins (coronary sinus or its major branches or venae cavae) are uncommon. The distribution varies between studies, likely related to the population being studied. Surgical series report 40% of fistulas draining into the right ventricle, 25% to right atrium, 15% to 20% to pulmonary trunk, 7% to coronary sinus, and 1% to superior vena cava. In large imaging series, the most frequent site of connection is the pulmonary trunk. An extensive study of 482 fistulas based on CTA by Cai and colleagues showed that almost 80% drained into the pulmonary artery, 7% into each the left and right ventricles, and 4% into the right atrium. This is likely explained by the fact that most fistulae from the left anterior descending artery to the pulmonary artery tend to be small weblike connections that do not need intervention.

Fistulae entering the right side of the circulation cause rapid systolic and diastolic runoff from the aorta and a left-to-right shunt. The Qp:Qs is seldom larger than 1.8 and is often less, and arterial pulse pressure is seldom greatly widened. About 8% of fistulae drain into left heart chambers or their tributaries. Left heart fistulae are not, of course, arteriovenous fistulae but arterioarterial (arteriocameral, arteriosystemic) and, therefore, do not produce a left-to-right shunt. There may be important runoff from the aorta during both systole and diastole when fistulae enter the left atrium, or only during diastole when they enter the left ventricle, because fistulae usually close off during systole, and because there is no pressure gradient. Volume overload on the left ventricle is therefore similar to that produced by aortic regurgitation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Size and Multiplicity of Fistulae</summary><div class="details-content">

In surgically treated cases, the fistulous opening, when single, is seldom larger than 2 to 5 mm (Fig. 38.24) and usually has fibrous margins. However, uncommonly it may itself be aneurysmal as in the first reported case. Occasionally, there may be several openings or a localized angiomatous network of vessels. Among 58 patients reported from the Texas Heart Institute, multiple fistulae were identified in 16% and an angiomatous lesion in 10%; the fistula was aneurysmal in 19%. In the study by Cai and colleagues, 24% of fistulae showed multiple vessel networks, with multiple fistulas being more common in those terminating in the pulmonary trunk. As in other sites, most fistulae to the left ventricle are single. In a small group of patients, however, there is a diffuse spongework of tiny connections from a number of, if not most, branches of the left coronary and sometimes also the right coronary artery (Fig. 38.25). These presumably represent persistence of embryonic trabecular spaces.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Cardiac Chambers</summary><div class="details-content">

The chamber or vessel into which the fistula connects is variably affected. When the right atrium receives the fistula, it tends to become considerably dilated. In contrast, the right ventricle and pulmonary trunk show less change (apart from that expected from an increase in pulmonary blood flow) until heart failure occurs, and they participate in cardiomegaly. Similarly, the left ventricle tends to remain normal in size despite a fistulous connection to it, probably because runoff occurring only in diastole is seldom large and seldom comparable with that occurring in severe aortic regurgitation. Left ventricular hypertrophy may be present. Rarely the left atrium becomes aneurysmally dilated. The coronary sinus may also become aneurysmal as it is possible that the coronary sinus ostium limits runoff through the coronary sinus. Arterialization of the coronary sinus occurs, and possibly in relation to this, there is an unusually high prevalence of heart failure in such patients.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rupture and Infective Endocarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

Rupture of coronary fistulae is extremely rare but has been reported, especially in elderly females. Coronary fistulae can present with endocarditis in 4% to 5% of cases, likely due to turbulent flow.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Associated Lesions</summary><div class="details-content">

Most coronary fistulae occur as isolated lesions, but there may be coincidental congenital or acquired lesions of almost any type. In the series reported by Urrutia-S and colleagues, 21 of 58 patients had associated lesions such as atrial septal defect, ventricular septal defect, and acquired valve or coronary disease.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Age at Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

Most patients present late in life, occasionally in childhood, rarely in infancy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Symptoms</summary><div class="details-content">

Most patients are asymptomatic and present either because of a continuous murmur or mild cardiomegaly and plethora on chest radiograph. Some 60% to 80% of patients under age 20 years are asymptomatic, whereas few of those older are without symptoms. Patients with small fistulae are detected incidentally as part of imaging studies such as echocardiography and cardiac catheterization for other conditions. They are typically asymptomatic and presumably will continue to be so.

The most common symptoms are *effort dyspnea* and *fatigue* from the left-to-right shunt. The occurrence of angina varies among studies between 7% and 39%, but myocardial infarction is rare (about 3%). It is postulated that these ischemic symptoms are due to coronary artery steal.

Heart failure occurs in 10% to 15% of patients presenting for operation but is much more common in older patients, as is angina. Thus, in the review by Liberthson and colleagues, only 6% of patients under age 20 years had heart failure, but 19% of those 20 years or older did. Heart failure can occur in infants with large shunts and in the occasional child with a large right coronary to left ventricular fistula comparable with an aortico–left ventricular tunnel. The likelihood of older patients having heart failure is not directly related to shunt size; rather, it is presumably related to a long-standing modest left-to-right shunt, as in the case of atrial septal defect. Heart failure is more common in patients with fistulous connection to the coronary sinus (50% vs. 14% in the overall group as reported by Ogden and Stansel). It may also be more common with onset of atrial fibrillation, which occurs more often when the connection is to the right atrium.

When infective endocarditis occurs, presentation may be with chills and fever, or more advanced signs of sepsis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Diagnosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Diagnosis is often strongly suspected from physical signs, but it may be difficult to distinguish coronary fistulae from other lesions with rapid aortic runoff and continuous murmurs such as patent ductus arteriosus, ventricular septal defect with aortic regurgitation, ruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm, and in infancy, aortico–left ventricular tunnel. Contrary to some other lesions where the murmur is loudest at the time of the second sound, the murmur of a coronary fistula tends to be a crescendo-decrescendo continuous murmur that is loudest in diastole. When the fistula enters the right atrium, the murmur is loudest along the right sternal border. In contrast, when the fistula enters one of the ventricles, the murmur is loudest at the lower left sternal edge. The murmur will be loudest at the left second intercostal space in fistulae to the pulmonary trunk, similar to the murmur of a patent ductus arteriosus.

A systolic thrill is occasionally palpable when the fistula lies anteriorly (entry into right atrium or right ventricle). When the shunt and aortic diastolic runoff are large, pulse pressure is wide, and the pulse jerky. Rarely, infants with a connection to the left ventricle may present with full-blown signs of severe aortic regurgitation.

The ECG is entirely normal in about half of surgical patients and shows evidence of right or left ventricular overload in the remainder. The chest radiograph may also be normal or may show mild cardiomegaly and plethora. Cardiomegaly is more marked when heart failure appears. There may be evidence of right or left atrial enlargement, and occasionally the dilated and tortuous or aneurysmal coronary artery or fistulous site may distort the cardiac silhouette. This is most obvious when a giant aneurysm of the right coronary artery drains to the left ventricle, although this is rare.

Two-dimensional (2D) echocardiography can detect importantly enlarged coronary arteries and may also confirm specific chamber enlargement. Thus, diagnosis of a coronary arteriovenous fistula can be made by 2D and Doppler echocardiography if the fistula is large enough (Fig. 38.26). Xie and colleagues retrospectively reviewed echocardiograms on 63 patients who had undergone coronary angiograms or surgical intervention for coronary fistulae and found diagnostic accuracy of 95%. Although echocardiography is not considered a definitive diagnostic modality for coronary fistulae, such studies are helpful to assess the hemodynamic effects of fistulae, exclude other associated abnormalities, and monitor response to treatment.

Cardiac catheterization, aortography, and selective coronary angiography have long been the gold standard for definitive diagnosis and planning of either surgical repair or coil occlusion by interventional catheterization (Figs. 38.27 and 38.28; also see Figs. 38.24 and 38.25). Left-to-right shunts are calculated, and right heart pressures are measured.

CTA can accurately define the morphology of the fistula in both adults and young children. In cases that will not require hemodynamic measurements to make management decisions, CTA may be the diagnostic procedure of choice. CTA may also be useful as an adjuvant preoperative imaging modality to better define the location of the fistula and its relationship to surrounding structures (Fig. 38.29). CMR has been used to visualize fistulae, but its spatial resolution limits its utility in this setting.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

The natural history of coronary arteriovenous fistula is not known precisely, but its general outlines are clear. The fistula, if not present at birth, develops early in life. Likely, small fistulae remain small, and moderate fistulae slowly increase in size, although there may be little change over 10 to 15 years. Onset of dyspnea, heart failure, and angina can occur in young patients with large fistulae. However, because the shunt is usually only moderate, symptoms often do not appear until later in life, consequent to long-standing moderate left ventricular volume overload. Daniel and colleagues found from a literature review that if heart failure did not occur early in infancy, it would be virtually unknown until age 20 years. The maximum prevalence of heart failure occurs in the fifth and sixth decades.

The other event that may precipitate symptoms and cause premature death is infective endocarditis, which occurs in about 3% to 5% of patients and may develop at any age. Aneurysm formation develops with increasing frequency over time, occurring in 9% of children and 14% to 29% of adults. Spontaneous rupture is rare, even though the feeding coronary artery or the fistula itself may become aneurysmal, and as with other aneurysms, there is progressive dilation of the sac. Rupture of coronary fistulae has also been reported in children.

Liberthson and colleagues found that among 173 reported patients, mean age of 24 years, fistula-related death occurred in 6%: 1% in those presenting younger than 20 years and 14% in those presenting later (mean age 43 years). Spontaneous closure of a fistula has been recorded but is rare.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Technique of Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

Approach in all patients is through a median sternotomy, with preparation made for CPB (see "Preparation for Cardiopulmonary Bypass" in Section III of Chapter 2). After opening the pericardium, the site of the fistula and location, size, and pathology of the coronary artery leading to it are noted. It is critical that closure of the fistula is performed without compromising normal coronary blood flow. The surgical technique is, therefore, tailored to the particular circumstance. CPB is indicated when:

1. Artery is dilated and tortuous, to prevent catastrophic hemorrhage during closure of the fistula
2. The fistula is relatively inaccessible, such as when it is in the left atrioventricular groove or distribution of the circumflex or distal right coronary artery
3. The fistula is in the course of the coronary artery rather than at its termination so the fistula itself cannot be easily closed without compromising the coronary artery
4. An aneurysm requires excision
5. There are associated intracardiac lesions that need to be addressed

Exact location of the coronary fistula is determined and marked with a stitch before establishing CPB because this is difficult to do later. After establishing CPB, the aorta is clamped, and cardioplegic solution administered. If the fistula is large and there is significant runoff, the fistula can be temporarily finger occluded. When the chamber into which the fistula opens is an atrium or the pulmonary trunk, the chamber is opened and the fistula closed from within. Fistulae into the right ventricle can be accessed through the right atrium and tricuspid valve (Fig. 38.30). The entry point of the fistula can usually be identified due to a subtle fibrous rind.

Cold cardioplegia solution can be infused to ascertain the entry point of the fistula in the opened chamber. A purse string suture is placed around the fistula and controlled with a snugger while cardioplegia is administered again. If the fistula has been completely controlled, the suture is tied. Additional sutures may be placed as necessary.

When the fistula enters the left ventricle or when the coronary artery is large and continues beyond the fistula, the coronary artery itself is opened and the fistula closed from within the coronary with a running suture, followed by closure of the arteriotomy with 7-0 or 8-0 polypropylene sutures. Use of a running mattress suture beneath the artery through the fistulous site is not recommended because this may lead to fistula recurrence.

When a large aneurysm is present (see Fig. 38.23), it should be excised. If the aneurysm is localized over the fistula site, excision entails trimming away the edges of the dilated vessel and re-suturing its walls to create an artery of near-normal size. This is possible because the posterior wall invariably consists of strong tissue. This is necessary only when the artery continues beyond the site of the fistula. When it is an end artery, the aneurysm is completely excised and the vessel remnants oversewn. When the aneurysm involves most of the feeding coronary artery (see Fig. 38.24), there is usually no option but to unroof it completely and close the coronary artery proximal and distal to the sac, the latter closure including the fistula site. In such circumstances, it is always appropriate to consider use of CABG using either a saphenous vein or internal thoracic artery to the vessel beyond the fistula, but this may not be possible when the coronary artery is too small.

The remainder of the operation is completed as usual (see "Completing Cardiopulmonary Bypass" in Section III of Chapter 2).

Fistulous connection may be safely closed without CPB when it represents the termination of a major coronary artery branch into an easily accessible site and indicators for CPB are absent. In such instances, a ligature is placed around the "feeding" coronary artery very close to the fistulous connection. The fistula is then temporarily completely closed with a snugger (verified by complete ablation of the thrill and by monitoring the transesophageal echocardiogram), and the ECG is monitored for several minutes. If there are no ECG changes, the ligature is tied down.

### Special Features of Postoperative Care</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients are managed as described in Chapter 4. The use of antiplatelet agents or systemic anticoagulation postoperatively is variable, and its effect on risk of late thrombosis is unclear. Li and colleagues found that antiplatelet therapy did not necessarily decrease risk of thrombosis but delayed its onset in an extensive series of pediatric surgical patients. Based on a large study by Gowda and colleagues, thrombosis after closure appears to be more prevalent in patients after closure of distal fistulae, especially with dilated coronaries. Their suggestion is to maintain all treated patients with coronary fistula on some form of antiplatelet therapy (patients without important proximal coronary dilation) or anticoagulation with warfarin (patients with dilated residual coronaries) for more than 1 year.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Results</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Early (Hospital) Death</summary><div class="details-content">

Hospital mortality for repair of coronary AV fistula in the *absence* of giant aneurysm formation approaches zero. Giant aneurysms almost always involve the right coronary artery, necessitating complete aneurysm excision and usually regrafting of the remaining right coronary system; risk of ischemia and arrhythmia increases in this situation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postoperative Complications</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative complications are rare. The two most important are myocardial ischemia and residual shunting. Myocardial ischemia, either temporary or with infarction, has been reported in 3% to 7% of cases and residual fistulae in 4% to 5%. In a series of 52 patients treated surgically by Wang and colleagues, 4 had postoperative ECG changes; 3 normalized within 1 week.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Time-Related Outcomes</summary><div class="details-content">

Late results of repair are relatively good. Edis and colleagues reported that essentially all patients in whom the fistula was eliminated remained in NYHA class I. Lowe and colleagues found no late deaths and no recurrent fistulas among 22 survivors of repair, with a mean follow-up of 10 years. The main concern in the long-term is the development of coronary artery thrombosis and myocardial ischemia. In a series of 79 pediatric patients who underwent surgical closure of coronary fistulae, Li and colleagues reported development of coronary thrombi in 34% of patients during an 11-year follow-up. Risk factors included large fistulae, distal-type fistulae, and older age at presentation. In another series of 29 patients that underwent surgical closure of fistulae from the left circumflex coronary artery, Hou and colleagues reported coronary thrombosis resulting in myocardial infarction in one patient 1 month after repair, and a total of three instances of coronary thrombosis (10%) during a 9-year follow-up period. Said and colleagues reported 46 patients who underwent surgical repair of coronary fistulae with a mean follow-up of 6 years. A total of 5 patients (11%) had myocardial infarctions: 3 during the perioperative period and 2 through follow-up. All these patients had large and important fistulous communications and importantly dilated coronary arteries. The cohort's long-term survival was substantially lower compared to the age- and sex-matched general population. Valente and colleagues found a 10% occurrence of late myocardial infarction in a cohort of 76 patients who underwent surgical or transcatheter closure of coronary fistulae. The relatively high occurrence of late coronary ischemia and thrombosis findings suggests that antiplatelet therapy or anticoagulation should be used in patients with dilated coronary arteries after fistulae repair. However, the effectiveness of such a strategy is unclear.

Other late complications include development of tricuspid or mitral valve regurgitation requiring intervention. Valves should be carefully assessed during initial surgical intervention to decide whether intervention is warranted.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Indications for Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

All coronary fistulae associated with symptoms or evidence of ischemia, heart failure, chamber enlargement, vessel rupture, or arrhythmias should be repaired regardless of size. There is also consensus that all large coronary fistulae should undergo transcatheter or surgical treatment, regardless of presentation. The management of asymptomatic small and moderate coronary fistulae is controversial. Given the probability that at least some of these fistulae will increase in size and, therefore, eventually produce symptoms and heart failure, the risk of delayed myocardial ischemia, the possibility of developing infective endocarditis, the low probability of spontaneous closure, and the safety and efficacy of closure, it is recommended that most coronary fistulae be closed unless the fistulae are very small and associated with a small amount of shunting (Qp:Qs <1.3).

There have been substantial improvements in transcatheter techniques for closure of coronary fistulae and their associated outcomes (see "Transcatheter Closure of Coronary Fistulae" in text that follows). In most centers, transcatheter closure has become the initial procedure of choice, with surgical intervention reserved for patients that either fail transcatheter intervention, have anatomy that makes transcatheter closure not feasible, or have other surgical indications.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Special Situations and Controversies</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transcatheter Closure of Coronary Fistulae</summary><div class="details-content">

Several devices have been used for closure of coronary artery fistulae. These devices include embolization coils, vascular occluders (devices made of braided nickel titanium), and covered stent grafts deployed in the native coronary artery to exclude the fistulae.

Procedural complications after transcatheter closure include vessel dissection or rupture, device embolization, and leaving a residual fistula. Most importantly, there is risk for myocardial infarction due to thrombus formation within the aneurysmal native coronary artery due to stagnant flow or thrombogenicity from the device. In a series of 45 patients undergoing transcatheter closure of 56 coronary fistulae by El-Sabawi and colleagues, there was 89% procedural success with small residual flow in 3 (5.4%) patients and large residual flow in 3 (5.4%). In 18%, complications occurred, including 3 instances of device migration, 1 intracranial hemorrhage from anticoagulation, and 4 myocardial infarctions. Wang and colleagues reported on 63 patients who underwent transcatheter coronary fistulae closure. One patient died from third-degree atrioventricular block, and there were 5 early reinterventions, 2 from device embolization, 1 for coronary stenosis, 1 for a myocardial infarction, and 1 for a large residual fistula.
