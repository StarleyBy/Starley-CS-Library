# Double Outlet Left Ventricle

##### Double outlet left ventricle (DOLV) is a congenital cardiac malformation in which both great arteries arise entirely or predominantly from the morphologic left ventricle (LV). DOLV may occur with atrioventricular (AV) concordant or discordant connection, as does double outlet right ventricle (DORV; see Chapter 45). DOLV with AV discordant connection is discussed in Chapter 47. DOLV, like DORV, may also occur in patients with univentricular AV connections (see Chapter 52) and in those with heterotaxy (see Chapter 53).

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Classification
* **Double Outlet Left Ventricle (DOLV):** Both great arteries arise entirely or predominantly (>50%) from the morphologic left ventricle. May occur with AV concordant or discordant connection.
* **Segmental Classification:** Most common in hearts with atrial situs solitus and ventricular right-handedness (D-loop) — S,D,D. Also occurs in S,D,L, I,L,L, and I,L,D arrangements.
* **VSD Types (in AV concordant DOLV):** Subaortic VSD (73% — most common), subpulmonary VSD (15%), doubly committed VSD (10%), remote VSD (1%). Intact ventricular septum is rare but has been described.

### Morphology
* **Conal Pattern:** Most often — absence of subaortic conus (aortic-mitral fibrous continuity) + subpulmonary conus displaced into LV. Bilaterally absent conus (aortic-mitral-tricuspid and pulmonary-tricuspid fibrous continuity) is rare. Bilaterally present conus or isolated subaortic conus can occur.
* **Pulmonary Stenosis:** Present in most cases — valvar (sometimes with anular stenosis) or subvalvar (restrictive subpulmonary conus with secondary fibrosis).
* **Right Ventricle and Tricuspid Valve:** Tendency toward hypoplasia. Extreme example: coexistence with tricuspid atresia. Ebstein anomaly of tricuspid valve may occur.
* **Conduction System:** AV node and bundle of His position is normal (posterior AV node, penetrating bundle through right trigone). Risk during repair depends on relationship of lower VSD margin to tricuspid anulus.
* **AV Discordant DOLV:** Aorta and pulmonary trunk arise from right-sided (in situs solitus) LV. Findings similar to congenitally corrected transposition but with both great arteries from LV.

### Clinical Features and Pathophysiology
* **AV Concordant DOLV:** LV is common mixing chamber (receives pulmonary venous blood via mitral valve and caval blood via RV and VSD). Cyanosis varies due to pulmonary stenosis and streaming. Subaortic VSD may direct desaturated RV blood into aorta → unexpectedly severe cyanosis. Without pulmonary stenosis → heart failure early in life from large Qp.
* **AV Discordant DOLV:** LV receives caval blood via mitral valve; pulmonary venous blood via RV and VSD → greater tendency for severe cyanosis.
* **Echocardiography:** Diagnostic challenge — distinguish from TGA and DORV. Define VSD-great artery relationship, presence/absence of conus under each great artery, pulmonary stenosis, great artery position, coronary artery pattern.
* **MRI:** Supplements echocardiography for intracardiac morphology. Cardiac catheterization remains necessary for hemodynamic and oximetric data (pulmonary vasculature physiology).

### Natural History
* **Without Pulmonary Stenosis:** Similar to isolated large VSD (progressive VSD narrowing/closure not documented in DOLV). Cyanosis variable due to streaming.
* **With Pulmonary Stenosis:** Similar to tetralogy of Fallot — hypoxia and clinical course directly related to severity of pulmonary stenosis.

### Operative Techniques
* **Preoperative Imaging:** Essential due to rarity and variability. Intraoperative confirmation of VSD position, great artery origins, and pulmonary stenosis required.
* **With Pulmonary Stenosis:** Usually cannot do completely intraventricular repair. Transanular patch often not possible (LAD coronary artery in front of pulmonary anulus). Options: (1) Valved extracardiac conduit (transect pulmonary trunk, oversew proximal stump, close VSD with patch, conduit from RV to pulmonary trunk); (2) Pulmonary trunk translocation onto RV + transanular patching of RVOT-pulmonary confluence (avoids conduit); (3) REV (Lecompte) operation; (4) Patch augmentation of RVOT (requires pulmonary trunk anterior or side-by-side to aorta, coronary arteries behind pulmonary root).
* **Without Pulmonary Stenosis:** May allow intraventricular tunnel repair (contoured patch in VSD so RV ejects into pulmonary trunk, LV into aorta). VSD may need enlargement anteriorly/superiorly. If tunnel not possible — close pulmonary orifice, close VSD (aorta from LV), place conduit from RV to pulmonary trunk; or pulmonary root translocation to RV (even with absent subpulmonary conus — Hanley technique); or arterial switch operation (rare).
* **RV and Tricuspid Hypoplasia (z-score < -2):** Fontan operation or one-and-a-half ventricle repair (intracardiac septation + bidirectional cavopulmonary anastomosis).

### Results
* **Early Mortality:** Literature review (37 cases over 45 years) — 8% hospital mortality. Current era expected ~5% for biventricular repair. Hanley's series of 6 patients — no early or midterm deaths.
* **Time-Related Survival:** 10-year survival 87% (Luciani series). Late reoperations — conduit replacement, conversion to Fontan for RV failure, residual VSD closure.
* **Toronto Sick Kids Series (1960-2008, n=19):** 7 biventricular repairs, 9 univentricular palliation. 5-year survival 70-75% for both strategies. Most survivors had residual abnormalities despite repair. Discrepancy in outcomes between series likely reflects true denominator of anatomic spectrum in Toronto series vs. selection bias in smaller reports.

### Indications for Operation
* **General:** Diagnosis of DOLV is indication for operation.
* **With Pulmonary Stenosis:** Main pulmonary artery translocation onto RV + pericardial patch augmentation of RVOT-pulmonary confluence may be optimal (young age not contraindicated). Conduit repair acceptable in infancy (accepts early reoperation/conduit replacement). Classic shunting operations remain alternative.
* **Without Pulmonary Stenosis:** Corrective operation usually in first 6 months if imaging supports intraventricular repair. Alternative: initial pulmonary artery banding then intracardiac repair.
* **RV and Tricuspid Hypoplasia:** z-value for tricuspid valve diameter < -2 → Fontan or one-and-a-half ventricle repair.

## Definition</summary><div class="details-content">

Double outlet left ventricle (DOLV) is a congenital cardiac malformation in which both great arteries arise entirely or predominantly from the morphologic left ventricle (LV).

DOLV may occur with atrioventricular (AV) concordant or discordant connection, as does double outlet right ventricle (DORV; see Chapter 45). DOLV with AV discordant connection is discussed in Chapter 47. DOLV, like DORV, may also occur in patients with univentricular AV connections (see Chapter 52) and in those with heterotaxy (see Chapter 53).

</div></details>

## Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

Marechal is credited with describing the first case of DOLV in 1819, but this was in a heart with double inlet LV with an infundibular outlet chamber. The first reported case in a heart with two ventricles and without pulmonary stenosis was that of Sakakibara and colleagues in 1967, for which they performed a successful intraventricular repair. DOLV is therefore another congenital cardiac anomaly that remained, for all practical purposes, undescribed until the advent of intracardiac surgery. It is also of interest that the first case reported by Potts and colleagues as tetralogy of Fallot and receiving a side-to-side aortopulmonary artery anastomosis underwent subsequent repair for well-documented DOLV with pulmonary stenosis (John Kirklin: personal communication; 1983). A unique case of DOLV with an intact ventricular septum was reported by Paul and colleagues in 1970, establishing with certainty the existence of the entity. The heart specimen they described had situs solitus, concordant AV connection, no ventricular septal defect (VSD), bilaterally absent infundibula, and both great arteries arising from the LV. Subsequent reports expanded the surgical possibilities by reporting reconstruction of the pulmonary pathway in cases in which a completely intraventricular repair was not possible, usually by a valved extracardiac conduit from the right ventricle to pulmonary trunk. Anderson and colleagues reported the sixth case of DOLV in 1974. Sharratt and colleagues in 1976 reported use of a Fontan-type procedure in hearts with DOLV and severe right ventricular hypoplasia.

Five cases were added to the literature in 1976. Additional cases have been reported by Urban and colleagues and Stegmann and colleagues.

## Morphology and Morphogenesis</summary><div class="details-content">

As with DORV, there is great variability among hearts with DOLV and AV concordant connection. Because of its rarity, generalizations are even more difficult than for DORV. Diagnosis can be ambiguous, because some override of one of the great arteries is commonly present. Defining a DOLV based on 50% or more of great arterial override may result in a substantially higher number of cases being reported. Many patients with transposition of the great arteries with subaortic VSD, pulmonary stenosis, and a variable degree of aortic override have been misclassified as having DOLV. An exclusive or near-exclusive origin of both great arteries from the LV will identify the classic form of DOLV and prevent misdiagnosis. Although it was initially thought bilaterally absent conus was a prerequisite for the diagnosis of DOLV, all possible conal configurations have been described: subpulmonic, subaortic, bilaterally present, and bilaterally absent. When present, the length of conus under either great artery is typically relatively short and is an important factor in causing the great artery to embryologically align with the LV.

A segmental approach is necessary for complete understanding of this malformation (see "Anatomic Considerations in Congenital Heart Disease" in Chapter 26). DOLV occurs in each of the four basic hearts (Fig. 46.1) but is most common in hearts with atrial situs solitus and ventricular right-handedness or Dloop (S,D,D). Morphologic characteristics of the VSD and its relationship to the great arteries at the level of the semilunar valves are similar to those in DORV and described by terms defined in "Ventricular Septal Defect" under Morphology in Chapter 45. Subaortic VSD is the most common, followed by subpulmonic, and then by doubly committed. VSD remote from the great arteries is rare (Table 46.1). Absence of a VSD (intact ventricular septum) is also rare but has been described.

### Atrial Situs Solitus and Ventricular Right-Handedness (D-Loop) (Atrioventricular Concordant Connection)</summary><div class="details-content">

In this subset, the aorta is usually in D-malposition (S,D,D), but examples occur with it in L-malposition (S,D,L) (see "Segmental approach of Van Praagh" in Chapter 26). In the former, the great arteries may appear in relatively normal position (aorta to the right and somewhat posterior to pulmonary trunk), side to side, or with aorta somewhat anterior to the pulmonary trunk.

*Ventricular Septal Defect.* The ventricular septum is rarely intact. Usually, a large VSD is present and lies between the limbs of the trabecula septomarginalis (septal band).

Most commonly, the VSD is *subaortic* in position (Fig. 46.2; see Table 46.1). It may extend back to the tricuspid anulus, or it may be separated from the anulus by a muscular bridge. When the aorta overrides the VSD and arises in part from the right ventricle, the VSD is *juxtaaortic*, and this entity begins to merge with transposition of the great arteries.

When the VSD is subpulmonary, it is usually more anterior and well separated from the tricuspid valve by a rather wide band of muscle. In some cases, the pulmonary trunk origin overrides the VSD and lies in part over the right ventricle. Malalignment of conal septum may be present and can cause aortic outflow obstruction and be associated with aortic arch hypoplasia.

Occasionally the VSD is juxtaarterial and lies immediately below both great arteries (doubly committed; see Chapter 45, Fig. 45.6). The VSD is typically very large, and there is absence or near absence of conus bilaterally, resulting in aortic-mitral and pulmonary-mitral fibrous continuity and side-by-side great arteries. It is frequently difficult to decide whether DOLV or DORV is present, in which case the term *double outlet both ventricles* is appropriate.

*Conal Pattern.* Most often there is absence of a subaortic conus and presence of aortic-mitral fibrous continuity and a subpulmonary conus displaced into the LV (Fig. 46.3). Rarely, bilaterally absent conus permits aortic-mitral-tricuspid and pulmonarytricuspid fibrous continuity (see Fig. 46.2). In this event, both semilunar valves arise at the same level. Very rarely, only a subaortic conus is present. There may be a conus bilaterally.

*Pulmonary Stenosis.* Pulmonary stenosis is present in most cases and is either valvar (sometimes with anular stenosis) or subvalvar when it is due to a restrictive subpulmonary conus with secondary fibrosis of the ostium. When the VSD is subaortic and there is infundibular pulmonary stenosis, the great arteries are usually relatively normally interrelated.

*Right Ventricular and Tricuspid Valve Hypoplasia.* There is a tendency for the right ventricular sinus and tricuspid valve to be at least somewhat hypoplastic. The extreme example is coexistence of tricuspid atresia, with the two reported cases having ventricular right-handedness (S,D,D). Rarely, the tricuspid valve may show an Ebstein anomaly.

*Left Ventricle.* The LV is usually well formed. One case of mitral atresia with large LV and infundibular outlet chamber (S,D,D segmental arrangement) has been reported.

*Conduction System.* Position of AV node and bundle of His is normal. Thus, the bundle penetrates from a normally positioned posterior AV node through the right trigone in the region of the commissure between tricuspid septal and anterior leaflets and at the base of the noncoronary aortic cusp, and its two branches distribute in normal fashion. Whether it is at risk during repair depends on the relationship of the lower VSD margin to the tricuspid anulus (see "Location in Septum and Relationship to Conduction System" under Morphology in Section I of Chapter 33).

## Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

In hearts with DOLV and AV concordant connection, the LV is a common mixing chamber, receiving pulmonary venous blood through the mitral valve and caval blood through the right ventricle and VSD. Clinical presentation, however, is dominated by varying degrees of cyanosis due to a combination of the frequent occurrence of pulmonary stenosis and streaming caused by the variable malposition of the great arteries and their relationship to the VSD. Thus, streaming of desaturated right ventricular blood into the aorta may occur when the VSD is subaortic, leading to unexpectedly severe cyanosis. In the absence of pulmonary stenosis, heart failure often develops early in life because of large pulmonary blood flow.

In hearts with DOLV and AV discordant connection, the LV receives caval blood through the mitral valve, and pulmonary venous blood through the right ventricle and VSD. The tendency to develop severe cyanosis is more likely than in AV concordant connection.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Examination</summary><div class="details-content">

Physical findings, chest radiograph, and electrocardiogram are not diagnostic, but reflect cardiopulmonary physiology in each case.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Echocardiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Echocardiography with color flow imaging may be diagnostic, and associated morphologic lesions are readily detected. The main challenge is to distinguish DOLV from other, more common conotruncal anomalies such as transposition of the great arteries and DORV (Fig. 46.4). The focus is on defining the VSD and the relationship of the VSD to the two great arteries, presence or absence of conus under each great artery, presence or absence of pulmonary stenosis, and positioning of the two great arteries in relation to each other. Coronary artery pattern is also important because some surgical repair techniques used for DOLV require specific knowledge of coronary artery course. Other imaging studies are not usually performed unless clinical presentation indicates that specific and quantitative knowledge of physiology of the pulmonary vasculature is important in formulating the management plan. Even with increased recognition of DOLV by echocardiography, diagnosis may be elusive. In a series with six patients, reported by McElhinney, Reddy and Hanley, with both echocardiography and cardiac catheterization available, diagnosis was made preoperatively in four patients and intraoperatively in two.

### Cardiac Catheterization and Cineangiography</summary><div class="details-content">

Biplane cineangiography, selecting injection sites and projections suited to the individual problem, is diagnostic but provides little additional morphologic information beyond that obtained by echocardiography. Because DOLV is rare, cineangiography was once performed to support a diagnosis made by echocardiography, but this role has now been overtaken by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Cardiac catheterization, however, remains the only way to obtain specific hemodynamic and oximetric data if it is necessary to define the physiology of the pulmonary vasculature.

When catheterization is performed, both left and right ventricular injections are desirable. With appropriate projections, angiography can confirm the diagnosis of DOLV and rule out other conotruncal anomalies, but it is rarely necessary in current practice. Angiography can also define the position and number of VSDs, presence and site of pulmonary and aortic stenosis, and size of the right ventricle and tricuspid valve relative to the LV and mitral valve (see Fig. 46.3).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Magnetic Resonance and Computed Tomography Imaging</summary><div class="details-content">

Computed tomography provides no specific advantages over MRI in diagnosing DOLV and thus is not routinely used. MRI may be used to supplement the intracardiac morphologic information obtained by echocardiography (Fig. 46.5).

## Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

Natural history of patients with DOLV without pulmonary stenosis appears to be similar to that of patients with isolated large VSD (see "Natural History" in Section I of Chapter 33), except that progressive VSD narrowing and closure has not been documented in DOLV. Cyanosis may be present because of the common mixing chamber beneath the great arteries, but considerable streaming of flow is often present and accounts for significant variability in arterial oxygen levels.

Natural history of patients with DOLV and pulmonary stenosis is similar to that of patients with tetralogy of Fallot (see "Natural History" in Section I of Chapter 34), and in both entities the degree of hypoxia and clinical course are directly related to severity of pulmonary stenosis.

</div></details>

## Technique of Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Identification of Morphology</summary><div class="details-content">

There are usually no clues to the specific diagnosis of DOLV from external examination of the heart when it is exposed at operation. A patient may be operated with the working diagnosis of Tetralogy of Fallot. A clue that the diagnosis may be DOLV is an unexpected markedly posterior location of the pulmonary trunk and the PV anulus. (Christo Tchervenkov personal experience). Generally, only AV connection—concordant or discordant—can be confirmed by external observation. For this reason, detailed and complete preoperative imaging must be performed to identify all aspects of the anomaly. Even when this has been done, relation of the great arteries to VSD, and VSD to AV valves, may be different from that anticipated from preoperative imaging. Thus, when the heart is opened, accurate evaluation must be made of all aspects of morphology.

When the AV connection is *concordant*, finding a large VSD located far downstream (distally) and anterosuperiorly in the ventricular septum mandates thorough consideration of all diagnostic possibilities associated with a VSD in this position, including not only DOLV but also ordinary subpulmonary VSD with ventriculoarterial concordant connection, tetralogy of Fallot with subpulmonary VSD (if pulmonary stenosis coexists), anterosuperior VSD with complete transposition of the great arteries, DORV with doubly committed VSD, and Taussig-Bing–type DORV (which has its VSD in this same position, but an aorta far removed from the VSD and clearly originating from the right ventricle alone).

When the AV connection is *discordant*, the same detailed observations must be made. These generally reveal findings similar to those of congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries (see Chapter 47), but in DOLV, the aorta as well as the pulmonary trunk arise entirely or in large part from the right-sided (in atrial situs solitus) LV.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Repair of Double Outlet Left Ventricle and Atrioventricular Concordant Connection</summary><div class="details-content">

Preparations for operation, sternotomy, and placement of pursestring sutures are those generally used. Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is established, the aorta clamped, and usual techniques of myocardial management instituted. The usual oblique right atriotomy is made and the interior of the right ventricle is inspected through the tricuspid valve. It can usually be confirmed from the right atrium that neither great artery arises wholly or in large part from the right ventricle, and that the VSD is in the outlet portion of the ventricular septum.

Usually, repair is made through a vertical incision in the distal portion of the right ventricle. After placing stay sutures, the following are verified: position of VSD, origin of aorta and pulmonary trunk from LV and their relationships to the VSD, and the nature of any pulmonary stenosis.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### With Pulmonary Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

When pulmonary stenosis is present in a DOLV, it is usually not possible to relieve it directly and do a completely intraventricular repair, as is described for patients with no coexisting pulmonary stenosis. However, if examination of the pulmonary valve through a vertical anterior incision in the pulmonary trunk or through the ventricle shows it to be widely patent and the subvalvar fibromuscular obstructing ring localized, the ring may be excised satisfactorily, permitting a completely intraventricular repair.

Usually, the pulmonary anulus is small and the subvalvar stenosis too long and narrow for a simple intraventricular repair to be effective. It is not possible to place a transanular patch, because the left anterior descending coronary artery is immediately in front of the pulmonary anulus, and a valved extracardiac conduit or a variant of the Lecompte operation (réparation à l'étage ventriculaire [REV]) is necessary. In the former, the pulmonary trunk is transected at the sinutubular junction, and the proximal pulmonary trunk stump is oversewn by placing two rows of continuous polypropylene sutures at the level of the valve. Then the VSD is closed by suturing into place a patch, taking the usual precautions to avoid damaging the bundle of His (Fig. 46.6A). An allograft-valved extracardiac conduit is prepared (see Figs. 35.16 and 35.17 in Chapter 35) and sutured distally to the pulmonary trunk and proximally to the right ventriculotomy (Fig. 46.6B). Returning to the right atrium, the foramen ovale is closed, and the remainder of the operation is completed in the usual manner.

Alternatively, a conduit can be avoided using a technique of the main pulmonary artery translocation onto the RV and a "transanular" patching of the RVOT-pulmonary trunk confluence with autologous pericardium (Christo Tchervenkov personal experience). The translocation of the pulmonary trunk to the right ventricle can be accomplished without the need to transect and reconstruct the aorta.

Patch augmentation of the right ventricular outflow tract may be possible in selected cases of DOLV with pulmonary stenosis. For this to be possible, the pulmonary trunk must be either anterior or side by side relative to the aorta, and the coronary arteries must course behind the pulmonary root (Fig. 46.7).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Without Pulmonary Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

When pulmonary stenosis is not present, the morphologic arrangements may allow intraventricular tunnel repair. A contoured patch is placed into the VSD so that the right ventricle ejects into the pulmonary trunk while the LV continues to eject into the aorta. The VSD may have to be enlarged anteriorly and superiorly before this is done. When an intraventricular tunnel repair is not possible, the pulmonary orifice is closed off from within the ventricle or from within the pulmonary trunk, or the pulmonary trunk is divided; the VSD is closed, leaving the aorta coming off the LV; and an allograft-valved extracardiac conduit is placed between the right ventricle and pulmonary trunk. Alternatively, the pulmonary trunk can be connected to the right ventricle by the Lecompte operation (REV). If the pulmonary valve is relatively normal in size (with or without subpulmonic stenosis), the pulmonary root, including the valve, can be translocated to the right ventricle in the same fashion. This has been described in patients with DOLV who have a subpulmonic conus. Additionally, the intact pulmonary valve and root can be translocated to the right ventricle even when a subpulmonary conus is absent, as described by Hanley and colleagues (Fig. 46.8). Pulmonary root translocation in the presence of severe pulmonary stenosis, using a monocusp valve and patch augmentation of the small pulmonary anulus, has also been described.

Rarely, in the absence of pulmonary stenosis, are these types of repair not possible. It may then be possible to close the VSD with a contoured patch so the right ventricle ejects into the aorta and the LV into the pulmonary trunk, and to complete the operation by performing an arterial switch operation, as recently reported.

## Results</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Survival</summary><div class="details-content">

*Early (Hospital) Death.* Luciani and colleagues recently reviewed the literature of patients with DOLV undergoing biventricular repair of DOLV.

Over a period of 45 years there were 37 cases of DOLV, including 3 from their personal experience. Median age at repair was 48 months, with 7 infants. There were 3 hospital deaths (8%) with median follow-up of 26 months.

In the current era, hospital mortality with biventricular repair of DOLV is expected to be about 5%, based on reports of patients doing well after surgical correction. Total number of surgically managed cases reported, however, is only several dozen. The experience of McElhinney and colleagues has been updated to include six patients undergoing intracardiac repair, with no early or midterm deaths (Hanley, FL: personal communication; 2002).

*Time-Related Survival.* The number of cases is so small, and variability of the anomaly and repairs so great, little useful information is available about time-related survival and risk factors for death. However, these are probably similar to those for DORV and transposition, VSD, and left ventricular outflow obstruction.

In the series by Luciani and colleagues mentioned in the previous section, there was 1 late death and 7 reoperations during a median follow-up of 26 months. Ten-year survival was 87%. Late reoperations included 3 conduit replacements, one conversion to a Fontan for right ventricular failure, one for residual VSD.

Fascinating information has been recently reported from Sick Kids in Toronto on the overall outcome and characteristics of the anatomical spectrum and treatment of patients with DOLV (See Fig. 46.9).

Between 1960 and 2008, 19 patients with DOLV were identified. Seven patients underwent biventricular repairs, 9 underwent univentricular palliation, 1 had palliative care and 2 were seen for consultation only. There were 6 deaths, 5 from cardiac causes and 1 due to a genetic syndrome. Five-year survival for both biventricular and univentricular repairs was 70%–75%. Most surviving patients had residual cardiac abnormalities despite surgical repair. The authors conclude that DOLV is a rare cardiac malformation associated with high mortality and guarded outcomes from residual lesions.

How do we explain the discrepancy in outcome between this important series from the Sick Kids and the other published reports? The Sick Kids series probably has the true denominator of the anatomic spectrum of all patients with DOLV. Due to the rarity of DOLV, most of the previous publications are small series or case reports with those patients undergoing biventricular repair.

Therefore, the small number of patients and variability in the morphology and surgical procedures mitigate against obtaining sufficient information in this area to draw reasonable inferences.

## Indications for Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

Diagnosis of DOLV is an indication for operation. When pulmonary stenosis coexists, main pulmonary artery translocation onto the RV and pericardial patch augmentation of the RVOT–pulmonary artery confluence may be considered optimal and young age is not a contraindication. Conduit repair may also be performed in infancy, accepting the need for early reoperation and conduit replacement. Classic shunting operations remain an alternative. Arguments for and against this approach are similar to those outlined for tetralogy of Fallot (see Chapter 34).

In the absence of pulmonary stenosis, corrective operation should usually be performed in the first 6 months of life if preoperative imaging studies support the likely success of intraventricular repair. Less desirably, initial pulmonary artery banding may be performed and with subsequent intracardiac repair. Ideal timing of the subsequent repair may be influenced by morphology.

When there is right ventricular and tricuspid valvar hypoplasia, with *z*-value for diameter of the tricuspid valve less than -2, a Fontan operation (see Chapter 52) or, alternatively, the "oneand-a-half" ventricle repair should be considered.
