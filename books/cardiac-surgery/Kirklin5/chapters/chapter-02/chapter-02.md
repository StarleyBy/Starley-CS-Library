# Chapter 2: Hypothermia, Circulatory Arrest, and Cardiopulmonary Bypass

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (HCA)
- **Definition:** Profound hypothermia (≤14°C), deep hypothermia (14.1–20°C), moderate hypothermia (20.1–28°C), mild hypothermia (28.1–34°C)
- **Oxygen consumption (VO₂):** Decreases with temperature (Q10 ≈ 2.7); van't Hoff law best describes relationship (Fig. 2.1)
- **No-reflow phenomenon:** Regional vascular occlusion preventing reperfusion after ischemia; hypothermia to 20°C prevents it
- **Brain damage risk factors:** Duration of arrest (>40–60 min), temperature, uneven cooling, cerebral blood flow, biochemical milieu (glucose, glutamate excitotoxicity), patient age (very young and very old at higher risk)
- **Safe duration:** 30 min at 15–20°C highly likely safe; 45 min probable; 60 min associated with irreversible damage in some patients (Fig. 2.9)
- **Monitoring:** EEG (electrocerebral silence at ~18°C), BIS, TCD, NIRS; none proven to improve outcomes
- **Spinal cord protection:** Hypothermia provides important protection during aortic clamping (Fig. 2.7)
- **Renal/liver protection:** Safe arrest times longer than brain; 60 min at 20°C produces no structural abnormalities

### Cardiopulmonary Bypass (CPB) Physiology
- **Controlled variables:** Arterial output (roller or centrifugal pump), venous input (VAVR, siphon, venous pump), gas exchange (membrane oxygenators), heparin (ACT >400–480 sec), perfusate (hematocrit 0.20–0.25 during hypothermia, higher during rewarming)
- **Response variables:** Whole-body inflammatory response (complement activation C3a/C5a, neutrophil activation, platelet dysfunction, cytokine release)
- **Complement activation:** Higher C3a levels associated with increased morbidity (Table 2.4, Fig. 2.14)
- **Oxygen consumption (VO₂):** Related to perfusion flow rate and temperature (Fig. 2.11, 2.15–2.16)
- **Cerebral blood flow:** Preserved autoregulation during normothermia; pressure-dependent during hypothermia in infants
- **Hemolysis:** Plasma free hemoglobin normally <100 mg/dL
- **Safe duration of total CPB:** Measured in hours; partial CPB safe for days/weeks (ECMO)

### Clinical CPB Methodology
- **Monitoring:** TEE essential; epiaortic ultrasound for atheroma detection
- **Cannulation:** Aortic (distal to brachiocephalic artery); venous (single two-stage or bicaval); peripheral (femoral, axillary) for reoperations or minimal access
- **Heparin/protamine:** ACT >400 sec (normothermia) or >480 sec (hypothermia); protamine 1–1.5 mg per 100 units heparin
- **De-airing:** Critical to prevent air embolism; CO₂ field flooding, needle vent in ascending aorta, TEE guidance
- **Ultrafiltration:** Modified ultrafiltration (MUF) beneficial in neonates/infants
- **Minimal incisions:** Partial sternotomy, thoracotomy, port access

### Clinical HCA Methodology — Adults
- **Brain protection strategies:** Antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP) preferred for prolonged arch repairs; retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP) also used
- **ACP flow:** 10–15 mL/kg/min, pressure ~50 mmHg
- **Bilateral vs unilateral ACP:** Bilateral may be superior for prolonged procedures
- **Temperature:** Moderate hypothermia (20–28°C) with ACP; deep hypothermia (14–20°C) with HCA alone
- **Left thoracotomy approach:** For distal arch/descending aorta; open proximal anastomosis under deep hypothermia

### Clinical HCA Methodology — Neonates/Infants/Children
- **Current practice:** Continuous perfusion (full-body CPB or ACP) preferred over HCA for most operations
- **HCA technique:** Cool for ≥20 min to nasopharyngeal 16–18°C; pH-stat strategy may be superior for neurologic outcomes; hematocrit ≥0.25
- **Continuous ACP:** Cannulate brachiocephalic artery; flow 40 mL/kg/min during arch reconstruction

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## SECTION I: HYPOTHERMIC CIRCULATORY ARREST</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

- **1950:** Bigelow introduced whole-body hypothermia for cardiac surgery
- **1953:** Lewis and Taufic (surface cooling) and Swan (series) repaired ASD using hypothermia
- **1958:** Sealy combined hypothermia with CPB
- **1960s:** Kirklin, Horiuchi, Dillard, Barratt-Boyes developed techniques for infants
- **1975:** Griepp first series of aortic arch aneurysms resected under HCA
- **1980s–1990s:** Ueda introduced retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP); Kazui introduced antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Hypotheses</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Safe duration hypothesis:** Safe circulatory arrest time is inversely related to temperature
- **Metabolic hypothesis:** Hypothermia reduces metabolic activity to allow cell viability during ischemia; VO₂ reduction directly relates to safe arrest duration

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Definition of Hypothermia</summary><div class="details-content">

| Grade | Temperature |
|---|---|
| Profound hypothermia | ≤14°C |
| Deep hypothermia | 14.1–20°C |
| Moderate hypothermia | 20.1–28°C (low-moderate 20.1–24°C, high-moderate 24.1–28°C) |
| Mild hypothermia | 28.1–34°C |

- **Best correlation with brain temperature:** Nasopharyngeal, tympanic membrane, CPB arterial outlet temperature

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Oxygen Consumption During Hypothermia</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Relationship between Oxygen Consumption and Body Temperature</summary><div class="details-content">

**Kinetics of oxygen consumption (Box 2.1):**

- **Arrhenius equation:** \( k = A e^{\frac{E_a}{RT}} \)
- **van't Hoff law (Q10):** \( Q_{10} = \left(\frac{k_1}{k_2}\right)^{\frac{10}{T_1 - T_2}} \)
- **Hyperbolic equation:** \( \frac{1}{\dot{V}O_2} = \frac{1}{c} + \frac{d}{\dot{Q}} \)

**Total body VO₂ after surface cooling (Fig. 2.1):**

Van't Hoff law best fits data; Q10 ≈ 2.7

$$ \log_{10} \dot{V}O_2 = -0.69 \pm 0.061 + 0.043 \pm 0.0021 \cdot T $$

![](_page_5_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.1** Temperature and oxygen consumption. (A) Data points relating VO₂ to body temperature in dogs. (B) Nomogram with VO₂ as percentage of control at 37°C.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Total Body Oxygen Consumption after Surface Cooling</summary><div class="details-content">

Kent and Peirce studied VO₂ in experimental animals during hypothermia produced by combined surface and core cooling. Their data are similar to those obtained from surface cooling alone.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Oxygen Consumption During Hypothermia in Tissue Slices and Isolated Organs</summary><div class="details-content">

Studies of tissue slices at various temperatures show that oxygen consumption is, in fact, reduced by hypothermia. These studies and those of isolated organs suggest that Q10, although differing from tissue to tissue, is on average about 2 (Table 2.1).

A striking fact is that VO₂ is not reduced to near zero at temperatures close to 0°C. Metabolic activity is therefore continuing, and the time limits of safe circulatory arrest must be finite.

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Other Phenomena During Hypothermia and Circulatory Arrest</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### No-Reflow Phenomenon</summary><div class="details-content">

- Obstructive lesion of microcirculation preventing reperfusion
- Caused by severe hypoxia/anoxia, not circulatory arrest per se
- Hypothermia to 20°C prevents no-reflow following 90 minutes of anoxia
- Mechanisms: hypoxic endothelial cell injury → endothelin-1 release, procoagulant response, edema, neutrophil/platelet plugging

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Changes in Plasma Volume</summary><div class="details-content">

- Progressive hemoconcentration and decreased plasma volume during surface cooling to 25°C
- Represents sequestration of plasma and leakage into interstitial fluid compartment

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Damaging Effects of Circulatory Arrest During Hypothermia</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Brain Function and Structure: Risk Factors for Damage</summary><div class="details-content">

**Temperature and Duration (Fig. 2.2):**
- 30 min at 15–20°C: tolerated without evident damage; ATP declines to 35% but recovers
- 45–60 min: associated with irreversible structural/functional damage in some
- Temporary neurologic dysfunction (TND) increases substantially >60 min

**Characteristics of the Cooling Process (Table 2.2):**
- Uneven cooling is risk factor
- Rapid core cooling alone associated with higher major neurologic events (6% vs 0% with surface + core cooling)

**Cerebral Blood Flow During Cooling and Rewarming (Fig. 2.3):**
- CBF decreases with temperature during cooling
- CBF reduced during rewarming after circulatory arrest

**FIGURE 2.3** Cerebral blood flow during rewarming after 48 min circulatory arrest at 18°C — lower than without arrest.

**Biochemical Milieu:**
- Hyperglycemia (glucose in prime) → excessive glycolysis → lactic acidosis → tissue damage
- Glutamate excitotoxicity: neuroexcitatory amino acids accumulate during energy depletion → intracellular calcium increase → neuronal injury/death
- Apoptosis demonstrated in piglets after 90 min HCA at 19°C (caspase 3 and 8 elevation)

**Electroencephalography (EEG) (Figs. 2.4–2.5):**
- Electrocerebral silence at mean nasopharyngeal 17.8°C
- Only 60% achieve silence by 18°C or 30 min cooling
- Limitations: monitors only superficial cortex; subjective interpretation

![](_page_9_Figure_8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.4** Distribution of nasopharyngeal temperatures at EEG landmarks.

![](_page_10_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.5** Interval from circulatory arrest to EEG quiescence vs nasopharyngeal temperature.

**Bispectral Index (BIS):**
- Monitors depth of anesthesia; same limitations as standard EEG

**Transcranial Doppler Ultrasonography (TCD):**
- Measures middle cerebral artery flow; detects emboli; requires expertise

**Near-Infrared Spectroscopy (NIRS):**
- Regional cerebral oxygen saturation; limitations include fixed arteriovenous ratio, anterior brain only

**Patients at High Risk of Cerebral Complications During Aortic Arch Surgery:**
- Shaggy aorta (severe atheromatous lesions)
- Leukoaraiosis (white matter hyperintensity on MRI) — associated with increased stroke risk

**Patient Age:**
- Infants <3 months: reduced cognitive, language, motor performance at age 4 years after HCA
- Adults: increasing age is predictor of both stroke and TND

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Effects of Brain Damage</summary><div class="details-content">

**Definition of Brain Complication:**
- **Permanent neurologic dysfunction (PND):** Focal stroke or coma with CT correlates, persisting at discharge
- **Temporary neurologic dysfunction (TND):** Confusion, agitation, delirium, obtundation resolving before discharge; normal CT

**Evidence of Gross Neurologic Damage:**
- Choreoathetosis: 1–12% of infants/children; may persist; related to prolonged HCA or very cold perfusion
- Seizures: 5–10%; associated with worse neurodevelopmental outcomes (lower IQ at age 4 years, OR 8.4)
- Stroke in adults: related to older age, atheroma, HCA >40 min

**Postoperative Intellectual Capacity (Table 2.3, Fig. 2.6):**
- 16% of children had IQ ≤80 late postoperatively; 6.2% unexplained
- Boston Circulatory Arrest Study: HCA associated with worse motor coordination at age 4 years; not with lower IQ

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Spinal Cord Function</summary><div class="details-content">

- Less susceptible to ischemic injury than brain
- In 161 patients with HCA (mean 38 min) for thoracoabdominal aorta, paraplegia/paresis <3.5% for ischemic intervals up to 138 min (Fig. 2.7)

![](_page_13_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.7** Risk of paraplegia or paresis vs duration of spinal cord ischemia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Renal Function and Structure</summary><div class="details-content">

**Experimental Studies:**
- At 37°C, 20 min safe; 30 min produces cell death; hypothermia prolongs safe time

**Studies in Humans:**
- Important oliguria occasionally complicates recovery; renal failure primarily from low cardiac output, not HCA
- In 161 adult patients, postoperative renal failure requiring dialysis 2.6%

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Liver Function</summary><div class="details-content">

- At 37°C, serious derangements follow 45+ min complete hepatic arrest
- At 20–22°C, 60 min circulatory arrest produces no structural/functional abnormalities

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Safe Duration of Circulatory Arrest (Fig. 2.9)</summary><div class="details-content">

- **30 min at 18°C:** Essentially complete safety (structural and functional)
- **45 min at 18°C:** Probable safety for most; damage likely structural without functional sequelae
- **60 min at 18°C:** Most will have structural damage; 10–20% functional damage (often transient)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## SECTION II: WHOLE-BODY PERFUSION DURING CARDIOPULMONARY BYPASS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

- **1953:** Gibbon — first successful operation using CPB (ASD repair)
- **1954:** Lillehei — controlled cross-circulation
- **1955:** Kirklin — first published series using Mayo-Gibbon pump-oxygenator

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Uniqueness of Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

- Blood passes across nonendothelial foreign surfaces → activation of inflammatory response
- Many physiologic variables under direct external control (flow, pressure, temperature, blood gases)
- Patient becomes unique organism for several days post-CPB

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Controlled Variables</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Arterial Output to the Patient</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Roller pump:** Nonpulsatile; simple, reliable; must be nearly occlusive
- **Centrifugal (vortex) pump:** Flow varies with resistance; ultrasonic flow meter required; less risk of tubing rupture

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Venous Input From the Patient</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Vacuum-assisted venous return (VAVR):** Controlled vacuum (40–60 cm H₂O); reduces priming volume
- **Siphon (gravity) drainage:** Simple but requires larger cannulae; increases priming volume
- **Venous pumping:** Centrifugal pump; permits smaller cannulae; facilitates minimally invasive surgery

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Gas Exchange</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Membrane oxygenators (hollow-fiber, microporous):** Less blood damage than bubble oxygenators
- **True silicone rubber membrane:** No blood/gas interface; allows extended use (ECMO)

**Arterial Oxygen Levels:**
- Maintain PaO₂ ~250 mmHg; higher unnecessary (oxygen toxicity risk)

**Arterial Carbon Dioxide Pressure (Fig. 2.10):**
- **Alpha-stat strategy:** pH 7.4 at 37°C (uncorrected); preserves cerebral autoregulation
- **pH-stat strategy:** pH 7.4 corrected to patient temperature; increases CBF ("luxury perfusion")

![](_page_17_Figure_15.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.10** Hemoglobin saturation and temperature during rewarming on CPB.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Heparin Levels</summary><div class="details-content">

- Dose: 300–400 units/kg (3–4 mg/kg)
- Goal ACT: >400 sec (normothermia) or >480 sec (hypothermia)
- Heparin resistance: antithrombin deficiency → fresh frozen plasma or antithrombin concentrate
- Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT): 1–5% of patients; use bivalirudin

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Perfusate</summary><div class="details-content">

**Diluent:** Balanced electrolyte solution without glucose or lactate

**Hemoglobin Concentration (Box 2.2):**
- Optimal hematocrit during hypothermic CPB: 0.20–0.25
- Higher hematocrit (0.25) associated with better neurologic outcomes in infants

**Box 2.2 — Algorithm for calculating patient-machine hematocrit:**

$$ VpB = 1000f \cdot wt \quad (f = 0.08 \text{ infants}, f = 0.065 \text{ older}) $$

$$ VpRBC = VpB \cdot HCTp $$

$$ VmRBC = VmB \cdot HCTm $$

$$ HCTpm = (VpRBC + VmRBC)/(VpB + VmB) $$

**Albumin Concentration:** No favorable effect from adding homologous albumin

**Other Additives:**
- Mannitol (0.5 g/kg): osmotic diuretic, oxygen free radical scavenger
- Furosemide (1–2 mg/kg at start of rewarming)
- Phentolamine (0.2 mg/kg) or phenoxybenzamine (1 mg/kg): α-blockade
- Corticosteroids: may attenuate complement-mediated leukocyte activation
- ε-Aminocaproic acid (EACA) or tranexamic acid: antifibrinolytics

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Changes During Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Total Systemic Blood Flow (Fig. 2.11)</summary><div class="details-content">

- At ≥28°C: 2.5 L/min/m² (infants/children), 2.2 L/min/m² (older), 1.8–2.0 L/min/m² (adults with BSA ≥2.0)
- At 18–20°C: 1 L/min/m² adequate; as low as 0.5 L/min/m² adequate for 30–60 min

**Relationship among flow, temperature, and VO₂:**

$$ 1/\dot{V}O_2 = 0.168 \cdot 10^{-0.0387 \cdot T} + 0.0378 \cdot \dot{Q}^{-1} \cdot 10^{-0.0253 \cdot T} $$

![](_page_21_Figure_8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.11** Nomogram of relationship of VO₂ to perfusion flow rate and temperature.

**Mixed Venous Oxygen Levels (Box 2.3):**
- \( C\overline{v}O_2 = 1.38 \cdot S\overline{v}O_2 \cdot [Hb] + P\overline{v}O_2 \cdot 0.003 \)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Arterial Pressure Waveform</summary><div class="details-content">

- Nonpulsatile flow is standard
- Evidence for benefit of pulsatile flow is inconclusive

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Systemic Venous Pressure</summary><div class="details-content">

- Should be kept close to zero (≤10 mmHg)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Pulmonary Venous Pressure</summary><div class="details-content">

- Should be kept at zero (≤10 mmHg) to prevent pulmonary edema

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Temperature</summary><div class="details-content">

- Blood temperature should not exceed 39.5°C during rewarming
- Water temperature should not exceed 42°C

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Response Variables</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Whole-Body (Nonspecific) Inflammatory Response to Use of a Pump-Oxygenator</summary><div class="details-content">

**Humoral Response:**
- Activation of Hageman factor (XII) → complement, kallikrein, fibrinolytic cascades
- Complement activation (alternative pathway) → anaphylatoxins C3a and C5a (Fig. 2.13)

![](_page_27_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.13** C3a levels at end of CPB — cumulative percentile plot.

**Cellular Response:**
- Neutrophil activation → sequestration in lungs, release of proteases and oxygen free radicals
- Platelet response: count decreases to ~60% of pre-CPB; GPIb and GPIIb/IIIa receptors reduced (Fig. 2.12)

![](_page_26_Figure_8.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.12** GPIb antigen on platelet membrane during CPB — untreated vs aprotinin.

**Metabolic Response:** Massive catecholamine release during CPB

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Details of the Whole-Body Inflammatory Response</summary><div class="details-content">

**Neutrophil Activation:**
- Pulmonary sequestration occurs during CPB
- Neutrophil elastase correlates with CPB duration

**Platelet Response:**
- Platelet count decreases to ~60% of prebypass value
- GPIb and GPIIb/IIIa receptors markedly reduced

**Complement Activation (Fig. 2.14, Table 2.4):**
- More than 50% of patients have C3a levels >1000 ng/mL at end of CPB
- Higher C3a levels associated with increased morbidity

![](_page_27_Figure_7.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.14** Probability of morbidity after CPB according to C3a level.

**Protein Denaturation:** Occurs at blood/gas interface

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Oxygen Consumption</summary><div class="details-content">

**Total Body Oxygen Consumption (Fig. 2.15):**
- Hyperbolic relationship with perfusion flow rate

![](_page_29_Figure_14.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.15** Relationship of total body VO₂ to perfusion flow rate at normothermia.

**Cerebral Oxygen Consumption (Fig. 2.16):**
- \( CMRO_2 = 0.021e^{0.1147 \cdot T} \)

![](_page_30_Figure_13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.16** Relation between body temperature and cerebral oxygen consumption during full flow CPB.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Mixed Venous Oxygen Levels</summary><div class="details-content">

- When most of microcirculation is perfused, \( P\overline{v}O_2 \) 30–40 mmHg, \( S\overline{v}O_2 \) 60–70% indicate adequate perfusion

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Metabolic Acid-Base Status</summary><div class="details-content">

- Metabolic acidosis tends to develop; lactic acid rarely exceeds 5 mmol/L

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Hemolysis</summary><div class="details-content">

- Plasma free hemoglobin: 8.3 ± 1.3 mg/dL before CPB, 91 ± 8.4 mg/dL after CPB
- Renal shutdown unlikely unless >100 mg/dL

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Systemic Vascular Resistance and Arterial Blood Pressure</summary><div class="details-content">

- Decreases abruptly at onset of CPB, then gradually increases
- Vasoplegic syndrome: low SVR, low MAP despite adequate flow; treat with vasopressin or methylene blue

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Distribution of Blood Flow</summary><div class="details-content">

**Cerebral Blood Flow:**
- ~25 mL/min/100g at 25°C
- Autoregulation preserved at MAP 60–150 mmHg during normothermia
- Pressure-dependent during hypothermia in infants

**Cutaneous Blood Flow:** Severely reduced during nonpulsatile CPB

**Venous Tone:** Increased (persists postoperatively)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Catecholamine Response (Fig. 2.18)</summary><div class="details-content">

- Massive catecholamine release during CPB
- Epinephrine increases at CPB onset; declines after bypass
- Norepinephrine increases in hypertensive patients

![](_page_33_Figure_13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.18** Plasma catecholamines and arterial pressures during CPB.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Adrenal Cortical Hormones</summary><div class="details-content">

- Large increases in cortisol and ACTH with initiation of CPB

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Vasopressin</summary><div class="details-content">

- Large increases in ADH concentration during CPB

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Body Composition (Fig. 2.19)</summary><div class="details-content">

- Extracellular fluid volume increased post-CPB (directly related to CPB duration)

![](_page_34_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.19** Relation between duration of CPB and increment in extracellular fluid.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Thermal Balance</summary><div class="details-content">

- Heat loss during CPB; patients leave OR with deficit of at least 330 kJ
- Hypermetabolic state exists for at least 6 hours after CPB

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Agents of Damage</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Foreign Surfaces</summary><div class="details-content">

- Primary inciting agent for damaging effects of CPB
- Greatest in oxygenator (largest boundary layer)
- Coated circuits reduce deposition of formed blood elements

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Shear Stresses</summary><div class="details-content">

- Damage leukocytes and erythrocytes
- Intracardiac sucker systems particularly damaging

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Incorporation of Foreign Substances</summary><div class="details-content">

- Air bubbles, particulate matter, platelet aggregates embolize to patient
- Microembolization correlates with neuropsychometric deficits

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Heparin</summary><div class="details-content">

- Imperfect anticoagulant; permits microthrombi formation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Protamine</summary><div class="details-content">

- Activates complement (classic pathway); may cause bronchospasm, hypotension

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Prevention of Undesirable Responses</summary><div class="details-content">

- Only palliative measures available; developments in molecular biology may provide future techniques

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Safe Duration of Total Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

- Duration is risk factor for morbidity (Fig. 2.20–2.21)
- Partial CPB safe for days/weeks (ECMO)

**FIGURE 2.20** Probability of morbidity after CPB according to duration.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## SECTION III: CLINICAL METHODOLOGY OF CARDIOPULMONARY BYPASS</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### General Comments and Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

- CPB should be flexible; combined with hypothermia in many situations
- Alternative techniques: off-pump CABG, bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt, percutaneous valve replacement

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Clinical Practice Guidelines for Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

- Limited number and scope due to paucity of RCTs

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Monitoring Devices</summary><div class="details-content">

- TEE essential for monitoring cardiac function and detecting intracardiac air
- Epiaortic ultrasound for aortic atheroma detection

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Positioning Patient</summary><div class="details-content">

- Both arms at side for median sternotomy
- Pad under back in infants

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Preparing Surgical Field</summary><div class="details-content">

- Both groins prepared for potential femoral cannulation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Incision</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Primary Median Sternotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

- Vertical midline from suprasternal notch to xiphoid
- Pericardium opened longitudinally

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Alternative Primary Incisions</summary><div class="details-content">

- Partial sternotomy, thoracotomy, port access (Fig. 2.23)

![](_page_48_Picture_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.23** Currently used sternotomy and thoracotomy incisions.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Preparation for Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

- Left atrial monitoring catheter inserted into right superior pulmonary vein (Fig. 2.22)

![](_page_38_Picture_12.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.22** Positions of cannulae and catheters.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Siting and Purse-String Sutures for Aortic Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Within pericardial reflection, proximal to brachiocephalic artery
- Two purse-string sutures of 2-0 or 3-0 polyester or polypropylene

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Siting and Purse-String Sutures for Venous Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Only right atrial appendage for single venous cannula
- SVC and IVC purse strings for bicaval cannulation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Heparinization and Later Protamine Administration</summary><div class="details-content">

- ACT baseline after sternotomy
- Heparin 300–400 units/kg; ACT >400 sec before cannulation
- Maintain ACT >400 sec (normothermia) or >480 sec (hypothermia)
- Protamine 1–1.5 mg per 100 units heparin

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Arterial Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Direct cannulation into ascending aorta
- In infants: side-biting clamp with longitudinal incision

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Venous Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Single two-stage (tip in IVC, side holes in RA) or bicaval with caval tapes

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Commencing Cardiopulmonary Bypass and Left Heart Venting</summary><div class="details-content">

- Perfusion flow increased gradually to ~2.2 L/min/m²
- Left heart vent: through right superior pulmonary vein, ASD, pulmonary trunk, or LV apex

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Cardiopulmonary Bypass During Operation and Rewarming</summary><div class="details-content">

- Nasopharyngeal temperature lowered to at least 32°C
- Rewarming: water at 42°C; blood not exceed 39.5°C

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### De-Airing the Heart</summary><div class="details-content">

- Critical to prevent air embolism
- Steps: fill heart with fluid, aspirate residual air, tilt head down, needle vent in ascending aorta, CO₂ field flooding

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Completing Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

- Temporary pacing wires (atrial and ventricular)
- Protamine administered slowly

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Positioning Chest Tubes</summary><div class="details-content">

- One or two tubes in anterior mediastinum and pericardial cavity
- Pleural tube if pleural space entered

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Completing Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Pericardium left open
- Sternum closed with stainless steel wires

</div></details>

## Pump-Oxygenator and Special Situations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Components</summary><div class="details-content">

- Venous reservoir (hard-shell for VAVR)
- Oxygenator (microporous or true membrane)
- Heat exchanger
- Arterial pump (roller or centrifugal)
- Arterial line filter (40 µm)
- Ultrafiltration device

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Priming Volume</summary><div class="details-content">

- Each component adds to priming volume

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Miniaturized Cardiopulmonary Bypass Circuits (Table 2.6)</summary><div class="details-content">

- Closed circuit: centrifugal pump + membrane oxygenator; no venous reservoir
- Meta-analysis: reduced neurologic injury (OR 0.30), fewer transfusions (OR 0.42)

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### One Versus Two Venous Cannulae</summary><div class="details-content">

- Single two-stage optimal for CABG, aortic valve, mitral valve
- Bicaval for congenital heart disease, tricuspid valve

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Techniques to Minimize or Avoid Particulate Embolization</summary><div class="details-content">

- Epiaortic ultrasound to detect atheroma
- Axillary artery cannulation preferred over femoral

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Cardiopulmonary Bypass Established by Peripheral Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Femoral Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

- For reoperations, minimally invasive surgery
- Seldinger technique with TEE guidance

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Axillary Artery Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Preferred for aortic dissection; avoids retrograde embolization; allows ACP

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Other Sites</summary><div class="details-content">

- Brachial, brachiocephalic, carotid arteries

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Indications</summary><div class="details-content">

- Small midline or lateral chest incisions, resuscitation, high-risk percutaneous interventions

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Blood Conservation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Pre-bypass autologous donation (PAAD): 500–800 mL
- Ultrafiltration to concentrate residual pump blood
- Cell salvage
- Antifibrinolytics (EACA, tranexamic acid)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Left Superior Vena Cava</summary><div class="details-content">

- Options: occlude with tourniquet (test pressure), cannulate via coronary sinus, or direct cannulation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Other Systemic Venous Anomalies</summary><div class="details-content">

- Hepatic venous anomalies (heterotaxy syndromes) — same considerations as LSVC

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Left Atrial Pressure Monitoring</summary><div class="details-content">

- Fine polyvinyl catheter into left atrium
- Alternative: pulmonary artery catheter

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Alternative Primary Incisions</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Minimal Sternotomy and Thoracotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

- Partial midline sternotomy, parasternal incision, lateral intercostal approach

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Right Anterolateral Thoracotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

- For mitral/tricuspid valve, ASD; cosmetic in young women

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Left Thoracotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

- For mitral valve procedures, CABG

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Port Access</summary><div class="details-content">

- Thoracoscopic/robotic; peripheral cannulation; shorter hospital stay

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
## SECTION IV: CLINICAL METHODOLOGY OF HYPOTHERMIC CIRCULATORY ARREST AND ITS ALTERNATIVES</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Adults</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### General Comments and Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

- HCA widely used for ascending aorta, aortic arch, descending thoracic and thoracoabdominal aorta

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Arterial Pressure Monitoring</summary><div class="details-content">

- Multiple arterial lines (bilateral radial, lower body); perfusion line pressure of ACP

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Brain Protection</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Antegrade cerebral perfusion (ACP):** 10–15 mL/kg/min, pressure ~50 mmHg
- **Retrograde cerebral perfusion (RCP):** Simpler; may extend safe HCA period
- Bilateral ACP may be superior for prolonged procedures

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Amount of Brain Perfusion in ACP</summary><div class="details-content">

- 10–15 mL/kg/min, pressure ~50 mmHg (Kazui)
- Lower flow (400–500 mL/min) with higher pressure (70 mmHg) at lower temperature (Bachet)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Bilateral Versus Unilateral Perfusion</summary><div class="details-content">

- Bilateral may be superior as time of ACP increases (circle of Willis incomplete in 20–30%)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Body Temperature</summary><div class="details-content">

- Optimal temperature with ACP: 20–28°C
- Nadir temperature >24°C associated with lower mortality/stroke
- Rectal temperature should be kept below 30°C during circulatory arrest

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Left Thoracotomy for Distal Aortic Arch Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Approach and Exposure</summary><div class="details-content">

- Right semirecumbent position
- Fourth intercostal space entry
- Unilateral ventilation to minimize lung injury

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Arterial Cannulation and Venous Drainage</summary><div class="details-content">

- Left common femoral artery for arterial return
- Right common femoral vein for venous drainage (24–27 Fr)
- Cooling to 18–20°C

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Proximal Open Anastomosis</summary><div class="details-content">

- Deep hypothermia (18–20°C)
- Aortic occlusion balloon catheter into ascending aorta
- After anastomosis, elevate CVP to 15–18 mmHg (deep Trendelenburg)

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
### Neonates, Infants, and Children</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### General Comments and Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

- **Current practice:** Continuous perfusion (full-body CPB or ACP) preferred over HCA
- HCA not essential for any operation

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Preparation for Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

- Cooling blanket and ice around head

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Aortic cannula in ascending aorta
- Single venous cannula or bicaval

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Cardiopulmonary Bypass for Cooling</summary><div class="details-content">

- Flows 2.2–3.0 L/min/m²
- Cooling at least 20 min (preferably 30 min) to nasopharyngeal 16–18°C
- pH-stat may be superior for neurologic outcomes
- Hematocrit ≥0.25

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Circulatory Arrest</summary><div class="details-content">

- 30 min highly likely safe; >45 min increasing risk

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Cardiac Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Right atrial cannula often removed; aortic cannula removed if arch reconstruction

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Rewarming</summary><div class="details-content">

- Extreme care to avoid air entry at initiation of perfusion

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
#### Continuous Antegrade Cerebral Perfusion</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Preparation for Cardiopulmonary Bypass</summary><div class="details-content">

- Regional cerebral oxygen monitoring (NIRS)
- Complete mobilization of arch vessels

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Cannulation</summary><div class="details-content">

- Brachiocephalic artery cannulation (6F or 8F in neonates) (Figs. 2.24–2.25)
- Bicaval venous cannulation

![](_page_53_Picture_10.jpeg)

![](_page_53_Figure_11.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.24** Standard setup for neonatal arch reconstruction using continuous ACP.

![](_page_54_Picture_3.jpeg)

![](_page_54_Picture_4.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.25** Arterial cannula for brachiocephalic artery cannulation — obturator flush with tip.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Cardiopulmonary Bypass and Cooling</summary><div class="details-content">

- Cool to 22–24°C for ≥15 min
- Alpha-stat initially; hematocrit 25–30%
- Reduce flow to 120 mL/kg/min; clamp aorta

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Preparation for Cerebral Perfusion</summary><div class="details-content">

- Clamp arch vessels with neurovascular clips (Fig. 2.26)
- Reduce flow to 40 mL/kg/min; shift to pH-stat

![](_page_54_Picture_13.jpeg)

**FIGURE 2.26** Position of arterial cannula and neurovascular clips for ACP.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Arch Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

- Any type of arch reconstruction can be performed

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Reestablishing Total Body Perfusion and Associated Intracardiac Repairs</summary><div class="details-content">

- Unclamp descending aorta; remove neurovascular clips
- Increase flow to 120–150 mL/kg/min

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>
  
##### Rewarming</summary><div class="details-content">

- Increase flow to 200 mL/kg/min; alpha-stat
- Remove arterial cannula from brachiocephalic artery immediately after separation from CPB

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>
