### **Definitions, Nomenclature, and Classification**

The definition of the widely used term "Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome (HLHS)" has been controversial. Over the years, other terms have also been used, such as "Hypoplasia of the Aortic Tract Complexes" by Lev,1,2 "Critical Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction", and "Hypoplastic Left Heart Physiology," a term used in the fourth edition of this textbook. Agreement on a most appropriate term is challenged by the fact that we are dealing with a spectrum of underdevelopment of the left ventricle and the components of the left heart aorta complex. Some have restricted the term HLHS to those patients undergoing the Norwood procedure. However, a widely accepted guiding principle is that one cannot define anatomy, morphology, and nomenclature by the type of treatment used, as treatment may evolve over time. A small number of patients within the spectrum of HLHS may be amenable to biventricular repair. Furthermore, application of the Norwood procedure for lesions other than HLHS, as well as for some complex two ventricle hearts, demonstrates that the HLHS is not synonymous with conditions for which the Norwood procedure is applied.

#### **Left Heart-Aorta Complex**

The left heart-aorta complex is made up of several related structures:

- LV inflow: mitral valve
- Left ventricle as the pump
- Left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT)
- Aortic valve
- Ascending aorta
- Aortic arch

The combination of obstruction and hypoplasia of these structures may impair the ability of the left heart to adequately maintain the systemic circulation, which may be ductal dependent.

#### **Definitions Pertinent to Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome3**

*Atresia* is the congenital absence or closure of a normal body orifice or tubular organ. It is derived from the Greek and Latin, where "a" means without and "tresis" is the word for perforation. Thus, aortic valvar atresia can be defined as either absence or closure of the orifice of the aortic root.

*Stenosis* is a narrowing or a stricture of a duct or a canal. Aortic stenosis encompasses the supravalvar, valvar, or subvalvar regions. More specifically, aortic valvar stenosis is a narrowing of the orifice of the aortic valve due to fusion of the leaflets causing obstruction of blood flow.

*Hypoplasia* is the incomplete development or underdevelopment of an organ or tissue. A hypoplastic aortic valve can cause obstruction to blood flow because of its inadequate size.

*Obstruction* is the act of blocking or clogging, or the state of being blocked or clogged. Obstruction to blood flow may be caused by atresia, stenosis, or hypoplasia of a given structure.

It is particularly important to differentiate the meaning of "stenosis" and "hypoplasia." Although atresia and stenosis are mutually exclusive terms, hypoplasia may be present with both atresia and stenosis or can exist in isolation. Hypoplasia implies that the organ or tissue is less than its normal size. The terms "aortic stenosis" and "left ventricular outflow tract obstruction," for example, are not synonymous, although they have been used interchangeably in the past. In the setting of obstruction to the left ventricular outflow tract, the obstruction to the flow of blood may be caused by either aortic atresia, aortic stenosis, or by hypoplasia of the aortic valve.

*Syndrome* is a group of signs and symptoms that occur together and characterize a particular abnormality.

*Complex* is a whole being made up of interrelated parts.

After several years of debate, the International Society for Nomenclature of Paediatric and Congenital Heart Disease (ISNPCHD) agreed on the following definition of HLHS:

*Hypoplastic left heart syndrome is a spectrum of congenital cardiovascular malformations with normally aligned great arteries without a common atrioventricular junction, characterized by underdevelopment of the left heart with significant hypoplasia of the left ventricle including atresia, stenosis, or hypoplasia of the aortic or mitral valve, or both valves, and hypoplasia of the ascending aorta and aortic arch.*3-7

#### **Nomenclature**

We prefer to use the term *Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome*, rather than *Hypoplastic Left Heart Physiology* (the term used in the fourth edition) for the following reasons. *Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome* is a spectrum of cardiac phenotypes, with both anatomic and physiologic characteristics. It is the most widely used term by the community of professionals dealing with congenital heart disease. For example, in a recent Medline PubMed search, the term *Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome* provided 4518 results, whereas the term *Hypoplastic Left Heart Physiology* provided 1772 results with almost all having the term of "*Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome*" in the title.

#### **Classification**

According to the ISNPCHD, certain cardiac phenotypes are considered part of the HLHS spectrum. At the severe end of the spectrum is aortic and mitral atresia with a virtually nonexistent left ventricle. At the mild end of the spectrum is hypoplasia of the aortic and mitral valve with associated hypoplasia of the left ventricle, without intrinsic valvar stenosis or atresia. Although most of the patients are only amenable to a pathway of single ventricle palliation, a minority may be amenable to biventricular repair. Each individual patient has a specific anatomic cardiac phenotype, while the physiology may be variable over time.

Main anatomic cardiac phenotypes of HLHS:

- Aortic Atresia, Mitral Atresia
- Aortic Atresia, Mitral Stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis, Mitral Atresia
- Aortic Stenosis, Mitral Stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis with Left Ventricular (LV) Hypoplasia
- Hypoplastic Left Heart Complex (Hypoplasia of the Left Heart without Intrinsic Valve Stenosis)

The overwhelming majority of the various anatomic cardiac phenotypes that are part of HLHS together represent a special example of *univentricular atrioventricular connection* (see Chapter 52). Because of the special clinical and surgical importance of this group of malformations, this subject is discussed separately from other forms of Functional Single Ventricle.

#### **Historical Note**

The first description of aortic atresia was apparently by Canton in 1850.8 The initial description of the pathophysiology of HLHS (Aortic atresia, severe mitral stenosis and LV hypoplasia) was by Bardeleben in 1851, recently translated into English by Gehrmann and colleagues.9 Although Abbott had recognized aortic and mitral atresia,10 Brockman in 1950 emphasized that in about 50% of cases of mitral atresia, there was coexisting aortic atresia and severe underdevelopment of the left side of the heart.11 Maurice Lev in 1952 described the tendency of hypoplastic and obstructive lesions of the left heart and aorta to occur together and used the term "hypoplasia of the aortic tract complexes."1,12

In 1958 Noonan and Nadas introduced the term Hypoplastic Left Heart Syndrome to include anomalies with obstructions on the left side of the heart.13 Based on the analysis of 101 postmortem hearts, they identified 5 groups:

- Aortic valvar atresia
- Mitral atresia
- Mitral stenosis
- Atresia of the aortic arch
- Hypoplasia of the aortic arch

Their definition of HLHS was broad as the syndrome shared similarities in clinical presentation. These patients often presented with biventricular failure, mild cyanosis, a nonspecific heart murmur, and weak or absent pulses in the extremities. In their series, by far the largest group with 71 cases was isolated hypoplasia of the aortic arch. Although the clinical presentation was often similar, the precise anatomic diagnosis was variable. In 1984, Bharati and Lev used the terms "hypoplasia of the aortic tract complex" and "hypoplastic left heart syndrome" as synonyms.2

Patients with hypoplasia of the left ventricle with aortic and mitral valve hypoplasia without intrinsic valve stenosis have been inadequately described previously, with the use of multiple inconsistent terms.14-19

For this reason, in 1998, Tchervenkov and associates introduced the term "Hypoplastic Left Heart Complex" (HLHC) to describe patients with significant hypoplasia of the structures of the left heart and aorta without intrinsic stenosis or atresia of the aortic and mitral valves.20

HLHC was accepted and incorporated into the nomenclature by the International Congenital Heart Surgery Nomenclature and Database Project in 20004 and by the International Society for Nomenclature of Paediatric and Congenital Heart Disease (ISNPCHD)3. It has also been incorporated in the International Paediatric and Congenital Cardiac Code (IPCCC) and the ICD-11.5

In 1976, Roberts and colleagues further organized the knowledge about this subject by emphasizing that in the presence of a large ventricular septal defect (VSD), aortic atresia can coexist with normal development of the left ventricle and mitral valve.21

#### **Multistage Reconstructive Surgery Based on Single Ventricle Physiology (Popularized by Norwood)**

The history of attempted reconstructive procedures for HLHS dates back to 1970 when Cayler and colleagues described an anastomosis between right pulmonary artery (RPA) and ascending aorta with placement of bilateral PA bands.22 Other variations in neonatal reconstructive procedures designed to allow survival without the use of prostaglandins to maintain ductal patency were described by Doty and colleagues in 1977, Norwood and colleagues in 1980, Levitsky and colleagues in 1980, Behrendt and colleagues in 1981, and others.23-31

Even though some of these reports noted short-term successes, there is no documentation of long-term survival. In 1983, Norwood and colleagues described for the first time neonatal palliative surgery leading to a subsequent successful Fontan procedure.32 Following this report and subsequent reports that systematically documented long-term survival of patients with HLHS, it has become widely accepted that several principles of the first stage palliation described by Norwood are critical to achieving long-term survival.30 These are:

- Establishment of permanent unobstructed communication between the right ventricle and the systemic circulation and avoiding pulmonary valve (neoaortic valve) distortion
- Balanced pulmonary and systemic blood flow
- Ensuring an unobstructed interatrial communication

Many of the procedures described before implementation of the Norwood procedure failed to address one or more of these issues critical for long-term survival.

#### **Cardiac Replacement (Popularized by Bailey)**

Allograft heart transplantation for HLHS dates back to 1985, when Bailey performed the first successful cases as primary therapy in neonates.33 Since then, Bailey and colleagues and a number of other groups have considered transplantation as one option for treatment, along with reconstructive surgery.

#### **Biventricular Repair for Favorable Subsets of HLHS**

In 1998, Tchervenkov and associates reported their early experience with biventricular repair for a favorable subset of patients with HLHS and introduced the term HLHC to describe these patients.20

#### **Hybrid Procedure for HLHS**

The hybrid approach was first developed in 1993 in response to poor outcomes following the Norwood procedure.34 The hybrid procedure combines surgical placement of bilateral branch PA bands, placement of a stent in the ductus arteriosus, and catheterbased atrial septostomy, avoiding cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Initially, it was not widely embraced because of poor interim outcomes; however, more recently it has been used by some programs as an alternative to the Norwood procedure in high-risk patients, and variations of the hybrid procedure have been used as a bridge to transplantation.35,36

# **Morphogenesis and Morphology**

#### **Morphogenesis**

Currently, it is unclear whether the etiology of HLHS is similar in all cases. There are genetic factors involved, although these are multiple, complex, and poorly understood at the present time.37 Available evidence suggests that a number of primary morphologic etiologies may lead to the end result of HLHS. Primary morphologic abnormalities at the aortic valve level, mitral valve level, LV myocardial level, or atrial septal level (intact atrial septum) could all in theory lead ultimately to hypoplasia of the entire left side of the heart as gestation progresses.

#### **Morphology**

In HLHS, the heart is enlarged to about twice normal weight for age. Its shape is determined by the large right and small left heart chambers41 (Fig. 51.1A). Beyond this, morphologic details vary widely. Several morphologic subtypes of HLHS can be defined based on status of the left heart valves:

- Aortic and mitral atresia
- Aortic atresia with mitral stenosis
- Aortic stenosis with mitral atresia
- Aortic and mitral stenosis
- Aortic Stenosis with LV Hypoplasia
- Hypoplastic Left Heart Complex (Hypoplasia of the Left Heart without Intrinsic Valve Stenosis)

Of these, aortic stenosis with mitral atresia is the least common subtype, representing approximately 5% of cases; aortic and mitral atresia is the most common, representing approximately twothirds of cases. Within these subtypes, the status of the atrial septum, size of the LV cavity and muscle mass, ascending aorta and aortic arch, and ductus arteriosus are also important.

*Aortic Valve and Ascending Aorta.* In aortic atresia, the aortic valve is totally absent. Diminutive aortic sinuses of Valsalva are frequently present, giving origin to relatively normally positioned right and left coronary arteries that have a normal distribution pattern. The ascending aorta is narrow, sometimes as small as 1.5 mm in diameter. The portion of the aorta between the atretic valve and brachiocephalic artery serves only as a conduit for coronary blood flow (Fig. 51.2).

At and beyond the brachiocephalic artery, the aortic arch gradually widens and is joined beyond the origin of the left subclavian artery by a large patent ductus arteriosus (PDA). The ductus carries blood from the right ventricle into the descending aorta and retrograde to the brachiocephalic and coronary arteries. A localized aortic coarctation exists in approximately 80% of cases and is usually juxtaductal in location (see Chapter 40).42-44 Prevalence of coarctation is highest in patients with the most severe hypoplasia of the ascending aorta.45 In some cases, there may be only mild infolding of the aortic media on the wall opposite the ductal insertion site, or there may be no aortic coarctation whatsoever.

When a patent but hypoplastic aortic valve is present, there may be a variable but still reduced amount of forward flow across the aortic valve. The ascending aorta and arch tend to be larger than in aortic atresia, with the diameter of the ascending aorta ranging from 2 to 6 mm. Aortic coarctation is common.

*Left Ventricle and Mitral Valve.* The left ventricle is severely hypoplastic in 95% of cases of aortic atresia.46 In this setting, the ventricular septum is intact.47,48 The mitral valve is either atretic (about one-third of patients) or patent but severely hypoplastic (about two-thirds of patients)21 (see Fig. 51.1B and C). When the mitral valve is patent in association with aortic atresia, there may be LV–coronary connections (Fig. 51.3) similar to those present in the right ventricle in cases of pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum.49-51 It is postulated that these connections serve to decompress the LV chamber. Localized thickening of coronary arteries occurs adjacent to these connections, and there is also a variable degree of endocardial thickening (endocardial fibroelastosis).

Rarely, there is focal calcification and scarring limited to the ventricular subendocardium.50 The hypoplastic left ventricle shows myocardial fiber disarray qualitatively similar to that present in hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy and in the right ventricle in pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum.52

In approximately 5% of cases of aortic atresia, the LV cavity is near normal size in association with a large VSD. In such cases, there may be mitral valve atresia or a normal mitral valve. In cases of a normal mitral valve, the malformation is not considered part of the HLHS but a related malformation. Such patients can undergo two-ventricle repair.

When aortic stenosis rather than atresia is present, the left ventricle tends to be larger than the typically minute, slit-like left ventricle of aortic atresia. Its size may vary widely from extremely hypoplastic with hypertrophic muscle and severe endocardial fibroelastosis to a dilated, thin-walled, poorly functioning chamber. The mitral valve is almost always hypoplastic and may be atretic when severe aortic stenosis is present.

*Right Ventricle.* The right ventricle is enlarged, with uniform hypertrophy and a marked increase in cavity size (to approximately three times normal).41,53 Both tricuspid and pulmonary valves are larger than normal, and tricuspid regurgitation of variable degree is common.

*Pulmonary Arteries.* The pulmonary trunk is large and continues directly into the large PDA. The right and left branches arise relatively posteriorly and at right angles from the short pulmonary trunk. Uncommonly in some patients the branch PAs may be small, which is a bad prognostic factor for reconstructive surgery.

*Atria and Atrial Septum.* The left atrium is relatively small and thick walled, with its long axis directed transversely toward the right atrium. With increasing experience, it has become apparent that the attachment and position of the atrial septum will significantly influence the hemodynamic state of the neonate. If the atrial septum is thick, it will make balloon atrial septostomy generally unsatisfactory.2,54 An atrial communication is usually present; in the great majority of cases, this communication is a stretched patent foramen ovale. The septum primum is thickened and stretched so that it herniates into the right atrium and allows left-to-right shunting (see Fig. 51.1B).

In many cases of HLHS, most often those with mitral and aortic atresia, the septum primum is displaced leftward and is attached to the roof of the left atrium. Sufficient lateral displacement can give the false impression of a large atrial septal defect, yet there is severe pulmonary venous obstruction.3

There may be an aneurysm of the septum primum projecting to the right. The right atrium is larger than normal, with uniform hypertrophy of its walls. When the atrial septum is intact or severely restrictive in association with mitral or aortic atresia or both, there is pulmonary venous hypertension and a variable degree of decompression of pulmonary venous return through connections to the systemic venous system. Pulmonary venous hypertension usually begins in fetal life. This may have important implications for fetal lung development.

Dilated pulmonary lymphatic channels form, and these can have an important effect on postnatal lung physiology and surgical outcome.55,56

*Other Associated Cardiac Anomalies.* Associated anomalies are uncommon.57,58 Structural abnormalities of the tricuspid and pulmonary valves are rare. Bicuspid pulmonary valve has been described in 4% of specimens; cleft tricuspid valve, tricuspid valve dysplasia, and double orifice tricuspid valve have also been reported.26 Other unusual cardiac anomalies include intact atrial septum, total anomalous pulmonary venous connection (TAPVC), levoatrial cardinal vein, coronary sinus atresia, atretic pulmonary veins, complete atrioventricular septal defect, transposition of the great arteries, and interrupted aortic arch.59-63 Coronary artery abnormalities are rare except in patients with aortic atresia and mitral stenosis, in which coronary fistulas occur in approximately 50% of cases.49,51

*Associated Noncardiac Anomalies.* Other abnormalities unrelated to the cardiovascular system are found frequently with HLHS. Chromosomal abnormalities, genetic defects, and major extracardiac structural malformations, including central nervous system abnormalities, occur in 28% to 40% of patients.64-66

## **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

Presentation is in the newborn period, with mild cyanosis, respiratory distress, and tachycardia. If supportive measures are not undertaken, there can be rapid deterioration, heart failure, and death due to a combination of pulmonary overcirculation and systemic obstruction from ductal closure. Ductal closure is almost inevitable, but its timing varies from hours to weeks. This event is followed by rapid circulatory collapse.

#### **Natural History**

Various morphologic forms of HLHS constitute the fourth most common congenital cardiac defect.57 About 70% of cases are boys.21,70 Severe heart failure usually develops in the first week of life. Many neonates die within 1 to 2 weeks of birth; only 40% survive the neonatal period, and survival beyond 6 weeks of age is uncommon11,57,71,72 (Fig. 51.6). HLHS accounts for 25% of cardiac deaths during the first week of life and 15% of those in the first month of life.13,57

The ductus arteriosus typically begins to close shortly after birth. In some infants, ductal closure leads to restriction of systemic perfusion, metabolic acidosis, and circulatory collapse, and death. If the ductus continues to remain patent, a progressive increase in pulmonary circulation and a subsequent decrease in systemic circulation lead to pulmonary edema, coronary hypoperfusion, generalized systemic hypoperfusion, and ultimately death. Rarely, long-term survival will occur if the ductus remains patent and pulmonary vascular resistance (Rp) fails to fall in the perinatal and neonatal period.

# **Technique of Operation**

There are two main surgical options for the majority of the cases with HLHS: multistage reconstructive surgery and cardiac transplantation*.* A small minority of patients may be amenable to biventricular repair: those with critical aortic stenosis without mitral stenosis and a left ventricle of adequate size and patients with the HLHC. Reconstructive surgery includes the Norwood procedure and its variants and the hybrid procedure. The hybrid procedure consisting of placement of bilateral PA bands and either maintaining on prostaglandins or placement of a PDA stent has gained increased application in patients with high-risk features.74,75

The overall goal of reconstructive surgery is similar to that for any patient with single-ventricle physiology (see Chapter 52) that is, establishing in the neonatal period an effective mixed circulation in which pulmonary and systemic blood flow are well balanced, followed by one or more operations performed later in infancy or early childhood after Rp has dropped to normal postnatal levels. The purpose of subsequent operations performed outside the neonatal period is to move away from the inefficiency of the completely mixed circulatory state. It should be emphasized that all repairs of HLHS are viewed as palliative.

The exact form of repair may vary from patient to patient based on the individual's physiologic status. Given the inherently limited reserve of the single right ventricle, it is generally agreed that a completely mixed circulatory state, even one that provides ideal balance between pulmonary and systemic blood flow, is not an acceptable definitive state for HLHS. The optimal completed repair includes the completed Fontan procedure or the Fontan procedure with fenestration. Intermediate repair includes superior cavopulmonary anastomosis and superior cavopulmonary anastomosis with additional limited systemic to pulmonary blood flow.

The principles of initial surgical management are generally agreed upon. Some variation of the Norwood procedure is considered optimal initial therapy. However, for high-risk patients initial staging with placement of bilateral PA bands or intraluminal flow restrictors and placement of a ductal stent or maintenance of the PDA with prostaglandins until delayed Norwood has gained favor.75 These high-risk patients are: very small neonates or neonates with significant concomitant risk factors (Weight , 2.5 kg, Gestational age , 34 weeks, restrictive or intact atrial septum, \$ moderate TR, \$ moderate systolic dysfunction, TAPVC, coronary sinusoids, interventricular hemorrhage grade .3, multisystem organ dysfunction, shock, genetic or significant extracardiac abnormalities).

Either way, the goal of initial palliation is to provide (1) a completely unobstructed systemic arterial pathway from the right ventricle to all organs, (2) a restrictive connection between the systemic and pulmonary circulations such that pulmonary and systemic blood flow are adequately balanced, and (3) unobstructed flow of pulmonary venous return across the atrial septum to the right atrium.

#### **Preoperative Management**

Perinatal preoperative management is critical to successful outcome. This may include prenatal transport of the mother and fetus to a cardiac center following fetal diagnosis.60,76 Circulatory collapse is usually the result of closure of the ductus arteriosus in the setting of undiagnosed HLHS. This may occur when the infant is still in hospital following birth or after discharge home. If prenatal diagnosis is made and mother and fetus are transferred to an appropriate facility where the infant will undergo surgery, circulatory collapse is all but eliminated. Late diagnosis of HLHS with ductal closure can result in an extremely sick baby who might be best managed with bilateral PA bands and prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) therapy.75

After diagnosis, the infant is resuscitated, and PGE1 therapy is initiated.31 Depending on details of the physiologic status of the infant and stability of the infant at initial diagnosis, subsequent preoperative management may vary from essentially no further intervention on the one hand to maximal intervention on the other. The typical patient, however, shows signs of pulmonary overcirculation, and preoperative management is aimed at reversing or

These maneuvers are aimed at achieving a balance of pulmonary and systemic blood flow and maintaining unobstructed and adequate systemic perfusion. Because flow into the pulmonary circuit is unobstructed, Rp at the microvascular level will determine pulmonary blood flow. Any maneuver that causes dilation of the pulmonary microvasculature will result in excessive pulmonary blood flow. Specifically, avoiding supplemental inspired oxygen is critical to the overall strategy; 21% oxygen or even lower Fio2 helps maintain tone in the pulmonary microvasculature. If this maneuver is not adequate, controlled ventilation through an endotracheal tube achieves moderate elevation of Paco2, causing acidosis, which further constricts pulmonary microvasculature. PGEl maintains ductal patency, ensuring unobstructed blood flow to the systemic circulation.

Inotropic agents can be used to enhance cardiac output (CO) in the setting of moderate pulmonary overcirculation, but this strategy must be undertaken cautiously because these agents also affect systemic and pulmonary vascular resistances and may unpredictably alter . Epinephrine and high-dose dopamine, which profoundly increase systemic vascular resistance, should be avoided. Low- to moderate-dose dopamine and dobutamine should be considered the first-line inotropic agents when supplemental cardiac output is considered necessary.

All these maneuvers are used to create optimal preoperative cardiopulmonary status. Assessing cardiopulmonary status is somewhat indirect. Currently, and cannot be easily directly measured in the cardiac intensive care unit (ICU). Indirect measures of adequate systemic output include normal peripheral perfusion, adequate urine output, and absence of metabolic acidosis. Evidence of a reasonable balance of and includes a Pao2 of about 40 mmHg (Torr) and a systemic diastolic blood pressure greater than 30 mmHg. Even these ideal values do not guarantee that the expected blood flow values in fact do exist. For example, Pao2 can be influenced by other factors: hemoglobin level, metabolic state, temperature, and presence of sepsis to name a few. Furthermore, the inevitable reduction in Rp that occurs over time commonly thwarts all efforts to maintain systemic output and balanced and . If this occurs, operation should be scheduled immediately.

For the typical neonate diagnosed early after birth and in whom circulatory collapse has not occurred, the ideal time for surgical intervention is about age 2 to 5 days. In this window of time, the infant completes the profound physiologic changes from fetal life to independent life, yet consequences of a continuously increasing pulmonary blood flow have not yet taken their toll. If circulatory collapse does occur and end-organ damage results, a longer time before operation is often necessary to allow end-organ recovery.

Although not always advisable, ideally, normal function of renal, hepatic, neurologic, gastrointestinal, and cardiopulmonary systems should be documented following resuscitation prior to proceeding with operation. It is not uncommon for organ systems to recover fairly rapidly but then plateau short of complete recovery. Further delay of operation at that point is usually detrimental.

Although mild obstruction of flow across the atrial septum is typical at the time of Doppler color flow interrogation during diagnostic echocardiography, severe obstruction at the atrial septum may occur, resulting in a clinical presentation similar to that found with obstructive TAPVC (see Chapter 30), with deep cyanosis, pulmonary edema, and eventual hemodynamic instability. This presentation evolves rapidly immediately after birth and must be addressed within hours. Such patients are best managed with percutaneous interventional techniques to create an adequate atrial septal opening, followed by several days of stabilization before proceeding with operation. Management as described earlier continues during transport to the operating room and during surgery until CPB is instituted. The operation can be performed using continuous CPB by way of antegrade cerebral perfusion or using hypothermic circulatory arrest, according to choice of the operating surgeon. Both techniques are described in text that follows.

Neurologic development following operations for HLHS is below normal. Although impaired neurologic development is multifactorial, there is little question that circulatory arrest is contributory. Antegrade cerebral perfusion provides the advantage of continuous blood flow and oxygenation to the brain; however, it is possible that this technique also introduces new risks. It is unlikely that techniques of reconstruction that avoid circulatory arrest will result in dramatic changes in short-term survival, because factors related to hypothermia, CPB itself, and myocardial ischemia are not avoided. Long-term benefits related to neurologic development may exist but are yet to be proven.

#### **Norwood Procedure Using Continuous Selective Cerebral Perfusion**

There are two major approaches to the conduct of the Norwood operation, one using continuous antegrade cerebral perfusion and one using deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. Excellent results have been achieved using both approaches and a consensus is emerging that the choice of technique is one that is best left to experience and preference of the operating surgeon and team, although one should be preferentially employed at a given institution so that maximal experience with a given technique is achieved.77 Within the category of selective cerebral perfusion, some centers advocate for beating heart arch reconstruction and others for continuous antegrade cerebral perfusion.77 Several techniques for accomplishing the Norwood procedure using continuous perfusion have been described.78,79 Fig. 51.8 describes in detail the various surgical steps with that technique.80,81

After median sternotomy, the thymus is subtotally removed and the anterior pericardium opened widely. Aortic arch vessels are dissected well above the brachiocephalic vein. The small aorta is separated from the pulmonary trunk and right PA, and the ductus arteriosus, aortic arch, and proximal descending thoracic aorta are dissected and mobilized.

The brachiocephalic artery is isolated, and a 3- or 3.5-mm polytetrafluoroethylene (Gore-Tex) graft is cut on a bevel and sewn to the brachiocephalic artery using fine polypropylene suture. Hemostasis is obtained at this site, after which heparin is administered. The graft is cannulated with an 8F arterial cannula. A purse-string suture is placed in the right atrial appendage, and the right atrium is cannulated with an appropriately sized metal tipped angled venous cannula. CPB is instituted, and the patient is cooled to 18°C. (Fig. 51.8A). The ductus arteriosus is isolated with a lubricated silk tie and tourniquet, and the tourniquet is cinched down. The aortic arch, bilateral branch PAs, and the distal aorta are mobilized. The recurrent laryngeal nerve is retracted with a coated malleable retractor, and three full levels of intercostals are identified and divided with electrocautery taking care to avoid injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve. Once the arch is completely dissected and mobilized, the main PA is transected 1 to 2 mm proximal to the bifurcation of the right and left PA (Fig. 51.8B and C). The atrial septum is resected completely under direct vision. The right atrial cannula is replaced, and selective cerebral perfusion is instituted at 18°C.

#### **Norwood Procedure Using Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest**

The majority of patients (512) in the Single Ventricle Reconstruction (SVR) trial underwent a period of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA), an established method for repair.77 Exposure is excellent, but time is more critical, and circulatory arrest times in excess of 40 minutes are associated with a greater risk of major complications as was identified in the Boston Circulatory Arrest Trial.77 Also, outcomes with selective cerebral perfusion have not demonstrated conclusive superiority over DHCA.77 The heart is exposed by median sternotomy, removal of most of the thymus gland, and opening of the pericardium. If the patient is unstable because of increased pulmonary blood flow, the right PA can be exposed immediately and clamped to reduce overall pulmonary blood flow and maintain systemic circulation until CPB is established.

The patient is prepared for CPB by placing a purse-string suture on the pulmonary trunk just distal to the pulmonary valve. A second purse-string suture is placed around the tip of the right atrial appendage. At that point, if the patient is physiologically stable, the pulmonary trunk is separated from the ascending aorta using either scissors or electrocautery. The ductus arteriosus, aortic arch, and arch vessels are then mobilized using scissors or electrocautery all the way to the first set of intercostal vessels on the descending aorta. Temporary snares are placed around all brachiocephalic arteries.

If the patient becomes physiologically unstable, CPB can be initiated at any time and the great vessel dissection performed with its support. CPB is established using an arterial cannula in the pulmonary trunk and a single venous cannula in the right atrial appendage (Fig. 51.9A). At initiation of CPB, the branch PAs are temporarily occluded with clamps or snares to eliminate pulmonary blood flow.

After initiating CPB, while the pulmonary trunk and aorta are still distended with blood, 7-0 monofilament sutures are placed on adjacent portions of the pulmonary trunk and ascending aorta to mark the point of eventual pulmonary trunk-to-aorta anastomosis. Positions of these marking sutures are chosen with great care because they will determine the correct orientation of, and incisions in, the aorta and pulmonary trunk necessary for creating a functional anastomosis; alignment of this anastomosis is critical for unobstructed coronary blood flow in aortic atresia. The first marking suture is placed in the adventitia of the pulmonary trunk 1 to 2 mm above the sinutubular junction and circumferentially exactly where the small ascending aorta lies against it. The second suture on the aortic adventitia is placed so that its position coincides exactly with the pulmonary trunk suture (see Fig. 51.9A).

When the nasopharyngeal or tympanic membrane temperature reaches 16°C to 18°C after cooling with CPB for an appropriate period (see Section IV of Chapter 2), the snares around the brachiocephalic vessels are tightened and circulatory arrest established. Snares around the left and right PA are removed, and cannulae are removed from the pulmonary trunk and right atrial appendage after draining as much blood volume from the patient into the pump-oxygenator as possible.

Management of myocardial protection is variable. Some experienced centers use no specific cardioplegia and rely on profound hypothermia as the only form of myocardial management. Other experienced institutions use cardioplegia, which can be supplied in several ways. Cannulation of the ascending aorta can be achieved with an appropriately small needle and cardioplegia delivered directly into the aortic root. Alternatively, the cardioplegia system can be connected to the arterial cannula in the pulmonary trunk, and cardioplegia delivered into this cannula after circulatory arrest has been established while the brachiocephalic vessel snares and PA branch snares are still in place. The only additional maneuver before proceeding with cardioplegia delivery using this method is to clamp the descending aorta distal to the ductal insertion site. The cardioplegic solution is delivered through the arterial cannula into the pulmonary trunk through the ductus and retrograde around the arch to the coronary arteries. All other peripheral runoff through this circuit must be reliably eliminated.

After myocardial protection has been addressed and circulatory arrest established, the ductus arteriosus is ligated distal to the origin of the left PA. A small atriotomy is made, and through it the entire septum primum is removed to create an unrestrictive interatrial communication. To avoid conduction problems, care is taken not to extend the resection beyond the septum primum. The atriotomy is closed (Fig. 51.9B).

The pulmonary trunk is divided transversely as proximally as possible without risking damage to the pulmonary valve, leaving the previously placed marking suture proximal to the transection. As the transection is made, particular care is taken to avoid the orifice of the right PA. The distal end of the divided pulmonary trunk is then closed with a patch (typically autologous pericardium or PA allograft) using continuous 7-0 polypropylene. Alternatively, the distal pulmonary trunk may be closed primarily in transverse fashion (Fig. 51.9*C*). Direct closure has the advantages of time efficiency and less bulk and in experienced hands has shown no greater tendency to result in pulmonary trunk stenosis than the patch technique.

The ductus is transected just beyond the previously placed ligature. Redundant ductal tissue is cut away from the distal aorta, leaving a small cuff of ductal tissue at the level of the aortic isthmus. A 5- to 10-mm incision is made from the ductal orifice into the descending aorta to the level of the first set of intercostal vessels (see Fig. 51.9C). A proximal incision is made beginning at the ductal orifice and moving retrograde toward the aortic valve. This incision proceeds along the undersurface of the aortic arch and extends down the hypoplastic ascending aorta to within several millimeters of the atretic or hypoplastic aortic valve, terminating at the same level as the transected pulmonary trunk at the point of the previously placed marking suture (see Fig. 51.9C).

The aorta is then augmented throughout its length from the level of the aortic valve around the arch, to the first set of intercostal vessels, using a patch of pulmonary or aortic allograft tissue (Fig. 51.9D). The patch is tailored to provide adequate but not excessive widening of the aorta. Suturing begins at the distal end of the incision beyond the isthmus on the upper descending aorta and progresses retrograde until the proximity of the brachiocephalic artery is reached. The posterior suture line is developed first, using a running technique and 6-0 or 7-0 nonabsorbable monofilament suture, followed by the anterior suture line.

The last step in the operation is creating a source of pulmonary blood flow. At the choice of the operating surgeon, this can be achieved using either a restrictive systemic arterial–to–pulmonary arterial shunt or a restrictive right ventricle–to–pulmonary arterial conduit. If a shunt is chosen, typically an interposition graft is sewn into place from the junction of the brachiocephalic and right subclavian arteries on the systemic side to the proximal portion of the right PA (see Fig. 51.9F). Both anastomoses are performed using end graft–to–side artery connections with running 7-0 monofilament or polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) suture. Variation in positioning of the PTFE shunt must be considered, based on individual patient characteristics, to achieve an appropriate balance of pulmonary and systemic blood flow.

In patients weighing less than 3 kg and in those demonstrating very low Rp preoperatively, it may be necessary to perform the systemic pulmonary arterial shunt anastomosis at a more distal site on the right subclavian artery. In most cases, a 3.5-mm diameter PTFE tube graft is used for the shunt procedure; a larger diameter is rarely necessary. Often in patients weighing less than 3 kg and most frequently in patients weighing less than 2.5 kg, a 3.0-mm diameter graft should be considered, although risk of shunt thrombosis may increase with a smaller-diameter shunt. If circulatory arrest is prolonged, or by surgeon preference, the shunt may be placed after reestablishing flow on CPB.

If a RV-PA conduit is chosen, a PTFE graft of appropriate diameter is selected. Alternatively, a composite graft consisting of a proximally positioned PTFE tube and a distally positioned small (6- to 7-mm diameter) allograft pulmonary or aortic valve can be used81 (Fig. 51.9G and H). Regardless of whether a simple conduit or composite conduit is chosen, the diameter of the PTFE tube determines the resistance to flow into the PAs. Typically, a 4-mm diameter graft is used for patients weighing less than 3 kg, a 5-mm diameter graft for those between 3 and 4 kg, and a 6-mm diameter graft for those greater than 4 kg.

An incision is made in the infundibular portion of the right ventricle, just below the pulmonary valve. A 5- to 6-mm diameter circular full-thickness resection of infundibular muscle is then made. It is extremely important that the caliber of the resulting hole in the infundibulum is maintained transmurally to prevent premature stenosis at this level postoperatively. Using a running 7-0 monofilament suture, the graft is first sewn end to side to the pulmonary trunk, either to the PA directly, adjacent to the suture line that previously closed the distal pulmonary trunk, or to the center of the patch that was used to close the distal pulmonary trunk. The graft is then positioned to the left of the reconstructed aorta and tailored in length to reach the infundibulotomy. The proximal end of the graft is carefully beveled to the appropriate angle to ensure a smooth course around the large, reconstructed aorta.

The anastomosis is performed using a running 6-0 monofilament suture. The right atrial and systemic arterial cannulae are reinserted, CPB is reestablished, and rewarming begun. If a systemic-to-pulmonary shunt has been placed, it is occluded with a vascular clamp during the rewarming phase of CPB. When the patient's tympanic membrane or nasopharyngeal temperature reaches 25°C to 30°C, perfusate ionized calcium concentration is measured and calcium chloride added to bring the ionized calcium concentration to a normal level. Separation from CPB is accomplished and decannulation achieved. Details of post-CPB management follow.

#### **Post–Cardiopulmonary Bypass Management**

Whether the operation is performed using continuous perfusion or circulatory arrest, post-CPB management is generally the same. Before the patient is separated from CPB, inotropic support is initiated with epinephrine at 0.05 mg/kg/hr with ideal dosing less than 0.1 mg/kg/min; milrinone is started at 0.5 mg/kg/min. We consider a milrinone load of 50 mg/kg for SVRi . 12 prior to separation from CPB. We avoid norepinephrine when possible. We pay particular attention to complete reexpansion of both lungs. Endotracheal suctioning by the anesthesiologist is routine. If a systemic-to-pulmonary shunt was used, after complete rewarming has been achieved, approximately 5 minutes before discontinuing CPB, the clamp on the shunt is removed.

Careful attention is given to the mean arterial pressure (MAP) on CPB at this point; typically, a decrease of 10 to 15 mmHg should be expected, indicating adequate runoff into the pulmonary vascular bed. If this decrease is not observed, the cause must be identified. The systemic-to-pulmonary trunk shunt should be immediately assessed for obstruction due to a technical problem. If a right ventricle-to-pulmonary arterial conduit was used, diastolic blood pressure is not affected, but care must be taken to ensure adequate ventilation of the lungs before removal of the clip on the conduit. Two common atrial lines and epicardial atrial and ventricular pacing leads are placed. Typical inotropic support includes epinephrine at 0.05 mg/kg/min and milrinone at 0.5 mg/kg/min. Target MAP is . 45 mmHg, target SpO2 is 75-85, and target central venous pressure (CVP) is 8 to 12. We categorize patients into one of physiologic quadrants: High SVR, High CO, Low SVR, Low CO based on MAP (./, 48) and rSO2R (./, 70).82 Physiologic targets to come off CPB include an MAP of 45-50, a Po2 of 35 to 45, a PCO2 of 35-45, a SpO2 75-85%, a CVP of 8 to 12, and a Hct of 40 to 45.

After rewarming has been completed, CPB is discontinued, and the patient undergoes modified ultrafiltration (MUF).83 TEE is routinely performed to visualize intracardiac air, function, AV valve function, and neoaortic valve function. The proximal right ventricle to PA conduit, arch, and pulmonary venous return are also assessed. Pressures are directly measured proximally and distally with the common atrial line advanced to the right ventricle and a femoral or umbilical artery catheter in place allowing for a direct determination and demonstration of the absence of any gradient across the arch. Significant residual lesions are immediately addressed with a return to CPB and revision. After TEE and MUF, heparin is reversed with protamine and hemostasis is obtained. Once bleeding is controlled, a sterile epicardial echocardiogram is performed with the surgeon driving the probe and the cardiologist interpreting the images to better assess the arch, the proximal and distal anastomosis, and branch PAs. Again, any residual lesion identified is addressed. Once anatomic interrogation is complete, and bleeding is controlled, the aortic and venous cannulae are removed. Atrial and ventricular temporary epicardial pacing wires are placed. Chest drainage tubes are placed appropriately for neonates undergoing CPB.

Postoperative care begins immediately. A quadrant analysis as described by Hoffman and colleagues is useful to characterize the hemodynamic state of the patient with regards to low or high CO)and low or high SVR82 (Fig. 51.10).

If the patient is in the low CO or low SVR quadrants, care focuses on optimizing preload using small volume boluses to assess CVP responsiveness and determine ideal filling pressure to optimize CO. Packed red blood cells are indicated for volume if the hematocrit is less than about 40. Atrial pacing is initiated if sinus rhythm is present with relative bradycardia. Low-dose epinephrine and milrinone (dose . 0.25 mg/kg/min) are utilized to increase contractility and decrease afterload. Afterload is augmented, if necessary, with norepinephrine. Calculation of : can facilitate optimization of respiratory parameters to augment systemic perfusion. If the patient is in the high SVR or high CO quadrants, volume replacement is reduced to prevent fluid overload. If ventricular function is good, epinephrine and norepinephrine are weaned to reduce afterload and prevent unnecessary overstimulation of the myocardium. If SVR remains high after weaning vasoconstrictors, milrinone can be added/increased to a maximum dose of 0.75 mg/kg/min.

#### **Technical Modifications of the Norwood Procedure**

A number of modifications of the standard ascending aorta and arch reconstruction described in the preceding text have been developed. However, the physiologic principles of providing unobstructed ventricular-to–systemic arterial output and appropriately balanced pulmonary and systemic blood flow, remain the same.

A reproducible method of arch reconstruction that minimizes residual arch obstruction was described by the Children's Wisconsin group. A formal coarctectomy with interdigitated posterior and anterior reconstruction is applicable in all cases except for those in which the aorta is reasonably sized in that area, and there is complete absence of a posterior shelf.84 A hemiarterial graft is used to reconstruct the arch cut so that the curve of the graft matches the idealized curve of the reconstructed arch. Tailoring the patch with the "cut as we go" technique provides an optimally tailored arch.85

For arch and neoaortic reconstruction, 7-0 or 8-0 polypropylene sutures are commonly used. Reinforcing the anterior and posterior anastomotic angles is advisable, as these areas are frequent sites of postoperative bleeding. When the ascending aorta measures 1.5 mm or less, a posterior medial cutback to the left of the posterior commissure of the pulmonary or neoaortic valve is recommended to optimize the positioning of the neoaortic anastomosis and enhance native coronary flow. In cases where the ascending aorta is greater than 2 mm, reimplantation of the coronary arteries just above the commissures is typically performed. This approach ensures a satisfactory neoaortic anastomosis while preventing excessive stretching of the neopulmonary valve and root, thereby reducing the risk of neoaortic insufficiency. The "Dunk" technique is also commonly applied for the proximal RV-PA conduit without the use of patch material.85 Other groups have reported success using the periscope technique.86

Several groups have introduced techniques of reconstructing the ventricular-to-systemic arterial outflow without use of patch material.87,88 Much experience has been obtained, and perioperative outcome and some midterm outcome data are available using these alternative techniques. Theoretical advantages include avoiding foreign patch material and the possibility of a modest reduction in circulatory arrest time. Disadvantages include potential problems with suture line tension, LPA and left bronchus compression, and increased resistance to flow to the coronary system.

Currently, there is no clear evidence that these alternative techniques are better or worse than the more standard arch reconstruction technique. Other modifications in the surgical treatment of neonates with HLHS have been reported.89,90

The RV-PA conduit, if used, can be placed to the right side of the reconstructed ascending aorta rather than to the left side as described. This is believed by some to have advantages particularly at the time of dissection for bidirectional Glenn.91

Other groups prefer leaving the right ventricle-to-pulmonary artery conduit to the left. The added length with the right sided RV-PA conduit results in increased resistance to pulmonary blood flow and must be considered in selection of conduit size. Several groups have explored the use of a valved conduit by interposing a bovine saphenous vein valve at the distal end of the conduit for a length of about 1 cm. 92

#### **Hybrid Procedure**

Hybrid stage I procedure consists of surgical bilateral pulmonary artery banding (PAB) and ductal stenting with or without manipulation of atrial septum (balloon atrial septostomy or stent implantation). Pulmonary blood flow is reduced, and systemic flow is maintained via an open duct. Hemodynamics are optimized by balancing the pulmonary and systemic circulations  $(\dot{Q}p/\dot{Q}s)$  without CPB in the neonatal period.

Ductal stenting for HLHS as a bridge to transplantation was first described by Ruiz and colleagues in 1993. Gibbs and colleagues published stenting of the arterial duct combined with banding of PAs and septectomy or septostomy as palliation for HLHS in the same year. Due to the poor outcomes of hybrid palliation at that time, the authors no longer recommended the use of this technique. The world first successful hybrid procedure combined with comprehensive stage II palliation was performed in Giessen (Germany) by Dietmar Schranz and Hakan Akintürk in 1998. Similar success was reported by Galantowicz and Cheatham (US).

The hybrid procedure was initially preferred to the Norwood procedure as a primary procedure in high-risk patients (i.e.,

prematurity, low birth weight, extreme shock, genetic or non-cardiac comorbid diseases, depressed RV function, severe tricuspid regurgitation, intact or highly restrictive atrial septum, and very small ascending aorta). 98-100 With increasing experience, the hybrid procedure can be applied successfully as a routine strategy in all variants of HLHS, based on outcomes reported by the Giessen and Columbus groups. 101,102

The hybrid procedure can be performed with two different techniques. In the first, the procedure is performed in a hybrid operating room, mainly involving a catheterization laboratory and an operating room with CPB support if needed. A median sternotomy is made, and the thymus is excised subtotally to provide exposure. The pericardium is opened, and the branch PAs are exposed and prepared. A pulmonary band is first placed on the RPA, followed by the LPA. For patients weighing 3 kg or more, a 3.5-mm diameter PTFE prosthesis is selected, and for patients weighing less than 3 kg, a 3.0 mm PTFE prosthesis is chosen. Two pieces of PTFE tubes are cut approximately 2 to 3 mm wide. Appropriate choice and width of the prosthesis play a crucial role for the efficiency of the banding and for avoiding possible injury of the PAs. Both ends of the bands are fixed to the adventitial layer of the PAs with 6-0 polypropylene sutures. Appropriate fixation of the bands on the PA adventitia avoids peripheral migration. The RPA band must be positioned between the small/diminutive ascending aorta and the takeoff of branch arteries. The LPA band must be placed immediately after its takeoff from the main PA. The bands are placed before the ductal stent implantation to avoid migration of the stent during manipulation of the PA branches (Fig. 51.11A). #### **Bidirectional Superior Cavopulmonary Anastomosis (and Hemi-Fontan Procedure)**

These procedures are performed as a second stage following the Norwood procedure. Bidirectional superior cavopulmonary anastomosis and the hemi-Fontan procedure are described under "Technique of Operation" in Chapter 52. To decrease the interstage mortality the second-stage procedure is typically performed between age 3 and 6 months. If a patient is hypoxic, early bidirectional Glenn (BDG) can be performed safely down to even 2 months of age.110

Preoperative considerations include the size of the PAs determined by catheterization and CT, the presence and degree of tricuspid or common AV valve insufficiency, the function of the right ventricle and RV end-diastolic pressure as well as measurement of pulmonary vascular resistance.

The conduct of the bidirectional Glenn procedure following the Norwood operation proceeds as follows. A median sternotomy is performed, with careful dissection to avoid injury to the systemic right ventricle. Both pleural spaces are entered, and any vascular adhesions are dissected. If present, the bilateral mammary arteries may be clipped to reduce collateral blood flow. The reconstructed aorta is exposed, and the superior vena cava (SVC) is carefully isolated using sharp dissection to prevent injury to the phrenic nerve. The azygos vein is identified, ligated between fine silk ties, and divided.

Heparin is administered, and purse-string sutures are placed in the distal reconstructed aorta, the SVC, and the right atrial appendage. The patient is cannulated, CPB is initiated, and the patient is cooled to 32°C. The Sano or modified Blalock-Taussig-Thomas (MBTT) shunt is then isolated, ligated, and divided. The RV-PA conduit is dissected off the ventricular free wall and clipped at the level of the right ventricle. The distal RV-PA conduit is mobilized to the branch PAs.

If intracardiac work is necessary, the heart is fibrillated or crossclamped, and cardioplegia is administered. The right atrium is opened, and any residual atrial septum is resected. To assess the tricuspid valve, the right ventricle is insufflated using a salinefilled bulb syringe. For anatomical tricuspid valves, posterior leaflet obliteration combined with a De Vega anuloplasty from the anterior to the septal leaflet using pledgeted 5-0 Prolene is a common and effective repair method.111 Other commonly used techniques include edge-to-edge repair, often between the anterior and septal leaflets, in combination with De Vega anuloplasty.111

If there is a common atrioventricular valve (AVV), a Gore-Tex or Silastic bridge, or a pledgeted bridging Prolene U-stitch, is typically placed from the center of the superior bridging leaflet anulus to the center of the inferior bridging leaflet anulus. This stabilizes the anulus and tightens the coaptation zone between the superior and inferior bridging leaflets.112-114 An appropriately sized Hegar dilator is used to ensure that the reconstruction is not stenotic, with the size benchmarked at 1 to 2 mm below a z-score of 0. A vent is placed through a purse string in the right atrium across the repaired valve, and the right atrium is closed.

The aortic root is vented, and either the cross-clamp is removed or fibrillation is stopped. The SVC is clamped at its junction with the right atrium, and EKG p-wave monitoring is performed to ensure that the clamping does not interfere with sinoatrial node function. The SVC is then transected off the right atrium, and an anterior stay suture is placed to maintain orientation. The bilateral branch PAs are fully mobilized and isolated using vessel loops or Yasargil clamps. A homograft branch patch is selected and thawed.

The distal portion of the Sano shunt is completely resected, and incisions are made in both the left and right PAs. The posterior wall of the bidirectional Glenn anastomosis is constructed using 7-0 or 8-0 Prolene with very fine bites. Rewarming is initiated, and vigorous spontaneous sinus rhythm typically returns. An anterior incision is made in the SVC, extending toward the cannulation site. If there is concern about narrowing at the cannulation site, the cannula can be removed, and the incision extended while controlling drainage from the SVC with a pump sucker.

A generous anterior triangular patch with rounded corners is tailored as the repair proceeds. A common right atrial line is placed, an echocardiogram is obtained, and the patient is weaned from CPB. Full MUF is performed.

The **hemi-Fontan procedure** is described under "Technique of Operation" in Chapter 52. Because there remains substantial risk of mortality in the interval period between hospital discharge following a successful first-stage reconstruction for HLHS and creation of the bidirectional superior cavopulmonary anastomosis,115 the second-stage procedure should be performed relatively soon, typically between age 3 and 6 months. Following creation of the bidirectional superior cavopulmonary anastomosis, hemodynamic efficiency is markedly improved and mortality risk substantially reduced.115 Data suggest that somatic growth does not occur in patients with first-stage palliation after age 4 months.116

Early construction of the bidirectional superior cavopulmonary shunt has two other advantages:

- 1. It allows use of a relatively small-diameter systemic-to-pulmonary shunt or RV-to-pulmonary conduit at the time of firststage reconstruction. This creates an ideal ratio during the first few months following birth and does not require the initial shunt or conduit to have a life expectancy of more than 6 months. As a result, the initial shunt or conduit does not have to be "oversized" in anticipation of the growing infant requiring increased pulmonary blood flow in later infancy. This promotes early hemodynamic stability.
- 2. It reduces duration of inefficient mixed circulation that is present with a shunt or conduit. This allows maximal preservation of RV function by reducing RV volume work, mortality risk, and chance of distortion of the PAs caused by tethering from the PTFE graft.

#### **Comprehensive Second-Stage Procedure Following Hybrid Procedure**

At least 4 months after hybrid stage I procedure and patient weight .5 kg, the comprehensive stage II operation is performed. The operation mainly involves an aortic arch reconstruction and a bidirectional cavopulmonary anastomosis. The operation is performed using antegrade selective cerebral perfusion combined with selective lower body perfusion without the need of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. The operation can be also performed with additional selective coronary perfusion on a beating heart in selective patients, especially those with an ascending aorta over 4 mm in size. For the aortic cannulation, the brachiocephalic trunk is cannulated with an 8F arterial cannula via a 3.5-mm PTFE prosthesis.

First, the 3.5-mm PTFE prosthesis is anastomosed end-to-side with 7-0 Prolene sutures on the brachiocephalic trunk. Then 8F arterial cannula is inserted and attached to the prosthesis. If the patient has aortic arch hypoplasia or aortic coarctation, the ductus should also be cannulated with a second 8F cannula to provide optimal lower body perfusion. Despite the presence of a ductal stent, the ductus can be easily cannulated. Venous cannulation is initiated through the distal superior vena cava and inferior vena cava. (Fig. 51.12A, B) Venting of the right atrium is also necessary. Under CPB, the patent ductus is ligated at the pulmonary side with 4-0 Prolene sutures. Then, the right atrium is opened and atrial septectomy is performed. If the patient has a previous stent in the atrial septum, this stent must be explanted totally. Thereafter, debanding is performed for each pulmonary artery, and subsequently the pulmonary trunk is transected distal to the origin of the right and left PA. The rest of the ductal stent is totally resected, which still remains in the PA after ductus ligation. Alternatively, the ductal stent can be totally excised from distal pulmonary artery, and this region can be reconstructed with a patch material. First LPA and then RPA are dilated with a 6-7 mm balloon catheter. (Fig. 51.12C) The LPA can be enlarged additionally with autologous pericardium at the site of PA banding. The proximal PA is reconstructed directly with continuous 6-0 PDS suture. The distal superior vena cava is transected from the right atrium, and the right atrium is closed directly with 6-0 PDS suture. The RPA is opened and the bidirectional cavopulmonary anastomosis is performed with continuous 7-0 PDS suture without patch material. The operation is performed on the beating heart up to the point of aortic arch reconstruction. (Fig. 51.12D)

The aortic arch reconstruction is performed using selective antegrade cerebral perfusion and retrograde lower body perfusion at 28°C. Lower body perfusion during arch reconstruction employs an additional line connected to the cardiopulmonary circuit via the femoral artery. For this purpose, a 3F, 4-cm arterial leader catheter is inserted percutaneously preoperatively. During aortic arch reconstruction, 15 mL/kg/min flow can be achieved with the femoral arterial cannula.

If the ascending aorta is of adequate size (over 4 mm), the operation can also be performed on the beating heart with moderate hypothermia. For this technique, a cardioplegia needle is placed in the aortic root. A cardioplegia line is connected to the cardiopulmonary circuit, and selective coronary perfusion is maintained through this line during aortic arch reconstruction. If the ascending aorta is small, the operation is performed under cardioplegic arrest. Cerebral and somatic perfusions are monitored via standard two-side simultaneous cerebral and somatic (renal) near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) monitoring. During selective antegrade cerebral perfusion, a flow of 25 to 40 mL/kg/ min is maintained. Intraoperative management includes the following parameters: MAP in the right radial artery \$ 40 mmHg, cerebral oxygen saturation . 65%, and hemoglobin \$ 10 g/dL.

Under antegrade selective cerebral, selective coronary, and retrograde lower body perfusion, supra-aortic vessels are snared and the ascending aorta is clamped distal to the coronary perfusion line. After extensive preparation of the descending aorta, the descending aorta is clamped, and ductal tissue with stent and isthmic region are totally excised. The aortic arch and ascending aorta are opened up to the clamp. For patients who have an ascending aorta under 4 mm in size, a small cardioplegia cannula is additionally inserted in the ascending aorta to maintain cardioplegic arrest. (Fig. 51.12E) The posterior wall of the descending aorta and the distal aortic arch is end-to-end anastomosed with a 7-0 PDS suture. After that, a partial side-to-side proximal aortic and pulmonary artery anastomosis is performed with a 7-0 PDS suture as in the classic Norwood procedure. #### **Special Features of Postoperative Care**

#### Postoperative Care after First-Stage **Reconstructive Surgery**

Care after first-stage reconstruction is particularly complex and important. The initial management after discontinuation of CPB is discussed earlier in "Post-Cardiopulmonary Bypass Management." Pulmonary and systemic blood flow must be balanced; an appropriately sized shunt or conduit makes this more manageable postoperatively. Maintaining adequate systemic CO despite wellbalanced systemic and pulmonary blood flow distribution is more of a challenge postoperatively because of depressed cardiac function resulting from operation. Use of inotropic support is usually more warranted postoperatively than preoperatively. Typical hemodynamic targets typically include: MAP of 45 to 50, Pao2 of 35 to 45, PCO2 of 35 to 45, SpO2 of 75% to 85%, CVP of 8 to 12, and Hct of 40 to 45. NIRS are used to guide therapy as well as targeting a somatic CAVO2<20 and a cerebral dAVO2 < 30.118 Patients are categorized into one of four physiologic quadrants (High SVR, High CO, Low SVR, Low CO) (Fig. 51.10), based on MAP (</>48) and rSO2R (>/<70). 82,118,119 Initial epinephrine is 0.05 µg/kg/min (max dose of 0.1µg/kg/min) and milrinone is set at 0.5 µg/kg/min, usually with a loading dose of 50  $\mu$ g/kg for SVRi $\geq$  12 prior to separation from CPB.

Neuromuscular blockade is administered on admission to the ICU and is discontinued the morning of postoperative day (POD) 1. Dexmedetomidine is run at 0.2-0.4 μg/kg/hr, and Ativan is used as needed. If fentanyl is  $> 2 \mu g/kg/hr$  on admission, then fentanyl is decreased to 2 µg/kg/hr on arrival and is stopped 12 to 24 hours after chest closure.

The infant's metabolic demands are minimized, and arterial blood gases, rate of ventilation, tidal volume, and Fio<sub>2</sub> can all be manipulated to maximize Qp/Qs balance. Usual respiratory goals are PCO2 of 35 to 45, PaO2 of 35 to 50, and SpO2 of 75% to 85%. The initial FiO2 is generally 40% to 50% and titrated down if PaO2 is >50 or SpO2 is >90. Qp:Qs is assessed with each arterial blood gas. An appropriate combination of tidal volume and ventilatory rate is used to achieve optimal Paco<sub>2</sub>. If pulmonary blood flow appears inadequate, either due to a restrictive shunt or conduit or pulmonary hypertension, ventilation is increased to reduce Paco<sub>2</sub> to approximately 30 to 35 mmHg. If pulmonary blood flow is excessive, ventilation is adjusted to allow Paco<sub>2</sub> to increase to approximately 45 to 50 mmHg. If pulmonary blood flow is assessed to be adequate, Paco<sub>2</sub> is adjusted to 40 mmHg. Similarly, Fio<sub>2</sub> can be adjusted over a range from 17% to 100%, depending on pulmonary blood flow and Rp. Higher levels of Fio<sub>2</sub>

are used when pulmonary blood flow is judged to be inadequate, and lower levels if it is judged to be excessive. In a patient whose pulmonary blood flow is considered adequate, Fio<sub>2</sub> is usually reduced to 25% to 30% within several hours after operation.

Some institutions suggest using additional inspired CO<sub>2</sub> both to limit excessive pulmonary blood flow and independently improve systemic blood flow. This practice is somewhat controversial.

Use of nitric oxide in the setting of reduced pulmonary blood flow should be limited to patients in whom there is clear evidence of an adequately sized shunt or conduit and elevated pulmonary Rp. Use of nitric oxide for a patient with inadequate pulmonary blood flow caused by an excessively restrictive shunt or conduit is ineffective; such a patient should immediately be returned to the operating room for shunt or conduit revision.

A typical level of inotropic support in the early postoperative period includes milrinone (0.5 µg/kg/min), with or without addition of low-dose epinephrine (0.03-0.05 µg/kg/min) (see Section IV of Chapter 4). Substantially larger doses of inotropic drugs are as likely to harm as help. Specifically, for the patient who shows evidence of excessive pulmonary blood flow and reduced systemic perfusion, increasing inotropic support is likely to exacerbate the physiologic imbalance and increase myocardial oxygen demand while contributing to coronary vasospasm.

Other maneuvers that maximize oxygen delivery to the systemic tissues include optimizing CO by taking advantage of the Frank-Starling curve. Typically, adjusting atrial filling pressures with volume supplementation to achieve pressures between 6 and 12 mmHg is desired. Optimizing oxygen-carrying capacity by adjusting the hematocrit to a minimum of 40% is advised. Despite maximizing CO and balancing pulmonary and systemic blood flow it is common for patients to show evidence of marginally adequate systemic perfusion during the first 24 hours after operation.

Metabolic acidosis with base deficits ranging from 0 to -5 are common and should be treated aggressively with either intermittent bicarbonate or continuous bicarbonate infusion.

Urine output is also commonly reduced during the first 24 hours ( $<1 \text{ mL} \cdot \text{kg}^{-1} \cdot \text{h}^{-1}$ ), and periods of anuria may occur. If anuria or oliguria persists beyond the first 6 hours postoperatively, serious consideration should be given to placing a peritoneal dialysis catheter (see "Renal Subsystem" in Section I of Chapter 4).

If the infant is returned to the ICU from the operating room with the sternum left open, it should not be closed until hemodynamic status is stabilized. This typically takes 18 to 96 hours. If instability persists, sternal closure can be delayed for longer periods of time. If the infant arrives in the ICU from the operating room with the sternal wound completely closed, consideration should be given to opening it in the ICU if hemodynamics are inadequate during the first 24 hours. Substantial cardiac and pulmonary stability can be achieved with this simple maneuver.

Even with an open sternum, neuromuscular blockade is discontinued typically on the morning of POD 1. Ventilator support is gradually weaned after chest closure with a goal of extubating 36 to 72 hours following closure.

Prolonged ventilatory support can occur following the Norwood procedure and may extend to 5 to 10 days, even in patients who have no definable cardiovascular or pulmonary problem. Under these circumstances, nutrition becomes a priority.

Typically, total parenteral nutrition is begun within 48 to 72 hours of operation and may be continued or converted to direct enteral feeding using a nasogastric tube. Enteral feeding should be used as soon as cardiovascular stability has been achieved and return of intestinal function is documented.

Heparin is started at 20 mg/kg/hr on the morning of POD 1 as line prophylaxis if there is no active bleeding. Aspirin is started at 20 to 25 mg when tolerating po feeds with a goal of starting on POD 3 to inhibit thrombosis of the shunt or conduit.

#### **Postoperative Care and Interstage Period after Hybrid Procedure**

According to Giessen experience, all patients are transferred mechanically ventilated to the ICU after the procedure with closed chest and without catecholamine support. Unlike after the Norwood procedure, the patients are not managed with aggressive sedation and pharmacologic paralysis; only morphine therapy is used as 0.2–0.3 mg 3 6/day if required. Also, furosemide 1–2 mg 3 4-6/day if needed and, as a beta blocker, bisoprolol 0.1–0.2 mg/ kg are administered. The patients are ventilated in an assisted mode on pressure-controlled ventilation with the following respiratory settings: peak inspiratory pressure of 16 to 20 cmH2O, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) 4-5 cmH2O, respiratory rate 20-25/min, tidal-volume 10 mL/kg, Fi02 0.21. Patients are mainly extubated on the day of the procedure. Galantowicz and colleagues reported 52% of patients are extubated in the operation room and 85% of patients within 24 hours after the hybrid procedure.120

The interstage period after hybrid procedure is a time of increased risk for patients born with HLHS. Success relies on heart protection and balancing of the systemic-pulmonary circulation. During the interstage period, controls are performed every one to two weeks with attention to weight gain and to respiratory rate during sleep. Peripheral oxygen saturation and blood pressure are measured in all extremities to record systolic and diastolic values. During echocardiographic assessment, unrestrictive interatrial communication, tricuspid valve competence, RV function, retrograde perfusion of the hypoplastic aortic arch including cerebral blood flow, and the blood flow pattern of the coeliac trunk are observed. The blood flow through the stented duct is evaluated aiming a systolic Doppler velocity of less than 2.5 m/s.121 While awaiting the comprehensive stage II operation, the patients receive a combination of bisoprolol (0.1–0.2 mg/kg), spironolactone (1–2 mg/kg), and lisinopril (0.1–0.2 mg/kg). This combination aims to decrease heart rate under 120/minute, regulate Rp/Rs balance, prevent RV remodeling, and avoid diastolic dysfunction. Patients with antegrade coronary flow (aortic stenosis) need no antithrombotic therapy; patients with retrograde coronary flow (aortic atresia) receive 1 to 2 mg/kg acetylsalicylic acid (ASA). Patients who had ductal reinterventions receive both ASA and clopidogrel (0.2 mg/kg).101,122

Comprehensive stage II is planned at the age of 4 months and with a weight over 5 kg. Before stage II, all patients are examined with cardiac MRI. Cardiac catheterization is not used as a routine examination.

#### **Postoperative Care after Comprehensive Stage II Operation**

Patients are transferred to the ICU with closed chest. They receive continuously infused milrinone (0.5–1.0 mg/kg/min), low dose norepinephrine (0.05–0.15 mg/kg/min), remifentanil (0.3 mg/kg/ min), and inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) in a dosage of 10-20 ppm. Postoperative tachycardia, increased body temperature, systemic inflammatory response (SIRS), pain, and insufficient spontaneous breathing increases oxygen consumption (VO2). Postoperative care aims to optimize oxygen delivery and reduce VO2. Patients are goaled to early extubation after the operation (,6–24h). Therefore, patients are ventilated in an assisted mode on pressurecontrolled ventilation with the following respiratory settings; respiratory rate ,16/min, mean airway pressure (MAP),10 cmH2O, positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) 4 cmH2O, and tidalvolume 10 mL/kg. Oxygen saturation value of 75% to 85% is desired. To reduce PAP (Rp)/SAP (Rs), iNO is usually continued in a dosage of 10 to 20 ppm and also combined with iloprost (1–2 ng/kg/min) if needed. To reduce systemic vascular resistance (SVR), heart rate, and oxygen consumption (VO2), clonidine (1–2 mg/kg/h), and dexmedetomidine may be combined with short acting betablockers.

After admission in the ICU, immediate imaging by echocardiography is necessary to assess ventricular function, tricuspid valve insufficiency, and to rule out cardiac tamponade. Intermittent echocardiographic evaluations are necessary to monitor cardiac function and to adapt therapy promptly. To evaluate branch PAs, especially the LPA, an exit angiogram may be performed in the ICU to detect any stenosis or thrombosis. If the patient has thrombosis or stenosis, transcatheter intervention is performed promptly.121

Care after the bidirectional superior cavopulmonary shunt and Fontan operation is described under "Special Features of Postoperative Care" in Chapter 52. Postoperative care following transplantation is similar to that for other cardiac transplant patients (see "Features of Postoperative Care" in Chapter 21).

#### **Results**

#### **First-Stage Reconstruction (Norwood Procedure)**

*Early (Hospital) Death.* Early mortality remains variable among institutions.73 However, advances in surgical and perioperative care of neonates have resulted in a current 80% to 95% survival to hospital discharge.77,123,124 The SVR trial, involving 555 patients from 15 North American centers recruited between May 2005 and July 2008, published a mortality of 16% for the Norwood operation through hospital discharge.77,123 At institutions with a large experience in neonatal cardiac surgery, mortality following first-stage reconstruction improved dramatically in the decades between 1985 and 2005, to hospital mortalities typically , 15%, and then began to plateau.124-129

Other single-institution studies reporting cases from 2002 to 2005 indicated hospital mortality of 6.2% (2/32; CL 2.2%– 14%), 9.1% (6/66; CL 5.4%–14%),130 and 11% (10/88; CL 7.8%–16%).81 The Congenital Heart Surgeons Society (CHSS) report of 622 cases performed between 2001 and 2004 indicated early mortality of 17% (CL 16%–19%).131

As of 2024, STS aggregate operative survival for the Norwood procedure approaches 90%. This improvement is likely related to improvements in surgical technique, increased popularization of the RV to PA conduit, standardization of care pathways, and commitment to early detection of and intervention on residual lesions. There is growing recognition of the risk factors linked to hospital morbidity and mortality following the Norwood operation, leading to better risk stratification and more deliberate use of bilateral PA banding and hybrid procedures before the Norwood operation. In a retrospective review of 177 patients who underwent Norwood palliation between 2006 and 2016, 50 were classified as high-risk and 127 as standard risk. The operative mortality rate was 20% in the high-risk group compared to 1.6% in the standard-risk group (*P* , .0001).132

*Time-Related Survival.* In the SVR trial, 1-year transplant-free survival was significantly different, with 64% in the modified Blalock-Taussig-Thomas shunt (MBTTS) group and 74% in the RVPAS group. However, by 6 years, survival rates were similar, with 59% in the MBTTS group and 64% in the RVPAS group, and at 12 years, survival was 54% in the MBTTS group compared to 59% in the RVPAS group.133 Pre-stage II survival was significantly better in the RVPAS group than in the MBTTS group (*P* 5 .008), and survival between stage II and stage III also favored the RVPAS group (*P* 5 .048). There was no significant difference in survival post-stage III (*P* 5 .26) (Fig. 51.13).134,135

Overall, 12-month survival after first-stage reconstruction has been approximately 60% to 70%, with almost all deaths occurring before the second-stage procedure (bidirectional superior cavopulmonary shunt). Recognition of this pattern of ongoing mortality after hospital discharge has spawned the term *interstage death*. Interstage death of 10%136 and 16%137 are typical.138

Establishment of home monitoring programs, designed to engage parents and primary cardiologists in recognizing early signs and symptoms of patient destabilization, has substantially decreased mortality between a successful first operation and the anticipated second-stage operation, with some reports showing no interstage deaths and many reports demonstrating improvement.138-140

*Incremental Risk Factors for Death after Norwood.* Morphologic factors have been associated with risk of death in some series. In an early study from 1986 by Norwood's group, atrial septal anatomy, preoperative RV hypertrophy, ascending aorta diameter, and coarctation were not correlated with early survival.141 Although not supported by the two largest institutional experiences, some institutions suggest that aortic atresia and very small size of the ascending aorta (diameter , 2 mm) may be associated with increased risk for death, both early142 and late60 after first-stage reconstruction. Intact or highly restrictive atrial septum has been widely associated with increased risk of both early and between stages.55,56,136

In the SVR trial, independent risk factors for intermediateterm morbidity and mortality after the Norwood procedure included lower birth rate, genetic abnormalities, lower gestational age, and lower socioeconomic status.77

The combination of mitral stenosis and aortic atresia have been identified as a risk, particularly when LV-to-coronary artery fistulae are present143,144 (Fig. 51.14). In one study, fetal diagnosis has been shown to decrease the risk of death following first-stage reconstruction, suggesting that factors such as prenatal transport and early postnatal stabilization may improve survival.145 In another, however, prenatal diagnosis was strongly correlated with superior preoperative clinical status but did not influence surgical outcome.146 Moderate or severe tricuspid valve regurgitation preoperatively147,148 and depressed RV function preoperatively142 have correlated with increased risk of death after first-stage reconstruction. RV dysfunction at initial palliation was associated with increased risk for transplantation.

Risk factors most prominently associated with hospital mortality post-Norwood include weight , 2.5 kg,77,142,148,150 gestational age , 34 weeks,150 presence of a restrictive or intact atrial septum,151 \$ moderate TR,114,152,153 \$ moderate systolic dysfunction,114,152,153 TAPVR,153 coronary sinusoids, interventricular hemorrhage grade .3, multisystem organ dysfunction, shock, and genetic or significant extracardiac abnormalities.151,153-155 Other factors variably reported include an initially low arterial pH60,156 and age older than 7 days at time of first-stage reconstruction.136

#### **Second-Stage Reconstruction (Bidirectional Superior Cavopulmonary Anastomosis)**

Outcome following bidirectional superior cavopulmonary anastomosis or hemi-Fontan procedure in HLHS is similar to that for patients with other forms of univentricular heart (see Section V in Chapter 52). This naturally follows from the fact that all patients with univentricular heart, including those with HLHS, are selected for bidirectional superior cavopulmonary shunt based on similar physiologic criteria. Because of the marked improvement in physiologic status following bidirectional superior cavopulmonary anastomosis, mortality following the perioperative period and before the Fontan procedure is generally extremely low.73

Current outcomes following stage II palliation after initial Norwood demonstrate continued but reduced attrition at rate of about 7% for the year following bidirectional Glenn. Among 1455 patients followed in the National Pediatric Cardiology Quality Improvement Collaborative who underwent staged palliation from 2016 to 2022 and survived to stage II Glenn, freedom from death or transplant was about 93% at 1 year post-Glenn.160

#### **Third-Stage Reconstruction (Fontan Operation)**

In theory, there should be no difference in outcome following the Fontan operation for patients with HLHS than for patients having other forms of univentricular heart. The logic behind this statement is the same as that given in the previous discussion of outcome following bidirectional superior cavopulmonary anastomosis. This has been confirmed by at least one large singleinstitution study.161 The outcomes discussed here for the Fontan operation in patients with hypoplastic heart syndrome should be supplemented by the general discussion of outcomes following the Fontan procedure for all forms of univentricular heart (see Results in Section VI, Chapter 52).

*Early (Hospital) Death.* In the early era, mortality after the Fontan operation for HLHS was high—8 patients in 50 (16%; CL 11%–23%) reported by Chang and colleagues from Norwood's group.157 More recent estimates of hospital mortality in larger patient cohorts from several institutions is about 5%73,127 Current STS outcomes following Fontan have a median operative survival of 98.8 % (STS 2023). However, several recent large series have identified HLHS as a risk factor relative to other Fontan cohorts162 (See further discussion in Section VI, chapter 52).

*Time-Related Survival.* Time-related survival and risk factors following the Fontan operation, including among patients with HLHS is discussed in detail in Section VI, Chapter 52.

#### **Other Commonly Performed Operations**

The two most commonly performed additional procedures performed in patients undergoing the standard three-stage protocol are repair of recurrent arch obstruction and tricuspid valve repair for regurgitation. These can be performed either at the time of the second- or third-stage procedure, or separately.

Aortic arch obstruction, including recurrent arch obstruction, is a significant risk factor for ventricular failure and death following staged palliation for HLHS at each stage. Isolated stenosis can often be effectively treated with catheter-based balloon dilation and stenting. Routine formal coarctectomy and interdigitated anastomosis during the Norwood procedure is effective in minimizing recurrent arch obstruction.84 Recurrent arch obstruction is typically detected in infancy, often within the first six months after the initial Norwood procedure163-165, and occurs in about 20% of survivors.163,165 Overall, the risk of catheter-base intervention is low, and gradient relief is usually excellent. Single and multiinstitutional studies report the procedure to be low risk, with an initial success rate approaching 90% and sustained gradient relief at midterm follow-up. Freedom from repeat arch intervention in about 75% at 18 months.163,165 Procedural risk is somewhat

higher in the presence of poor ventricular function. Tricuspid valve insufficiency is a major risk factor for failure at all three stages of palliation for HLHS. Moderate to severe tricuspid regurgitation should be addressed at presentation and concomitant with the next sequential stage of palliation or before.111 At the time of Fontan, tricuspid valve repair can consist of posterior leaflet obliteration with a posterior De Vega annuloplasty combined with an edge-to-edge repair as needed or with creation of a bridging band across a common AV valve to stabilize the anulus, and some centers are performing artificial chord implantation111,112. A recent meta-analysis of 203 patients from 9 studies reported that the median age for TV repair was 0.02 to 1.9 years of age; 24.6% were performed at Norwood, 4.4% during interstage I, 38.9% at bidirectional Glenn, 7.4% during interstage II, 30% at Fontan and 4.4% following Fontan.113 Mean follow-up ranged from 0.4 to 7.9 years. In hospital mortality was 9% and follow-up survival was comparable to matched controls who did not require or undergo tricuspid valve repair.113

#### **Hybrid Procedure**

*Early (Hospital) Mortality after Hybrid Stage I Procedure.* Early mortality is variable after hybrid stage I. The institutions who used hybrid procedure as a routine fashion for HLHS and variants with higher patient volumes reported early mortality rates of 3.6% between 1998 to 2021 (7/197) and even lower early mortality rates of 2.8% (4/141) between 2010 to 2021. Others have reported early mortality in the recent era between 8% and 13%.166,167 Even with identifiable risk factors, hospital mortality between 4% and 10% have been reported.168,169

*Time-Related Survival after Hybrid Stage I Procedure.* Timerelated survival after the hybrid stage I procedure includes interstage survival until comprehensive stage II. With experience, interstage mortality appears similar or only slightly higher than after the Norwood procedure in experienced centers. High volume centers such as Giessen (Germany) reported interstage survival of 95% (181/190) and transplant-free survival of 94% (178/190) over 23 years,101 Another high-volume center in Columbus, Ohio (USA) reported interstage survival of 85% (157/185) and transplant-free survival of 83% (154/185) over 16 years.166

*Incremental Risk Factors for Death after Hybrid Stage I Procedure.* Aortic atresia with retrograde aortic arch and coronary flow appears to be a risk factor for death after the hybrid procedure.170 However, Schranz and colleagues observed no difference in mortality between aortic atresia and aortic stenosis in HLHS patients.106 Mortality appears to be reduced by additional second stent implantation in the isthmic region at the time of hybrid procedure in patients with associated retrograde aortic arch obstruction. Akintuerk and colleagues reported 8% (2/25) mortality in this subgroup after the hybrid procedure.101

*Hemodynamic and Morphologic Results after Hybrid Stage I Procedure.* Reinterventions after hybrid stage I procedure are mostly related to the implanted ductal stent, PA bandings, and interatrial septum. About 50% of patients undergoing the hybrid procedure require an interstage catheter-based or surgical reintervention before comprehensive stage II palliation.171 The Giessen group reported 4.9% surgical and 36.3% catheter-based reinterventions, most commonly for ductal stents.101 Baba and colleagues observed 13.5% (5/37) ductal stent reinterventions.172

*Early (Hospital) Mortality after Comprehensive Stage II Operation.* Early mortality of comprehensive stage II reported by the Giessen (Germany) group was 5.8% (10/1.2) between 1998 and 2021and improved to 1.8% (2/113) in the last 11 years.101 The Columbus group (US) reported hospital mortality of 12.9% (19/147) between 2002 and 2018,166 with mortality decreasing to 4% (2/55) in the recent era.173,174 A multicenter study of 49 centers including 209 patients showed a hospital mortality of 12.4% (26/209) from 2010 to 2016.174

*Time-Related Survival after Comprehensive Stage II Operation.* Survival after comprehensive stage II until Fontan completion is 94% and transplant-free survival is 93% by the Giessen group. Similar survival rates have been observed by the Columbus group.166

*Incremental Risk Factors for Death after Comprehensive Stage II Operation.* Preoperative depressed RV function, severe tricuspid regurgitation and postoperative Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) requirement are the major risk factors for death after the comprehensive stage II procedure.166,174

*Hemodynamic and Morphologic Results after Comprehensive Stage II Operation.* LPA stenosis or thrombotic occlusion represent the major limitation of the comprehensive stage II procedure. LPA stent implantation is required in 31% to 38% of patients after the operation106,167 but appears to have no negative impact on survival.106

Surgical and catheter-based reintervenions for aortic arch are published as 1.7% and 12.7%, respectively.107

*Long-term Survival after Hybrid Stage I, Comprehensive Stage II Operation, and Fontan Completion.* In the Giessen experience, completion rate is 78 %, with 73% transplant-free survival.101 Outcomes after the Fontan procedure in this and other settings are discussed in detail in Section VI in Chapter 52.

## **Indications for Operation**

HLHS is a fatal condition; death usually occurs within 1 month of birth with no treatment (see Fig. 51.6) and certainly within 1 year with optimal medical treatment (see Fig. 51.7). Surgical intervention is therefore advisable unless economic conditions or lack of institutional capability deny this possibility or the patient's legal guardians choose to withhold surgical therapy. Curiously, this latter practice remains relatively widespread, even though current treatment outcomes for HLHS are now comparable with outcomes for other cardiac defects for which the option to withhold support is not offered by managing physicians and other healthcare providers.

If intervention is to be accomplished, management should begin as soon as possible, preferably with *prenatal* arrangements for delivery at an institution capable of perinatal resuscitation and subsequent surgical management. Treatment begins at birth or as soon as diagnosis has been made and consists initially of intensive preoperative therapy. Neonates with HLHS usually can be resuscitated and maintained in stable condition with such intervention, and the surgical procedure can be performed as an elective procedure, ideally between 2 and 5 days of life.

#### **Special Situations and Controversies**

#### Aortic Atresia with Large Ventricular Septal Defect

This lesion includes aortic atresia or severe aortic stenosis (functional aortic atresia) and large VSD with two adequate ventricles. Despite two adequate ventricles, systemic circulation is ductal dependent. A two-ventricle repair is potentially executable because such patients commonly have a normal mitral valve and left ventricle. The VSD is mainly of membranous or conotruncal type. If the VSD exists in the inlet or midmuscular region, biventricular repair can be challenging and univentricular palliation can be considered.

There are different strategies to achieve biventricular repair. One strategy is to perform a typical first-stage Norwood reconstruction in the neonatal period followed by a biventricular repair, in which the left ventricle is baffled to the pulmonary trunk using a Rastelli-type repair. <sup>175-178</sup> In this repair, LV outflow tract is baffled through the VSD to the pulmonary trunk by an intraventricular patch. Then, right ventricle is connected to the distal PA with valved or valveless conduit. A previously existing systemic to pulmonary shunt is removed.

Instead of applying first-stage Norwood reconstruction, hybrid stage I procedure can alternatively be performed without CPB in the neonatal period followed by definitive biventricular repair in infancy. 179,180 During the biventricular repair, first debanding of

the PAs and closing the ductus and ductal stent are performed. Then, PAs are dilated with balloon catheter or enlarged with a patch. The pulmonary trunk, hypoplastic aortic arch and ascending aorta are reconstructed as in Norwood first-stage reconstruction. Then, the left ventricle is baffled through the VSD to the pulmonary trunk with a Rastelli-type intracardial patch. After intracardial repair, right ventricle to distal PA connection is made with a conduit (Fig. 51.16A-C).

In Giessen, Germany, a hybrid procedure was initially performed in eight such patients between 2002 and 2022, followed by a successful biventricular repair in infancy. <sup>180</sup> One of eight patients had inlet type of VSD. There was no early or late mortality during a mean follow-up of 10 years. Surgical reinterventions were required in two patients (25%) and catheter reinterventions in four (50%). Nakano and colleagues also reported no early mortality and a survival rate of 88% at 10 years in eight patients. <sup>179</sup>

The third strategy is one-stage complete biventricular repair in the neonatal period. This technique was first described by Yasui and colleagues in 1987. <sup>181</sup> In this repair, Norwood-type aortopulmonary reconstruction is combined with the Rastelli operation. Single institution series, reporting experiences ranging from 6 to 21 cases, indicate that the operation can be performed with low early and mid-term mortality but with higher reinterventions rate. <sup>177,178,182-184</sup> Carillo and colleagues reported 6.7% early mortality and 85% survival rate at 5 years in 15 patients. <sup>178</sup> Kanter and colleagues compared one stage complete biventricular repair with biventricular repair after initial Norwood-like reconstruction in 21 patients. They observed no difference between the two strategies regarding early and late mortality. <sup>177</sup>

In 1998, Tchervenkov and associates published their experience with biventricular repair in 11 patients with significant hypoplasia of the left heart without intrinsic valvar stenosis and coined the term of HLHC.4 The differentiation between critical AS with LV hypoplasia and the HLHC is of paramount importance because the surgical approach is very different. Patients with HLHC do not require aortic valvotomy because the aortic valve leaflets are hypoplastic but normal.

The criteria for biventricular repair are:

- Forward flow through the left heart into the ascending aorta and the aortic arch branches
- No intrinsic aortic and mitral valve stenosis
- Adequate LV function
- No endocardial fibroelastosis Biventricular repair consists of the following:
- Elimination of all extracardiac afterload by aortic arch repair
- Full preloading of left heart by eliminating of intracardiac shunts—ASD and VSD closure
- Conservative approach to mitral valve, LVOT, and aortic valve Pulmonary autograft aortic valve replacement (Ross procedure) in infants and neonates in recent years introduces another factor into this decision-making process (see "Autograft Pulmonary Valve" under Technique of Operation in Chapter 12 and "Repair of Tunnel Stenosis by Aortoventriculoplasty [Konno Operation]" and "Modified Konno Operation" in Section II of Chapter 50). The ability to perform the Ross procedure along with Konno enlargement of the LV outflow tract, in combination with arch reconstruction and resection of LV endocardial fibroelastosis, provides the opportunity to create two-ventricle repairs in patients considered to be poor candidates using older criteria.189,190

Using aggressive left heart reconstructive techniques, it is possible to physiologically essentially normalize the entire LV outflow tract and improve LV cavity size and diastolic function by resecting the constricting endocardial fibroelastosis. The true limiting factor for successful two-ventricle repair in many instances is size and function of the mitral valve. Except for the mitral valve, it appears that all morphologic components of the left heart can be adequately addressed surgically using these techniques. Although long-term success has been achieved in a small number of patients, it remains controversial regarding when these aggressive techniques should be applied rather than opting for the singleventricle approach.

Fetal balloon valvuloplasty provides an additional therapeutic option for patients with a borderline left heart. The sole purpose of the procedure is to convert patients to biventricular physiology who otherwise are destined for single-ventricle physiology. This procedure was first performed in 1989, but only 12 cases were performed between 1989 and 1997. Recently, McElhinney and colleagues reported their experience in 47 cases.191 After a learning curve, they currently quote 75% technical success and 10% fetal demise. Thirty percent of those undergoing a technically successful fetal procedure ultimately achieved a two-ventricle circulation. In all of these, additional postnatal, surgical, or interventional procedures were necessary. This experience demonstrates that fetal balloon valvuloplasty can be technically performed with reasonable safety in the majority of selected patients. However, it is not clear that the procedure plays a causal role in converting patients from a univentricular to a biventricular circulation. The physiologic and morphologic selection criteria for entry into the fetal treatment program were not dissimilar to those of the patients treated by biventricular repair after a postnatal Ross Konno, arch repair, and endocardial fibroelastosis resection—without fetal intervention.

#### **Use or Avoidance of Circulatory Arrest**

When performing the Norwood procedure, the decision to use either hypothermic circulatory arrest or continuous perfusion, most commonly in the form of antegrade cerebral perfusion, is made based on surgeon preference, which is most heavily influenced by personal experience and expert opinion, and less so by objective data.192 Hypothermic circulatory arrest techniques have been available for more than 40 years and continue to evolve. Antegrade cerebral perfusion techniques have been available for a little over a decade and are rapidly evolving as well.

Techniques are now available for reliably performing complex neonatal cardiac surgery, including the Norwood procedure and its variants, without using total body circulatory arrest.80,193-196 Only limited evidence unequivocally demonstrates that avoiding circulatory arrest is beneficial, but a number of compelling arguments can be made supporting the position that continuous circulation, especially to the brain, should be maintained.

Although numerous maneuvers have been used to minimize unwanted sequelae of controlled cerebral ischemia that attends circulatory arrest, both clinical and animal studies indicate that profound metabolic changes take place within minutes of cessation of blood flow, regardless of temperature of the brain tissue (see Section I of Chapter 2 for details and references). Based on these studies, it is difficult to pinpoint the threshold for a safe period of circulatory arrest with confidence, although several clinical studies have attempted to define that point. The safe period concept is only valid as a tool to be applied prospectively in clinical decision making for individual patients if one assumes that all patients are equally susceptible to cerebral ischemia. There is increasing evidence that this is not the case; it is becoming recognized that genetic polymorphism can influence vulnerability of the brain to ischemic insult from individual to individual.197

Based on data showing that metabolic derangements begin immediately with circulatory arrest, and on carefully conducted animal and clinical studies demonstrating that neurologic sequelae correlate with increasing length of circulatory arrest (see "Safe Duration of Circulatory Arrest" in Section I of Chapter 2), it can be argued that clinical studies failing to show sequelae following circulatory arrest are simply using end-point criteria that are insensitive to subtle cognitive injury that may occur with shorter arrest periods or are underpowered to demonstrate a difference. The strongest justification for using circulatory arrest has been that instrumentation, cannulae, and techniques are not available to allow repair of complex aortic arch problems in neonates using continuous perfusion. This justification, however, no longer exists.80,193-196 Although it is clear that continuous perfusion can eliminate the metabolic derangements that occur with cessation of cerebral blood flow, potential complications relating to techniques required to maintain ongoing perfusion can also occur, and these must be defined and studied.

Safeguards that are necessary to avoid perfusion-related complications, however, should be no different from those of standard CPB. There are limited data directly comparing continuous perfusion and hypothermic circulatory arrest in patients undergoing the

Norwood procedure and its variants. There is a single randomized prospective study comparing it with antegrade cerebral perfusion. 198 That study did not examine early mortality or morbidity but focused on neurodevelopmental outcome at midterm followup in a single institution; no difference in outcome between the two techniques was demonstrated. This conclusion is of limited value, however, because the methodology used for the antegrade cerebral perfusion group involved periods of hypothermic circulatory arrest, multiple cannulation maneuvers, and baseline perfusion rates (20 mL · kg<sup>-1</sup> min<sup>-1</sup>) that many would consider in the low flow range. The critique of this manuscript, which accompanies the publication, emphasizes these limitations and further points out that the compelling theoretical advantages of continuous perfusion must be supplemented by both developing a rigorous physiologic understanding of techniques such as antegrade cerebral perfusion, and standardizing operative techniques that minimize morbidity. The current state-of-the-art of antegrade cerebral perfusion lacks these important components. 195,199 Several single-institution studies have established that the Norwood procedure can be performed using continuous perfusion, with early morbidity and mortality comparable with outcomes achieved using hypothermic circulatory arrest. 80,194-196 Some studies indicate an early survival advantage using antegrade cerebral perfusion. 193

#### Comparison of Right Ventricle-to-Pulmonary Trunk Conduit and Systemic-to-Pulmonary **Artery Shunt as Source of Pulmonary Blood** Flow in First-Stage Reconstruction

For decades, a systemic-pulmonary arterial shunt has been the mainstay for providing regulated blood flow to the lungs in first-stage reconstruction. 123,200 The most commonly used procedure for accomplishing this is the modified Blalock-Taussig-Thomas shunt using a PTFE graft. Its potential disadvantages include excessive volume load, acute thrombosis, and low diastolic blood pressure leading to coronary insufficiency. Although Norwood originally experimented with right ventricle-to-pulmonary artery conduits as the source of pulmonary blood flow in first-stage reconstruction, 201,202 this was quickly abandoned.

Over the past 2 decades, the concept of the right ventricle-topulmonary trunk conduit as the source of pulmonary blood flow has been reexamined.<sup>203,204</sup> These techniques use either a valved or nonvalved conduit between right ventricle and central distal pulmonary trunk. In the early 2000s, Sano proposed use of a more restrictive conduit (4-mm and 5-mm PTFE graft). 205 This approach has several potential advantages, the most important of which is lack of diastolic runoff from the systemic circulation. This provides importantly elevated diastolic blood pressure in the aorta and coronary arteries compared with the systemic-pulmonary shunt, with the potential advantage of promoting more stable cardiac performance by reducing myocardial oxygen supply/demand mismatch that undoubtedly occurs in the standard Norwood operation, in which the single right ventricle has marked pressure and volume load, yet decreased coronary perfusion. An additional potential advantage is improved development of the LPA system because the source of pulmonary blood flow enters the PAs more centrally. The potential disadvantages of this technique are (1) a right ventriculotomy is required to place the conduit, which may lead to ventricular dysfunction and dysrhythmias; (2) damage to the pulmonary valve may occur at the time of the ventriculotomy; (3) excessive thrombosis is theoretically possible because of relative stasis during diastole; and (4) central pulmonary arterial distortion may occur.

Over the past 2 decades, use of the right ventricle-to-pulmonary trunk conduit has increased steadily, and currently it is chosen more frequently than the systemic-to-pulmonary artery shunt.

Superiority of one technique over the other has not been unequivocally demonstrated, but a number of studies address this question, most prominent of which was the multi-institutional SVR Trial.<sup>123</sup> The SVR trial started in 2005 as a prospective randomized trial of 555 neonates undergoing Norwood palliation, randomized to either MBTT shunt or RV to PA conduit as source of pulmonary blood flow. This trial identified improvement in operative 30 day survival in the RV to PA conduit group, particularly in full-term neonates with aortic atresia. Twelve-month transplantation-free survival was better with the RV to PA conduit than with the MBTT shunt, although after 12 months there was no significant difference between the two groups. 123

Several studies note the requirement for reintervention for inadequate pulmonary blood flow prior to standard second-stage intervention in patients who receive the RV-to-pulmonary trunk conduit.81,206,207 When this problem occurs, it can be managed either by surgical or interventional revision of the conduit<sup>206,207</sup> or by placing a systemic-to-pulmonary artery shunt, preferably with takedown of the conduit.<sup>208</sup>

The question remains whether or not right ventricular function is impaired as a result of the right ventriculotomy or other factors related to the conduit procedure.

Although there are currently no definitive answers, several studies and the literature review by Raja and colleagues<sup>209</sup> identify no clear evidence that RV function is impaired either early or at midterm follow-up. In the SVR trial, ventricular function was similar in both groups. 123

To summarize, current evidence suggests that the right ventricle-to-pulmonary trunk conduit is associated with improved early and midterm survival and more favorable early hemodynamics, including diastolic blood pressure and shorter hospital length of stay, and does not negatively affect RV performance at midterm follow-up. Branch PA development may be improved. An increased number of unintended procedures have been documented using this technique, but it is likely these will decrease as experience with the technique grows.

#### **Hybrid Versus Norwood Procedure**

More recently, the hybrid approach of restricting pulmonary blood flow with bilateral PA bands combined with either ductal stent or maintenance of the patency of the ductus on PGE has been employed in the treatment of patients with significant risk factors for primary Norwood palliation and also as a primary option in several centers to avoid the Norwood procedure in the neonatal period entirely.75,77,202,210

It is not easy to compare the two procedures because hybrid procedures are usually preferred in higher-risk patients in many centers. STS Database 2019 (105 participants) revealed an operative mortality of 15% (IQR, 8.3%-25%) in 2737 Norwood stage I procedures between 2014 and 2017.<sup>211</sup> Wilder and colleagues reported in 574 neonates (2005–2014; 20 American Institutions) risk-adjusted survival rate for Norwood-RVPA shunt, Norwood-BT shunt, and hybrid procedure: 76%, 60%, and 61%, respectively, at 4 years.<sup>212</sup> However, in this study hybrid procedures were performed in 110 patients who were mostly in higher risk groups (i.e., significantly older at stage I, lower birth weight, higher prevalence of important baseline right ventricle dysfunction, and significant baseline tricuspid valve regurgitation).

It might be more useful to compare the results of high-volume institutions who apply particular techniques in a routine fashion not only in higher-risk patients but also in all patients. Akintuerk and colleagues101 reported 3.6% (7/197) hospital mortality after the hybrid stage I procedure and interstage mortality of hybrid stage I survivors as 4.2% (8/190). Hospital mortality at comprehensive stage II was 5.8% (10/172) between 1998 and 2021. Fontan completion rate was 75.6% (149/197). Overall survival for all patients who underwent stage palliation was 84%, 80%, 75%, 75%, 68%, at 1, 5, 10, 15, and 20 years, respectively (Fig. 51.15).

Sano and colleagues published 8.1% (11/136) hospital mortality of Norwood-RVPA shunt procedure and interstage mortality of stage I survivors as 6.4% (8/125). Hospital mortality of stage II (BCPC) was 3.6% (4/110) between 1998 and 2017. Fontan completion rate was 68.3% (93/136). Overall survival for all patients is reported as 80.9%, 72.3%, and 62.8% at 1, 5, and 20 years, respectively.213(Fig. 51.17).

Gellings and colleagues published 12.7% (24/196) hospital mortality using both Norwood BT shunt and Norwood RVPA shunt between 2005 and 2017.214 Kaplinski and colleagues reported interstage mortality after Norwood procedure as 9.3% (49/529) between 2002 and 2017.215 The same group published the largest patient cohort who underwent stage II (BCPC) after initial Norwood procedure. They reported 1.5% hospital mortality of stage II (7/465) between 2002 and 2017.216

Hospital and interstage mortality after hybrid stage I is lower than Norwood procedure if this procedure is used not only in higher-risk patients but rather in all patients in a routine fashion. Hospital mortality of stage II is higher in the hybrid group because the comprehensive stage II operation includes more complexity than bidirectional cavopulmonary connection. However, Akintuerk and colleagues reported hospital mortality of comprehensive stage II as 1.8% (2/113) between 2010 and 2021.101 Stage II completion rate, Fontan completion rate, and 20 years estimated overall survival seems to be slightly higher in the hybrid procedure than in the Norwood procedure with right ventricle to PA conduit.

In the last 20 years major strides have been made in the surgical treatment of HLHS. In addition to the classic Norwood operation with a systemic pulmonary shunt, the advent of the RV to PA conduit as a source of pulmonary blood and the hybrid procedures, have added additional surgical options. We hope that in the future with increasing experience, the optimal approach will emerge!
