# Cor Triatriatum

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Classification
* **Cor triatriatum:** Rare congenital anomaly in which left or right atrium is subdivided into "proximal" and "distal" chambers by a membranous partition. Left atrial form (cor triatriatum sinister) occurs in 0.1-0.4% of congenital heart defects. Right atrial form (cor triatriatum dexter) occurs in 0.025-0.1%.
* **Cor triatriatum sinister:** Pulmonary venous inflow enters proximal chamber and encounters restricted access to distal chamber (which contains left atrial appendage – distinguishes from supravalvar mitral ring) through one or more restrictive ostia. Proximal chamber typically larger than distal chamber; membrane usually thick and fibromuscular.
* **Cor triatriatum dexter:** Membrane divides smooth posterior portion of right atrium from trabeculated anterior portion (contains right atrial appendage, leads to tricuspid valve). Membrane represents persistence of embryonic sinus venosus valves (normally regress to become Eustachian and Thebesian valves).

### Morphology and Associated Anomalies
* **Classic cor triatriatum sinister:** Proximal chamber receives all pulmonary veins, is thick-walled; distal chamber thin-walled, leads to mitral valve. Right ventricle usually enlarged (depends on left-to-right shunting at atrial level). Foramen ovale usually patent and stretched. Left ventricle normal or small.
* **Relationship to anomalous pulmonary venous connection:** Embryologically – normal heart: complete incorporation of pulmonary venous confluence into left atrium; TAPVC: no incorporation; cor triatriatum: partial incorporation. Proximal chamber entry of pulmonary veins analogous to common pulmonary venous sinus in TAPVC.
* **Partial cor triatriatum:** Some pulmonary veins drain to proximal chamber, remainder drain anomalously (e.g., left pulmonary veins to left vertical vein/brachiocephalic vein, or to coronary sinus) or normally to left atrium.
* **Associated anomalies in cor triatriatum sinister (33-67% of cases):** Patent atrial defect (most common), left superior vena cava (more frequent than with other CHD), VSD, coarctation of aorta, AV septal defect, tetralogy of Fallot, asplenia/polysplenia (rare).
* **Associated anomalies in cor triatriatum dexter:** Atrial septal defects, tricuspid stenosis, RV hypoplasia, Ebstein anomaly, tetralogy of Fallot, PAPVC/TAPVC, persistent LSVC, AV septal defect.

### Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria
* **Cor triatriatum sinister – infants (restrictive aperture):** Signs of low cardiac output (pallor, tachypnea, poor peripheral pulses, growth failure). With associated left-to-right shunting from proximal chamber to right atrium or PAPVC: pulmonary overcirculation, pulmonary venous obstruction on CXR, RV enlargement.
* **Cor triatriatum sinister – children/young adults:** Signs/symptoms of pulmonary venous hypertension (dyspnea, tiredness, recurrent respiratory infections) – similar to mitral stenosis.
* **Cor triatriatum dexter:** Depends on degree of tricuspid inflow obstruction and desaturated blood streaming across atrial septum. Most cases asymptomatic (incidental finding). Rarely: obstruction to RV filling, cyanosis (differential diagnosis of neonatal cyanosis), intermittent cyanosis, syncope, right heart failure, cryptogenic cirrhosis, atrial tachycardia in older patients. Stroke and coronary/pulmonary embolism reported in adults.
* **Diagnosis:** Primarily by transthoracic cross-sectional echocardiography. 3D and transesophageal echocardiography for associated anomalies. MRI/CT with 3D reconstruction for complex morphology when 2D echo not definitive. Cardiac catheterization rarely needed (only if major associated anomalies suspected).

### Natural History
* **Cor triatriatum sinister:** Depends on effective size of aperture. Small aperture: critically ill in early infancy – without surgery, 75% die in infancy. Larger aperture: presents in childhood/young adulthood with signs/symptoms/prognosis of mitral stenosis. Better prognosis when proximal chamber communicates with right atrium through fossa ovalis ASD (decompresses into RA) – presents with large left-to-right shunt, survives longer. Rare adult discovery (minimal obstruction or associated anomalies like unilateral PVO ameliorating clinical impact).
* **Cor triatriatum dexter:** Severity related to degree of obstruction and patency of interatrial septum. Significant hypoxia may dictate surgical repair in first days of life. Mild hypoxia often well tolerated into adult life.

### Technique of Operation
* **Classic cor triatriatum sinister:** Median sternotomy, mild hypothermic CPB with bicaval cannulation. Deep hypothermic circulatory arrest option for very small infants.
  - **Left-side approach (older children/adults, especially when proximal chamber enlarged):** Incision anterior to right pulmonary veins (as for mitral valve surgery). Excise most of obstructing partition to create large opening.
  - **Right atrial approach (neonates/small infants, or when proximal chamber small and right atrium enlarged):** Open right atrium, enlarge foramen ovale/ASD or incise septum primum to access left-sided chambers. Identify partition, excise completely. Close atrial septum opening with pericardial patch.
* **Atypical cor triatriatum sinister:** Preoperative determination of all pulmonary and systemic venous connections (echocardiography, CT/MRI, possibly catheterization). Open largest "atrial" chamber on right side first, then right atrium if needed. Repair combines techniques for typical cor triatriatum, PAPVC, TAPVC, and unroofed coronary sinus syndrome.
* **Cor triatriatum dexter:** Open right atrial free wall, excise dividing membrane completely (protecting conduction tissue). Close any atrial septal defect primarily or with patch. Percutaneous dilation of membrane and device closure of ASD may be considered in older children/adults, but surgery preferred.

### Postoperative Care
* Support respiratory system (may be compromised by preoperative pulmonary venous obstruction). Recognize possibility of reactive pulmonary vascular bed sensitive to abrupt ventilation changes.

### Results
* **Cor triatriatum sinister – children (186 cases, 6 studies):** Early mortality 3.8% (7 deaths). Most deaths in critically ill infants, more likely in early institutional experience. Single ventricle associated with mortality.
* **Cor triatriatum sinister – adults (93 cases, 2 studies):** 0% hospital mortality.
* **Cor triatriatum dexter:** No operative mortality in 9 reported cases since 1980 (Kalangos) plus 3 cases (Mackman) – hospital mortality near zero with accurate preoperative diagnosis.
* **Time-related survival (cor triatriatum sinister):** Approaches general population, especially when operation in infancy. Toronto (46 patients, 1983-2005): survival 94% at 3 months, 88% at 1 year, 86% at 5 years. Boston (65 patients, 1963-2010, median follow-up 5.4 years): only one late death (complex single ventricle). No reinterventions for recurrent left atrial obstruction; 6 patients required intervention for pulmonary vein stenosis.
* **Functional status (cor triatriatum sinister):** Most patients asymptomatic or favorable NYHA class. Boston study: 81% NYHA I, 15% NYHA II.
* **Cor triatriatum dexter long-term:** Limited data, but surgical removal of obstructing membrane and ASD closure should return patient to normal.

### Indications for Operation
* **Classic cor triatriatum sinister (infancy with restrictive aperture):** Urgent indication – 75% die in infancy without surgery.
* **Older patients with chronic symptoms:** Urgently indicated.
* **Atypical cor triatriatum sinister (proximal chamber opens to RA, restrictive/no opening between proximal/distal chambers, small PFO between RA and distal chamber):** Physiologic instability with large left-to-right shunt + restricted LA/LV inflow – urgent operation in early infancy.
* **Cor triatriatum dexter:** Indicated when creates symptomatic obstruction to RV inflow and right-to-left shunting at atrial level in neonate/infant. Also indicated in older patients with similar findings not satisfactorily addressable percutaneously.

## Definition</summary><div class="details-content">

Cor triatriatum refers to two rare congenital cardiac anomalies in which the left or right atrium is subdivided into "proximal" and "distal" chambers by a membranous partition.

The anomaly occurs more commonly in the left atrium (0.1% to 0.4% of congenital heart defects) and is frequently referred to as *cor triatriatum sinister* to distinguish it from the far less common *cor triatriatum dexter* found in the right atrium (0.025% to 0.1%).

In cor triatriatum sinister, pulmonary venous inflow enters the "proximal" left atrial chamber and encounters restricted access to the "distal" left atrial chamber by a membrane in which there is one or more restrictive ostia. In the Congenital Heart Surgery Nomenclature and Database Project, cor triatriatum sinister is classified as a pulmonary venous anomaly, with subclassifications as described in Box 31.1.

In cor triatriatum dexter, the right atrium is divided by a membrane that restricts systemic venous return into the right ventricle and may promote a right-to-left shunt at the atrial level. Although both left and right atrial forms of cor triatriatum exist, the clinical pictures of the two entities are disparate. It is also noteworthy that the term "cor triatriatum" has come under criticism by several congenital cardiac pathologists and anatomists who prefer the more descriptive term "divided atrial chambers" and remark that neither anomaly results from the formation of a new atrial chamber.

## Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

The anatomic arrangement of cor triatriatum sinister (divided left atrium) was illustrated by Andral in 1829 but not identified as a distinct malformation until 1868 by Church. The term "cor triatriatum sinister" was applied to the malformation by Borst in 1905. The first angiographic diagnosis was made by Miller and colleagues in 1964. Echocardiographic diagnosis of this cardiac anomaly was described by Ostman-Smith and colleagues in 1984 and by Wolf in 1986. The first surgical correction is believed to have been performed by Vineberg and Gialloreto in 1956 using a closed finger-fracture technique via the left atrial appendage; in the same year, Lewis and colleagues corrected the defect under direct vision using hypothermia and inflow occlusion.

The anatomy of cor triatriatum dexter (divided right atrium) was described by Yater in 1929 but not recognized as having clinical implications until Doucette and Knoblich described the associated clinical findings in 1963. The first successful surgical repair was reported by Hansing and colleagues in 1972. Due to the significant rarity of cor triatriatum dexter reports of the anomaly have been sporadic and usually limited to single cases; however, within the last two decades, the number of reports has increased including one report of three cases at one center over 13 years.

## Morphology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Morphology of Classic Cor Triatriatum Sinister</summary><div class="details-content">

In this anomaly, the morphologic left atrium is divided by a fibromuscular shelf that extends diagonally, separating the chamber into a proximal (pulmonary venous) compartment and a distal (vestibular) compartment. The vestibular chamber contains the left atrial appendage, and this anatomic factor distinguishes cor triatriatum sinister from a supravalvar mitral shelf or ring. The proximal (common pulmonary venous) chamber is typically larger than the distal (vestibular) chamber. The diaphragm or membrane partitioning them is usually thick and fibromuscular and has one or more openings (apertures) (Fig. 31.1). The aperture(s) is usually two-dimensional but occasionally exhibits a tubular or tunnel-like configuration.

![](_page_2_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 31.1** Autopsy specimen of cor triatriatum sinister showing opened proximal chamber separated by thick partition *(between arrows)* from opened distal chamber. The partition has a restrictive aperture (not illustrated here) in its center. The relationship of this anomaly to total anomalous pulmonary venous connection is evident from this specimen. *DIST,* Distal chamber; *LA,* left atrium (distal chamber); *LAA,* left atrial appendage; *LPV,* left pulmonary veins; *LV,* left ventricle; *PROX,* proximal; *RPV,* right pulmonary veins.

In the classic entity, the proximal chamber receives all pulmonary vein connections and is thick-walled, whereas the distal chamber is thin-walled and leads into the mitral valve (Fig. 31.2). Despite high pressure in the proximal chamber, the pulmonary veins are usually not dilated. Entry of the right pulmonary veins into the proximal chamber usually bears the same relationship to the right atrium and superior vena cava as in the normal heart (Fig. 31.3).

![](_page_2_Picture_4.jpeg)

Arrows indicate two small apertures in the partition that communicate with the proximal chamber, to which all pulmonary veins are attached. *DIST,* Distal chamber; *LAA,* left atrial appendage; *LV,* left ventricle; *MV,* mitral valve; *RPV,* right pulmonary veins.

![](_page_3_Picture_2.jpeg)

Note the normal relationship between right pulmonary veins and superior vena cava and right atrium. *RA,* Right atrium; *RPV,* right pulmonary veins; *SVC,* superior vena cava.

The right ventricle is usually enlarged, but this enlargement depends on the presence and degree of left-to-right shunting at the atrial level and whether that communication is with the proximal or distal chamber. The foramen ovale within the oval fossa is usually patent and stretched, thereby permitting interatrial shunting. The left ventricle is usually normal in size or small.

### Relationship of Cor Triatriatum Sinister to Partial and Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection</summary><div class="details-content">

In classic cor triatriatum sinister, the pulmonary veins enter the proximal chamber more posteriorly and medially than what is seen in a normal left atrium. That entry is analogous to that into the common pulmonary venous sinus found in many patients with total anomalous pulmonary venous connection (TAPVC; see Chapter 30). A proposed embryologic perspective is that in a normal heart, the confluence of the pulmonary veins is completely incorporated into the left atrium, is not incorporated into the left atrium in TAPVC, and is only partially incorporated into the left atrium in cor triatriatum sinister. Just as TAPVC does not always involve all pulmonary veins connecting to a single pulmonary venous sinus (i.e., mixed TAPVC), cor triatriatum sinister does not always involve all pulmonary veins draining to the proximal chamber. There are several examples of cor triatriatum sinister in which some of the pulmonary veins drain to the proximal chamber, and the remainder drain anomalously to the systemic venous circulation.

Partial anomalous left pulmonary venous connection (from left upper lobe only or from entire left lung) may connect to a left vertical vein that connects to the left brachiocephalic vein, with all other pulmonary veins entering the proximal chamber. Partial anomalous venous connection may consist of all left pulmonary veins connecting to the coronary sinus, with the proximal atrial chamber receiving only the right pulmonary veins and connecting through the usual small orifice into the distal left atrial chamber. Comparisons to partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection (PAPVC) also exist. In these examples, some pulmonary veins connect to the proximal chamber, and the remainder connect normally to the left atrium.

In "partial" cor triatriatum sinister, the right pulmonary veins alone may connect to the proximal chamber, with the left pulmonary veins connecting normally to the left atrium. Other unusual variants have been documented. The proximal chamber, receiving all pulmonary veins, may have an imperforate portion between it and a typical distal chamber and instead connect to the *right* atrium, whereas the right and left atria are in communication through a coronary sinus atrial septal defect, with the coronary sinus itself being completely unroofed (see Chapter 29). In another example, all pulmonary veins may fail to join an otherwise typical proximal chamber separated from the distal chamber by a perforated partition and instead connect to a common pulmonary venous sinus behind the heart that may connect to the coronary sinus, superior vena cava, or infradiaphragmatically. This combination of divided left atrium and TAPVC puts into question the aforementioned embryologic origin of cor triatriatum sinister.

</div></details>

### Other Associated Anomalies</summary><div class="details-content">

In one-third to two-thirds of cases of cor triatriatum sinister, additional congenital anomalies may affect clinical presentation, pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment. A patent atrial defect is the most commonly associated cardiac lesion. In addition to PAPVC, it should be noted that a left superior vena cava is a commonly associated anomaly coexisting with cor triatriatum sinister, considerably more frequently than with other types of congenital heart disease. Other associated anomalies include ventricular septal defect, coarctation of the aorta, atrioventricular septal defect, tetralogy of Fallot, and (rarely) asplenia and polysplenia.

Several classification schemes have been proposed because of the variety of complex morphologies of which cor triatriatum sinister is a major part. The scheme proposed by Herlong and colleagues focused on pulmonary venous anomalies and depicted in Box 31.1 is preferred by many. Nevertheless, acknowledging and looking for the potential complicating cardiac anomalies is more important for successful patient management than determining what classification best fits that patient's anatomy.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Morphology of Classic Cor Triatriatum Dexter</summary><div class="details-content">

In patients with cor triatriatum dexter (divided right atrium), the embryologic origin of the defect is clearer. In this anomaly, the membrane divides the smooth posterior portion of the right atrium from the trabeculated anterior portion, which contains the right atrial appendage and leads to the tricuspid valve. The membrane is thought to be a persistence of the embryonic valves of the sinus venosus that, in fetal life, direct oxygenated blood from the inferior vena cava across to the left atrium and then to the aorta. These valves normally regress to become the Eustachian and Thebesian valves. When they fail to regress, hypoplasia of the right ventricle or tricuspid atresia may result. When their failed regression results in a clear division of the right atrium and the tricuspid and pulmonary valves remain patent, the very rare condition of cor triatriatum dexter exists. The resulting dividing membrane may become aneurysmal or sail-like and obstruct systemic venous return. In the presence of a patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect, that return may be directed across to the left atrium with a variable hypoxic effect. As with cor triatriatum sinister, cor triatriatum dexter is commonly associated with other important congenital cardiac lesions. In addition to atrial septal defects, tricuspid stenosis and right ventricular hypoplasia, Ebstein anomaly, tetralogy of Fallot, anomalous pulmonary venous connection (partial and total), persistent left superior vena cava, and atrioventricular septal defect have been reported.

## Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Cor Triatriatum Sinister</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinical features of cor triatriatum sinister depend on the degree of pulmonary venous obstruction and the presence of associated cardiac anomalies. Infants with classic cor triatriatum sinister and a narrow opening between the proximal and distal chambers will demonstrate signs of low cardiac output, including pallor, tachypnea, poor peripheral pulses, and growth failure. When there is associated left-to-right shunting from the proximal chamber to the right atrium or because of associated PAPVC, pulmonary overcirculation, and pulmonary venous obstruction may be noted on chest radiography along with right ventricular enlargement. In children and young adults, the classic presentation is with signs and symptoms of pulmonary venous hypertension, including dyspnea, tiredness, and recurrent respiratory infections. Like mitral stenosis, cor triatriatum sinister may present with less classic symptoms.

![](_page_4_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 31.4** Two-dimensional echocardiogram of cor triatriatum sinister. Computer-enhanced image shows partition subdividing the left atrium into proximal and distal chambers.

Diagnosis is usually made by transthoracic cross-sectional echocardiography (Fig. 31.4). Three-dimensional and transesophageal echocardiography have been of value in selected cases, especially in identifying and sorting out associated anomalies. Magnetic resonance and computed tomographic imaging with three-dimensional reconstruction are being increasingly applied to delineate both simple and complex morphology, especially when standard two-dimensional echocardiography is not definitive. Cardiac catheterization and cineangiographic studies are no longer considered necessary unless major associated cardiac anomalies are suspected. However, if that information is important for decisionmaking, further evidence may be obtained from selective cineangiographic studies and pressure measurements.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Cor Triatriatum Dexter</summary><div class="details-content">

The clinical features of cor triatriatum dexter depend on the degree of obstruction to blood flow through the tricuspid valve and the amount of desaturated blood that streams across the atrial septum. It is likely that in most cases, the degree of obstruction is minimal, with the patient having no symptoms and the membrane discovered incidentally during an echocardiogram for unrelated reasons or discovered on autopsy after death from an unrelated cause. However, the membrane rarely creates enough obstruction to compromise right ventricular filling and pulmonary blood flow. In some cases, the membrane streams blood across the atrial septum into the left atrium, resulting in varying degrees of cyanosis. As this lesion has been increasingly recognized, it has been added to the differential diagnosis of neonatal cyanosis. The anomaly has also been reported in older children and adults with presentations that included intermittent cyanosis, syncope, right heart failure, cryptogenic cirrhosis, and atrial tachycardia.

Diagnosis is primarily by echocardiography, including three-dimensional and transesophageal techniques, with saline contrast injection. Magnetic resonance imaging has also become a valuable tool in the diagnosis of cor triatriatum dexter. As with cor triatriatum sinister, cardiac catheterization is rarely required, only to sort out the complexity suggested by the available imaging.

</div></details>

## Natural History</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Cor Triatriatum Sinister</summary><div class="details-content">

The natural history cor triatriatum sinister depends on the effective size of the aperture in the partition between the proximal and distal chambers. When it is small, the infant becomes critically ill during the early months of life, with signs of pulmonary venous obstruction (Fig. 31.5A). Without surgical treatment, such patients die at that young age. If the aperture is larger, the patient presents in childhood or young adulthood with the signs, symptoms, and prognosis of mitral stenosis.

![](_page_5_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 31.5** Morphology of cor triatriatum sinister. (A) Cor triatriatum sinister with patent foramen ovale below the obstructing partition. Proximal chamber is separated from distal chamber by an obstructing partition which is attached to the atrial septum medially and immediately below left inferior pulmonary vein laterally. The lateral attachment is also closely related to the mitral valve. Left atrial appendage is in the distal chamber. Clinical presentation is that of pulmonary venous obstruction (like mitral valve stenosis) when the aperture in the partition is small. Distal chamber communicates with right atrium through foramen ovale, but left-to-right shunt is small. (B) Cor triatriatum sinister with patent foramen ovale above obstructing partition, through which the proximal chamber may communicate with the right atrium. Clinical presentation in this situation is that of a large left-to-right shunt or may mimic total anomalous pulmonary venous connection. Right ventricle may be enlarged.

In most patients, the aperture is severely restrictive. At one time it was thought that approximately 75% of persons born with classic cor triatriatum died in infancy. However, the prognosis is better when the proximal chamber communicates with the right atrium through a fossa ovalis atrial septal defect because the proximal chamber decompresses into the right atrium (Fig. 31.5B). These patients present with signs of a large left-to-right shunt and generally survive longer than those with an obstructive aperture. Rarely, cor triatriatum sinister may be discovered in an adult. Presumably, in these patients, the diagnosis was made much later because of significantly less obstruction between the proximal and distal chambers and associated anomalies such as unilateral pulmonary venous obstruction that ameliorated the clinical impact of the lesion.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Cor Triatriatum Dexter</summary><div class="details-content">

As cor triatriatum dexter is so much rarer than its left atrial counterpart, assessing the natural history of the lesion is limited to what has been noted in case reports. Like its counterpart, the severity and clinical effect are related to the degree of obstruction created by the dividing membrane in addition to the patency of the interatrial septum. As most reports relate to neonates and infants, it must be considered that the severity of cyanosis will determine the urgency of any intervention. While significant hypoxia may dictate surgical repair as early as the first days of life, mild hypoxia is often well tolerated and can be followed into adult life, recognizing that stroke and coronary and pulmonary embolism have been reported in adults with this anomaly.

</div></details>

## Technique of Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Classic Cor Triatriatum Sinister</summary><div class="details-content">

Cor triatriatum sinister is treated primarily by operation. A right atrial approach is the preferred approach for neonates and small infants. An incision directly into this chamber is optimal for older children and adults, especially when the proximal chamber is enlarged. In this case, the approach is the same as that afforded to access the mitral valve for repair or replacement (Chapter 11).

Via a standard median sternotomy, mildly hypothermic cardiopulmonary bypass is established with aortic and bicaval cannulation. In a very small infant, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest might be preferred. The aorta is cross-clamped, and cold cardioplegia is instilled into the aortic root to achieve cardiac arrest.

When the approach is through the proximal chamber, an incision is made anterior to the right pulmonary veins (Fig. 31.6A), exactly as for mitral valve surgery (see "Technique of Operation" in Chapter 11). Once the chamber is opened, the obstructing membrane or diaphragm is visually delineated, and the opening(s) into the distal chamber identified. Carefully incising an aperture will promote exposure and assist with identifying the extent of the membrane so that it can be fully and safely removed (Fig. 31.6B). Most of the partition between the proximal and distal chamber is then excised to make as large an opening as possible (Fig. 31.6C). This procedure is usually easily and quickly done.

![](_page_6_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 31.6** Technique of operation, left-side approach. (A) When the proximal chamber is enlarged in older children and adults, incision is made into it on the right side behind the interatrial groove, as for mitral valve operations. (B) Anterior retraction of atrial septum exposes obstructing partition with its central aperture. Positions of the four pulmonary veins are located, noting proximity of left inferior pulmonary vein to lateral attachment of partition. Structures in the distal chamber are not visible. Excision of the obstructing partition is started by opening the aperture. Structures below the obstructing partition are identified as opening is enlarged. (C) Excision of obstructing partition proceeds to the left once its position relative to the left inferior pulmonary vein and mitral valve is clarified. Partition is completely excised, taking care not to penetrate free wall of left atrium. Foramen ovale and left atriotomy are closed to complete the repair.

Once the obstructing membrane has been excised, the orifices of all four pulmonary veins are clearly located, and the position of the interatrial septum identified. To allay any uncertainty regarding the atrial septum, the right atrium can be opened to visualize the septum's right atrial aspect, and if there is an atrial septal defect connecting with either proximal or distal chamber, the defect can be closed primarily or with a patch from this exposure.

When the approach is through the right atrium, it is opened, as in atrial septal defect closure. The atrial septum is examined, and the fossa ovalis identified. If a foramen ovale or atrial septal defect is present, this is enlarged by incision to gain access into the left-sided chamber with which it communicates, either proximal or distal. If there is no atrial septal defect, the septum primum within the fossa ovalis is incised. The partition between the proximal and distal chambers is then identified. In Fig. 31.7, the exposure provided by an atrial septal incision leading into the proximal chamber is shown (Fig. 31.7A). This approach makes the partition easy to identify and expose (Fig. 31.7B). The procedure described for the left-side approach is carried out, and the atrial septum opening is closed with a pericardial patch (Fig. 31.7C). After either approach, the cardiotomies are closed, careful deairing is performed, and the aortic cross-clamp is removed. Once fully rewarmed and with return of function, cardiopulmonary bypass is weaned, and the operation completed (see "Completing Cardiopulmonary Bypass" in Chapter 2).

![](_page_7_Picture_2.jpeg)

**Figure 31.7** Technique of operation, right-side approach. (A) When the proximal chamber is small and right atrium enlarged from communication of the proximal chamber directly with right atrium through a patent foramen ovale, or when the patient and heart are small, as in neonates and most infants, it is preferable to approach the obstructing partition through the right atrium. Standard cardiopulmonary bypass, two venous uptake cannulae, and caval tourniquets are usually employed, although a short period of hypothermic circulatory arrest may provide the best exposure in a neonate or small infant. Foramen ovale (atrial septal defect) is enlarged by excision of atrial septum to provide access to proximal and distal chambers. (B) Obstructing partition is seen as atrial septum is excised. A retractor placed anteriorly on the septum provides exposure for identifying the left inferior pulmonary vein and mitral valve. (C) Obstructing partition is completely excised. Atrial septum is repaired using a pericardial patch.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Atypical Cor Triatriatum Sinister</summary><div class="details-content">

When cor triatriatum sinister is complicated by other associated cardiac anomalies such as a persistent superior vena cava or various anomalies of pulmonary venous connection, the operative repair is considered that for atypical cor triatriatum sinister. Details of the surgical approach to each possible combination are not described here; however, a general approach is provided.

Before operation, the surgeon must determine with reasonable certainty the connections and drainage of all pulmonary and systemic veins, including the possible presence and connection of a left superior vena cava. In addition to echocardiography, diagnostic techniques, such as computerized tomographic and magnetic resonance imaging, and possibly cardiac catheterization and angiography, should be considered to best prepare for the operation. The possible presence of both a confluence of all (or some of) the pulmonary veins behind the heart *and* a proximal left atrial chamber should be investigated. All possible connections, through apertures in the dividing membrane and proximal chamber with both the distal chamber *and* right atrium, are determined. Initially, the largest "atrial" chamber appearing on the right side of the heart should be opened. If this is not the right atrium, the right atrium often should be opened subsequently to complete a thorough examination of the morphologic details, all of which must be verified at operation. The repair is usually some combination of the repair of typical cor triatriatum sinister, PAPVC, and TAPVC (see "Technique of Operation" in Chapters 29 and 30), and unroofed coronary sinus syndrome (see Chapter 29).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Cor Triatriatum Dexter</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgery is also the primary treatment for cor triatriatum dexter, especially in neonates or infants, whereas single atrial cannulation and deep hypothermic circulatory arrest is an option for the very small neonate or infant. After aortic occlusion and cardioplegic arrest, the right atrial free wall is opened, and the intraatrial anatomy assessed. The dividing right atrial membrane is easily noted between the coronary sinus and the tricuspid valve and may have a saccular or windsock character. The membrane is completely excised, protecting conduction tissue. The interatrial septum is carefully examined, and any defect is closed primarily or with a patch. The atriotomy is closed, and reperfusion, rewarming, and weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass proceed as usual.

In older children and adults, percutaneous dilation of the obstructed membrane and device closure of any atrial septal defect may be considered, but surgery is the preferred approach in most cases.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Special Features of Postoperative Care</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative care is as usual (see Chapter 4), with special attention given to support of the respiratory system, which may be compromised by pulmonary venous obstruction existing before operation. The possibility of a reactive pulmonary vascular bed sensitive to abrupt changes in ventilation must be recognized.

Atypical cor triatriatum sinister can also be successfully repaired. Many studies documented in Table 31.1 report successful repairs in patients in this more complex subset but also emphasize that mortality may be higher when the additional anomalies require repair.

Cor triatriatum dexter's greater rarity limits data on early outcomes following surgical repair; however, Kalangos and colleagues reviewed reported cases going back to 1963. They found no operative mortality in the 9 reported cases since 1980. Not included in that analysis was the case series reported by Mackman and colleagues in 2015 in which two neonates and one infant were surgically repaired without hospital mortality. Because the surgical repair of this rare lesion is straightforward, the hospital mortality should be near zero if the preoperative diagnosis is accurate.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Time-Related Survival and Functional Status</summary><div class="details-content">

Life expectancy after repair of classic cor triatriatum sinister approaches that of the general population, especially when the operation is done in infancy. In the experience at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto, Canada, between 1983 and 2005, Humpl and colleagues reported that survival in 46 patients after surgery was 94% at 3 months, 88% at 1 year, and 86% at 5 years. The main factor associated with mortality was the presence of a single ventricle. In the experience at Boston Children's Hospital from 1963 through 2010, Kazanci and colleagues reported on 65 patients with a median follow-up of 5.4 years, noting only one late death and that patient suffered from complex single ventricle disease. None of their patients required reintervention for recurrent left atrial obstruction, although six patients with pulmonary vein stenosis did require intervention. Follow-up studies after repair of cor triatriatum sinister suggest that most patients are either described as asymptomatic or are documented to be in a favorable New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class. In the Boston Children's study, 81% were in NYHA class I and 15% were in NYHA class II.

As with short-term outcomes, information regarding long-term outcomes for patients undergoing repair of cor triatriatum dexter is limited primarily to case reports. Among the reports included in this chapter, each surgical patient survived to discharge with little more than several weeks or months of follow-up available, and no reports of significant late problems have surfaced. Thus, in the absence of any additional complex cardiac anomaly, the surgical removal of an obstructing right atrial membrane and closure of any atrial septal defect should return the patient to normal cardiac anatomy, function, survival, and functional status.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Indications for Operation</summary><div class="details-content">

Classic cor triatriatum sinister presenting in early infancy with a restrictive aperture in the partition between the proximal and distal chambers is an urgent indication for operation, because 75% of patients with such malformations die in infancy. When older patients present with chronic symptoms, operation is also urgently indicated.

In atypical cor triatriatum sinister, when the proximal chamber opens into the right atrium, a restrictive opening or no opening is present between the proximal and distal chambers, and only a small patent foramen ovale exists between the right atrium and distal chamber, physiologic instability will be present. A large left-to-right shunt combined with restricted left atrial and left ventricular inflow produces severe symptoms during the early months of life, and operation is urgently indicated.

When cor triatriatum dexter creates symptomatic obstruction to right ventricular inflow and right-to-left shunting at the atrial level in the neonate or infant, operation is indicated. Operation is also indicated in older patients with similar findings that cannot be satisfactorily addressed percutaneously.
