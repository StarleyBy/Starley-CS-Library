#### **Definition**

The defining feature of hearts with an atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD) is the presence of a common atrioventricular junction that is guarded by a common atrioventricular valve with associated deficiency of the atrioventricular septum. <sup>1-3</sup> Abnormal development of the atrioventricular junction results in associated abnormalities of the left ventricular outflow tract, atrioventricular valve morphology, and position of the conduction tissue.

In addition to AVSD, these defects have also been called atrioventricular (AV) canal defects, AV defects, endocardial cushion defects, ostium primum atrial septal defects (when there is no interventricular communication), and common AV orifice (when there is only a single AV valve orifice). 1

#### **Historical Note**

#### Morphology

Abbott apparently recognized ostium primum atrial septal defect (ASD) and common AV canal defect, but it was Rogers and Edwards who in 1948 recognized their morphologic similarity. <sup>4,5</sup> This concept was further elaborated by Wakai and Edwards in 1956 and 1958. <sup>6,7</sup> The terms *partial* and *complete atrioventricular canal defects* were introduced by these investigators, who realized nonetheless that not all cases fit their definitions. During this period, Lev was formulating his concepts of ostium primum ASD (or partial AV canal) and common AV orifice (or complete AV canal), and he described the position of the AV node and bundle of His in these malformations. <sup>8</sup> Wakai and Edwards and later Bharati and Lev became dissatisfied with trying to compress all cases into two categories and added the terms *intermediate* and *transitional*. <sup>6,7,9</sup> During this period, Van Mierop's scholarly studies added a great deal of knowledge about the overall anatomic features of AV septal defects. <sup>10</sup>

By the early 1960s, surgical treatment of these defects provided a stimulus to further morphologic studies. In 1966, Rastelli and colleagues at the Mayo Clinic described in more detail the morphology of AV valve leaflets in cases with common AV orifice. <sup>11</sup> The error made in this study was to compress into the *common anterior leaflet* designation a leaflet that was in fact divided in two by a commissure (i.e., the divided common anterior leaflet of type A).

The description of AV valve leaflets by Rastelli and colleagues was accepted for some years, but in 1976 a publication by Ugarte and colleagues emphasized the idea of leaflets *bridging* the ventricular septum, a concept also held by Lev. <sup>12</sup> Meanwhile, based on anatomic and cineangiographic studies and in accordance with the description of Baron and colleagues and Van Mierop and colleagues, it was recognized in the late 1960s that the basic defect in these malformations was *absence of the AV septum*. <sup>10,13,14</sup> These concepts were further expanded by Piccoli and colleagues under the direction of Anderson, who further emphasized that all the variations of the defect were part of a spectrum (Fig. 32.1). <sup>15</sup>

#### **Surgical Treatment**

In 1951 at the University of Minnesota Hospital in Minneapolis, after a long period of laboratory investigation, Dennis and Varco attempted a cardiac operation in a human using a pump-oxygenator for the first time. The preoperative diagnosis was ASD, and at operation the defect was thought to be closed. The patient died, and autopsy showed the true diagnosis to be partial AV septal defect. The first successful repair of a complete AV septal defect was performed by Lillehei and colleagues in 1954, using cross-circulation and direct suture of the atrial rim of the septal defect to the crest of the ventricular septum. In 1954, Kirklin and colleagues successfully repaired a partial AV septal defect through the atrial well of Watkins and Gross, and in 1955 began repairing AV septal defects by open cardiotomy and use of the pump-oxygenator.

Early experiences with complete AV septal defects were all associated with a high hospital mortality, often related to complete heart block, postrepair left AV valve regurgitation, or creation of subaortic stenosis. <sup>22-24</sup> Interestingly, in many of these early operations, a two-patch technique was used (see "Two-Patch Repair Technique" under Technique of Operation later in this chapter). In 1958, Lev's description of the location of the bundle of His provided the basis for repair techniques that avoid heart block. In 1959, Dubost and Blondeau reported their early experience and emphasized that the "cleft" (zone of apposition; ZoA) in the "mitral leaflet" need not be sutured in repairing partial AV septal defects, a concept that is today largely refuted, although not uniformly. <sup>25</sup> In 1962, Maloney and colleagues described two cases in which a single patch was used to close both defects and with the

# **Morphology and Embryology**

#### **Embryology of Atrioventricular Septal Defects**

Septation of the common AV canal into right and left components in the developing heart with corresponding formation of separate AV valves was historically attributed to, and substantial insight has been gained into, the complexity of the process underlying AV junction formation based on elucidation of molecular signalling, cellular mechanisms, and detailed embryologic anatomy of the developing heart. <sup>59,60</sup> It is now understood to be a highly complex process involving structures referred to as the AV mesenchymal complex that include the mesenchymal cap of the septum primum, the ventral (superior) and dorsal (inferior) major endocardial cushions, and the vestibular spine (dorsal mesenchymal protrusion). All of these structures contribute to septation of the AV junction, whereas the major endocardial cushions in concert with the smaller

lateral endocardial cushions contribute to formation of the AV valve leaflets.62,63 The vestibular spine is formed by ventral ingrowth of extracardiac mesenchyme derived from the secondary heart field64 and is a key contributor to formation of the normal AV junction and atrial septation.65 Evidence currently suggests that deficiency of the extracardiac mesenchyme contributing to the vestibular spine and reduced ventral protrusion of the vestibular spine contribute to the pathogenesis of AVSD, persistence of the ostium primum,66 and failure of fusion of the endocardial cushions.67

#### **Nomenclature**

Historically, hearts with this anatomy have been referred to as having an AV canal defect, and this terminology is still used by some. While the term "AV canal" is consistent with the fundamental morphologic defect (see later), the more precise and literal term "atrioventricular septal defect" is now preferred.68

AVSD accounts for 3% of all cardiac malformations, and 75% of AVSDs are complete. The International Pediatric and Congenital Cardiac Code classifies AVSD into four main groups: partial (or incomplete), intermediate (or transitional), complete, and unbalanced (www.IPCCC.net).69,70 Thus, the spectrum of AV septal defects is categorized based on the presence and size of the ventricular septal defect (VSD) together with the configuration of the AV valves (a common orifice vs. two orifices). Partial AV septal defects, also known as primum ASDs, are characterized by absence of a VSD with two separate AV valve orifices created by fusion of the AV valve leaflets to the crest of the interventricular septum. Intermediate or transitional AV septal defects are defined by the presence of a shallow, restrictive VSD and a common AV valve that is divided into left and right orifices by what is usually a thin bridging ribbon of valvar tissue (Fig. 32.2).98 Complete AV septal defects are characterized by a large unrestrictive interventricular communication beneath a common AV valve. There are several variations to these three basic subtypes of AVSD relating to abnormalities of the interatrial septum, variations of AV valvar and subvalvar anatomy, morphology of the septal defects, as well as commitment of the AV valve components to the respective ventricles, which will be reviewed in further detail in subsequent sections. All of these subtypes may be either balanced or unbalanced forms of AVSD. The following content is in specific reference to balanced forms of AVSD. Unbalanced atrioventricular septal defect (uAVSD) encompasses a wide range of morphologic abnormalities that are described and considered separately at the conclusion of this chapter.

# **General Morphologic Characteristics**

The main anatomic feature of the AVSD is a deficiency of the AV septum. This is a portion of the membranous septum that lies between the left ventricle and the right atrium, created by the attachment of the mitral valve at a slightly higher level (cephalad) than the tricuspid valve. In patients with AVSD, the mitral and tricuspid valves attach to the septum at the same level. AV septal defects have as defining characteristics a deficiency or absence of the AV septum, resulting in an ostium primum defect immediately above the AV valves and a deficiency (or scooped-out area) in the inlet (basal) portion of the ventricular septum immediately below the AV valves.3,68,71

AV septal defects comprise a spectrum of malformations. At one end is the simplest type, in which there is an interatrial communication but no interventricular communication and a connection of variable width between the LSL and LIL; this is

called a partial AV septal defect or ostium primum defect. At the other end of the spectrum is the most extreme form, with large deficiencies in atrial and ventricular septa, a common AV valve orifice, and large interatrial and interventricular communications; this is called a complete AV septal defect. Because a continuous spectrum of gradations lies between these extremes, some anomalies have been described as intermediate or transitional AV septal defects, although from a strictly morphologic perspective, there is no difference between any of the varieties of AVSD (partial, transitional, or complete). # **Atrial Septal Deficiency and Variations of Interatrial Communications with Partial, Transitional, and Complete AVSD**

*Primum ASD.* Usually there is an interatrial communication related to *deficiency of the AV septum,* the so-called *ostium primum ASD* (Fig. 32.6). The defect is bounded below by the inferiorly displaced AV valve leaflets and above by a crescentic ridge of atrial septum that fuses with the AV valve anulus only at its extremities.

Generally, there is little atrial septal tissue at the superior point of fusion of the atrial septum with the valve anulus adjacent to the aorta, but more tissue is usually present inferiorly adjacent to the coronary sinus (Fig. 32.7). The distance between the crescentic atrial margin of the defect and the AV valves (and thus the size of the interatrial communication) is variable. In most cases, the fossa ovalis is normally formed and there is a patent foramen ovale or an associated secundum ASD. Usually the interatrial communication through the ostium primum defect is moderate in size. When the interatrial communication is small, the atrial septal deficiency is restricted to the area normally occupied by the AV septum.79 The communication may be still smaller due to fusion of the base of the LSL or LIL to the edge of the adjacent portion of the atrial septum. Rarely, there may be an accessory "parachute" of fibrous tissue that narrows or obstructs the defect. Under such circumstances, a pressure difference exists between the two atria.

*Common Atrium.* Deficiencies in the anterior limbus or fossa ovalis may be associated with AV septal defects, resulting in a larger interatrial communication. Occasionally the entire limbus and fossa ovalis are absent, along with the AV septum. The condition is then termed *common atrium.*80-83

# **Ventricular Septal Deficiency and Interventricular Communications**

*Partial Atrioventricular Septal Defect.* Some degree of deficiency of the inlet portion of the ventricular septum immediately beneath the AV valves is a constant finding even when there is no interventricular communication. Thus, the inlet portion of the ventricular septum is shortened. There is usually no interventricular communication when the LSL and LIL are connected and attached to the downwardly displaced crest of the septum throughout its length (Fig. 32.8; see also Fig. 32.5), a situation described as a *partial AV septal or primum atrial septal defect.*

*Intermediate or Transitional Atrioventricular Septal Defect.* A restrictive deficiency of the inlet component of the ventricular septum beneath the AV valves combined with two distinct AV valve orifices within the common AV junction defines an intermediate or transitional AVSD. One or several small interventricular communications are present beneath the attachments of the AV valve to the septum (Fig. 32.8B) resulting in a shallow, restrictive VSD.

*Complete Atrioventricular Septal Defect.* When the ventricular septal deficiency is substantial, a moderate or large unrestrictive interventricular communication is present, and the LSL and LIL are separate, thus forming a common AV valve. #### **Atrioventricular Valves**

Consequent to the deficient septation of the AV junction seen throughout the spectrum of AVSDs, AV valve morphology is in no way comparable to that in a normally septated heart with a normally developed AV septum. As briefly described earlier, a common AV valve that usually has five leaflet components forms over the common AV junction. Attachments of the AV valves to the crest of the ventricular septum in partial and intermediate AV septal defects, as well as their chordal attachments in complete AV septal defects, are displaced toward the apex of the heart because of deficiency of the inlet portion of the septum. This alters orientation of the AV orifices relative to the aortic orifice (i.e., the aortic valve is no longer wedged between the AV valves) and eliminates normal off-setting of the right and left AV valves. This lack of off-setting is an echocardiographic hallmark of AVSD.14,89,90

*Two Atrioventricular Valve Orifices.* Typically when two AV valve orifices are present, as in partial AV septal defects, the LSL and LIL are joined together to a variable extent anteriorly by leaflet tissue near the crest of the ventricular septum (see Figs. 32.1 and 32.5). Consequently, the left AV valve is trifoliate, being composed of the LSL, LIL, and left lateral (LLL) or mural leaflet, and is oriented differently than the normal mitral valve (see Figs. 32.1 and 32.2).73,91 Specifically, the LSL and LIL components together occupy two-thirds or more of the circumference of the left AV valve orifice, whereas the LLL or mural leaflet accounts for the remaining one-third or less of the circumference, a ratio that is the proportional inverse to that of a normal mitral valve.68,92 Commissures exist between the left superior and mural leaflets and between the left inferior and mural leaflets and are usually supported by respective papillary muscles. These commissures are positionally very different than a normal mitral valve, being posteriorly displaced and much closer together as they bound the margins of the base of the narrower LLL. Therefore, their position is defined by the width of the LLL, which is variable. The ZoA or "cleft" of the left AV valve resides between the LSL and LIL. The connection between the LSL and LIL may be only a thin strand of tissue (complete cleft), but more commonly it is 2 to 4 mm or more deep. This connection, too, is usually fused to the crest of the ventricular septum in partial AV septal defects. Occasionally, chordae pass from opposing edges of the LSL and LIL to the muscular ventricular septum beneath.93 The chordae that originate from the central edges of the LSL and LIL attach to different papillary muscles, which can cause a distracting force on the leaflets during closure at the ZoA. This contrasts with the normal commissure in which the chordae from adjacent leaflet edges attach to a single papillary muscle, encouraging coaptation. Rarely, separation into LSL and LIL is represented only by a notch in the center of the free edge of a nearly normal "anterior mitral leaflet." The LLL is usually smaller than the other two leaflets and is triangular (Fig 32.9).94 The morphology of the LLL including surface area and leaflet tip angle is variable and importantly contributes to left AV function in AVSDs.95 Rarely, the LLL can be absent, which may negate the ability to close the zone of opposition between the LSL and LIL.96,97

In aggregate, these left AV valve leaflet anomalies may make the valve regurgitant to a variable degree, sometimes severely. When LSL and LIL are nearly completely separated, an appreciable gap may occur during systole, producing regurgitation. When there is failure of valve coaptation at this site, leaflet tissue forming the margin usually becomes thickened and rolled. In other cases, regurgitation appears to be due to deficiency of leaflet tissue, particularly in the LIL,98,99 which may contain an accessory cleft. The mechanism of severe left AV valve regurgitation is, however, not evident in some cases. Rarely, the left or right AV valve is stenotic, but this usually is associated with hypoplasia of the corresponding ventricle.100

The right AV valve is also abnormal when there are two AV orifices, although less attention has been paid to it. It may consist of three leaflets—right superior leaflet (RSL), right lateral leaflet (RLL), and right inferior leaflet (RIL)—or of two or four leaflets (see Figs. 32.1 and 32.2). Leaflet tissue attached directly or by chordae to the crest or right side of the crest of the septum, and thus contributing to closure of the right AV valves, is considered to represent bridging of the LSL or LIL (see Fig. 32.1).

Usually in cases without an interventricular communication, the LSL does not bridge at all (previously, this finding was interpreted as absence or hypoplasia of the superior part of the tricuspid septal leaflet) and the LIL bridges moderately (see Fig. 32.2A). Even with abnormalities of the right AV valve, it is unusual to see significant regurgitation.

This variability formed the basis for the classification by Rastelli and colleagues into types A, B, and C.11 Chordal attachments of the right ventricular extremity of the LSL vary according to degree of bridging (Fig. 32.11).101 When there is no bridging, chordal attachments are to the ventricular crest (Rastelli A; Fig. 32.11A). When the LSL bridges the septum moderately or markedly and extends into the right ventricle, it is usually unattached to the underlying ventricular crest (free-floating), but it may occasionally be attached by chordae (tethered).

The LIL typically bridges moderately, but it, too, varies in this respect. It is not uncommon for a bridging LIL to be attached to the underlying ventricular crest either completely or by short, thick chordae with interchordal spaces.

Chordal attachments of the leftward components of the common AV valve in the left ventricle may be relatively normal, but variation in chordal number and distribution is a consistent feature of hearts with AVSD.47 In addition, chordal attachments to the interventricular septum are frequently present. Leaflet and subvalvar abnormalities can exist that result in commissural deficiencies (particularly diminished or absent LIL-mural commissure) and/or a single dominant papillary muscle or more closely spaced papillary muscles than usual.96 The leftward components of the LSL or LIL may have secondary or tertiary chordal attachments to the ventricular septum that may contribute to later development of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction after AVSD repair. There may be only one papillary muscle, producing a parachute-type valve that is difficult to repair.47 This was true in 7 of 53 (13%) cases in the GLH autopsy series, in 14% of the specimens described by David and colleagues, and in 4% of 155 surgical cases reported by Ilbawi and colleagues.102,103 Abnormalities of the subvalvar apparatus and hypoplasia or deficiency of LLL can contribute to left AV valve dysfunction following AVSD repair.95,104 The right ventricular portion of the common AV valve has superior, lateral, and inferior leaflets, but as in partial AV septal defects, they vary considerably in number and size (see Fig. 32.2). When bridging of the LSL is absent or mild, the RSL is large, whereas with more extensive bridging, it is smaller.101

The mechanism of the regurgitation is likely to be multifactorial and may include valvar dysplasia and left heart dilation due to volume-loading from the VSD shunt.

Anatomic studies by Kanani and colleagues have emphasized the marked valvar abnormalities in this malformation, not only of the anular component but also of the subvalvar apparatus (with deficiency of chordal arrangement) and leaflet tissue (which is often deficient in coaptation surface and pliability following repair).105

#### **Ventricles**

In AVSD, the inflow portion of the left ventricle is foreshortened due to the downward displacement of the LAVV (see "General Morphologic Characteristics" under Morphology, earlier in this chapter). The left ventricle will often appear smaller than the right ventricle due to left-to-right shunting and resultant right ventricular enlargement; however, absolute volumetrics should generally be within normal limits in balanced AVSD. LV hypoplasia is usually associated with an unevenly distributed AV valve complex. This is referred to as right dominant unbalanced AVSD and is addressed later in this chapter (see Special Situations and Controversies).

The right ventricle has no specific anomalies but is usually enlarged secondary to the left-to-right shunt. It, too, may be frankly hypoplastic with associated maldistribution of the AV valve, in which case the arrangement is referred to as left dominant unbalanced AVSD.

#### **Left Ventricular Outflow Tract**

The LV outflow tract is characteristically elongated and narrowed (Fig. 32.13) in all types of AV septal defect (see "General Morphologic Characteristics" under Morphology earlier in this chapter).

#### **Septal Malalignment**

Septal malalignment may refer to either the atrial or ventricular septum. True atrial septal malalignment is unusual in balanced AVSD but quite common in unbalanced AVSD.

In a single center retrospective analysis of 31 patients with AVSD including 22 patients undergoing biventricular repair, Ahmad and colleagues described the angle formed by the axis of the atrial and ventricular septa (termed the AV septal angle). In the 22 patients undergoing biventricular repair, the mean AV angle was 7.4 1/- 11.1 degrees, whereas in patients requiring univentricular repair it was 28.6 1/- 3.04 degrees (*P* , .0002) using multiplanar three dimensional datasets.121 Consequently, the AV septal angle provides useful information to guide assessment for ventricular hypoplasia and imbalance when assessing complete AVSD anatomy.

# **Left Ventricular Outflow or Inflow Obstruction**

Important LV outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO) occurs rarely in unoperated AVSD, unless severely unbalanced right dominant AVSD is present.76,78,122-124 It most often becomes apparent in the postrepair setting, occurring in about 5% of AVSD patients at a mean interval of 5 to 7 years following primary repair.125-127 It is surprising that it is not more frequent, in view of the elongation and narrowing of this area in affected hearts.128,129 The incidence of LVOTO after AVSD repair is similar in patients with partial and complete AVSD.

As reviewed previously, the elongation and narrowing of the LVOT results from multiple morphologic inputs including "unwedging" of the aortic root, discrepancy between the inflow and outflow septal lengths which is a consequence of the downward displacement of the AV valve and a more extensive area of direct fibrous continuity between the aortic valve and the LSL than is present normally between the aortic and mitral valves.130 Short, thick chordae may anchor the LSL to the crest of the ventricular septum thereby crowding the distal LVOT.131 The anterolateral

*Patent Ductus Arteriosus.* A patent ductus arteriosus is present in about 10% of patients with AV septal defects. It is particularly common in those with an interventricular communication and more common in patients with Trisomy 21.135

*Tetralogy of Fallot.* Typical tetralogy of Fallot is present in about 5% of patients with complete AV septal defects, and about 1% of patients with tetralogy of Fallot have associated complete AV septal defects.44,102,103 The LSL bridges markedly and is freefloating over the crest of the ventricular septum, and the interventricular communication beneath it is large and juxtaaortic.136,137 The LIL is undivided and attached to the septal crest by thickened, short chordae in about half of cases.138 Rarely the LSL and LIL are connected by a fibrous (or valvar) band, beneath which also is a large interventricular communication. The right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) has typical tetralogy morphology (see Chapter 34) that may be so severe that pulmonary atresia is present. Anterior malalignment of the conal septum and varying degrees of aortic override make surgical repair challenging.138 Patients with significant early cyanosis from RVOT obstruction may benefit from interim palliation with an additional source of pulmonary blood flow to enable staging to repair at an adequate size and age to deal with these anatomic challenges.

*Double Outlet Right Ventricle.* Double outlet right ventricle (DORV) without pulmonary stenosis complicates complete AV septal defect in about 2% of cases.11,78,139-141 As in tetralogy of Fallot, deficiency of the ventricular septum is typically large and juxtaaortic beneath the extensively bridging and free-floating LSL. However, occasionally the interventricular communication is remote from the aortic and pulmonary valves and may therefore be described as noncommitted.140,142 Rarely, Taussig-Bing type of DORV is present.139,141 DORV combined with severe pulmonary stenosis coexists with complete AV septal defects in about 1% of cases.78 These combinations of DORV and AV septal defect with large interventricular communication frequently are associated with heterotaxy syndrome with varying combinations of atrial isomerism, thoracic and/or abdominal situs inversus, common atrium, left superior vena cava to an unroofed coronary sinus, other systemic venous anomalies, and/or total anomalous pulmonary venous connection.141,143 While many of these hearts are amenable to complex biventricular repair, univentricular palliation may be necessary in particularly challenging substrates. The most important anatomic factor in this decision is the location of the VSD relative to the aorta, and the impact of that relationship on the geometry of the left ventricular outflow tract and right ventricular volume postrepair. Severe ventricular imbalance, usually with RV dominance, is frequent in heterotaxy-associated DORV with complete AVSD and severe pulmonary stenosis and requires univentricular palliation.

*Transposition of the Great Arteries.* Very rarely, transposition of the great arteries (discordant ventriculoarterial connection) is associated with an AVSD.139

*Truncus Arteriosus.* Though exceedingly rare, truncus arteriosus can occur in the setting of AVSD, and the published experience is largely limited to case reports.144-146

*Left Superior Vena Cava.* Some have suggested that a left superior vena cava affects the inflow physiology of hearts with AVSD, although this has not been definitively established.147 Certainly, the presence of an left superior vena cava impairs visualization of the LAVV and can thereby make valve repair considerably more difficult148

Unroofed coronary sinus with persistent left superior vena cava (see Chapter 29) to the top of the left atrium occurs in about 3% of patients and is often associated with a common atrium.111,149 When complete AV septal defect is associated with persistent left superior vena cava and unroofed coronary sinus, atrial isomerism is also frequent (see Chapter 53).150

# **Pulmonary Vascular Disease**

In partial AV septal defects, as in other types of ASDs, pulmonary vascular disease is uncommon, whereas in complete AV septal defects, as with large VSDs, the risk of pulmonary vascular disease begins to rise by 6 months of age and progresses.151,152 Morphologically, pulmonary vascular disease associated with complete AV septal defects is similar to that associated with large VSDs (see "Pulmonary Vascular Disease" under Morphology in Section I of Chapter 33). However, it tends to progress more rapidly in patients with complete AV septal defects. Correlation between histologic findings and pulmonary vascular resistance is similar in the two conditions. The pulmonary vascular changes are more frequent and can occur as early as 6 months of age in patients with Down syndrome with complete AV septal defects compared with patients without Down syndrome.153-155

# **Down Syndrome**

Down syndrome is rare in patients with partial AV septal defects but common (50%-75%) in those with complete AV septal defects.156 While advanced pulmonary vascular disease is more frequent in patients with Trisomy 21, left-sided obstructive lesions are 10 times less common in such patients, and other associated anomalies are probably also less common.73

#### **Inlet Septal Type of Ventricular Septal Defect**

It is important to note that an isolated inlet (AV septal) type of VSD (see "Morphology" in Section I of Chapter 33) occurs without any of the features of an AV septal defect as defined in this chapter, except that it involves the inflow portion of the ventricular septum beneath the septal tricuspid valve leaflet and usually also the area of the membranous ventricular septum. The AV septum, however, is intact, and the mitral and tricuspid annuli and aortic orifice lie in normal positions with normal off-setting of the AV valves. This feature allows these VSDs to be readily differentiated echocardiographically and angiographically from AV septal defects. Interestingly, in isolated inlet VSD, the anterior mitral leaflet is often cleft.

#### **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

#### **Prenatal/Fetal Diagnosis**

In the contemporary era with access to standard pregnancy care, most congenital heart defects are detected prenatally by screening morphologic ultrasound and confirmed by dedicated fetal echocardiography. Diagnosis can be made between 18 and 24 weeks of gestation but is also possible as early as 11 to 14 weeks and includes all forms of AVSD with complete AVSD being the most straightforward to detect. The detection rate for AVSDs with obstetric ultrasound is approximately 43%.157 The four-chamber obstetric ultrasound view is optimal for detection with the ability to screen for absence of the cardiac crux, absent differential insertion of the AV valves, and defects in the atrial and/or ventricular septum at the crux cordis.158 At least 50% of fetuses with complete AVSD will have associated cardiac and noncardiac anomalies and 60% will have associated Trisomy 21. Multiple other chromosomal abnormalities including trisomy 18 and 13 are also associated with complete AVSD.158 In cases of unbalanced complete AVSD, 25% to 50% will have associated heterotaxy syndrome. For cases of complete AVSD, around 30% of parents will elect to terminate the pregnancy and there is a significant rate of fetal demise, still birth and neonatal death, particularly for complex AVSD associated with other cardiac and/or extracardiac anomalies.159

The mechanisms of AV valve regurgitation in AVSD may be related to chamber and anular enlargement due to chronic leftto-right shunt, exacerbated by valve regurgitation. However, the far more common etiology is related to the structure of the AV valve itself. Structural attributes associated with preoperative regurgitation include leaflet dysplasia, deficient leaflet surface area and resulting shallow or absent leaflet coaptation, anomalies of chordal length and/or insertion, and papillary muscle abnormalities restricting leaflet motion. In patients with partial AV septal defects and important left AV valve regurgitation, for instance, the LIL is commonly severely hypoplastic.

#### **Symptoms and Physical Findings**

Patients without an interventricular communication (partial AV septal defect) and with absent or mild left AV valve regurgitation often have minimal symptoms and their clinical presentation is virtually identical to that of patients with secundum ASD (see "Fossa Ovalis Defect" under Morphology in Chapter 29). If there is audible left AV valve regurgitation, then the patient may have an apical systolic/holosystolic murmur which radiates toward the axilla. The electrocardiogram (ECG) is quite distinct, characterized by the presence of a superior axis directed more toward the left and commonly with signs of RV volume overload such as rsR9 or rR9. 162,163 Patients with moderate or severe left AV valve regurgitation with partial AV septal defects may be symptomatic earlier, and such patients may develop congestive heart failure and require surgical repair in infancy.37 In these patients with heart failure, in addition to the usual signs of ASD with a wide and fixed split S2, the heart is more active in association with a loud apical pansystolic murmur, mid-diastolic murmur (relative AV valve stenosis), apical displacement of apical impulse, and possible S3 gallop rhythm. Tachypnea, tachycardia, sweating while feeding, failure to thrive, and hepatomegaly can also be present.

In patients with a large interventricular communication (complete AV septal defect), symptoms of pulmonary overcirculation develop usually before the age of 6 months. Typically, left-to-right shunting is minimal in the first few weeks of life, becoming more pronounced at 4 to 8 weeks as pulmonary vascular resistance falls. Tachypnea and failure to thrive emerge as congestive failure physiology supervenes. Clinical signs and symptoms are similar to those of patients with isolated VSD. The larger the VSD, the softer the systolic murmur, but a systolic murmur can be heard at the left sternal border with a mid-diastolic rumble often heard in the apex. In patients with important AV valve regurgitation, a systolic murmur with radiation toward the axillae is present. Tachypnea, tachycardia, sweating with feeds, failure to thrive, and hepatomegaly can be present. Occasionally, heart failure symptomatology is minimal or absent and the patient has good weight gain. Chest radiograph will characteristically show clear lung fields, a relatively normal heart size, and prominent pulmonary vasculature at the hila. This clinical picture is a marker of elevated pulmonary vascular resistance and should prompt preoperative evaluation for pulmonary vascular disease, including either noninvasive or catheterization-based assessment of pulmonary vascular resistance and reactivity. It is extremely rare in the developed world, but when presentation is delayed until some years later, there is frequently severe pulmonary vascular disease and Eisenmenger complex (see "Clinical Findings" under Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria in Section I of Chapter 33).

In those patients with morphology intermediate between the partial and complete AV septal defects, clinical features depend on the size of the interventricular communication and severity of left AV valve regurgitation. In particular, the auscultatory examination will feature a higher-pitched and louder murmur as the VSD becomes more restrictive.

In the current era, infants with Trisomy 21 routinely undergo screening echocardiography. If diagnosed in utero with Trisomy 21, then a fetal echocardiogram is indicated and can rule out AVSD. However, for subtle and smaller defects, postnatal echocardiography is still indicated. Given this current approach, the vast majority of infants with Trisomy 21 and AVSD are diagnosed prior to becoming symptomatic. It should be noted that patients with Trisomy 21 are at higher risk of developing pulmonary vascular disease with unrepaired intracardiac shunts.

#### **Chest Radiograph**

In patients with partial AVSD without important left AV valve regurgitation, the chest radiograph is the same as in other large ASDs with right heart enlargement and increased pulmonary vascular markings. When moderate or severe left AV valve regurgitation is present, the radiograph usually shows marked cardiomegaly with evidence of cardiac chamber enlargement (can be LA/LV and/or RA/RV depending on the degree of MR and shunt size at the atrial level) and pulmonary plethora.

In complete AVSD, cardiomegaly and pulmonary plethora are evident in infants and young children presenting with heart failure. In patients who have elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, the heart is less enlarged, central pulmonary arteries are large, and lung fields can be relatively clear.

# **Electrocardiogram**

ECG findings are rather specific. The most characteristic feature of the ECG in AVSD is a superior QRS axis directed more toward the left, although this has been noted by several investigators to be variable and affected by the underlying anatomic substrate.164,165 The PR interval is frequently prolonged. Partial right bundle branch block with rsR9 and RV enlargement is also typical. A superior axis of the P wave is seen in left atrial isomerism.162,166

#### **Echocardiography**

Echocardiography is, today, the gold standard for characterization of essentially all aspects of AVSD anatomy. In the majority of cases, it is the only diagnostic modality (with the exception of standard chest radiograph (CXR) and ECG) required prior to definitive repair. Many sophisticated and nuanced echocardiographic measures have been introduced and refined over the last decade and are beyond the scope of this chapter. However, there are well-established fundamental echocardiographic features of AVSD.167,168 These include the following:

- Loss of normal off-setting of AV valves (absence of the AV septum)
- Abnormal configuration of the AV valves
- Trifoliate appearance of the left AV valve (so-called "cleft")
- Unwedged or "sprung" aortic valve
- Left ventricular inlet-outlet disproportion (so-called "gooseneck deformity")
- Abnormal position of the left ventricular papillary muscles (counterclockwise rotation)

The four-chamber view clearly demonstrates the loss of normal off-setting of the AV valves. That is, the valvar complex lies in the same plane rightward and leftward of the interventricular septum, as opposed to the slight cephalad displacement of the mitral valve in normal hearts. This finding is true regardless of whether a VSD is present or not and is essentially diagnostic of AVSD. Also in this view, chordal attachments and degree of leaflet bridging can be assessed. The elongated left ventricular outflow tract and unwedged position of the aortic valve are best characterized in the precordial long-axis view, although unwedging is also appreciated on the fourchamber view. Valvar abnormalities such as leaflet dysplasia, accessory "clefts," location of regurgitant jets, and papillary muscle abnormalities require skill and experience to precisely identify. By using a thorough combination of parasternal and subcostal shortaxis and long-axis sweeps, in addition to four- and five-chamber views, many, if not all, of these important features can be identified. The degree of AV valve regurgitation can be evaluated with the use of color flow Doppler. Double orifice left AV valve may be challenging to image, particularly in hearts with a large ventricular septal defect (VSD), but is best seen in short-axis views. Using the subcostal 30-degree left anterior oblique (LAO) view, the degree of commitment of the common AV orifice to the right and left ventricles can be ascertained. Other measures of unbalance are also obtained by echocardiography (see "Unbalanced Atrioventricular Septal Defect" under Special Situations and Controversies). Likewise, echocardiography can be used to diagnose associated malformations such as tetralogy of Fallot, double outlet right ventricle, transposition of the great arteries, and additional VSDs.

A patent ductus arteriosus can be identified by echocardiography unless pulmonary vascular resistance is elevated and leftto-right shunting thereby minimized. For this reason, empiric ligation of the PDA or ligamentum arteriosum is a reasonable practice at the time of AVSD repair.

While patients suspected of having pulmonary vascular disease and elevated pulmonary vascular resistance should undergo preoperative catheterization for proper assessment (see "Cardiac Catheterization" later), echocardiography can also be employed to assess this possibility. In the setting of unrepaired VSD, the direction and magnitude of ventricular level shunting (right to left, bidirectional, left to right) with and without oxygen and/or inhaled nitric oxide can be ascertained. Evaluation of the pulmonary regurgitation velocity and presence of notching in the pulmonary arteries can also be clues to elevated pulmonary artery pressures, as are interventricular septal position, PR velocity, and presence of RV hypertrophy. Using continuous wave Doppler across the tricuspid valve to assess peak tricuspid regurgitation (TR) velocity, right ventricular pressure is easily measured in the septated heart (postrepair or prerepair in hearts with absent or restrictive interventricular communication). This can be particularly useful in the postrepair setting wherein pulmonary hypertension may occur suddenly and unexpectedly.

#### **Cardiac Catheterization**

Atrial pressure, right and left ventricular systolic and diastolic pressure, and the presence or absence of pressure gradients across AV valves and/or outflow tracts, arterial and venous saturations, direction and magnitude of shunting, pulmonary and systemic pressures, resistances, and relative flows can all be measured and calculated from data obtained at cardiac catheterization.169 These studies are now required only when major cardiac anomalies coexist or when operability is questioned because of clinical evidence of pulmonary vascular disease, particularly with late presenting cases.

Anatomic angiocardiographic features of AV septal defects were well described by Baron and colleagues in 1964 and further refined by the work of Brandt and colleagues, Bargeron and colleagues, and Macartney and colleagues13,14,90,170,171 in the era prior to development of echocardiography. These are well portrayed by the line drawings of Baron and colleagues and representative cineangiograms (Figs. 32.14 through 32.16).13 Detailed cineangiography is now largely of historic interest, being mostly supplanted by enormous advances in echocardiocardiography and, more recently, advanced cardiac imaging.172-174

# **Special Situations and Associated Defects**

Presence of *common atrium* generally can be identified preoperatively by echocardiography and shows nearly complete absence of both atrial and AV septum (Fig. 32.17). Finding mild arterial desaturation in a patient with clinical findings of an AV septal defect, but without an interventricular communication or pulmonary artery hypertension, suggests presence of common atrium.82,83,175,176 Presence of a left superior vena cava in such a setting suggests both unroofed coronary sinus syndrome and common atrium, because they frequently coexist.83,149 In patients with common atrium and atrial isomerism, even more complex associations occur including DORV, partial or total anomalous pulmonary venous connection, and azygos extension of the inferior vena cava (see "Morphology" in Chapter 53).109,143,177,178

It is essential to recognize functionally important *LVOTO*. The distinctive geometry results from the disproportionate lengths of the inflow and outflow axis of the left ventricle and anterior displacement of the left AV valve complex (see "Morphology" previously) (Fig. 32.18).179 It rarely results in preoperative LVOTO in balanced AVSD. Instead, and in the vast majority of cases, important obstruction only becomes evident postoperatively, early or late. It is typically multifactorial, secondary to the elongated LVOT, abnormal or accessory valvar tissue or insertions, as well as discrete fibromuscular obstruction (see "Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction" later). Infrequently, it occurs as a result of the ventricular patch component pulling the left AV valve apically and anteriorly such that the left AV valve apparatus narrows the outflow tract. Thus, the initial repair must place the left AV valve at the appropriate level: caudad toward the apex may result in LV outflow tract obstruction; cephalad toward the atria may result in left AV valve regurgitation. When the former situation occurs, either in the setting of no or a very small interventricular communication or as a result of repair, resuspension of the LSL after patch closure of a created or enlarged VSD has been described as a technical approach to relief of LVOTO.131,180,181

Predictors of postoperative LVOTO have been sought by many investigators. Evidence is mixed with regard to the impact of subtype of AVSD (partial, transitional, or complete) on the incidence of LVOTO after repair. Most recent series report similar reoperation rates among AVSD subtypes.126,182,183 Similarly, Rastelli type A configuration of the common AV valve has been widely understood to predispose to LVOTO after AVSD repair compared with types B and C, but data from the recent literature do not support this view.126,182,184 However, there are data to suggest that a number of specific echocardiographic metrics are associated with the development of LVOTO after AVSD repair. # **Technique of Operation**

#### **Technical Overview**

The goals of AVSD repair are to septate the heart and establish two normally functioning AV valves while maintaining AV conduction and normal function of the AV node. Surgical treatment of AV septal defects is thereby directed toward (1) closing the interatrial communication, which is virtually always present; (2) closing the interventricular communication if one is present; (3) avoiding damage to the AV node and bundle of His; and (4) maintaining or creating two competent, nonstenotic AV valves.

Repair techniques vary considerably, but when used properly, all can provide good results.87,181,198-200 For example:

- One or two patches may be used to repair the malformation when there is large interventricular communication.27
- A markedly bridging LSL may be divided to facilitate the repair, or it may be left intact.29,78,201
- The AV node and bundle of His may be avoided by staying on the LV and left atrial side of the interatrial septum (known as the McGoon technique),29 by staying on the right side of the septum and deviating the suture line inferiorly toward the coronary sinus,78,202 or a hybrid of these two strategies.203
- Contiguous surfaces of the LSL and LIL may be sutured together, or the left AV valve may be left as a tricuspid structure if it is competent at baseline.20,47
- The patch may be attached to leaflet tissue by simple sutures or by pledgeted mattress sutures and an assortment of techniques may be employed to establish AV valve competence.47,78

Several techniques for repair of complete atrioventricular septal defect (CAVSD) have been developed and modified over time as experience has been gained across many institutions internationally. Not every repair technique is applicable to the entire morphologic spectrum of CAVSDs, and therefore an individualized approach to CAVSD repair based on patient anatomy and surgeon preference is key to optimizing surgical outcomes.181 Repair of CAVSD in small infants is increasingly commonplace with excellent long-term outcomes and mostly obviates the need for interim palliation to enable somatic growth prior to complete repair. Repair of AVSD in the absence of an interventricular communication (partial AVSD) is much more standardized and largely revolves around management of the left AV valve.

Methods described in this chapter are the product of diverse experience across many institutions and surgeons. They provide complete and permanent closure of interatrial and interventricular communications while avoiding clinically important damage to the conduction system. They maintain AV valve competence when it is present, minimize the occurrence of valve repair dehiscence, and optimize valve performance when regurgitation is present preoperatively. They are applicable to cases with major associated cardiac anomalies and are suitable for the many variations across the spectrum of AV septal defects. Use of intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) to assess completeness of repair provides a further safeguard against an inadequate anatomic and functional result.204,205 Intraoperative epicardial echocardiography can be used in place of TEE to assess the repair in very small infants.

As noted by LeCompte and Crupi, a distinguishing feature of AVSD is its variability.181 Surgical techniques must account for and adapt to this variety. Historically the Rastelli classification of complete AVSD has been used to inform understanding of AVSD anatomy and guide surgical techniques employed for repair, but it has a diminished role in determining surgical approach in the current era. Size and shape of the atrial and ventricular septal defects, AV valve morphology, depth of coaptation, crenations or additional "clefts," leaflet dysplasia or fragility, varying location and length of chordal attachments, and patient size and age are all factors that influence the appropriate choice of repair technique. Individual surgeon and institutional experience also influence this decision. Established techniques for repair of complete AVSD have been refined with experience and include:

- Two-patch technique
- Single patch technique
- Modified single patch technique

# **General Principles for the Approach to Repair of Complete Atrioventricular Septal Defects**

For all CAVSD repair techniques, after the usual anesthetic preparations and draping, a median sternotomy is made, a generous portion of autologous pericardium is removed and set aside for the atrial septal patch and pericardial stay sutures are placed. External cardiac anatomy is evaluated and the presence or absence of a left superior vena cava verified. The presence or absence of an adequate-sized left brachiocephalic vein is assessed if an left superior vena cava (LSVC) is present. If the brachiocephalic vein is absent or diminutive, or if the LSVC is large, drainage of the LSVC via direct cannulation or introduction of a malleable vent catheter through the coronary sinus os is required. The ductus arteriosus, if identified as patent preoperatively, is dissected and looped. It is ligated either prior to or immediately after the onset of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Purse-string sutures are placed.

Standard aortobicaval cannulation for CPB is utilized with either mild or moderate hypothermia and cold antegrade cardioplegia. Deep hypothermia can also be used when there is significant pulmonary collateral return that inhibits visualization of the intracardiac anatomy. The use of Del Nido or Custodiol (histidinetryptophan-ketoglutarate [HTK], or Bretschneider) cardioplegia can facilitate the conduct of the operation by extending the intervals between each cardioplegia dose (see Chapter 3). The cavae can be cannulated directly with thin-walled, right-angled plastic or metal-tipped cannulae (see "Venous Cannulation" in Section III of Chapter 2), or the superior vena cava (SVC) cannula can be placed via the right atrial appendage into the SVC in small infants. Caval snares are placed to enable total CPB. If drainage of the heart is not adequate with the institution of CPB, a patent ductus arteriosus should be sought and ligated if this has not already been done. Placement of a snare around the main pulmonary artery or insertion of a pulmonary artery vent can assist when intracardiac visualization is limited by pulmonary collateral return. If collateral return is particularly problematic, more pronounced cooling may be employed thus allowing CPB flow to be reduced. As cooling proceeds, the aorta is cross clamped, and cold antegrade cardioplegic solution is administered. The right atrium is opened obliquely parallel to the AV groove, and a sump sucker can be passed through the foramen ovale, if present, into the left atrium to capture pulmonary venous return. Right atrial stay sutures are applied.

The malformation is examined and each morphologic detail noted (Fig. 32.22) including outlet and inlet extents and depth of the VSD, AV valve chordal attachments to the ventricular septum, AV valve morphology, and configuration of the ASD.

Morphology of the LSL and LIL is noted carefully with the leaflets in both the closed and open positions. Cold saline solution is injected through the valve and the closure pattern and any regurgitant leaks studied (Fig. 32.22A-C). The most central point of coaptation between the LSL and LIL opposing edges is identified and double-armed 6-0 or 7-0 polypropylene sutures are placed there.

#### **Two-Patch Repair Technique**

Static fluid testing of the common AV valve is performed by injecting cold crystalloid solution into the left ventricle across the LAVV component. The plane of division of the common AV valve into right and left components is assessed and guided by the plane of the ventricular septal crest. Importantly, it is often helpful to "borrow" some of the superior and inferior bridging leaflet tissue from the right AV valve to use for the left AV valve and septate the common AV valve somewhat rightward of the anatomic plane of division. This optimizes leaflet tissue available to construct the septal leaflet components of the LAVV formed by the LSL and LIL (Fig. 32.23). A 6/0 or 7/0 polypropylene marking suture is placed through the apex of the ZoA of the LSL and LIL in the plane of septation of the common AV valve. Next the VSD is inspected in detail and a crescentic half to quarter moon-shaped patch of double velour Dacron, Gore-Tex, or glutaraldehyde-fixed autologous pericardium is fashioned. The interventricular patch must have appropriate dimensions and configuration. If the patch is excessively wide (in a superior-inferior direction), there is greater likelihood of postoperative LV outflow tract obstruction. If the patch is excessively long (deep, in a caudad-cephalad direction), there is a greater likelihood of left AV valve regurgitation (Fig. 32.24). The patch is positioned behind the right AV valve chordal components; chordae of the RSL and RIL stay on the right ventricular side of the patch while those of the LSL and LIL stay on the LV side. The VSD patch is then sutured to the right side of the ventricular septum using 6/0 polypropylene interrupted pledgeted sutures or a continuous suture technique. It is important to avoid suturing directly on the septal crest to preserve the conduction pathways (Fig. 32.25). Multiple interrupted 6/0 polypropylene horizontal mattress sutures are then placed through the superior margin of the VSD patch, through the AV valve leaflets and through the free margin of the autologous pericardial patch that will be used to close the primum ASD (Fig. 32.26). In the case of a Rastelli A type CAVSD with a nonbridging LSL these sutures are passed through the anterior free edge of the LSL. For Rastelli B and C anatomic variants the sutures are passed up through the superior bridging leaflet in the plane of planned septation of the common AV valve. The

#### **Single-Patch Repair Technique**

The single-patch technique was more commonly employed early in the development of AVSD repair techniques and has largely been supplanted by the two-patch and modified single-patch techniques. Repair using a single patch differs from the two-patch technique in the following ways: (1) the single patch is almost always pericardium, (2) tailoring the waist of the patch (at the level of the common AV valve anulus) is critical, (3) the bridging leaflets are always divided to allow for patch placement, and (4) both the LSL and RSL and the LIL and RIL are sutured to the patch. Setup using bicaval cannulation is the same as for the twopatch technique, making sure that the inferior vena cava cannula is placed well caudad at the cavoatrial junction. CPB is established between 28°C to 32°C, and antegrade cardioplegia is infused after aortic clamping. The right atrial incision is made, and atrial exposure is arranged as for the two-patch technique. The most anterior point of LSL-LIL apposing edges is identified, and a 6-0 polypropylene suture is placed to retain that apposing relationship. Bridging leaflets superiorly and occasionally inferiorly are incised laterally to the valve anulus (Fig. 32.27A). This maneuver allows easy later exposure for closing the intraventricular portion of the defect and accommodating the waist of the patch.

The patch for the ventricular septal closure is inserted using continuous or interrupted mattress sutures. Generally, the initial suture is placed at the midpoint of the ventricular defect and is continued upward anteriorly (Fig. 32.27B). Inferiorly, the suture line stays on the right ventricular aspect of the septal crest to avoid conduction fibers. Both left and right AV valve leaflets are then anchored to the waist of the patch using double-pledgeted horizontal mattress sutures (Fig. 32.27C). Pledgets are generally of pericardium; often a single strip is used on the left-sided aspect. The strip is made a little shorter than the superior-inferior width of the anulus to somewhat narrow the anulus and contribute to AV valve competency. It is extremely important that the level of this suture line (in a cephalad-caudad direction) must ensure that height of the AV valve anulus is such that left AV valve regurgitation is avoided (suture line too high) and LV outflow obstruction is not produced (suture line too low). At this point, the left ventricle is loaded by crystalloid injection to test valve competency and VSD closure. The left AV valve ZoA is closed with fine interrupted sutures at its opposing edges to the previously placed marking suture as described earlier for the two-patch technique. At this point, if necessary, annuloplasty sutures are placed at the two lateral commissures. Repair of the remaining atrial defect proceeds as for the two-patch technique (Fig. 32.27D), outside the rim of the coronary sinus and including the foramen ovale defect. The completed single pericardial patch has a lopsided, dumbbell-shaped configuration (Fig. 32.27E).

#### **Modified Single-Patch Repair Technique**

The concept of the modified single patch technique was preceded by Lillehei's original approach to the first successful repair of complete AVSD by closing both the atrial and VSDs primarily without patch material.17 In 1997, Wilcox and colleagues reported direct suturing of the AV valves to the ventricular septum in complete AV septal defects with a small to moderate ventricular component and use of a patch to close the ASD.206 Nunn and colleagues extended the application of this modified single patch technique to all forms of complete AV septal defects.207,208 The advantages of this procedure are (1) simplicity by avoiding a separate patch for VSD closure, (2) avoidance of division of valve leaflets or chordae, (3) reduced operative time, and (4) ease of teaching the technique.

Key features of the procedure are illustrated in Fig. 32.28. Interrupted pledgeted 5-0 braided or polypropylene sutures are placed on the right side of the interventricular septal crest (as in the ventricular septal suture line of the standard ventricular patch technique) and passed through the bridging LSL and LIL. These sutures are then passed through the edge of the autologous pericardial patch used to close the atrial defect and through a thin strip of polyester whose length is slightly shorter than the corresponding ventricular septum, with the intent of producing a central anuloplasty of the LSL and LIL. These sutures are tied, and the remainder of the operation proceeds as in the single- or two-patch techniques.

# **Repair of Partial Atrioventricular Septal Defect with Mild or No Left Atrioventricular Valve Regurgitation**

Repair is similar to that described for complete AV septal defects, but no interventricular patch is required. After CPB is established, cold cardioplegic solution infused, intra-atrial exposure is arranged, and morphology studied. Attachments of the LSL and LIL to the crest of the ventricular septum are probed to be certain there are no small interventricular communications.

The ZoA in the left AV valve is then identified and its medial and septal extremities noted (Fig. 32.29A-C). In the past, if there was no left AV valve regurgitation, the ZoA was not intervened at the time of partial AVSD repair. More recently, most surgeons close the ZoA, typically using interrupted sutures. The group at Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne reported equivalent or better late LAVV performance in complete AVSD patients whose non-regurgitant "clefts" were not closed.52 However, this experience has not been reproduced elsewhere, and the preponderance of current late follow-up literature strongly favors closing the zone of apposition to minimize the incidence of LAVVR. The margins here are characterized by slightly thickened and rolled edges, and it is at these margins that sutures are based taking care not to consume leaflet surface area in the process.

A Kaye-Reed anuloplasty suture may also be placed at the LSL-LLL and/or LLL-LIL commissures to optimize valvar competence. Annuloplasty sutures are frequently critical to optimizing leaflet coaptation and minimizing LAVVR.214 Mild LAVV stenosis, if created by this arrangement, is usually well tolerated and should be considered an acceptable, if not ideal, result. Sizing of the mitral orifice is optimally targeted at a value corresponding to a z-score of –1.0.51 The intra-atrial pericardial patch is sewn into place in the same way used for repairing complete AV septal defect (Fig. 32.29D; see also Fig. 32.25). Autologous pericardium is usually used and the patch is sewn directly to the fibrous AV valve tissue attached to the crest of the interventricular septum using a continuous monofilament suture (see Fig. 32.25). The suture line inferiorly continues along the right AV valve tissue and then onto the right atrial wall until level with the coronary sinus where the suture line then deviates to the mouth of the coronary sinus, along its medial wall and then onto the crest of the primum septum to continue superiorly.

# **Repair of Atrioventricular Septal Defect with Small Interchordal Interventricular Communications (Transitional or Intermediate Form)**

When two AV valve orifices are present but small interventricular communications exist between thick, short chordae attaching the LSL to the crest of the ventricular septum, repair can be achieved by either of two methods. If the overall VSD is moderate in size a patch is sutured to the right side of the crest of the ventricular septum with the superior sutures taken through the superior leaflet to secure the remainder of the patch. If the VSD is small and shallow interrupted pledgeted sutures are taken through the right side of the crest of the ventricular septum and passed through the RSL and then through the autologous pericardial patch to obliterate the VSD between the AV valve leaflet and crest of the septum as with the modified single-patch technique.

When the interventricular communications are beneath the LIL, the same maneuvers may be followed. However, because of the bundle of His, care must be taken to keep the suture line well away from the crest of the septum and to attach the patch in the same manner in which the interventricular patch is attached in repair of complete AV septal defects.

# **Repair of Atrioventricular Septal Defect with Common Atrium**

When a common atrium is present, either with or without an interventricular communication, special effort is made to ensure that there is no left superior vena cava or, if one is present, that there is not a completely unroofed coronary sinus, which would change the repair (see "Technique of Operation" in Chapter 29).

Repair is accomplished by sewing an appropriately larger pericardial patch to the atrial wall on the left atrial side of the orifice of the inferior vena cava, to the right of the right pulmonary veins, and beneath the superior vena cava (where there is usually a small septal remnant).

# **Repair of Complete or Partial Atrioventricular Septal Defect with Moderate or Severe Left Atrioventricular Valve Regurgitation**

Repair of AVSD in the setting of moderate to severe LAVVR is challenging, and reported outcomes vary considerably. Preexisting regurgitation may be secondary to volume overload and anular dilation in patients with severe heart failure but is more commonly due to structurally related factors producing insufficient coaptation of the common AV valve leaflets. There are often multiple jets of insufficiency by color Doppler. Understanding the location, severity, and structural abnormalities producing the regurgitation is critically important in achieving an optimal outcome at the time of repair. When moderate to severe insufficiency is present on the postrepair transesophageal echo, improvement in the result should be pursued under a second cross-clamp. In the case of moderate LAVVR, accurate assessment of the etiology of ongoing regurgitation, as well as prudent surgical judgment regarding the probability of success with further intervention are key to optimizing early outcome. Severe regurgitation mandates revision of the repair.

# **Right Atrioventricular Valve**

In hearts with or without interventricular communications, the right AV valve usually does not require the same level of attention as the left AV valve. Irrespective of repair technique, the right AV valve "neoseptal leaflet" will often be deficient or incomplete, but significant regurgitation usually does not result. This is, in part, due to the lower pressure environment of the right ventricle (a fact unmasked by the frequent production of moderate to severe right atrioventricular valve regurgitaton (RAVVR) when patients manifest significant pulmonary hypertension after repair of AVSD). In addition, the RSL and RIL (and the RLL) are well supported by chordae, and this, combined with their closure against the polyester patch or bare ventricular septum, generally results in adequate valve function. That said, the right AV valve should be analyzed separately with saline injection and Hegar sizing (valve diameter should provide a *z* value of –2 or greater). If important leakage is identified, it should be surgically addressed. Often, attachment of the RIL to the "neoseptal" leaflet remnant will substantially reduce the leakage. Creating a "flange" which extends rightward at the lower margin of the tied-down ASD patch, analogous to that described by Najm and Coles for the LAVV but utilizing the autologous pericardial ASD patch and leaving the "flange" toward the right, is also a useful technique to address the deficiency of the "neoseptal" leaflet. Both the RIL and RSL can be attached to this flange, obliterating the central gap so frequently present.

# **Replacement of Left Atrioventricular Valve**

As documented in clinical reports dating back over 50 years, when severe left AV valve regurgitation cannot be repaired, left AV valve replacement may be necessary.221 In the setting of clinical stability and adequate growth, valve replacement should be deferred until childhood, when operative risk is substantially reduced as compared to infancy.222,223 When clinical conditions demand it, this decision is particularly difficult in small infants, for whom no device of appropriate size is available. Moderate or severe left AV valve regurgitation may be accepted if clinically tolerated so as to enable interim somatic growth prior to left AV valve replacement. Options for left AV valve replacement include a mechanical prosthesis (small mitral or inverted aortic prosthesis), a modified and surgically implanted stented bovine jugular valve (BJV), and a variety of operations referred to collectively as "chimney" techniques. Historically, outcomes after mechanical LAVV replacement have been poor, particularly in infants.96 While prohibitively high operative mortality rates have been substantially mitigated, most series still report sobering morbidity and mortality rates, particularly in infants.224,225 Some recent reports suggest, however, that mechanical LAVV replacement can be performed with low early and late mortality.226,227 Longitudinal morbidity and mortality related to chronic anticoagulation are known disadvantages of mechanical valve replacement and redo LAVV replacement is a certainty due to somatic growth as well as pannus ingrowth.

Because the left AV valve nearly always encroaches on the LV outflow tract (see Figs. 32.7A and Fig. 32.3), traditional intra-anular valve replacement may accentuate or produce subaortic stenosis. A mechanical prosthesis which is large for a given anulus must be kept away from the subaortic area and this may be accomplished by attaching a rectangular piece of polyester or bovine pericardial or Dacron patch to the anulus of the left AV valve anteriorly in the subaortic area (Fig. 32.31).239 The prosthesis is then sutured to this artificial mitral-aortic anulus and to the natural anulus for the rest of its circumference. To minimize the chance of damage to the AV node and bundle of His, particular care is taken anteriorly and inferiorly to sew only to the fringe of left AV valve that has been preserved. Alternatively, the prosthesis can be attached in a slightly supra-anular position using atrial wall to base the sewing ring with the valve tilted away from the posterior left AV valve anulus.226 Circumferential supra-anular placement of the prosthesis entirely within the left atrium should be avoided, however, as it has been associated with pulmonary vein obstruction and lower survival.240 Pledgeted sutures should be avoided in these operations wherein there is little room to spare.

BJV replacement of the LAVV was first reported by Emani in 2012.228 The technique is particularly applicable in the very small infant with a severely regurgitant, unrepairable LAVVR with a small anulus. Early results are promising,229,230 and the valve has the theoretical advantage of being dilatable in the catheterization laboratory as the child grows.231-233 Robust long-term data regarding this technique are not currently available. Nevertheless, BJV may be considered a useful option in the ill and very small infant to enable somatic growth before mechanical valve replacement. Early experience with balloon expandable pericardial valves (Sapien) seems to indicate that these valves have limited durability in the mitral position.234

Left ventricular outflow tract obstruction has been a particular concern with BJV LAVVR. To minimize this risk the prosthesis is modified at the time of implantation by shortening the stented conduit length by folding over the cells at both ends of the conduit as well as resecting the cells of the stent that face the LVOT (Fig. 32.32).241 Other techniques used to avoid this complication focus on implanting less of the length of the stented conduit below the level of anulus and placing more of it within the atrium (with or without atrial wall augmentation).242,243

# **Repair of Complete Atrioventricular Septal Defect with Tetralogy of Fallot**

In complete AV septal defect associated with tetralogy of Fallot, the large VSD is also juxtaaortic. The LSL is usually free-floating (Rastelli C) and is not attached to the crest of the ventricular septum. Any of the types of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction associated with tetralogy of Fallot may be present (see "Convenient Morphologic Categories of Right Ventricular Outflow Obstruction" under Morphology in Section I of Chapter 34), and anterior deviation of the infundibular septum is always present and must be considered in the repair. Preoperative cineangiographic studies may be necessary to delineate the surgically important details of right ventricular outflow obstruction and morphology of the pulmonary arteries; however, most of this detail is delineated on transthoracic echocardiography. Advanced cardiac imaging such as computed tomography (CT) angiography or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are increasingly important in the precise elucidation of both intra- and extracardiac anatomy and should be considered for these often difficult substrates.244

The operation begins as for repair of any complete AV septal defect. Before heparinization, however, if a right ventricular outflow patch is required, an appropriately sized patch is prepared as described (see "Decision and Technique for Transanular Patching" under Technique of Operation in Section I of Chapter 34). The VSD patch is trimmed with a wide superior aspect creating a teardrop or comma shape, which is important in providing an adequate size and shape for the left ventricular outflow tract (Fig. 32.34).245

Usually, the anteriorly deviated parietal extension of the infundibular septum (band) can be seen through the AV valve, and it is incised or resected (see "Repair of Uncomplicated Tetralogy of Fallot with Pulmonary Stenosis via Right Atrium" under Technique of Operation in Section I of Chapter 34). Other muscular infundibular obstructions can be visualized and resected. Extensive muscular resection should be avoided. The pulmonary valve can be visualized and fused commissures opened. The right ventricular outflow tract is sized with Hegar dilators. At this point, the surgeon may decide on a combined right atrial–right ventricular (RA-RV) approach. If so, a small vertical incision is made in the right ventricular outflow tract, and parietal and septal extensions are mobilized and partially resected. This ventriculotomy is later closed with a small patch (see Fig. 32.34).

The interventricular communication is closed by suturing into place a polyester or pericardial patch from this right atrial approach, sequencing the repair exactly as described for other complete AV septal defects. This can be accomplished with a single- or two-patch

Stay sutures are placed as usual for transatrial repair. Repair may begin at the AV valve anulus superiorly, or along the midpoint of the interventricular septal crest according to surgeon preference. These are large and irregularly shaped interventricular communications, and therefore a running suture technique may be preferable to an interrupted technique which can be timeconsuming and limits modifiability of patch shape and suture placement as the repair proceeds. It may also be useful to detach part of the RSL from its anular insertion to allow adequate access to the superior margin of the VSD. The RSL is then reattached once the VSD patch is secured in place.

Care must be taken that the VSD patch repair is not positioned too far rightward, in which case the right side of the surgically partitioned AV valve orifice will be too small. Because of the dextroposed aorta, attachment of the VSD patch to tissue over the aortic root (and beneath the commissural tissue and RSL) is particularly challenging. The patch is generally beneath (on the *left* ventricular side of) chordae attached to the right extremity of the bridging LSL and beneath this leaflet itself. Because this leaflet will be attached to the VSD patch, some of these chordae, if they are second or third order chords, may be cut to facilitate exposure and avoid leaflet entrapment by patch placement. The suture line is then carried around the interventricular communication and along the base of the RIL as described earlier for the usual complete AV septal defect. The anterior and rightward portions of the aortic anulus may be quite difficult to expose through an atrial approach. This is particularly true if aortic overriding is pronounced or if DORV is present. If this is the case, the juxtaaortic portion of the VSD closure may be completed through an infundibular incision. The AVSD repair is then carried on as described earlier.

# **Repair of Complete Atrioventricular Septal Defect with Double Outlet Right Ventricle**

When the interventricular communication is large and subaortic, configuration and insertion of the interventricular patch are similar to that just described for tetralogy of Fallot. Pulmonary stenosis, when coexisting, is also treated similarly.

When the interventricular communication is not subaortic (that is, "remote" or "noncommitted") and cannot be converted into one by septal excision (see "Intraventricular Tunnel Repair of Double Outlet Right Ventricle with Noncommitted Ventricular Septal Defect" under Technique of Operation in Chapter 45), Fontan palliation (see "Technique of Operation" in Section VI of Chapter 52) is necessary.

#### **Repair with Transposition of the Great Arteries**

When size of the left ventricle allows it, usual repair of the AV septal defect is carried out and an arterial switch operation is performed (see "Arterial Switch Operation" under Technique of Operation in Chapter 44).

#### **Repair with Truncus Arteriosus**

Experience with repair of truncus arteriosus with AV septal defect is limited to a few case reports. A staged approach using bilateral pulmonary artery banding is usually employed, but biventricular repair with utilization of an right ventricle (RV) to pulmonary artery (PA) conduit is the desired end state when the VSD is subarterial.246,247 If the VSD is noncommitted, then single ventricle palliation is necessary.248

# **Special Features of Postoperative Care**

Usual care as described in Chapter 4 is accorded patients after repair of AV septal defects, but certain features require emphasis. Because of the complexity of repair and despite the generally good results now obtained, vigilance must be exercised to detect any important imperfections in the repair. Intraoperative TEE is uniformly employed at the time of repair. Identification of residual shunts, quantification of the grade and nature of AVV regurgitation and/or semilunar valve insufficiency, detection of left or right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, and assessment of biventricular function are all required to properly design and articulate a perioperative care strategy to the multidisciplinary team members. Emphasis should be placed on assiduous blood pressure control so as to avoid exacerbating any existing AVV regurgitation, minimize the forces exerted on repair sutures in the hours immediately following operation, as well as to optimize myocardial working conditions. Episodic pulmonary hypertension is not unusual and can significantly increase right atrioventricular valve (RAVV) regurgitation and diminish cardiac output. Sedation, inotropic support, and inhaled nitric oxide are the mainstays of treatment in this situation, and transition to an oral pulmonary vasodilator should be strongly considered for such patients.

While changes in the auscultatory examination and plain chest radiography are important potential indicators of a suboptimal or deteriorating repair, they are relatively insensitive and unreliable. Therefore, a low threshold for repeat echocardiographic interrogation should be maintained throughout the postoperative period, particularly if there is an acute clinical change. If severe LAVVR is demonstrated, reoperation is indicated. Likewise, if the patient's condition is unsatisfactory and a large residual left-to-right shunt is present, reoperation is indicated.

Echocardiography should be performed routinely prior to discharge. Left AV valve repair failure predisposes the patient to death at late term follow-up, either via death at the time of LAVV repair or replacement or death with LAVVR prior to reintervention, with a cumulative survival of 88% at 10 years.249 Therefore, strong consideration should be given to early reoperation if severe regurgitation is present. Moderate LAVVR is also associated with significant attrition. However, regurgitation will remain stable in one-quarter and improve in another one-third of patients. Deterioration of valve function usually occurs within the first year after surgery. Therefore, moderate left AV valve regurgitation warrants close follow-up for the first year after repair of AVSD, with reoperation indicated for progression to severe.250

**Preoperative Clinical Status.** A patient's overall clinical status can exert a substantial impact on expected outcomes. The presence of severe congestive heart failure, preoperative mechanical ventilation, and the need for preoperative inotropic support have all been identified as risk factors for early mortality after repair.57

**Prematurity and Young Age at Repair.** Extreme prematurity introduces many deleterious comorbidities that impact expected outcomes, including bronchopulmonary dysplasia, pulmonary vascular disease, and an increased prevalence of noncardiac abnormalities, which also negatively impact outcomes.259

Young age has been an incremental risk factor for mortality in essentially all historical series of AVSD repair.40,48,260 This risk has been substantially reduced across surgical eras, but it is inaccurate to say it has been abolished.261 In the STS CHSD study of St. Louis and colleagues, operative mortality was over 15% and over 18% in patients weighing less than 3.5 and 2.5 kg, respectively.57 Conversely, in a binary separation based upon size, Goutallier found no difference in outcomes above or below 3.5 kg in the experience at Royal Children's Hospital in Melbourne. This series, however, had only 2 patients weighing less than 2500 grams and no patients under 2 kg.262 The Swedish experience documented similar findings, albeit again with very small numbers of patients under 3 kg.56 In a single institution experience spanning over 30 years, Hoohenkerk and colleagues found younger age to be an independent predictor of early mortality by multivariable logistic regression. Notably, surgical era was likewise found to be an independent risk factor, suggesting that younger age may have now been at least partially neutralized as a risk factor, consistent with the report by Ramgren and colleagues.148

**Accessory Valve Orifice.** Accessory orifices may predispose patients to reoperation for left AV valve regurgitation after primary repair (see earlier section on "Accessory Orifice" under Morphology).110,111

**Major Associated Cardiac Anomalies.** Complex forms of AVSD continue to pose significant risk of operative mortality compared with isolated balanced AVSD. Recent data from the STS CHSD, for instance, demonstrate an operative mortality after repair of tetralogy of Fallot/AVSD of 6.9%, a more than two-fold increase in risk compared to repair of isolated balanced AVSD. AVSD in the setting of heterotaxy may be amenable to complex biventricular repair but often involves intra-atrial baffling of anomalous pulmonary veins, intraventricular baffling of the VSD and, in some, addition of a bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (so-called one-and-a-half ventricle repair). Others will require single ventricle palliation, although the presence of Trisomy 21 is associated with dismal outcomes, with few survivors through completion Fontan. Consequently, the presence of Trisomy 21 is a strong inducement to achieving a biventricular repair (BVR) whenever possible. Pulmonary artery banding may offer the longest survival with the highest quality of life in those who cannot be offered BVR.

**Down Syndrome.** Historically, Trisomy 21 was considered a risk factor for mortality. This was followed by a period in the literature wherein Trisomy 21 was shown to not be a risk factor. In the current era, there has been demonstrated a clear divergence of outcomes based upon the presence or absence of Trisomy 21, with inferior outcomes observed for non-Trisomy 21 patients. This is most likely a function of more difficult common AV valve anatomic characteristics frequently encountered in the non-Trisomy 21 population.57,148

**Need for Early Reoperation.** The need for reoperation to close a residual interventricular communication or address important left AV valve regurgitation is associated with added risk of death.263 Early reoperation is extremely unusual after PAVSD repair but is required in 5% to 7% of patients after repair of CAVSD, usually for LAVVR. Early reoperation for LAVVR is associated with a roughly 10% risk of mortality or an odds ratio for death of over 16.264 There is a significant era effect on these outcomes,52 with historical series reporting risk of mortality as high as 50% for early reoperation.265

#### **Heart Block and Other Arrhythmias**

Lev described the architecture of the conduction system in AV septal defects in 1958.8 The incidence of acquired heart block after AVSD repair in the current era is 0.2% to 1.6%.58,161,254 The incidence of heart block is minimized by understanding and giving appropriate attention to the anatomy of the conduction system such as suturing the patch along the medial wall of the coronary sinus (see also discussion under "Special Situations and Controversies").266 Most surgeons place the atrial patch in a manner that maintains the coronary sinus within the right atrium with excellent results. Incorporation of the coronary sinus on the left atrial side of the patch is an alternative technique with equivalent outcomes.204,267 Historically, heart block was reported to be more frequent after repair of AV septal defect associated with tetralogy of Fallot and after LAVV replacement although in the contemporary era this is not case, possibly due to improvements in surgical techniques and an improved understanding of conduction tissue anatomy in complex hearts.45,78,148,161

First-degree AV block, present in about 30% of patients preoperatively, has been found in about 50% of patients after repair.88 Right bundle branch block is common after repair of complete AV septal defects but uncommon after repair of partial AV septal defects.88,268

The incidence of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death is reported to be 0.5% and 1.7%, respectively, from a European multi-institutional study of 415 patients with repaired partial or complete AVSD.268 From another multi-center analysis of 391 patients, the risk of developing atrial arrhythmia at 20, 40, and 60 years of age was found to be 3.7%, 17.8%, and 55.3%, respectively (see also Fig. 32.20). Occurrence of atrial arrhythmia was associated with a nearly 25% incidence of heart failure.189 Thus, the burden of arrhythmia, while small in early to intermediate follow-up, is substantial and a source of significant morbidity in late follow-up.

#### **Functional Status**

 Most long-term survivors are in excellent health. In one center, 88% of surviving patients were in NYHA functional class I, and 11% were in class II.78,269

#### **Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction**

The prevalence of clinically significant LVOTO is roughly 5% after repair of both partial and complete AV septal defect. Reoperation for LVOTO is the second major cause of reoperation (after LAVV regurgitation) and occurs in 2% to 5% of patients after AVSD repair. Mean time to reintervention for LVOTO is ap-

proximately 7 years in most series.127 Although previously thought to be more common following partial AVSD repair, the long-term incidence of LVOTO is similar following both complete and partial AVSD repairs in the current era, with a freedom of reintervention of 85% to 95% at 15 years.76,183,272,273,276,285-287 The development of LVOTO after repair of AVSD has an important impact on late survival although less than LAVVR.288 LVOTO in repaired AVSD is typically multifactorial and this distinguishes its surgical treatment from other forms of subaortic stenosis.179,289,290

The key elements and their resulting contribution to LVOTO in postrepair AVSD include:

- The fundamental morphology of the LVOT in AVSD, described earlier, which constitutes the substrate for "tunnel-like" LVOTO. This requires adequate proximal extension of muscular resection on the LV side of the interventricular outlet septum to minimize recurrent LVOTO.
- Abnormal AV valve leaflet and chordal apparatus which can occupy and crowd the mid or distal LVOT as well as contribute to flow turbulence. Removal of any secondary or tertiary LAVV chords that are present in LVOT should be performed to minimize subsequent flow disturbance and its contribution to recurrence.
- Discrete subaortic membrane formation, a function of the altered flow dynamics and, in some instances, encroachment on the LVOT of the VSD patch in repaired complete AVSD. Careful attention to complete removal of all fibromuscular material, including that found distributed across the undersurfaces of the mitral and aortic valves, is central to an effective operation for LVOTO.
- Originally described by Moulaert and Oppenheimer-Decker, the anterolateral muscle bundle of the left ventricle occupies the leftward to anterior portion of the LVOT.132 It is present in only 40% of normal hearts but 100% of hearts with AVSD.128 The anterolateral muscle bundle of the LV does not in and of itself cause LVOTO. However, it can be entirely resected safely and doing so often results in a dramatic caliber change in the mid- to distal LVOT, maximizing its dimension and minimizing the risk of recurrence.

# **Residual Pulmonary Hypertension**

When important pulmonary hypertension is present preoperatively, it can be expected to regress postoperatively in the large majority of patients, particularly when there is clinical and/or hemodynamic evidence of a large left-to-right shunt and the operation is undertaken within the first 6 months of life.291,292 In patients for whom there is a concern for significant pulmonary hypertension postoperatively, accurate data regarding pulmonary vascular resistance and reactivity should be obtained preoperatively. If confirmed, inhaled nitric oxide should be employed postrepair to address residual or episodic pulmonary hypertension. Consideration should be given to transitioning these patients to an oral agent such as sildenafil or tadalafil with hemodynamic reevaluation after several months.

Atrial fenestration is sometimes employed as a means to maintain cardiac output in patients at risk for significant pulmonary hypertension postoperatively. While intuitively logical, it is unclear how effective this strategy actually is. Callahan and colleagues, analyzing the CHSS AVSD Inception Cohort, found that utilization of atrial fenestration was associated with increased risk of mortality, suggesting that its effectiveness at mitigating risk was limited (see "Unbalanced AVSD" later).293

# **Indications for Operation**

Presence of an AV septal defect indicates need for operation because an important hemodynamic derangement is nearly always present and spontaneous closure does not occur.

#### **Partial Atrioventricular Septal Defects**

In partial AV septal defects, pulmonary hypertension is usually absent, and the optimal age for operation is during early childhood and ideally after infancy, assuming a competent left AV valve. If moderate or greater AV valve regurgitation is present, earlier operation may be indicated in infancy (ideally at 9–12 months of age) to prevent progressive left AV valve dilation and worsening valve regurgitation. In the unusual circumstance wherein severe heart failure and failure to thrive are present, operation is indicated at that time. This subset of partial AVSD patients carries measurably higher early and late mortality risk than those with a conventional presentation.

# **Complete Atrioventricular Septal Defects**

Operation is indicated early in the first year of life. When the infant's general condition is good, repair is electively performed at 4 to 6 months of age. When refractory heart failure or severe growth failure is evident at an earlier age, repair at that time is indicated. Ideally, surgical repair should be delayed until at least 6 weeks of age to ensure adequate AV valve leaflet tissue maturity and integrity to tolerate repair. Similarly, patient weight \$3.0 kg is preferred to enable satisfactory repair. Interim pulmonary artery banding should be considered in infants who develop intractable heart failure prior to 6 to 8 weeks of age. When patients present late for evaluation, pulmonary vascular disease may already be too severe to permit repair. Criteria of inoperability are the same as described for patients with VSD (see "Indications for Operation" in Section I of Chapter 33).

#### **Coexisting Cardiac Anomalies**

Repair of tetralogy of Fallot or DORV with complete AVSD is a significantly more challenging operation than isolated AVSD repair. The conduct of the operation often places the AV valve apparatus at risk for injury. Pulmonary insufficiency in the setting of a transanular patch places a premium on optimal postrepair function of the right AV valve, and diastolic dysfunction, often present after TOF repair, can make management of patients with residual AV valve regurgitation after AVSD repair that much more difficult. In addition, patients with this anatomy will often have a "protected" pulmonary vasculature due to pulmonary stenosis, and the risk of developing pulmonary vascular disease is therefore low. For these reasons, complete repair of tetralogy of Fallot or DORV with complete AVSD can most often be electively performed at closer to 12 months of age.

Isolated ligation or catheter-based closure of a patent ductus arteriosus may be employed to improve hemodynamic status followed by later complete AVSD repair if heart failure symptoms improve following ductal closure. Similarly, aortic coarctation without arch hypoplasia, most often presenting in the neonatal period, may be repaired via a left thoracotomy followed by later repair of the AVSD after further somatic growth.

#### **Special Situations and Controversies**

#### **Pulmonary Artery Banding**

Pulmonary artery banding (PAB) may be used to ameliorate heart failure symptoms and enable somatic growth prior to staged repair.294 While primary repair is normative for AVSD, there are circumstances wherein PAB is a preferred treatment strategy. Some have advocated for primary complete repair irrespective of age and size, but recent literature suggests this may not be an optimal approach due to near term morbidity and mortality risk associated with complete repair, mostly in the very young, low birth weight and/or very ill. Examples where consideration for utilization of PAB may be appropriate include significant prematurity and corresponding small patient size with large left-to-right shunt early in life, important co-morbidities wherein control of left-to-right shunting is urgently required, such as necrotizing enterocolitis in a newborn, or noncardiac conditions requiring urgent therapy that are importantly disadvantaged by left-to-right shunting and present prohibitive risk for complete repair.

Recent studies have demonstrated worse long-term survival when interim PAB is utilized prior to complete biventricular repair,254,295 suggesting that primary complete AVSD repair may be a better strategy to optimize long-term outcome. Excellent outcomes achieved following primary repair of complete AVSD in infants ,3 months of age56 and ,3.5 kg in weight262 support an approach which strongly favors primary repair over PA banding in infants. However, there is some evidence to suggest that interim PAB may be a reasonable strategy when patient factors might compromise biventricular repair such as in the setting of moderately unbalanced AVSD.296 In this group of 50 patients, which included both balanced and unbalanced AVSD patients, those who underwent PAB with an intent for biventricular repair had the same 4-year survival as patients from the cohort who had primary repair (93% vs. 91%, respectively). Notably, AV valve performance was unchanged (n 5 27) or improved (n 5 14) after PAB in 41 patients and worsened in only 9. Thus, rather than the often-stated concern for worsening AV valve regurgitation, PAB may actually more often have a salutary impact on common AV valve regurgitation in AVSD patients. Investigators from Japan and South Korea have published similar results recently, employing PAB selectively in very young infants and achieving better results than a prior policy of uniformly employing primary repair.297,298

# **Septal Patches**

Patch materials suitable for VSD closure include woven Dacron, polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE), and glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium. Fresh or glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardium is usually employed for the ASD patch, although bovine pericardium is also suitable.

# **Avoiding Heart Block**

A detailed and clear understanding of the anatomy of the conduction system is fundamental to minimizing the incidence of acquired postoperative heart block (see "Conduction System" earlier). The conduction system is at risk during placement of sutures at the inferior-most extreme of the VSD component of the AVSD, if present, as well as in the placement of the ASD patch in the region of the triangle of Koch.

*Atrial Septal Defect Closure.* Placement of the ASD patch so as to locate the coronary sinus in the right atrium or the left atrium have both been described, although the former is utilized by most surgeons. Either method is associated with low rates of permanent complete heart block when executed properly.119 A fairly standard approach to avoidance of the conduction tissue is to carry the atrial septal patch suture line along the RIL in the plane of the ventricular septum then along the right atrial wall in the same plane until level with the ostium of the coronary sinus. The suture line is then continued across to and along the medial wall of the coronary sinus and then up onto the underside of the primum septal crest. Alternatively, the suture line can be carried even further inferiorly around the coronary sinus ostium thereby directing the coronary sinus drainage into the left atrium.

*Ventricular Septal Defect Closure.* The AV node and bundle of His are at particular risk in the inlet region and inferior septal crest during closure of the VSD in complete AVSD. The key to avoiding heart block it to place superficial sutures in the inlet region and to keep the suture line on the right side of the interventricular septum away from the septal crest. Where the ventricular septal crest meets the AV valve tissue at the inlet extent of the VSD, the suture line should be displaced inferiorly away from this junction point that may contain the bundle of His in the septal crest. All of these maneuvers mitigate the risk of injury to the AV node and conduction system during repair of complete AVSD.

# **Surgery for Late Left Ventricular Outflow Obstruction**

A transaortic approach for resection of LVOTO is normative. There are three critical elements of a thorough operation for LVOTO after AVSD repair.127 See also earlier discussion of "Left Ventricular Outflow Tract Obstruction" in "Results."

- Careful attention to complete removal of all fibromuscular material, including that found on the mitral and aortic valves.
- Aggressive muscular resection that addresses the abnormal geometry of the LVOT in AVSD (such as incision and partial resection of an encroaching anterolateral muscle bundle of the left ventricle and adequate proximal extension of muscular resection to address "tunnel" obstruction).
- Removal of anomalous secondary and tertiary left AV valve chordae that reside within the LVOT.

Importantly, the incidence of acquired complete heart block is quite high in this setting, at least 15%.257 This undoubtedly relates to risk to the left bundle of His during resection of LVOTO in hearts wherein the right bundle has already been injured as a function of prior VSD closure.

LVOTO recurs in anywhere from 15% to 25% of patients undergoing operation for LVOTO after AVSD repair (37% in one series). Repeat transaortic resection is reasonable, particularly if there is evidence to suggest that important sources of LVOTO were unaddressed at the prior intervention. In patients with recalcitrant and complex LVOTO, a modified Konno operation affords excellent relief of LVOTO while avoiding manipulation of the AV valve apparatus and minimizing the risk of heart block.299,300 In light of the extensive nature of the operation, the modified Konno should very rarely be considered as a first-line operation for LVOTO after AVSD repair.

Lastly, it should be mentioned that detachment and resuspension of the LSL, particularly in partial or transitional AVSD and Rastelli type A complete AVSD, have been described by several investigators.131,179,180 Although the technique has theoretical appeal, there are no long-term data on its effectiveness.

#### **Unbalanced Atrioventricular Septal Defect**

Abnormalities of ventricular balance and common AV valve distribution occur in 7% to 10% of patients with complete AV septal defects.301

*Historical Note.* Historically, ventricular size dominated the diagnostic identification of unbalance in AVSD. However, attributes of the distribution of the common AV valve over the ventricles, septal alignment, and what has been termed "inflow physiology" have come to occupy a far more important aspect of proper identification of unbalanced atrioventricular septal defect (uAVSD).302 In uAVSD, then, the common AV valve is unequally distributed over the right or left ventricle, which may exhibit varying degrees of hypoplasia, with or without malalignment of the atrial and ventricular septa. Unbalanced AVSD and its surgical management has been the focus of many clinical investigations in the last 10 to 15 years.

Cohen and colleagues introduced the AV valve index (AVVI) in 1996. It is an echocardiographic measure of balance obtained in the 30-degree LAO subcostal view, wherein a line is drawn in the plane of the interventricular septum, dividing the common AV valve orifice into right and left components. By tracing the outlines of the two AV valve orifices thereby created, a ratio of the RAVV and LAVV areas can be calculated (Fig. 32.37).303 The AVVI was later modified by Jegatheeswaran and colleagues (mAVVI) using data from a multi-institutional retrospective cohort. The mAVVI simplifies the expression of unbalance and dominance by placing all balanced and unbalanced forms of AVSD on the same numeric scale (LAVV area/total AVV area) (see Fig. 32.37).304 An mAVVI of 0.4 to 0.6 is considered balanced, an mAVVI ,0.4 right dominant uAVSD, and .0.6 left dominant uAVSD. In severely uAVSD, single ventricle palliation is indicated, although left dominant uAVSD affords the option of utilizing a small right ventricle in a "one-and-a-half ventricle" arrangement. In contrast, right dominant uAVSD forces a more or less binary choice between univentricular and biventricular repair strategies.302

*Right Dominant Unbalanced Atrioventricular Septal Defect.* In its most severe form, right dominant uAVSD mimics hypoplastic left heart syndrome with severe LVOTO, aortic valve hypoplasia, ascending aortic and aortic arch hypoplasia, and coarctation. These patients initially require either Norwood or Hybrid Stage I palliation similar to the hypoplastic left heart syndrome (HLHS) population (see Chapter 51) with subsequent staged palliation along the Fontan pathway. Trisomy 21 occurs at a significantly lower frequency in right dominant uAVSD compared with balanced complete AVSD and left dominant uAVSD, an important epidemiologic attribute as many of these patients require single ventricle palliation.71

*Predictors of Surgical Strategy.* In the analysis by Jegatheeswaran, all patients with an mAVVI ,0.19 had a univentricular repair (UVR) and all patients with an mAVVI between 0.4 and 0.6 had a BVR. However, in the range of mAVVI between 0.2 and 0.4, there was heterogeny of surgical strategy and a clustering of mortality, suggesting mismatching of surgical strategy and underlying anatomic substrate (Fig. 32.38).304 Attempts have been made to address this mismatching of strategy and anatomy, and additional metrics have been identified that are associated with outcomes after BVR for uAVSD. The presence of a large VSD was associated with uncertainty of successful BVR in Cohen's original AVVI manuscript, and thus a restrictive or small VSD lends support to a BVR strategy. Left ventricular inflow angle (LVII), introduced by Szwast and colleagues, is the ratio between the narrowest width of color inflow divided by the estimated width of the hypothetical AVV anulus in the fourchamber view (Fig. 32.39). *Ventricular Growth or Recruitment.* Ventricular volume is perhaps the most modifiable element of uAVSD. Ventricular volume is not a fixed phenomenon and can be significantly affected by loading conditions alone.308 The concept of ventricular growth or recruitment was introduced by Foker in 1999 in a small series of uAVSD patients.309 Subsequent efforts at ventricular recruitment by del Nido and Emani have built upon this early work. The premise of this surgical strategy is that the underlying anatomic substrate, rather than being immutable, is modifiable through a series of operations. There are clearly patients in which an increase in the size of hypoplastic structures can be achieved. Thus far, however, these series are marked by significant attrition or residual pulmonary hypertension in BVR survivors.310 Other features such as the presence or absence of endocardial fibroelastosis, mitral valve size and architecture, and the anatomy of the left ventricular outflow tract also exercise an important impact on the prospects for success or failure of left ventricular recruitment in uAVSD. Thus, accurate predictors of success or failure of BVR in uAVSD will continue to be sought.

Atrial fenestration is commonly employed as a strategy to mitigate severe left or right atrial hypertension or to provide a "pop-off" in the setting of significant or episodic pulmonary hypertension in the setting of BVR for uAVSD.311 In a recent analysis by Callahan and colleagues from the CHSS, however, atrial fenestration was found to be associated with increased mortality after BVR for AVSD, balanced or unbalanced.293 Ascribing causality to this association is difficult, surgical intent is also difficult to apprehend consistently, and outcomes had a fenestration *not* been employed are unknown (that is, mortality could conceivably have been worse). Nevertheless, if fenestration is meant to diminish risk in BVR, these data suggest it does so only incrementally, if at all.

*Left Dominant Unbalanced Atrioventricular Septal Defect.* Left dominant uAVSD occurs at a significantly lower frequency than the right dominant form. In addition, the incidence of Trisomy 21 in left dominant uAVSD is as high as 80%. In its most extreme form, this anatomy also requires single ventricle palliation. However, even a very hypoplastic right ventricle may support a "one-and-a-half ventricle" or "pulsatile Glenn" arrangement.312 When this strategy is undertaken, the AVSD is repaired in the usual manner and a superior cavopulmonary connection is added.311 Thus, a large majority of patients with left dominant uAVSD can avoid single ventricle palliation.
