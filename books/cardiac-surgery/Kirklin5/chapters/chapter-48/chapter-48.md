# **Definition**

Ebstein anomaly (EA) is a congenital cardiac malformation of the tricuspid valve and right ventricle (RV) that is characterized by incomplete delamination of the septal and inferior (posterior) tricuspid valvar leaflets from the myocardium of the RV, and varying degrees of downward (apical) rotational displacement of the functional anulus.1

The tricuspid valve leaflets are variably deformed. Characteristically, the anterior leaflet is enlarged and "sail-like." There is a wide spectrum of anatomic and clinical severity; in the mildest asymptomatic forms, the tricuspid valve may appear normal at first sight, and classification as Ebstein anomaly may be debatable.2

This chapter discusses Ebstein anomaly in hearts with atrioventricular (AV) concordant connection and without other major cardiac anomalies. Ebstein-type tricuspid atresia and Ebstein anomaly associated with pulmonary atresia and with AV discordant connection are discussed elsewhere (see Chapters 36, 47, and 52). Details of Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome, which is occasionally associated with Ebstein anomaly, are presented in Chapter 15.

# **Historical Note**

Wilhelm Ebstein's scholarly description of the tricuspid valve abnormality bearing his name was published in 1866.3 His report describes a single autopsy specimen and includes a hypothesis of pathophysiology based on correlation of the morphology with clinical notes on the deeply cyanosed patient supplied by a colleague (Ebstein did not apparently see the patient, Joseph Prescher, alive).4,5 According to the historical review by Mann and Lie, the second case was not described until 20 years later, and the first description in the English literature was by MacCallum in 1900.6 The eponym *Ebstein's disease* was first suggested by Arnstein in 1927 and was used by Yater and Shapiro in their 1937 review article that reported the 16th case and the first to be examined by both radiography and electrocardiography.7,8 These investigators commented that "it would appear impossible to make the diagnosis during life." In 1950, Engle and colleagues as well as Reynolds suggested that the disease was associated with a clinical syndrome that should make diagnosis possible.9,10 In 1951, Van Lingen and colleagues and Soloff and colleagues made the diagnosis during life using cardiac catheterization and angiography, respectively.11,12 In 1955, Lev and colleagues described a patient with coexisting WPW syndrome and provided histologic details of the course of the conducting tissue in this anomaly.13

Palliative surgery was attempted unsuccessfully using a Blalock-Taussig shunt in 1950.14 A superior vena cava–to–right pulmonary artery anastomosis (Glenn procedure15) was used successfully by Gasul and colleagues in 1959 and subsequently by McCredie and colleagues and Scott and colleagues.16-19 Barnard and Schrire were the first to report the use of prosthetic valve replacement in 1962, followed by Cartwright and colleagues and Lillehei and colleagues.20-22 Hardy and colleagues reported the first successful valvuloplasty in 1964 based on the concepts of Hunter and Lillehei.23,24 A similar technique was used by Bahnson in 1965.25

# **Morphology and Morphogenesis**

## **Tricuspid Valve**

Both the origin of the tricuspid valve from the AV ring and its chordal attachments within the RV are malpositioned, and the leaflets are malformed.26-31 The leaflets are either enlarged or reduced in size and are frequently dysplastic (thickened and distorted). These deformities vary widely in severity (Table 48.1). In the mildest forms, the valve is functionally near normal32; in the fully developed syndrome, tricuspid function is severely compromised. Displacement of the origin of the leaflets from the AV ring is reasonably constant. The septal leaflet appears always to be affected, the inferior (posterior) leaflet nearly always, and the anterior leaflet least affected (Fig. 48.1). As noted by Anderson and colleagues, when both the septal and inferior leaflets are displaced,27 the point of maximum displacement is usually at the commissure between them. Thus, the functional tricuspid anulus is rotated apically and anteriorly toward the right ventricular outflow tract (Fig. 48.2). In the few cases in which the anterior leaflet is displaced, the commissural area between it and the septal leaflet, attached to the right trigone at the point of penetration of the bundle of His, remains in normal position13 (see Fig. 48.1). In many patients, the apparent displacement is due to adherence of the base of the leaflet to the right ventricular endocardium (see Table 48.1 and Fig. 48.1). Adherence of leaflet tissue to the underlying myocardium is thought to represent a "failure of delamination" during development.33 This failure effectively moves the hinge points of the septal and inferior leaflets (the functional anulus) into the ventricular cavity.

The septal or inferior leaflet may be partially absent, in which case the belly of the leaflet is small or absent (see Fig. 48.1). The inferior leaflet is more often elongated than reduced in size (see Table 48.1), due in part to lack of a commissure between the inferior and anterior leaflets. This was the case in 8 of 15 hearts examined at autopsy at Green Lane Hospital, Auckland, New Zealand. The septal-inferior leaflet commissure may also occasionally be absent. Leaflet enlargement and elongation are characteristic of the anterior leaflet, which has been described as sail-like. The leaflet is usually diffusely thickened or ridged and occasionally consists partly of muscle. All the leaflets are frequently dysplastic, and isolated accessory leaflets may occur.29

Distal leaflet attachments are variable and usually abnormal. Displaced and dysplastic inferior and septal leaflets frequently have multiple short chordae connecting to multiple small papillary muscles (see Fig. 48.1). The sail-like anterior leaflet may also have multiple short chordae arising around most of its free edge, binding it relatively closely to the septum and occasionally to the free wall, or the leaflet edge may be directly adherent to the anterior papillary muscle and moderator band or the posterior edge of the septal band.31 Presence of a free anterior leaflet is an important morphologic detail, because it greatly increases the likelihood of a successful repair. Leaflet fenestrations are common, with the opening typically guarded by a single papillary muscle, which gives rise to chords that attach to the periphery of the fenestration.

If the entire free margin of the leaflets is adherent and imperforate, (i.e., a linear attachment), one variety of tricuspid atresia is produced (see Chapter 52).31,34 When the adherence is partial, the large anterior leaflet produces a variable degree of stenosis between the portion of ventricle proximal to it (atrialized ventricle) and that distal to it (functional or ventricularized ventricle), because blood can pass only between openings that remain between the leaflet margin and ventricular wall (or through the commissures when these are present) (Fig. 48.3). Stiffness of the anterior leaflet can contribute to stenosis.

Although important stenosis is uncommon, most Ebstein valves are regurgitant, often severely so (Fig. 48.4). This is contributed to by marked dilation of the true tricuspid anulus and the RV, as well as by morphologic abnormalities of the tricuspid valve.

# **Right Atrium**

The right atrium is enormously dilated in advanced cases. There is usually an interatrial communication (60% of autopsy specimens, 42% at catheterization, and 21 of 22 surgical cases in Watson's collective review), most commonly a patent foramen ovale, although an atrial septal defect (ASD) of any type may be present.40 Interatrial communication was present in 94% of patients at operation in the series reported by Danielson and colleagues.41 Rarely, an ostium primum AV septal defect coexists.

The bundle of His and AV node lie in their usual locations, although the right bundle and node may be compressed by thickened endocardium (a possible explanation of the frequent right bundle branch block pattern in the electrocardiogram).13,29,42,43 WPW syndrome is present in about approximately 14% of persons with Ebstein anomaly.41,44

# **Left Ventricle**

Monibi and colleagues and Ng and colleagues report that abnormal left ventricular contraction and contour and mitral valve prolapse are frequently present.45,46 Marked dilation of the RV produces leftward septal shift and compression and posterior displacement of the left ventricle (LV). Daliento and colleagues studied nine autopsied hearts and found the ventricular septum normal in six and thin in three, which could account for the exaggerated leftward diastolic movement observed by angiography in 24 of 26 patients.47 Severe leftward displacement produced a "banana" appearance of the LV. Regional left ventricular wall motion abnormality was observed in 67% of patients. In a Mayo Clinic experience of over 500 surgical patients with Ebstein anomaly, 9% had moderate or severe left ventricular dysfunction.48 When mitral valve prolapse is present, the valve is frequently nodular and thickened.13,47,49,50 A Mayo Clinic study of 106 patients identified left ventricular abnormalities in 39%, of whom 18% had left ventricular dysplasia resembling noncompaction.51

### **Lungs**

In severe Ebstein anomaly associated with fetal and neonatal distress or death, both lungs are usually hypoplastic but otherwise normal. The hypoplasia is secondary to gross cardiomegaly from severe tricuspid regurgitation.52

# **Associated Anomalies**

An ASD is present in 80% to 95% of patients with Ebstein anomaly.41,53 *Pulmonary atresia* or *stenosis* may be present in up to one-third of autopsied hearts (see Chapter 36).28 Others include ventricular septal defect, tetralogy of Fallot, patent ductus arteriosus, transposition of the great arteries, coarctation of the aorta, and congenital mitral stenosis.28,29,33,40

In *congenitally corrected transposition* with atrial situs solitus, the tricuspid valve lies within a left-sided, systemic, morphologic RV. Although 30% of these left-sided valves are regurgitant, this is frequently due to Ebstein anomaly.27,54 This Ebstein anomaly differs from the usual right-sided form in that dilation of the AV ring and separation of the morphologic RV into atrialized and ventricularized portions are uncommon. The anterior leaflet is also less prominent and may be cleft (see Chapter 47).

# **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

# **Mechanisms Underlying Clinical Presentation**

Three primary pathophysiologic features predominate in patients with Ebstein anomaly:

- Right ventricular abnormalities
- Tricuspid valve abnormalities
- Accessory conduction pathways (WPW syndrome)

Their severity determines secondary pathophysiologic features, clinical presentation, and natural history.

*Right Ventricular Abnormalities.* Severity of right ventricular abnormality is partly related to the extent to which the number of muscle fibers in the ventricular wall is reduced.26 Nearly all patients have at least mild right ventricular cavity enlargement and wall thinning. In the most severe cases, the right ventricular cavity is greatly enlarged and free wall extremely thin.55 The ventricular septum is often abnormal as well, with leftward bulging and consequent reduction in cavity size of the LV, impairing left ventricular function.47,56 In the most advanced examples, the right ventricular free wall is paper thin, a condition referred to as *Uhl disease.*

Extensiveness of the atrialized portion of the RV, which exhibits systolic expansion rather than contraction, importantly affects right ventricular performance. Right ventricular systolic dysfunction, along with tricuspid valve regurgitation, is responsible for right atrial enlargement and its wall thickening, which are at times extreme.

The abnormalities of right ventricular structure and function, exhibited by patients with Ebstein anomaly, may result in variable cardiomegaly and right heart failure: hepatomegaly, ascites, and fluid retention that may be advanced. Cyanosis, occasionally extreme, results from right-to-left shunting across a patent foramen ovale or ASD. There may be paroxysms of supraventricular and occasionally ventricular tachyarrhythmias that can cause advanced disability and sometimes sudden death.

*Tricuspid Valve Abnormalities.* Tricuspid regurgitation, usually present to some degree, exacerbates the abnormalities of right ventricular structure and function. Degree of regurgitation is determined by the valve's morphologic anomalies. Those valves in which the anterior leaflet is tightly tethered and adherent to the underlying right ventricular free wall and septum and, to a lesser extent, those in which the inferior leaflet is displaced and immobilized, are more likely to be regurgitant.55

Pulmonary hypoplasia, a feature that contributes to neonatal death, is correlated with the degree of tricuspid regurgitation, occurring in association with severe regurgitation and gross cardiomegaly. If these features are corrected, the lungs presumably grow normally.52

*Wolff-Parkinson-White Syndrome.* Arrhythmic features of WPW syndrome, present in about 14% of patients, may dominate the clinical picture in patients whose tricuspid valve and right ventricular anomalies are mild (see Chapter 15).

*Associated Anomalies.* In general, coexisting cardiac anomalies have little impact on the clinical features and course of patients with Ebstein anomaly. Exceptions are congenital pulmonary stenosis or atresia; when these coexist with Ebstein anomaly, death in utero or soon after birth is common. A patent foramen ovale or ASD, present in many patients, is necessary for right-to-left shunting and for the occasional paradoxical embolism or cerebral abscess that develops, but otherwise usually plays little role in the etiology of signs and symptoms. However, in the uncommon circumstance of only mild right ventricular and tricuspid abnormalities, important left-to-right shunting may occur across an ASD.

# **Symptoms and Signs**

Breathlessness in association with cyanosis, severe cardiomegaly, and often heart failure may appear during the first week of life. However, many patients have milder symptoms and do not present until later in life. Nonetheless, objective exercise testing shows that their functional capacity is less than normal.57 Oxygen saturation at rest is a major predictor of exercise tolerance.58 Mild dyspnea and fatigue may become evident in childhood or early adult life, or more severe symptoms and signs of various types may develop. If heart failure develops, the patient becomes severely limited by breathlessness and fatigue.

Cyanosis is a common sign of Ebstein anomaly, occurring in more than half of patients and severe in about one-third.59 It may appear at birth, but in most patients, onset is in infancy or early childhood.

Palpitations caused by various types of arrhythmia are also common.60 Severe arrhythmic symptoms are frequent in patients of all ages and may be disabling. WPW syndrome is the best known type of arrhythmia, but less specific types of supraventricular tachycardias are more common. Numerous electrophysiologic abnormalities have been identified, which no doubt account for the frequent occurrence and persistence of arrhythmic symptoms even after operation.43,61 Symptoms are more severe when there are important associated cardiac anomalies.

A malar flush, similar to the so-called mitral facies, was noted by Ebstein's colleague and occurs in about one-third of patients.62 It is unrelated to cyanosis or polycythemia or to cardiac output.59

The left anterior chest is often prominent in association with marked cardiomegaly, and there may be a systolic thrill along the left sternal edge originating from the tricuspid valve. Characteristically, the precordium and apex remain quiet despite marked cardiomegaly. Jugular venous pressure is generally unremarkable and rarely suggests tricuspid regurgitation or stenosis, even though free tricuspid regurgitation is revealed by imaging studies.63 This is related to the large size and compliance of both the right atrium and atrialized RV, as well as the low right ventricular and pulmonary artery pressure. Congestive hepatosplenomegaly is commonly observed and may eventually lead to hepatic fibrosis.64

On auscultation, the most consistent finding is wide splitting of the first sound with accentuation of the delayed component caused by closing (or termination of motion65,66) of the large anterior tricuspid leaflet. Delayed tricuspid valve closure is probably mechanical rather than related to right bundle branch block, although Crews and colleagues considered it correlated with the latter.65,67 The large anterior leaflet is also responsible for an opening snap in diastole, and there may be an atrial fourth sound. The pulmonary component of the second sound is delayed and soft (in relation to the right bundle branch block) or absent when pulmonary artery pressure is low. A pansystolic murmur maximal at the lower left sternal edge is due to tricuspid regurgitation and is heard in about one-third of patients. Finally, there is usually a diastolic murmur, often low pitched and sometimes scratchy in quality, commencing with the opening snap and sometimes augmented by the fourth sound. The diastolic murmur is presumably due to movement of blood across the malformed tricuspid orifice.

# **Chest Radiography**

In classic Ebstein anomaly, the chest radiograph shows marked cardiomegaly with a rounded or boxlike cardiac contour beneath a narrow pedicle (Fig. 48.8). In the posteroanterior view the whole of the silhouette is then formed by the right atrium and RV, and because of their minimal excursion and the normal or oligemic lung fields, the silhouette has a peculiarly sharp edge. However, as with other features of the disease, there is wide variation in heart size. <sup>62</sup> In a few cases, it remains normal; in most, it is only moderately enlarged. <sup>59</sup>

# Electrocardiography

A right bundle branch pattern together with a relatively low-amplitude R wave in right-sided chest leads and right atrial hypertrophy are characteristic of the anomaly. According to Kumar and colleagues, height of the P wave varies inversely with arterial oxygen saturation (r = .82, P < .001). <sup>59</sup> In addition, the taller the P wave, the shorter the survival time (P < .001). Right ventricular hypertrophy does not occur in uncomplicated Ebstein anomaly, and inverted T waves in leads  $V_1$  to  $V_4$  are fairly common. Using intraluminal electrode catheters, Kastor and colleagues demonstrated prolonged intra–right atrial and infranodal conduction in patients with a large right atrium and well-defined atrialized ventricle. <sup>61</sup>

In approximately 14% of patients, the electrocardiogram shows type B (right-sided) WPW syndrome. In any cyanotic patient with this type of preexcitation, Ebstein anomaly should be considered. Supraventricular arrhythmias occur in more than half of patients, and they may be paroxysmal and recurrent. Paroxysmal atrial tachycardia, atrial fibrillation, and nodal rhythm can all occur, as can first-degree heart block. Serious ventricular arrhythmias resulting from right ventricular dilation may also occur. <sup>64</sup>

# **Echocardiography**

Two-dimensional echocardiography has become definitive for diagnosis and anatomic evaluation of Ebstein anomaly,  $^{68-71}$  (Fig. 48.9). It is possible to identify Ebstein anomaly by the characteristic degree of displacement of the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve (>8 mm · m $^{-2}$ ), variable degrees of leaflet tethering (i.e., failure of delamination), and the presence of an elongated, redundant anterior tricuspid valve leaflet.  $^{72,73}$  The feasibility of tricuspid repair rather than replacement can be determined with this method of imaging.  $^{71}$  A right-to-left atrial shunt and tricuspid regurgitation can be demonstrated using Doppler color flow interrogation.

Historically, M-mode echocardiography usually demonstrated delayed tricuspid valve closure and displacement of the tricuspid valve to the left. According to Giuliani and colleagues, the greater the delay in tricuspid valve closure, the more severe the disease. Wide excursion of the anterior leaflet, a decreased E-F slope, increased right ventricular dimensions, and paradoxical septal motion can also be demonstrated.

# **Cardiac Catheterization and Cineangiography**

Currently, cardiac catheterization and cineangiography are required only when specific hemodynamic details need to be identified,<sup>71</sup> or in patients over about age 40, when the coronary tree should be examined.

In the past, cardiac catheterization showed that the mean right atrial pressure is often modestly elevated, and the pressure pulse may have either a dominant a or v wave. These correlate poorly with the degree of tricuspid regurgitation or stenosis. An additional s wave preceding the v wave and interrupting the c wave is said to indicate tricuspid regurgitation. 59,62,67 The right atrial waveform is also recorded in the atrialized portion of the RV so that the tricuspid valve is noted to be displaced well toward the left of the spine. An electrode catheter may define the position and size of the atrialized right ventricular chamber. 18,74 Right ventricular systolic pressure is normal or low, and right ventricular enddiastolic pressure is frequently elevated, more so when there is the syndrome of chronic heart failure. It is uncommon to record an important gradient across the tricuspid valve, although Takayasu and colleagues noted this in 8 of 26 cases and considered that stenosis could still be important in its absence.<sup>59,75</sup>

When there is an interatrial communication, the shunt through it is usually right to left in association with systemic arterial desaturation and a reduced pulmonary blood flow ( ). The right-to-left shunt can be quantified by indicator dilution. The shunt may occasionally be left to right, and the resultant increase in may be associated with heart failure.76 Direction of shunting is no doubt influenced by right ventricular compliance.

In the newborn with Ebstein anomaly, a functional pulmonary "atresia" can occur.77 Thus, a normal pulmonary valve may fail to open following a right-sided injection of contrast media because of the combination of massive tricuspid regurgitation, poor right ventricular contraction, and a high neonatal pulmonary vascular resistance with or without a large shunt at ductal level. Highquality imaging is therefore necessary to distinguish this situation from true pulmonary atresia.

Cineangiography (see Figs. 48.3, 48.4, 48.6, 48.7) is usually diagnostic as long as there is important displacement and dysplasia of the septal or inferior leaflets.78 In the right anterior oblique projection, the conjoined inferior and anterior leaflets, and therefore the distal limit of the atrialized ventricle, can frequently be identified. Contrast media trapped beneath the inferior leaflet can indicate the degree of its adherence to, and level of its origin from, the diaphragmatic border of the RV, which may be notched at this point.11 The site of the true anulus is also visible more proximally. An injection into the functional RV enables tricuspid regurgitation to be assessed together with size and behavior of the functional ventricle. Angiography in left anterior oblique projection also permits visualization of the right-to-left shunt at atrial level, and any ventricular septal defects can be localized by left ventriculography.

# **Natural History**

Ebstein anomaly is uncommon, with a prevalence of 0.5% and occurring in less than 1% of subjects with congenital heart disease.79 The incidence is equal in both sexes. Familial Ebstein anomaly has been reported rarely.14,80,81 It occurs with more frequency in babies of mothers who are on lithium medication during pregnancy.82,83

The natural history is determined primarily by the three primary pathophysiologic features described earlier in the Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria section. These are: right ventricular abnormalities, tricuspid valve abnormalities and the presence of the accessory conduction pathways (WPW syndrome). Extent and severity of these relate to age at presentation; thus, age at presentation tends to correlate with prognosis. The presentation takes two general forms: symptomatic neonatal and infant presentation, and presentation in older children and adults.

### **Presentation during First Week of Life**

Ebstein anomaly is a recognized cause of death in utero.52,84,85 When severe tricuspid regurgitation accompanies Ebstein anomaly at birth, the elevated newborn pulmonary vascular resistance worsens it. Pulmonary blood flow may be ductal dependent. The resultant severe hypoxemia (right-to-left shunting through an ASD), coupled with marked right-sided heart failure, may also be accompanied by low cardiac output if the ASD is restrictive. This is because maintenance of normal cardiac output requires shunting at the atrial level through a nonrestrictive ASD. Low cardiac output may also result from abnormal ventricular interactions caused by paradoxical septal bowing and flattening of the LV. The hypoxemia may be aggravated by pulmonary dysfunction secondary to lung compression by marked cardiomegaly.

# **Presentation in Childhood and Adult Life**

Patients presenting after infancy often have mild symptoms. Prognosis is generally good, consistent with less severe right ventricular and tricuspid valve pathophysiology.93 Patients presenting in adult life are often acyanotic and have a normal-sized or mildly enlarged heart.84 However, even these patients, who report few symptoms, have clearly definable abnormalities. Without preexcitation, nearly half have episodic supraventricular tachyarrhythmias, although these usually produce few symptoms.94 Exercise tolerance measured by objective testing is often reduced.57,58,95,96

The degree of tricuspid regurgitation does not correlate with symptoms, but as time passes, left ventricular dysfunction often appears as symptoms develop.96

Death in this group of patients is often due to paroxysmal embolism or paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia, with heart failure appearing either much later or not at all. An atrial level communication increases the risk of paradoxical embolism, brain abscess, and sudden death.

# **Modes of Death**

Heart failure is the mode of death in somewhat less than half of patients who die of causes related to their heart disease after the neonatal period.40,59 Sudden death, presumably caused for the most part by arrhythmias other than those associated with WPW, occurs in about 60% (CL 43%–77%) of these patients.40,84,90 Sudden death correlates with marked cardiomegaly, rather than with New York Heart Association (NYHA) status.90 Cerebral abscess and paradoxical emboli account for most of the remaining deaths, particularly in patients older than about 50 years.84,97 Infective endocarditis is rare.

Tricuspid valve repair with ASD closure (or subtotal closure) is the preferred operation.98 The earliest repairs were designed to convert the tricuspid valve into a monoleaflet valve using the anterior leaflet to establish valve competence. The most important features that predict successful repair are a mobile, free leading edge of the anterior leaflet and attachment of more than 50% of it at the anatomic tricuspid anulus. A variety of maneuvers have been used to mobilize and extend this leading edge, with or without plication of the atrialized portion of the RV.

The method devised by Danielson and colleagues was the most popular early repair.68,99-102 It plicates the atrialized portion of the ventricle, narrows the tricuspid orifice in a selective manner, and results in a monoleaflet valve that coapts with the ventricular septum or diminutive septal leaflet and is usually competent or nearly so. # **Simple Atrial Septal Defect Closure**

Simple ASD closure may be done as described for closure in conjunction with valve replacement. However, this procedure is rarely indicated as a sole procedure in patients with EA.

# **Ventricular Exclusion in Critically Ill Neonates (Starnes Operation)**

The decision to pursue a single ventricle pathway or a biventricular repair is dependent on valve anatomy and institution/surgeon preference. The Starnes operation is designed to abolish the detrimental tricuspid regurgitation by fenestrated patch closure of the tricuspid valve, creating a nonrestrictive ASD, and establishing controlled pulmonary blood flow through a systemic-to-pulmonary artery shunt. A single ventricle palliation strategy can be pursued,94 or conversion to a two ventricle or a one-and-a-half ventricle repair can be considered later in infancy. It is important to leave the tricuspid valve leaflets untouched prior to patch closure of the tricuspid orifice if future tricuspid valve repair is desired.

The tricuspid valve orifice is closed by sewing into place an appropriately sized pericardial or Gore-Tex (preferred if future tricuspid repair is possible) patch, sewn just cephalad to the anatomic level of the tricuspid anulus. The coronary sinus is maintained on the atrial side of the patch. The patch is fenestrated with a 4-mm punch (Fig. 48.20). The right atrium is significantly reduced in size by removing a segment of the right atrial free wall and then is closed. If pulmonary regurgitation is present, the main pulmonary artery is closed.

A systemic-pulmonary shunt is created and can include a long central shunt of 3.5- or 4-mm polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) tube between the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk,94 or a 3.5- or 4-mm PTFE graft between the brachiocephalic–right carotid artery junction and the medial aspect of the right pulmonary artery. After rewarming has been accomplished, the remainder of the operation is completed in the usual manner. Delayed sternal closure is generally performed.

Dearani and colleagues at the Mayo Clinic38 recommend routine electrophysiologic evaluation preoperatively in children and adults undergoing Ebstein repair when preexcitation or other atrial tachyarrhythmias are present. # **Special Features of Postoperative Care**

Postoperative care strategies following valve repair includes relative hypovolemia (right atrial pressure 8–12 mmHg) and slight tachycardia (atrial pacing as needed) to a heart rate of 100-110 bpm to minimize diastolic filling time that keeps the RV smaller and minimizes stretch/tension of suture lines (see Chapter 4). Convalescence is generally rapid and normal when right ventricular dysfunction is not severely depressed and inotropic support is weaned over 24 hours. When moderate-severe right ventricular dysfunction is present, inotropic support is weaned more slowly over 48 to 72 hours. Short-term aspirin therapy is used when pericardial patch augmentation is used for tricuspid valve repair, and heparin and warfarin anticoagulation is used for bioprosthetic tricuspid replacement for 3 to 6 months followed by long-term aspirin therapy.

Critically ill neonates undergoing the Starnes operation require ICU care accorded to all neonates in whom pulmonary blood flow originates exclusively from the systemic circulation (see "General Care of Neonates and Infants" in Chapter 4). Additionally, pulmonary artery vasodilation is utilized with nitric oxide administration (see Chapter 4). Lowering pulmonary vascular resistance decreases the tendency to right ventricular distention (see "Special Situations and Controversies"). Further operations and procedures follow the Fontan pathway strategies (see Chapter 52).

# **Results After Repair**

### **Survival**

*Mortality After Surgery in the Neonatal Period.* Historically, hospital mortality has been high (.50%) for critically ill neonates who undergo emergency or urgent operation. However, with current techniques of intensive neonatal medical management and surgical and percutaneous therapy as needed, survival has significantly improved. Thus, in the 40-institution report of the PHIS, overall hospital mortality of neonates initially managed by percutaneous procedures with or without surgery was 22% (9/40; CL 15%–31%), for those managed by systemic-to–pulmonary shunting 27%, for those undergoing tricuspid valve repair 31%, and for those receiving a transplant 0%.92 Notably, half the neonates were medically managed, and 15% were managed by placement of a systemic-to-pulmonary system shunt, 9.6% by a percutaneous procedure, 8.7% by single ventricle palliation, and 3.9% by tricuspid valve repair.

Among 16 neonates undergoing surgical intervention between 1992 and 2005, Starnes and colleagues reported hospital mortality of 31% with a variety of surgical procedures.123 The best survival occurred in the 10 patients (80% hospital survival; CL 59%–93%) who underwent patch fenestration as part of a right ventricular exclusion procedure (Starnes operation). No late deaths were reported, all had undergone a subsequent bidirectional cavopulmonary anastomosis, and three had a completed Fontan. See additional discussion under "Special Situations and Controversies: Recent Trends on the Management of Critical Neonatal Ebstein Anomaly."

Knott-Craig and colleagues124 have pursued tricuspid valve repair and two-ventricle physiology, creating a competent monoleaflet valve based on the anterior leaflet, partially closing the ASD, and performing a reduction atrioplasty. Hospital survival among neonates exceeded 65%, but mortality was 55% (6/11; CL 35%–73%) among patients with anatomic pulmonary atresia.

An experience with surgical intervention in 24 neonates with Ebstein anomaly reported by Shinkawa and colleagues125 supports a policy of shunt only in the presence of refractory cyanosis without severe tricuspid regurgitation. In the presence of severe regurgitation and refractory heart failure, right ventricular exclusion provided possibly superior hospital (73%, 8/11; CL 53%– 87%) and late survival (63%) compared with tricuspid valve repair (25%, 1/4; CL 4.0%–62% for hospital and late survival).

*Early (Hospital) Death in Children and Adults.* Thirty-day mortality in the Mayo Clinic series of 539 patients (1972– 2006) undergoing valve repair or replacement was 6.1% (33 deaths; CL 5.0%–7.4%), but , 2% after 2001, which is representative of what can be accomplished in these often ill patients.48 Contemporary results of cone repair in 235 patients (134 children and 101 adults) have demonstrated low early mortality (0.4%).48

Augustin and colleagues reported two hospital deaths among 60 patients (3.3%; CL 1.1%–7.7%) (1974–1995).126 These results are improved over those obtained in an earlier era. A multicenter study from the European Congenital Heart Surgeons Association reported a hospital mortality of 8.3% (CL 5.4%–12%) among 96 patients undergoing tricuspid valve repair or replacement between 1992 and 2005.127 In patients operated on from September 1993 to September 2008, Malhotra and colleagues report no early (0%; CL 0%–3.3%) or late mortality in a group of 57 non-neonatal patients (median age 8 years, range 7 months to 40 years), among whom 95% had valve repairs and 54% had concomitant cavopulmonary shunts.109 Using near routine excision of atrialized RV, detachment and repair of the tricuspid valve leaflets, and anular plication, Wu and colleagues128 reported no early mortality (0%; CL 0%–2.3%) among 83 patients operated on between 1997 and 2006.2,128-134 Among 52 children aged 5 months to 12 years undergoing repair of EA at the Mayo Clinic between 1974 and 2003, early mortality was 5.8% (3/52; CL 2.6%–11%), with no deaths since 1984 (0/31; CL 0%–5.9%).135 Da Silva and colleagues reported a 2.5% hospital mortality (1/40; CL 0.4%– 8.2%) among 40 patients undergoing the cone procedure between 1993 and 2005.105

Mode of death is usually acute cardiac failure, probably related to preoperative RV enlargement and dysfunction. Improved methods of myocardial management, increasing prevalence and success of tricuspid valve repair rather than replacement, and possibly selective use of bidirectional cavopulmonary shunts have contributed to reduced early mortality.

*Time-Related Survival.* The seriousness of Ebstein anomaly is well demonstrated in a long-term follow-up study of a heterogeneous group of patients without major associated cardiac anomalies (n548), 18 of whom underwent 22 operations.90 Median survival for the overall group was 47 years. Survival was similar in patients who presented during the first 3 days of life but survived to 6 months of age, and those who presented after 3 months of age. All four patients in whom permanent atrial fibrillation developed died within 5 years.

Late deaths are uncommon after tricuspid valve repair (or replacement) and ASD closure, with about 80% of patients surviving long term.100,115,136 In the Mayo Clinic cohort of 539 patients, 10- and 20-year survival was 85% and 71%, respectively, with no important difference in late survival between patients undergoing valve repair vs. replacement.137 Among 52 children undergoing repair, 15-year survival was 90%.135 Deaths are often sudden and attributable to rhythm disturbances. Although it seems that late survival and freedom from reoperation would be better after successful valve repair than replacement, the Mayo Clinic analysis indicates no difference in late reoperation-free survival between patients receiving tricuspid valve repair vs. replacement among patients 12 years of age or older.135 However, among patients under age 12, reoperation-free survival is superior for those undergoing valve repair.137

Preoperative risk factors for late mortality include higher hematocrit (worse cyanosis), severe mitral valve regurgitation, prior cardiac operations, and moderate to severe reduction in right ventricular systolic function.137 Left ventricular dysfunction is also a risk factor for early and late mortality, despite the finding of improved left ventricular systolic function in most such patients following Ebstein anomaly repair.137

Intermediate-term survival and quality of life in patients undergoing cone repair have been excellent (Fig. 48.21).105,114,138

*Ventricular Function.* Contemporary results with the cone procedure from the Mayo Clinic demonstrate sustained reduction in tricuspid regurgitation (*P* , .0001), a progressive decline in right ventricular size (*P* , .0001), and late increase in right ventricular fractional area change after initial decline (*P* , .0001).48 These findings are consistent with other reports of favorable changes in the right ventricular remodeling at midterm follow-up (Fig. 48.22).114,139

Little is known about the long-term fate of right ventricular function after *neonatal surgery* for EA. However, Reemtsen and colleagues compared echocardiographic findings at 6 to 204 months (median 30 months) with those immediately prior to the Starnes procedure performed in the neonatal period. The Great Ormond Street ratio (see Table 48.2) and right ventricular size decreased prior to the bidirectional Glenn, indicating right ventricular regression.140 Left ventricular septal impingement on left ventricular cavity dimensions decreased an average of 38%, with normalization of left ventricular morphology and systolic function. These findings have important implications in predicting favorable outcomes after the Fontan operation if a singleventricle strategy is selected in the neonatal period.

### **Functional Status**

Most patients (about 85%) achieve good functional status (NYHA class I or II) and improved performance during objective exercise testing postoperatively, although many are severely limited preoperatively.48,95,141-143 Residual symptoms, when present, are usually related to troublesome, non-WPW supraventricular arrhythmias.115 Failure to plicate the atrialized portion of the RV has not been shown to decrease the probability of a good functional result.134 The effect of a bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt on late functional status has been incompletely studied, but favorable midterm functional status has been reported.109,144 Children with Ebstein anomaly following cone reconstruction have excellent quality of life comparable with healthy peers and significantly better than other children with chronic health conditions. Families of children with Ebstein anomaly can expect excellent quality of life, long-term health status, and social functioning following cone reconstruction.138

# **Postrepair Rhythm Disturbances**

Complete heart block is uncommon after tricuspid repair, despite the distorted morphology in the region of the bundle of His.

WPW has mostly been cured by concomitant sectioning of the accessory pathways (see Chapter 15), which can be accomplished without increasing operative risk.116,117 However, episodic non-WPW supraventricular tachycardia continues late postoperatively in about 20% of patients.39,115 Uncommonly, even after successful surgical relief of WPW syndrome, late sudden death occurs. The substrate for atrial arrhythmias is enhanced by the large right atrium, right ventricular dysfunction, and tricuspid regurgitation that frequently accompany EA late after operation. # **Results of Other Procedures**

# **Repair of Atrial Septal Defect Alone**

*Isolated closure of the ASD (usually percutaneous*) is indicated when there is a large left-to-right shunt ( . 2), with or without symptoms of heart failure, and less than moderate tricuspid regurgitation. Patients with moderate to severe tricuspid regurgitation and ASD should undergo surgical repair. The risk associated with simple ASD closure is low in properly selected patients. In the combined UAB-GLH experience from nearly 40 years ago, 9 patients with either left-to-right or right-to-left shunts and competent but abnormal tricuspid valves underwent simple ASD closure, with no hospital deaths (CL 0%–19%). It is surprising that closure of the ASD was well tolerated in the face of preoperative right-to-left shunting (5 of 9 patients), but this was also the experience at the Mayo Clinic and has been the case in pulmonary stenosis with intact ventricular septum (see Chapter 36).151 When a left-to-right shunt is present preoperatively, late results are generally good. Thus, in the combined UAB-GLH experience, four patients in this category were hospital survivors; three had good long-term results, and one had WPW syndrome with episodic arrhythmias.

# **Tricuspid Valvotomy**

The rare occurrence of tricuspid stenosis invites tricuspid valvotomy, but severe regurgitation may result.132 If the Carpentier technique of fenestration to restore or substitute for interchordal spaces is not satisfactory, valve replacement is indicated.39

# **Pulmonary Valvotomy**

Pulmonary valvotomy can have low hospital mortality in patients with Ebstein anomaly when indications are appropriate. This procedure may be lifesaving in neonates, because it decreases the degree of tricuspid regurgitation.

# **Indications for Operation**

Neonates presenting in extremis, usually in the first week of life, are immediately intubated, paralyzed, and begun on nitric oxide and prostaglandin E1, appropriate catecholamine support, aggressive treatment of metabolic acidosis, and other intensive therapy (see "Special Situations and Controversies"). Once stabilized, a Starnes procedure is usually preferred, particularly if the patient has a Celermajer grade of 3 or 4 (see "Special Situations and Controversies") or if there is concomitant anatomic pulmonary atresia. If the patient's condition does not stabilize, operation is performed at an exceptionally high risk. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) should be considered in such circumstances, either before to facilitate resuscitation or following emergency operation. In the setting of more hemodynamic stability, a biventricular repair can be performed; decision-making is often dependent on surgeon experience.

In the absence of severe cyanosis or symptoms of heart failure, medical management is advisable. Surgical intervention should be considered with the development of heart failure symptoms, worsening cyanosis, decreased exercise tolerance, progressive increase in heart size, reduction in ventricular systolic function, or the appearance of atrial or ventricular tachyarrhythmias.38 The presence of severe tricuspid regurgitation and moderate cyanosis secondary to right-to-left shunting through the ASD should prompt earlier surgical intervention. Severe limitations and severe hypoxia are not contraindications to repair, although increased hospital mortality in patients in functional NYHA class IV argues strongly in favor of advising operation before disability is this advanced. The correlation between sudden death and cardiomegaly argues for operation when the cardiothoracic ratio reaches 0.60 or greater, regardless of symptoms.90

In children and young adults, valve repair is preferable and can almost always be performed since the introduction of the cone technique and its modifications. Tricuspid valve replacement is reserved for infrequent situations when successful repair appears unlikely.

In patients with WPW syndrome that is producing life-threatening arrhythmia, the accessory conduction pathways should be ablated preoperatively in the cath lab, and the valve defect should be treated following percutaneous ablation.2,119,152 The onset of atrial fibrillation or flutter are often less well tolerated in the presence of severe tricuspid regurgitation, cardiomegaly, and cyanosis, and can then be an added indication for operation on the valve.

# **Special Situations and Controversies**

### **Morphology**

Confusion as to morphologic classification has arisen in some infants who have pulmonary atresia and minor downward displacement (or adherence) of the septal tricuspid leaflet origin (see Chapter 36).136 When this is the only Ebstein-like leaflet abnormality present, the defect is not classified or treated as Ebstein anomaly. In a number of infants with pulmonary atresia, however, the tricuspid valve shows additional typical and often florid features of EA in association with severe regurgitation. Such patients form one variety of complex EA.

# **Management of Critically Ill Neonates with Ebstein Anomaly**

When neonates with Ebstein anomaly present with severe cyanosis, prostaglandin infusion is initiated to maintain or restore ductal patency. Urgent echocardiographic evaluation is performed to establish the diagnosis and evaluate the right ventricular outflow tract. If antegrade flow through the pulmonary valve is absent, the pulmonary atresia may be functional (elevated pulmonary vascular resistance coupled with severe tricuspid regurgitation and right ventricular dysfunction) or anatomic.153 A trial of inhaled nitric oxide is warranted to lower pulmonary vascular resistance and promote antegrade flow through the pulmonary valve if it is patent.153 If nitric oxide is helpful in bridging a marginal patient through the neonatal period, the addition of oral sildenafil may be useful.154 Other standard measures for critical ill neonates with Ebstein anomaly include placement of umbilical arterial and venous catheters and mechanical ventilation with the minimum effective airway pressure and tidal volumes of 10 to 15 mL · kg<sup>2</sup><sup>1</sup> to recruit areas of atelectasis.155,156 If the right ventricular outflow tract is functional, a trial of prostaglandin weaning is indicated. If Sao2 is maintained at 70% or greater without heart failure, surgical intervention may not be necessary.

If prostaglandin weaning is not successful, two situations may occur. If the circulation is stable without evidence of heart failure and the only problem is unacceptable cyanosis, a systemic-to– pulmonary shunt may be the only necessary procedure. However, if heart failure and circulatory compromise persist, the tricuspid valve itself must be addressed, either through the Starnes procedure or valve reconstruction (see "Technique of Operation").

In planning for continued medical therapy versus the likelihood of needing surgical intervention, calculation of the Celermajer score86 is useful in predicting risk of death (see Table 48.2).

# **Recent Trends on the Management of Critical Neonatal Ebstein Anomaly**

Ebstein anomaly diagnosed in fetal life is associated with high perinatal mortality. A recent multicenter study showed that the risk of fetal demise or neonatal death was approximately 45%. Additionally, multivariable analysis showed that the lack of antegrade pulmonary valve flow or the presence of pulmonary regurgitation at the time of diagnosis were significant hemodynamic risk factors. Pulmonary regurgitation with circular shunt was the most powerful predictive risk factor for perinatal mortality.157 In a recent Society of Thoracic Surgeons database study by Holst and coauthors examining surgical treatment of neonatal Ebstein anomaly, overall mortality was 27.4%, and the mortality related to the primary tricuspid valve repair was 39.6%.158 This high mortality in a contemporary era emphasizes the need for continued improvement in this challenging group of patients. An important issue is the presence of a circular shunt, which is caused by the combination of both pulmonary and tricuspid regurgitation, resulting in continuous retrograde ductal flow and intracardiac recirculation. The circular shunt provokes low tissue perfusion and metabolic acidosis. Therefore, effective treatment of fetuses and neonates with Ebstein anomaly with a circular shunt is an urgent task that should start soon after the fetal diagnosis. The high fetal demise rate associated with the circular shunt has encouraged the use of nonsteroid antiinflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) given to the mother, which constricts the fetal ductus arteriosus, improving fetal survival.159-161 Circular shunt in neonates creates hemodynamic instability, with severe hypoxia and low cardiac output syndrome. It requires intubation, paralysis, inotropic support, and surgery usually includes a Starnes procedure.161 Newborns with Ebstein anomaly requiring prostaglandin or mechanical ventilation, worsening cyanosis or heart failure, and anatomic pulmonary atresia will require surgical intervention during the neonatal period.

The application of cone repair in neonatal Ebstein anomaly should only be considered in highly selected situations given procedure complexity, delicate leaflet tissues and the associated lung immaturity.162,163 When neonatal cone procedure is being considered, it should be applied only to a small, ideal subgroup of older neonates (over 2 weeks old) that are hemodynamically stable, have favorable tricuspid valve morphology, have a reasonably-sized functional RV with good systolic contractility, and excellent pulmonary artery and valve anatomy. Finally, an occasional situation can result in a good outcome and should only be advised with an experienced medical and surgical team. The primary cone repair in a newborn is shown in Fig. 48.25A–D. In general, if hemodynamic stability can be achieved with progressive weaning of inotropes and respiratory support, surgery should be delayed until later in infancy.

A PDA stent can be the initial intervention in selected situations when there is inadequate forward pulmonary flow and a competent pulmonary valve, causing cyanosis but without severe cardiomegaly, or septal deviation to the LV. The PDA stenting procedure can stabilize oxygen saturations allowing cone repair later on infancy.

However, as described earlier, most newborns with Ebstein anomaly presenting with heart failure should be addressed with the Starnes procedure.94 This operation decompresses the RV, allows more space for the lungs and is a more predictable and reproducible operation in the hands of most pediatric heart surgeons. The decompression of the malformed RV ameliorates the ventricular septal impingement to the left. The Starnes procedure facilitates filling of the LV thereby improving its capability of supplying systemic and pulmonary circulations.123 Any pulmonary regurgitation should be eliminated with PA ligation, and the coronary sinus must stay on the atrial side of the patch to ensure effective decompression of the RV. Reduction atrioplasty opens space inside the chest for lung expansion/development. The modified Starnes procedure is indicated for neonates who are hemodynamically unstable or require preoperative resuscitation with ECMO support. The advantageous anatomy and physiology of single-ventricle palliation with the Starnes procedure is translated into outcome improvement. Starnes' group has reported survival rates of 87%, 87%, and 81% at, respectively, 1, 5, and 10 years, on 24 patients who underwent the modified operation with the fenestrated tricuspid valve patch.164 This group also highlights the important anatomic changes that occur when the RV is effectively decompressed, such as, nonfunctional RA and RV regress in size and the ventricular septum continuing to shift away from the LV.140

# **Cone Repair After the Starnes Procedure**

The Starnes approach excludes the RV from the pulmonary circulation, and historically, these patients were traditionally committed to the single ventricle repair pathway164 and eventual Fontan procedure. However, it is possible to take down the tricuspid patch later on infancy and apply the cone technique to rehabilitate the RV in patients with Ebstein anomaly and pulmonary atresia, achieving one-and-a-half- or two-ventricle repair.165 It was also demonstrated that patients with fetal circular shunt physiology who underwent the Starnes procedure as a newborn, can also benefit from the rehabilitation of the RV and the pulmonary valve, resulting in a two-ventricle physiology.161,166

Bearing in mind that the cone repair can follow the Starnes procedure, it is preferable to use a Gore-Tex patch to exclude the RV since it causes less adhesions to the leaflets as compared to bovine pericardium facilitating its takedown during the cone repair. Furthermore, this patch should be sutured above the tricuspid valve anulus and in the Todaro's ligament at the septal area but maintaining the coronary sinus to drain into the right atrium. These technical measures aim to facilitate patch removal without damaging the tricuspid valve leaflets or the AV node at the time of cone repair. The images obtained by successive echocardiograms demonstrate the cardiac evolution of a neonatal Ebstein submitted to the Starnes procedure and later to the cone repair (Fig. 48.26A–C).

When discussing the best surgical management of the critically ill neonate with Ebstein anomaly, the Starnes operation can be reproduced in most pediatric heart centers and provides a consistent physiology.167 On the other hand, cone repair in infancy or cone repair following the Starnes procedure can be achieved in referral centers with surgeon/team expertise. Another new and relevant point to consider is that Starnes palliation is no longer considered a final commitment to a Fontan circulation.168 The Starnes procedure helps the critically ill newborn with Ebstein anomaly to survive the neonatal period, preparing them for more definitive procedures in the future when the clinical condition is more stable.

# **Type of Prosthesis for Tricuspid Valve Replacement**

A stent-mounted glutaraldehyde-preserved porcine bioprosthesis (more commonly) or bovine pericardial bioprosthesis may be considered optimal in older adults with Ebstein anomaly, but both are subject to the usual problems of a bioprosthesis.169 Mechanical prostheses are rarely considered in Ebstein anomaly given the combined presence of poor right ventricular systolic function and low right atrial pressure, which in combination often results in poor disc motion and valve thrombosis even in the setting of adequate warfarin anticoagulation. The relatively large prosthesis requires a high target INR that is undesirable for the patient.170 An analysis of the Mayo Clinic experience identified use of a bioprosthetic valve (vs. mechanical prosthetic valve) as an independent predictor of long-term survival.171,172 An allograft, either a stent-mounted pulmonary or aortic valve, or a directly attached mitral allograft supported by an anuloplasty ring, could also be considered.123,173,174

# **Indications for Plication (or Resection) of Atrialized Ventricle**

While plication of the atrialized RV is generally performed at the time of tricuspid repair, indications for plication of an atrialized ventricle at the time of tricuspid valve replacement are controversial. Potential *advantages* of plication (or resection) include (1) reducing the size of the dilated dyskinetic (inferior wall) RV, potentially increasing transit of blood by improved systolic function; (2) improving left ventricular function by alleviating left ventricular compression and septal bowing; (3) moving the anterior leaflet closer to the septal leaflet and septum; and (4) providing more space for lung expansion, especially in neonates. The major potential disadvantage relates to possible distortion of or injury to the right coronary artery and its branches, which could worsen right ventricular function and contribute to ventricular arrhythmias.48 Some suggest that omitting routine plication compromises right ventricular function postoperatively175,176; others believe that plication is indicated only when the atrialized portion of the RV is moderately enlarged, very thin walled and aneurysmal (probably about 10% to 15% of cases).130,132,177 Plication may be considered unnecessary in even these cases, because satisfactory right ventricular function has resulted without it.65,115,178

Plication of the atrialized ventricle is performed routinely in some methods of tricuspid valve repair, particularly cone repair, for which it is an integral part of the anuloplasty portion of the repair. However, several reported techniques emphasize the need for only partial plication near the functional anulus109 as part of the anuloplasty procedure, without needing to plicate the remainder of the atrialized portion.

# **Excision or Plication of Right Atrial Wall**

Excision or plication of the right atrial wall was suggested by Timmis and colleagues, who considered it important "to promote atrial emptying and lessen the likelihood of clot formation" when a mechanical prosthesis was used.176 Danielson and colleagues incorporate excision of the right atrial wall routinely in their repair.101,131 The simplicity of this maneuver during valve replacement or repair makes it an attractive step when the right atrium is markedly enlarged.

# **Role of Bidirectional Cavopulmonary Anastomosis and One-and-a-Half Ventricle Repair**

The vast majority of patients with Ebstein anomaly can undergo successful biventricular repair. However, creating a "one-and-ahalf" ventricle repair can be an effective strategy if reducing the tricuspid anulus to a *z* value of less than 22 (or about 2.5 cm in the adult) is necessary to eliminate important tricuspid regurgitation in an infant or child with normal pulmonary vascular resistance. In this instance, functional tricuspid stenosis is managed by constructing a bidirectional cavopulmonary anastomosis with division of the superior vena cava at its junction with the right atrium (see Chapter 52).109 This strategy may also be useful when the RV is severely dysfunctional,112 as long as the left atrial and pulmonary artery pressures are low. By reducing the right ventricular stroke volume required to maintain effective cardiac output, right ventricular work requirement is reduced and preload of the LV optimized.109,113 Furthermore, the adverse effect on left ventricular function by a massively enlarged RV can be ameliorated.104 Bidirectional cavopulmonary anastomosis may also reduce residual tricuspid regurgitation.179 Dearani and colleagues have recommended the following criteria for constructing a bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt in the setting of severe right ventricular enlargement or dysfunction: left ventricular end-diastolic pressure less than 15 mmHg, transpulmonary gradient less than 10 mmHg, and mean pulmonary artery pressure less than 18 to 20 mmHg.104,113 Malhotra and colleagues109 recommend performing a bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt if, at the end of the repair, right atrial pressure is more than 1.5 times left atrial pressure.

The potential disadvantages of this approach include possible facial suffusion secondary to elevated jugular venous pressure and pulsations in the head and neck veins, development of collateral veins and pulmonary arteriovenous fistulas, and compromise of access for electrophysiologic testing/ablation or pacemaker leads.

Malhotra and colleagues109 reported an experience of valve repair in 54 of 57 patients with Ebstein anomaly, of whom 55% received a bidirectional cavopulmonary shunt (half of these in adults). Freedom from subsequent valve replacement was 92% at 4 years, with 95% of patients in NYHA class I. Van Arsdell and colleagues reported 90% 10-year survival following one-and-ahalf ventricle repair.150

### **Indications for Single-Ventricle Pathway**

The potential advantages of right ventricular exclusion (Starnes procedure) for severely ill neonates with Ebstein anomaly are discussed under "Results after Repair: Survival." Such patients, if they survive the initial neonatal procedure, have a high likelihood of survival through the bidirectional Glenn stage. At 2 to 3 years of age a decision can be made as to whether Starnes takedown with tricuspid repair and a 1.5 ventricle circulation can be considered as an alternative to Fontan completion.

A particularly high-risk morphologic subset of Ebstein anomaly is hearts with the combination of severe tricuspid regurgitation and anatomic pulmonary atresia.123,124 Symptomatic infants and children with this combination are likely best treated with a single-ventricle strategy. Although Polimenakos and colleagues applied this strategy successfully in an *adult* Ebstein patient with severe RV dysfunction, tricuspid regurgitation, and RV outflow obstruction,180 the Fontan pathway strategy is rarely indicated beyond infancy.

# **Special Anatomic Types of Ebstein Anomaly**

Complex anatomical subtypes of Ebstein anomaly such as extreme rotation of the tricuspid valve into the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT), or with hypoplastic tricuspid valve leaflets are more difficult to repair. However, the cone procedure, with some technical maneuvers, can effectively address these anatomical variations.

Ebstein anomaly with extreme rotation of the tricuspid valve into the RVOT were considered not amenable to tricuspid valve repair with the monocusp repair technique137; however, it can be repaired using the cone technique149 with some technical maneuvers to achieve an adequate valve size. The RVOT distal attachment related to the anterior and medial septal leaflets must be cut. The orifice exiting toward the RVOT is closed horizontally during the cone construction. This maneuver allows the leaflet to move toward the cone center, helping the tricuspid valve closure mechanism. The cone technique can also treat patients with Ebstein anomaly with Carpentier's type D anatomy by taking down the leaflets as a single piece, retaining only the distal direct linear attachment of the leaflet to the RV. Vertical fenestrations (autologous neochordae) are provided at the distal third of this large leaflet. Then the lateral and medial edges of this leaflet can be sutured together, creating a cone-like structure. If insufficient leaflet tissue is present for an adequate-sized cone, autologous pericardial patch augmentation can be applied (see later). As in all other cases, any holes/fenestrations in the proximal two-thirds of the cone's membranous tissues are closed to achieve a similar circumferential depth and to prevent regurgitation leaks. Furthermore, natural, or surgically created fenestrations should be present at the distal onethird of the cone to permit unrestricted blood flow in diastole.

Patients with hypoplastic tricuspid valve leaflets may require leaflet augmentation to achieve an effective repair (i.e., adequate circumference with the cone procedure). The Mayo Clinic group initially reported using CorMatrix membrane or autologous pericardium to enlarge either the anterior or the septal leaflet. The use of CorMatrix has been abandoned since marked leaflet thickening was observed early on in a few patients that required reoperation. At the Children's Hospital of Pittsburgh, fresh or glutaraldehydetreated autologous pericardium is used to enlarge the septal leaflet. # **Important Lessons Learned on the Cone Procedure**

Creating a competent tricuspid valve using the cone technique requires (1) extensive mobilization of the displaced and tethered leaflets all the way to their leading edges, (2) precise side-to-side approximations to minimize dehiscence that result in the absence of any commissures, (3) reinforcement of any anular reduction sites to minimize dehiscence, and (4) ensuring an adequate-sized cone if autologous pericardial patch augmentation is applied to minimize tricuspid stenosis. The septal leaflet is frequently incorporated into the septal aspect of the cone, in combination with the inferior tricuspid leaflet. Adhering to these principles is essential to minimize the incidence of tricuspid regurgitation or stenosis. Failure to honor these concepts will result in leaflet coaptation failure or excessive tension along suture lines due to pulling of the leaflet that remained improperly attached to the free right ventricular wall.
