## **Introduction**

Cardiac imaging has undergone significant advancements since the start of the 21st century. The last two decades have seen improvements in all imaging modalities used to visualize the heart, including echocardiography, cardiac computed tomography (CT), and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). These advancements, including new equipment and techniques, offer better image quality, increased accuracy, and a wider range of capabilities. As a result, more sophisticated imaging options are now available for evaluating cardiac anatomy, ventricular function, and blood flow across valves, leading to a deeper understanding of coronary artery disease, heart failure, and structural heart diseases.1 The integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning in cardiac imaging has even led to the discovery of previously unnoticed disease patterns and a more personal approach to clinical care.2

The evolution of cardiac imaging has also revolutionized the field of cardiac surgery by streamlining the preoperative planning phase and guiding complex procedures. Preoperatively, these imaging modalities are used to identify, quantify, and understand the underlying cardiac disease, informing surgical decisions. Intraoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) provides realtime monitoring of underlying pathophysiology, guiding beating heart procedures, supporting surgical planning, and allowing for assessment of surgical results. Cardiac imaging is also crucial in the postoperative period to diagnose and manage complications. Lastly, noninvasive imaging is often employed to evaluate and monitor long-term surgical results.

Intraoperative TEE was first introduced in the 1980s, initially only for monitoring left ventricular (LV) function before and after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).3 Since then, echocardiography has become an essential tool for the preoperative and intraoperative management of patients undergoing a range of cardiac procedures.4 In the last two decades, three-dimensional (3D) echocardiography has emerged as a means of quantifying LV function and structural heart disease, offering more accurate and reproducible measurements of LV volumes and systolic function.5 In 2003, guidelines for the use of echocardiography, including TEE, were presented by the American College of Cardiology, American Society of Echocardiography (ASE), and American Heart Association,6 building on previous guidelines from the American Society of Anesthesiologists and Society of Cardiovascular Anesthesiologists (SCA).7 Echocardiography is now often the first choice for cardiac evaluation due to its high resolution, portability, affordability, and lack of radiation exposure.

# **Echocardiography**

#### **General Principles**

Echocardiographic images are constructed by transmitting highfrequency sound waves from a transducer composed of piezoelectric crystals. These waves reflect off cardiac structures, and the same transducer receives the returning signals. By knowing when the signal was sent, the speed of the sound in the tissue, and the time it takes for the reflected signal to return to the transducer, the position of the structure responsible for the reflection can be calculated. An image from these signals can thus be created. The quality of the image relies on many factors, including the media through which the sound is traveling, the orientation of the structures in relation to the ultrasound beam, and the composition of the structure. Sound travels incredibly well through water and blood, reasonably well through tissue, but poorly through air and bones. Therefore, echocardiographers use windows (between the ribs, sternum, and lungs) such as parasternal, apical, subcostal, suprasternal, transesophageal, or intracardiac to ensure good penetration of ultrasound. The fact that sound transmitted from a TEE transducer has less distance to travel (which means less signal lost to scatter) and mainly travels through muscle and blood (and rarely air) explains why the quality of TEE images is usually better than transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) imaging.8

Echocardiographic displays include M-mode, two-dimensional (2D), and 3D imaging. The M-mode echocardiogram has superior temporal resolution and can be thought of as a display of the motion of a single cut through the heart over time (Fig. 6.1). It is now used primarily to quantify the timing of intracardiac events.

Two-dimensional echocardiography (Fig. 6.2) provides a display with better spatial resolution and excellent temporal resolution. Three-dimensional echocardiography provides the best display of the spatial relationships of various structures and flow patterns, and its resolution continues to improve.

Doppler echocardiography and color flow imaging, which provide reliable hemodynamic assessment, not only have replaced many invasive hemodynamic procedures but can also be superior to them under certain circumstances. Intracardiac blood flow velocities and myocardial motion are assessed by the Doppler principle, first described by the Austrian physicist Christian Doppler in 1842. The wavelength of sound reflecting off moving blood particles will be shorter if blood is moving toward the transducer and longer if moving away. This difference in frequency, known as the Doppler shift, can be used in the calculation of blood velocity (Fig. 6.3).9

The angle of the ultrasound beam and the direction of blood flow are critically important in this calculation; thus, the beam should be aligned as parallel to the blood flow as possible. Several modalities of Doppler echocardiography can be used. Pulsed wave (PW) Doppler has excellent spatial resolution, permitting sampling of local blood flow velocities at a specific region (sample volume). This modality is particularly useful for assessing the relatively low-velocity flows associated with LV outflow tract, pulmonary venous flow, or left atrial appendage flow (Fig. 6.4).

Continuous wave Doppler records signal along the entire length of the ultrasound beam and permits measurement of very high velocities, such as in aortic stenosis (Fig. 6.5).

#### **Transthoracic Echocardiography**

*Standard Views.* TTE is a widely available, reproducible, noninvasive ultrasound imaging modality, often used as a first-line cardiac imaging modality. Comprehensive echocardiographic examination typically involves integrating imaging of the heart from multiple orientations to fully visualize cardiac structures. There are four standard transducer positions: the parasternal, apical, subcostal, and suprasternal windows (Fig. 6.7).

**Parasternal Long-Axis (PLAX) View.** The TTE examination usually begins with the patient in the left lateral decubitus position and by placing the transducer in the left parasternal region, usually in the third or fourth left intercostal space.10 From this position, images can be acquired along the long and short axes (Fig. 6.8).

**Parasternal Short-Axis (PSAX) View.** After acquiring the PLAX images, PSAX views of the heart are obtained by rotating the transducer clockwise (Fig. 6.9).

Superior and inferior transducer manipulations permit the demonstration of cardiac structures at the base (aortic valve), basal LV (mitral valve), mid-LV (papillary muscle), and apical LV levels (Figs. 6.10, 6.11, and 6.12).11

Parasternal views are used for the assessment of LV wall motion and coronary artery disease (Fig. 6.13).

**Apical Views.** The next set of images is acquired from the apical window (Figs. 6.14, 6.15, and 6.16).

**Subcostal Views.** The subcostal views are obtained by placing the transducer in the midline, with the patient supine. This position allows visualization of the liver parenchyma, hepatic vessels, and inferior vena cava (IVC), as well as of the right ventricle (RV), the inferior interventricular septum, and anterolateral LV walls (Fig. 6.17).

#### **Intraoperative Transesophageal Echocardiography**

The use of intraoperative TEE began in 1971, with the measurement of flow in the aortic arch,15 followed by the use of M-mode and 2D imaging.16 Since then, TEE has become the main imaging modality in the operating room and a critical component in surgical planning and timely assessment of surgical intervention. It also enables the determination of LV systolic function early after cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and provides an opportunity to correct suboptimal surgical results before leaving the operating room. For example, intraoperative TEE has particular prognostic importance in patients undergoing mitral valve (MV) repair and can help identify the need for additional pump runs as a result of findings.17

TEE provides superior quality in imaging of the heart and great vessels owing to excellent ultrasound penetration from the esophagus. The probe location directly behind the heart and associated structures allows for better image resolution and avoidance of transthoracic imaging artifacts.18 Since its initial development, the field of TEE has improved significantly, and the current TEE transducer is flexible, better controlled, and provides up to 28 views,19,20 utilizing four levels of imaging (Fig. 6.26). Standard TEE views are illustrated in Figs. 6.27-6.35.

The general goals of intraoperative echocardiography are displayed in Fig. 6.36.

#### **Application of Echocardiography to Surgical Practice**

*Assessment of Mitral Valve Regurgitation.* Echocardiography is the primary method for diagnosing and making treatment decisions for patients with MR. The initial evaluation includes assessing the anatomy and movement of the valve, as well as the size and function of the left ventricle, to identify the cause of the regurgitation, particularly if the valve dysfunction is due to a structural or functional problem.

The pathophysiologic triad of MV diseases, first described by Carpentier and colleagues in the 1980s, includes the *etiology* of the disease, the *specific lesions* caused by the disease, and the *dysfunction* it creates in the MV apparatus.27 This description still helps clinicians characterize different types of MV regurgitation (MR). MR is commonly classified as primary MR, a disease of one or more components of the valve apparatus, or secondary MR, a disease secondary to left atrial or LV dysfunction.28 A useful scheme for designating regions of the anterior and posterior leaflets is shown in Fig. 6.40.

**Aortic Dissection.** Aortic dissection is typically seen as a fluctuating intimal flap (Fig. 6.56) and should be distinguished from artifacts often seen in the aorta (Fig. 6.57), which can make the diagnosis challenging.

For example, artifacts caused by strong reflections from the aortic sinutubular junction, known as side-lobe artifacts, may be misinterpreted as flaps associated with aortic dissection.35 Mirror image artifacts in the aorta are another common artifact seen on echocardiographic images, which can be caused by the reflection of the ultrasound beam off the interfaces of the aortic wall, such as the intima-media and adventitia layers. This results in the creation of an inverted or "mirror image" of the true aortic lumen on the echocardiographic images. Moreover, a combination of different imaging modalities, such as CT scan, MRI, and TEE, might be used to confirm the diagnosis of aortic dissection. TEE has excellent sensitivity in diagnosing aortic dissection (99%)36 and is very accurate in determining the type and extent of the lesion. The intraoperative TEE study should demonstrate the origin of the dissection flap, describe the type and extent of the dissection, and evaluate aortic valve function.

**Aortic Aneurysm.** Echocardiography is used to evaluate the presence and degree of aortic dilation, as well as to assess the involvement of the aortic valve. In patients with a bicuspid aortic valve, routine measurement of aortic dimension is necessary.37 In addition to dilation, the shape of the aortic sinuses and sinutubular junction may be altered in some disease states. The measurement of aortic diameter on echocardiography is accurate and repeatable when care is taken to obtain a true diameter in the center of the vessel, appropriate gain settings are used, and standard measurement conventions are followed. In patients with aortic dilation, it is important to measure aortic diameter at multiple locations and to specify the measurement site and timing in the cardiac cycle. The aorta is pulsatile, and the change in dimension from end-diastole to end-systole is dependent on aortic compliance. Aortic compliance may be significantly altered in different disease states, but a typical normal change in aortic diameter during the cardiac cycle is 2 to 3 mm.38 Endsystolic measurements are often used in children, but in adults, measurements are most reliable at end-diastole because the timing in the cardiac cycle is reproducible, and end-diastolic measurements are least affected by aortic compliance or loading conditions. Measurements (Fig. 6.22B) are made at end-diastole at the:

- Aortic anulus
- Aortic sinus level
- Sinutubular junction
- Ascending aorta
- Aortic arch
- Descending thoracic aorta

Although not all measurements are required in every patient, quantitatively evaluating the degree and seriousness of aortic dilation is extremely beneficial when monitoring and treating patients who have chronic, progressive aortic dilation and aneurysm. Echocardiographic imaging of aortic dimensions correlates well with measurements from CT scans (Fig. 6.58) and also can detect associated aortic valve regurgitation (Fig. 6.59, Table 6.2).

*Cardiac Masses, Tumors, and Thrombi.* Cardiac masses include tumors (primary benign, malignant, and metastatic), which are infrequent, as well as thrombi (LV, left atrial, and right-sided heart thrombi), which are far more common. Advances in echocardiography, including real-time 3D echocardiography, have enabled clinicians to better refine the differential diagnosis of cardiac masses. The echocardiographic features of different cardiac masses are important clues for making an accurate diagnosis, which is crucial for management decisions, including surgical intervention. Common intracardiac locations of cardiac tumors are illustrated in Fig. 6.60.

As discussed in Chapter 17, myxomas (Fig. 6.61) and papillary fibroelastomas (Fig. 6.62) are the most common benign tumors of the heart. Malignant tumors may be primary (e.g., angiosarcoma) or metastatic and infiltrative, as seen in the echocardiographic images in Fig. 6.63.

Intracardiac thrombi may occur in any cardiac chamber and are often confused with cardiac tumors. The most common site for thrombus with systemic embolic potential is the left atrium, and the typical appearance of a thrombus within the left atrial appendage is shown in Fig. 6.38.

*Mechanical Complications of Myocardial Infarction.* Echocardiography is the preferred method for initially evaluating a postmyocardial infarction patient who is suspected to have mechanical complications (see Chapter 10). It helps in quickly assessing structural and hemodynamic aspects, which are crucial in the management of acute MI mechanical complications.

**Papillary Muscle Rupture.** Papillary muscle rupture is a rare but serious complication of acute MI, occurring in 1% to 3% of acute MIs and having a mortality rate of 80% with medical therapy alone.39 The posteromedial papillary muscle, which is supplied by a single coronary artery, is more likely to rupture compared to the anterolateral papillary muscle, which has a dual coronary supply.40 A high index of suspicion is necessary to identify patients with significant MR associated with an acute MI, and echocardiography is crucial in differentiating the cause of MR and ruling out other causes of a new systolic murmur. Echocardiography can reveal features such as a papillary muscle head moving freely into the left atrium (Fig. 6.66).

LV chamber size is typically normal, but LV function may be hyperdynamic due to a sudden decrease in afterload, and regional wall motion abnormalities may be difficult to detect.41 The color Doppler assessment usually shows an eccentric MR, which may result in an underestimation of the severity of MR. Patients with papillary muscle rupture usually present with significant distress and hemodynamic compromise, requiring TEE for a definitive diagnosis. The main management options are afterload reduction and emergency surgery.42

**Ventricular Septal Rupture.** Rupture of the ventricular septum following acute MI is rare, occurring in less than 1% of all heart attacks.43 An echocardiographic examination must carefully examine both regions of the ventricular septum: posterior-basal and apical. If there are severe wall motion abnormalities in the apical ventricular septum, a postinfarction ventricular septal rupture (VSR) should be suspected, even if visualization of the defect is difficult. Color Doppler imaging usually shows a flow from the left ventricle to the RV (Fig. 6.67).

The peak flow velocity across the site of the rupture, measured by continuous-wave Doppler, corresponds to the pressure difference between the left ventricle and RV and can be used to estimate the right ventricular systolic pressure. Pulsed-wave and continuous-wave Doppler are highly effective in locating postinfarction VSRs. It is also important to note that the size of the infarct and residual LV systolic function affect the magnitude of the left-to-right shunt and the intensity of the systolic murmur.

**Left Ventricular Free Wall Rupture.** LV free-wall rupture is the second leading cause of death in patients with acute MI, following cardiogenic shock.43 It often presents as a sudden event with acute cardiac tamponade due to the extravasation of blood into the pericardial space. Echocardiography is the preferred diagnostic tool, and any pericardial effusion with hemodynamic compromise should raise suspicion of LV free-wall rupture. If uncertainty persists, microbubble contrast administration can be done to confirm the diagnosis.44 LV apical aneurysms can develop from scarring and thinning of the heart wall and may also form pseudoaneurysms (Fig. 6.68).

Echocardiography can detect these complications, which show a pseudoaneurysm cavity communicating with the LV chamber through a narrow neck and often contain thrombus.

# **Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Imaging**

MRI is a valuable tool for noninvasive assessment of cardiac anatomy and function. In the past, its use was limited due to challenges posed by heart movement, but advances in MRI technology over the past 20 years have significantly improved spatial and temporal resolution, expanding its range of applications in cardiovascular disease.45 Initially used to assess complex heart conditions, tumors, and pericardial problems, it has since been established as a noninvasive way to evaluate biventricular shape and function and is the reference standard for measuring ventricular volumes.46 Cardiac MRI (CMR) stands out for its ability to identify tissue changes such as scarring, fibrosis, edema, and inflammation, making it the preferred method for investigating myocarditis, cardiomyopathies, ischemic heart disease, and myocardial viability.

#### **Imaging Planes**

A CMR examination typically begins with sequences captured in the coronal, axial, and sagittal planes, which are oriented orthogonal to the long axis of the body.49 These sequences can be obtained in under 2 minutes and serve as a reference for generating the necessary cardiac imaging planes.50

The standard MRI planes do not follow the cardiac orientation, so specific sections aligned with the cardiac axes are critical for proper cardiac imaging (Fig. 6.69).

The cardiac planes consist of short axis, horizontal long axis (four-chamber view), and vertical long axis (two-chamber view). They are determined by a line extending from the cardiac apex to the center of the mitral valve along the long axis of the heart using axial body plane images. Short-axis views are commonly used to derive ventricular volumetric measurements. Other frequently used planes include the oblique sagittal (parallel to the aorta axis), "three-points" (connecting three points along a chosen coronary artery), LV outflow (three-chamber view), and RV outflow tract view.

#### **Clinical Applications of Cardiac MRI**

*Cardiomyopathies.* CMR has proven to be highly accurate and consistent in visualizing the morphology and function of the left ventricle and RV. It is quickly becoming the preferred method for identifying the characteristics of different cardiomyopathies (Fig. 6.70).

**Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy.** CMR is an effective tool for evaluating hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM). (see chapter It can determine LV mass, patterns of regional hypertrophy, systolic anterior motion of the MV, and various disease phenotypes such as apical HCM.51 MRI can also evaluate postmyomectomy changes and can be used to monitor patients after surgical, pharmacologic, or alcoholic septal artery ablation interventions.52

**Myocarditis.** Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images can be useful in detecting changes in acute myocarditis. This is because late gadolinium accumulation, known as late gadolinium enhancement, is thought to be the result of slow wash-in wash-out kinetics and diffusion into necrotic cells.53 These images can also increase the sensitivity of endomyocardial biopsy by visualizing inflamed areas, making it easier to determine a biopsy site.

*Congenital Heart Disease.* CMR plays a crucial role in the evaluation and serial monitoring of patients with various congenital heart diseases. It complements echocardiography by identifying septal defects in the atria and ventricles, and phase velocity mapping at the level of the shunt can be used to calculate the shunt fraction (Qp/Qs ratio).54 CMR can also identify lesions such as anomalous pulmonary venous return, transposition of great vessels, aortic rings, truncus arteriosus, double outlet RV, tetralogy of Fallot, pulmonary atresia, and pulmonary artery stenosis using different imaging sequences, including MR angiography. MRI can also be used to monitor patients for effects and complications after corrective surgery. Magnetic resonance angiography can also be used to diagnose venous anomalies such as persistent superior vena cava or interruption of the IVC with azygous continuation.

The use of cardiac CT has greatly increased in the past decade, thanks to the introduction of multidetector computed tomography (MDCT).
