# **Definition**

The combination of pulmonary atresia (PA) and ventricular septal defect (VSD) is an extremely heterogeneous cardiopulmonary malformation. The intracardiac anatomy usually consists of an outlet VSD, while the pulmonary circulation is marked by extreme variability in the sources and distribution of pulmonary circulation. Tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia (TOF/PA) constitutes the overwhelming majority of cases of PA and VSD.

The definition of *tetralogy of Fallot (TOF) with pulmonary stenosis* (see Chapter 34) applies to TOF/PA, except that in the latter there is no luminal continuity between the right ventricle (RV) and pulmonary trunk or both the right pulmonary artery (RPA) and left pulmonary artery (LPA).

The International Congenital Heart Surgery Nomenclature and Database Project distinguishes between PA and VSD and the major subset TOF/PA as follows: "Pulmonary atresia and ventricular septal defect is defined as a group of congenital cardiac malformations in whom there is lack of luminal continuity and absence of blood flow from either ventricle and the pulmonary artery, in a biventricular heart that has an opening or a hole in the interventricular septum. In its severe form there is either partial or complete absence of the native pulmonary arteries."1

"Tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia is a congenital cardiac malformation, characterized by the extreme underdevelopment of the right ventricular infundibulum with marked anterior and leftward displacement of the infundibular septum often fused with the anterior wall of the right ventricle resulting in complete obstruction of blood flow into the pulmonary artery and associated with a large outlet, subaortic ventricular septal defect."2

Within the entire spectrum of TOF, a minority of cases (10%– 15%) will have TOF/PA, implying that there is no luminal continuity between the RV and the pulmonary trunk. Most cases are congenital, but rarely the atresia is acquired in cases of TOF with severe pulmonary stenosis (PS) where progressive hypertrophy and fibrosis of the outflow tract leads to complete closure of the RV ouflow.3,4

The most important feature of TOF/PA is the variation in pulmonary blood supply. About 40% of cases have a normally developed native pulmonary artery system supplied by a patent ductus arteriosus. The remaining 60% of cases have multifocal pulmonary blood supply by systemic-to-pulmonary collateral arteries. These vessels usually arise from the thoracic aorta or its branches, take a variable route to reach the lung, and are known as major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs). They are variable in size, number, arborization patterns, and in their relationship with the native PA system. These cases with MAPCAs are sometimes referred to as "complex pulmonary atresia."5

# **Historical Note**

Lillehei's first series included the first case of TOF/PA,6 and Rastelli and Kirklin reported the first use of an extracardiac conduit in repair of TOF/PA in 1965.7 The first use of a valved RV-PA conduit was reported by Ross and Somerville in 19668 among some of the earliest uses of homografts. The use of systemic-PA shunts (and RV-PA conduits) to promote growth of hypoplastic branch PAs was described by McGoon and colleagues and by Kirlin and colleagues in 1977.9,10

The recognition of MAPCAs and the concept of multifocal blood supply in some cases of TOF/PA was first described by Macartney and colleagues in the early 1970s,11 who identified that both MAPCAs and native pulmonary arteries (PAs) could coexist in these patients.12 In 1980, Haworth and Macartney described in detail the large aortopulmonary (AP) collateral arteries supplying blood to some pulmonary arterial segments by endto-end anastomosis, and to others by end-to-side anastomosis.13 They went on to introduce the concept of "unifocalization" as an important step in the management of patients with TOF/PA/ MAPCAs who have multifocal blood supply in 1980.14

Single-stage repair in infancy through unifocalization of MAPCAs combined with intracardiac repair and RV-PA conduit was pioneered by Reddy and Hanley in 1992 and reported in 1995.15 The role of a central shunt to "rehabilitate" hypoplastic native PAs was introduced by Watterson and Mee in Melbourne in 1991.16 Surgical techniques and approaches have been refined considerably over recent years and have been dominated by the extensive experience and outstanding results from the Stanford program under Hanley and colleagues as well as important contributions from Brawn and colleagues in Birmingham, United Kingdom, and Mee and colleagues in Melbourne and Cleveland Clinic.

The important role of catheter interventions was first described by Lock and colleagues in 198317 and the use of catheterization to occlude unwanted AP collaterals by Yamamoto in 1979.18 Cases with a single large MAPCA to each lung arising from the descending aorta were initially mislabeled as a variant of truncus arteriosus ("Type IV" in the Collett and Edwards classification), but it was realized that these are actually TOF/PA with a morphologically normal aortic root and valve.12,19

#### **Morphology**

In the overwhelming majority of cases, the intracardiac anatomy is essentially the same as in TOF with PS (see Chapter 34). The atresia can be membranous or muscular. In some cases, the main pulmonary trunk is very hypoplastic or even absent. Infundibular atresia is the most common finding, seen in 70% of TOF/ PA20 (Fig. 35.1). In valvar or membranous atresia, there is a patent infundibulum, often very hypertrophied.

#### **Classification**

There have been several classification systems based on the variation in sources of pulmonary blood flow: exclusively from the native PAs as opposed to those with multifocal pulmonary blood supply (which can be a combination of MAPCAs and native PAs, or exclusively from MAPCAs) as shown in Fig. 35.221:

*Type I*: Intrapericardial PAs are present and fully developed (normal native PA system) with pulmonary blood supply coming exclusively from the patent ductus arteriosus (PDA). There are no MAPCAs.

*Type II*: Absent main PA but normally developed intrapericardial confluent PAs, with blood supply exclusively from the PDA. No MAPCAs

*Type III*: Multifocal pulmonary blood supply from MAPCAs. There are still confluent intrapericardial PAs (variable in size and distribution), and there is no PDA.\*

#### **Pulmonary Arteries**

There is great variation in the morphology of the pulmonary vasculature in TOF/PA, and the condition can be classified according to the nature and origin of the pulmonary vessels (see later). Even when the branch PAs are well developed, the pulmonary trunk is often hypoplastic (Fig. 35.4) and may be a fibrous cord or even absent in 5% of cases.20

Approximately 40% to 50% of patients with TOF/PA have native PAs alone (Type A), in which the native system supplies all 20 pulmonary arterial lung segments.27 The other 50% to 60% of cases will have a mixture of native PA and MAPCAs supplying the lung (Types B and C) and usually no PDA. There is considerable variability in how many lung segments are supplied by native PAs in these cases13,27,28; usually the larger the native PA, the more segments it supplies. If there is nonconfluence of the central PAs, then 80% of cases will not supply all lung segments with the native PA system.27

This morphologic variation in pulmonary blood supply is central to the concept of "sole supply" verses "dual supply" of lung segments in this population. In Type B and C, some segments may be supplied only by MAPCAs, whereas in others, there are intrapulmonary connections between the MAPCA and the native PA system, such that these areas are said to have dual supply. Thus, there is an inverse relationship between the numbers of segments supplied by MAPCAs and those supplied by the native system (Fig. 35.5).22 Understanding the exact nature of blood

#### **Ductus Arteriosus**

If a ductus arteriosus is present and the branch PAs are confluent, arborization abnormalities and large MAPCAs are rarely if ever seen (Type I and II).13 The ductus is typically downwardly directed and serpiginous.30 If a ductus arteriosus is present and the branch PAs are discontinuous, the branch pulmonary artery (usually the left) that receives flow from the ductus rarely if ever has arborization abnormalities, and that lung rarely if ever has large AP collaterals.22 In this setting, the contralateral branch PA (usually the right) is almost always hypoplastic, has arborization abnormalities, or is completely absent, and the lung blood supply is solely from large MAPCAs.

In most cases of Type B and C the ductus is absent.

#### **Major Aortopulmonary Collateral Arteries**

These are large embryonic (not acquired) muscular arteries that arise from the aorta or its branches to supply the pulmonary vasculature (Fig. 35.6). Most commonly, they arise from the descending aorta, typically around the level of T4, and run anteriorly to reach the lung31 (Fig. 35.7). They can arise from the head and neck vessels, the internal mammary arteries, the coronary arteries, and occasionally even from the abdominal aorta.32-34 Regardless of origin, MAPCAs typically take a serpiginous course to reach the lung, where they usually connect with an interlobar or intralobar pulmonary artery.11,13 Their extrapulmonary segment is muscular and prone to develop stenoses (intimal pads) in as many as 60% of cases, often at points of bifurcation or at the connection to a native PA.13,35,36 Stenoses may not be present at birth but can progress quickly, having been described at 3 to 4 months in vessels that were originally unobstructed23;some may even acquire complete occlusion. The extrapulmonary course of MAPCAs is highly variable, and they can run posterior to the esophagus, posterior to the bronchi, or even pass through the wall of the esophagus. MAPCAs that remain posterior to the bronchi usually enter the lung posteriorly and can be difficult to access from midline-sternotomy. It is common for MAPCAs to originate around the T4 level of the descending aorta, where they usually run anteriorly beneath the carina to then find their way to the hilum of the lung. Retroesophageal MAPCAs are found in up to 60% of cases and always supply the lung on the opposite side to the aortic arch. They are commonly sole supply vessels to regions of the lung, and over 80% have some degree of stenosis.39,40

Once an MAPCA enters the lung, the arterial wall resembles that of a normal pulmonary artery. If the MAPCA joins the interlobular and intralobular artery as an end-to-end connection, this is said to be "sole supply" to that region. However, if the MAPCA creates an end-to-side connection with the native system (often joining near the hilum of the lung), this is referred to as "dual supply," and the native system can be seen filling retrogradely on injection of the MAPCA at angiography.41

Despite these characteristics of MAPCAs previously described, the distribution and branching pattern within the lung often include abnormal short and long stenotic regions or hypoplasia.13,42

#### **Bronchial Collateral Arteries**

Large systemic collateral arteries that arise from the midthoracic aorta and follow the course of the major bronchi and often are adherent to them are called bronchial collateral arteries. They are typically thin-walled and tortuous, and there is debate as to whether these are true MAPCAs or acquired dilation of bronchial arteries. However, if they supply important regions of the lung, then unifocalization and recruitment of these vessels is still recommended.43 These may reflect collateral arteries that develop much later in the embryonic course of the condition.44 Multiple small, acquired collaterals are common in cyanotic patients and increase over time, but these vessels are very different in size and number from MAPCAs, and there is no role for recruitment. They may regress once cyanosis has been corrected.

#### **Aortic Arch**

The aortic arch is right-sided in 25% to 30% of cases. Furthermore, the descending thoracic aorta may continue as a right-sided structure in more than half of these cases, descending on the right side of the vertebral column and usually returning to midline only at the level of the diaphragm. This is an important feature, as it will dictate the course of any MAPCAs arising from the descending aorta and inform surgical planning.

#### **Associated Conditions**

A third of cases with MAPCAs are associated with chromosome 22q11 deletion, of whom 90% will have DiGeorge Syndrome (Velo-Cardio-Facial Syndrome) and 22q11 deletion. The finding is more common in those with absent intrapericardial PAs and is also associated with pulmonary artery arborization abnormalities.24,45-49 Other associated conditions include Alagille Syndrome and CHARGE association. The presence of genetic abnormalities/ syndromes (especially 22q11 and Alagille) constitutes a significant risk factor for survival outcomes.50,51 Other than in these syndromes, TOF/PA is not commonly associated with other noncardiac features.

# **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

#### **Clinical Presentation**

PA, VSD with no MAPCAs will present like any duct-dependent lesion. Many are now diagnosed prenatally and so are placed on a Prostaglandin E2 infusion at birth. If the PAs are well developed and the PDA is large, then neonates may not appear obviously cyanosed but may develop signs of overcirculation. If not prenatally diagnosed, then neonates usually present as the duct closes with severe cyanosis and cardiovascular collapse.

PA, VSD with MAPCAs has a much more varied presentation, both in terms of symptoms and age at presentation. This reflects the great heterogeneity in the size and number of MAPCAs, presence or absence of natural stenoses in the vessels, and the overall quality of the pulmonary vasculature. Clinical phenotypes can be summarized as follows:

- 10% have large unobstructed MAPCAs. This can lead to overcirculation and high output cardiac failure with tachypnea, poor feeding, and weak pulses; typically present at 4 to 6 weeks and often with minimal cyanosis due to high pulmonary blood flow.
- 80% have a balanced circulation. MAPCAs either are relatively small or have natural stenoses limiting flow. These cases are not usually in heart failure and are mild-moderately cyanosed. However, there still may be some regions of the lung being overperfused and others at risk of losing their blood supply, so a clinically balanced situation still requires urgent imaging to assess pulmonary blood supply.
- 10% have small MAPCAs or severely stenosed MAPCAs, often with poorly developed pulmonary vasculature. These cases can be severely cyanosed and need ventilatory support. This can be the most challenging group to manage.

Primary and secondary airway problems may also contribute to the clinical presentation. The reasons are many, including upper airway extrinsic compression from a large aorta or pulsatile collateral arteries52 but can also be related to small airway hyperresponsiveness.20

Most cases will present or be diagnosed as a neonate or in early infancy. Occasionally, well-balanced patients only present later in childhood when a murmur is detected or they are noted to be mild-moderately cyanosed.

### **Echocardiography**

Echocardiography is diagnostic for intracardiac anatomy and for defining the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT). In Type A, it will delineate the ductal anatomy and the central PAs. In straightforward cases of Type A, no further imaging may be necessary. Echocardiography can usually demonstrate nonconfluent PAs, and doppler flow imaging may detect MAPCA origins, but other two-dimensional imaging is required to confirm presence of MAPCAs and define their anatomy, size, and distribution.

# **Catheterization and Angiograph**

This is essential in all cases Type B and C of PA, VSD with MAPCAs, and also in cases of Type A where echocardiography alone cannot clearly delineate central PA anatomy. Angiography should define *every* MAPCA individually and demonstrate its size, origin, course, position, and the segments of the lung supplied. Follow-through imaging will demonstrate whether the MAPCA communicates with the native PA system ("dual supply") or if it is the sole supply to that region of the lung. The native PA system often includes small, intrapericardial, native PAs that do not connect with the blind-ending RVOT and create a characteristic appearance described as a "seagull sign," as these slender vessels move up and down with the movement of the heart (Fig. 35.8). Pressure measurement in the proximal and distal vessels identifies areas of the lung at risk of pulmonary hypertension and also proximal stenoses within MAPCAs.

Angiography will also demonstrate ductal anatomy (if present) and should include imaging of the head-and-neck vessels, descending thoracic and abdominal aorta, as well as the coronary arteries to identify any MAPCA origins from these vessels. Anteroposterior and lateral imaging of each MAPCA is helpful to understand the extrapulmonary course of each MAPCA and relationship to the main airways.

If the pulmonary blood supply to some regions of the lung is not clear, then pulmonary venous wedge injections should be performed, as these may identify pulmonary vasculature fed by MAPCAs that have acquired origin atresia or even a native PA system fed by a ductus that has closed (so called "orphaned pulmonary artery" or "ghost pulmonary artery").

Cardiac catheterization is usually performed in the neonatal period or early infancy to confirm the diagnosis of MAPCAs and to inform surgical planning. Stable patients may not need immediate intervention but repeat angiography of the MAPCAs may be necessary according to the planned surgery. The reason may be to reevaluate an infant at age 3 to 4 months immediately before a one-stage unifocalization22 and intracardiac repair, because collaterals can change dramatically in the first few months of life, or it may be to assess the results of a previous palliative procedure performed in the neonatal period. Clinical stability (i.e., a thriving infant with SaO2 of 80% 6 5%) is not an indication to postpone cardiac catheterization. Such infants are likely to have important maldistribution of pulmonary blood flow despite their clinical stability, putting some lung segments at risk of developing pulmonary vascular disease due to overcirculation and other segments at risk of being lost due to collateral occlusion.

# **Computerized Tomographic Angiography**

Computerized tomographic angiography (CTA) has become an essential adjunct to cardiac catheterization in defining the anatomy, course, and position of MAPCAs. The rapid advances in the quality and definition of CTA has made this extremely helpful in both precise anatomic diagnosis and in surgical planning (Fig. 35.9). Three-dimensional reconstructions further aid surgical planning (Figs. 35.10 to 35.11). Separate segmentation of the airways and esophagus help to define the relationship of individual MAPCAs with these structures. Advanced 3-dimensional imaging tools can create holographic images or virtual reconstructions where the clinician can use augmented reality and interactive software to understand the exact course of MAPCAs and their relationship to the airways, lung tissues, and the heart. These new technologies can also be employed in the operating room to further augment the surgeon's ability to apply the imaging information to the surgical anatomy.53

#### **Cardiac MRI**

This is less commonly needed than CTA, as anatomic definition of the MAPCAs is usually better with CTA. However, MRI can give valuable information on flow in individual larger collaterals and in shunts or in native vessels, calculate total collateral flow, and calculate Qp:Qs. Three-dimensional reconstructions may also help give similar spatial understanding to the course of MAPCAs in relation to other mediastinal structures.

# **Natural History**

The natural history of patients with TOF/PA cannot be described simply, in part because of the great variability of morphology.54

This is reflected in the more complex hazard function for death compared with that for TOF and PS (see Fig. 35.12C).55 It consists of a short-lasting early hazard phase over the first year of life, considerably more acute than for TOF/PS, that merges with a constant hazard phase (exponentially decreasing survival) that lasts to about age 50, followed by an increasing late hazard (with wide confidence limits).

Underlying PA morphology is highly variable and has a strong contribution to natural history. In general, among patients of Type A with duct-dependent flow and a fully developed native PA system, about 50% survive to 6 months of age and 10% to 1 year.

Patients of Type B and C have a variable natural history depending on the size number and morphology of the MAPCAs, but generally these patients are cyanotic and become progressively cyanosed over time as the vessels tend to become stenosed and even occlude. Those with unrestricted MAPCA flow develop Eisenmenger physiology and may present with hemoptysis.56 There is evidence that areas of the lung supplied exclusively by large MAPCAs (i.e., no native PA system) are particularly prone to developing early pulmonary hypertension.57 Without intervention, survival in Type B and C is estimated at 10% at 10 years of age.5

Patients with TOF, PA, and MAPCAs probably differ from other patients with TOF with respect to pulmonary arterial disease. The phrase "pulmonary arterial disease" rather than "pulmonary vascular disease" is used because of uncertainty regarding how much the increased pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) is due to hypoplasia and stenoses of the distal PAs themselves and of the distal portions of the MAPCAs, versus how much is due to microvascular hypertensive pulmonary vascular disease.15 Both mechanisms occur.

Pulmonary arterial disease tends to develop in those pulmonary arterial segments that are centrally connected. It progresses at a considerably accelerated rate, and perhaps even in fetal life or in the first few months after birth, in segments whose only or major source of pulmonary blood flow is large AP collateral arteries.58 Patients who survive without correction into the second and third decades of life often develop massive and sometimes fatal hemoptysis related to these large AP collaterals.59

# **Surgical Planning and Timing**

#### **General Concepts**

The surgical management of *duct-dependent TOF with pulmonary atresia* without MAPCAs is relatively straight forward: either complete repair, initial palliation with a systemic-to- pulmonary artery shunt or ductal stenting.

The management of patients with *MAPCAs* is much more complicated. Concepts of *unifocalizatio*n and r*ehabilitation* of pulmonary arterial vasculature are central to this process.

*Unifocalization* implies bringing together all sources of pulmonary arterial supply surgically: both MAPCAs and native PAs if present.

*Rehabilitation* has the goal of promoting growth of the hypoplastic native PAs so that it can be suitable for a two-ventricle repair. Rehabilitation can be anatomic to increase the size of the arteries by providing more systemic blood flow largely by means of an aortopulmonary shunt or sometimes stenting a PDA or severely stenotic collateral with hypoplastic distal bed. Rehabilitation can be physiologic where the PVR is higher and precludes a two-ventricle repair. These patients

### **Repair of Pulmonary Atresia, VSD, and MAPCAs (Type B and C)**

This group of patients will include confluent and nonfluent PAs or absent right or left or both PAs with aortopulmonary collateral arteries and sometimes a PDA that supply pulmonary blood flow.

The hallmark of this group is the highly variable vascular (collateral, pulmonary arterial, and dual) blood supply to the lungs. This often dictates the surgical approach along with the expertise available at a given institution. The experience accumulated over more than two decades supported by the follow up studies and several institutional experiences suggest that a majority of patients with PA, VSD, and MAPCAs can be managed with a one-stage unifocalization. This approach can be applied to most categories of patients, as long as the intraparenchymal vessels are acceptable; 80% percent of these can be fully repaired in infancy with onestage left and right pulmonary unifocalization, VSD closure, and RV outflow tract reconstruction.23,64-68 Individual surgeons sometimes prefer a staged approach. In certain morphologic and physiologic situations, a one-stage repair or unifocalization is contraindicated. These are described later in this chapter.63,66,69-75

#### **One-Stage Complete Unifocalization and Repair**

Approach to one-stage unifocalization and repair follows several basic principles to achieve a satisfactory repair:

- 1. *Evaluating and Controlling the Pulmonary Vascular Supply*
- 2. *Managing Cardiopulmonary Bypass*

- 3. *Unifocalization of the Pulmonary Arteries and Collaterals*
- 4. *Assessing Adequacy of Unifocalization: Pulmonary Flow Study*
- 5. *Cardiac Repair: VSD Closure and RV to PA Conduit*
- 6. *Completing and Assessing the Repair*

The operation is generally performed between 3 to 6 months of age and sometimes later in infancy if the preoperative assessment reveals that the pulmonary bed is well protected. Very uncommonly a repair is warranted in the neonatal period for significant hypoxia or due to pulmonary over circulation and failure to thrive. Infants with discontinuous PAs, each supplied by a PDA or collateral, should undergo intervention in the neonatal period, unless flow into the MAPCAs is secure, in which case unifocalization can still be planned for 3 to 6 months.

Single-stage repair requires a sternotomy approach, and the initial steps until the pericardium is harvested are similar to those for TOF/PA with confluent PAs.

*Evaluating and Controlling the Pulmonary Vascular Supply.* With current imaging capabilities, the topography of the pulmonary vascular supply is well established, provided the surgeon has a clear understanding of the PAs, MAPCAs, and their relation to other vascular and mediastinal structures. However, it is not uncommon for intrapericardial hypoplastic PAs to be undetected by imaging, or for some occluded collaterals to be poorly visualized due to retrograde filling. Therefore, it is essential to assess the intrapericardial PAs, if present, and dissect and mobilize them all the way into the pulmonary parenchyma, sometimes extending to their segmental locations.

Next, the collaterals should be evaluated. This requires a thorough study of angiograms and CT scans to develop a clear mental image of the vascular morphology. Intraoperative access to imaging can be beneficial. The preferred approach for dissecting and controlling the collaterals is through the mediastinum as suggested by Tchervenkov and associates and only rarely through the pleural cavities.76 Dissection is performed via the transverse pericardial sinus in the subcarinal region, between the aorta and superior vena cava (SVC), and between the ascending and descending aorta (see Fig. 35.17).

The posterior pericardium is opened, and mediastinal dissection is carried out using cautery. Each collateral is identified and dissected along its entire length until it enters the lung, and often beyond, especially in cases of stenotic segments. The esophagus and descending aorta can be identified, and collaterals located behind the esophagus or passing through the esophageal wall can be traced. The origin of each collateral should be tracked to its aortic source. Posterior mediastinal dissection in infants provides access to most of the collaterals, extending down to the diaphragm. Occasionally, the pleura must be opened to gain control of the lowermost collaterals or those originating below the diaphragm. Collaterals arising from the brachiocephalic or subclavian arteries can be identified and dissected along their course. The dissection and control of collaterals can sometimes take several hours, particularly in repeat surgeries.

*Managing Cardiopulmonary Bypass.* The initiation of CBP is critical and requires careful planning. All sources of pulmonary blood flow, including collaterals, PDA, or surgical shunts, must be controlled. One effective technique for ligation is to use 5/0 polypropylene sutures passed through the adventitial layers at the base of the collaterals and the PDA, which can be tied off as soon as cannulation is complete. Alternatively, some surgeons use hemoclips for this purpose.

Following full heparinization, the aorta, SVC, and inferior vena cava (IVC) are cannulated with appropriately sized cannulas. Once cannulation is complete, all sources of pulmonary blood flow are ligated, and CPB is initiated. Despite controlling the major sources, there are numerous small collaterals that cannot be managed due to their size and number. In such cases, placing a left ventricular (LV) vent can help decompress the LV or allow the heart to continue beating during cooling.

Establishing CPB with warm prime (34°C-36°C), along with corrected calcium and bicarbonate levels, helps prevent ventricular fibrillation when bypass is initiated. This provides enough time to place an LV vent and ligate any residual sources of pulmonary blood flow. LV vents of appropriate sizes should be used based on patient weight: a 10 Fr for patients under 4 kg, a 13 Fr for patients 4-10 kg, a 16 Fr for 10-30 kg, and adult-size vents for larger patients. The vent is placed at the junction of the right superior pulmonary vein and the atrium. The position can be confirmed using transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) or manually if TEE is unavailable. The vent should cross the mitral valve (MV).

In patients with good oxygen saturation and dominant collaterals on one side, unifocalization of the contralateral side may be performed before initiating bypass. In older patients with chronic cyanosis or those who have undergone previous palliative procedures, the burden of acquired collaterals (despite ligating the larger ones) may require a larger LV vent and higher perfusion flow rates on CPB. The patient's temperature is gradually cooled to 25°C. To prevent cerebral blood flow runoff, arterial pCO2 is maintained at 45 mmHg or higher during bypass, which helps protect brain function.

*Unifocalization of the Pulmonary Arteries and Collaterals.* To achieve complete unifocalization without peripheral conduits and maximize native tissue-tissue anastomosis, the following peripheral and central reconstructive techniques are employed (Fig. 35.18).

- Side-side anastomosis of collaterals to other collaterals or to true PAs
- Oblique end-side anastomosis of collaterals to other collaterals or to true PAs
- Long onlay or side-side anastomosis of collaterals to the central PAs
- End-end or end-side anastomosis of collaterals to a central conduit
- Allograft patch augmentation of collateral stenoses
- Allograft patch augmentation of the reconstructed central PAs
- In rare cases, allograft or synthetic conduit reconstruction of central PAs

Almost all unifocalization procedures can be performed on a well-perfused, beating heart while on CBP. Depending on the number and condition of the collaterals, this process may take anywhere from a few to several hours. Most collaterals can be directly anastomosed to native PAs with long side-to-side anastomoses, extending to where the arteries enter the lungs, using 8/0 or 7/0 polypropylene sutures.

For collaterals that are not parallel to the native PAs, end-to-side anastomoses are used for unifocalization. When rerouting the collaterals around the bronchi, care must be taken to avoid acute angles, which may require patching. All collaterals are unifocalized using these techniques. Typically, collaterals are connected to the superior, posterior, or inferior sides of the native PAs, leaving the anterior aspect untouched. These collaterals are then dissected along their entire course until they enter lung parenchyma. It should be noted that collateral arteries commonly pass superficially through esophageal musculature; this must be recognized during the process of collateral dissection to avoid injuring the esophagus. Collaterals are commonly intertwined with secondary vagal nerve fibers, and it may be necessary to divide these to fully mobilize the collateral arteries. With rare exceptions, such as a small collateral with one or fewer segmental distributions, all isolated MAPCAs need to be unifocalized to increase the cross-sectional area of the pulmonary vascular bed and prevent loss of lung segments. Dual-supply collaterals to normally sized PAs can be ligated, but if this is not feasible, the MAPCAs should be unifocalized. This process helps augment the diameter of the true PAs.

If there is a complete absence of true PAs within the mediastinum, different techniques must be employed. If PDA vessels or major collaterals supply each lung, they can be connected across the midline posteriorly and augmented anteriorly with a homograft pulmonary artery patch. If multiple MAPCAs exist on either side, all ipsilateral collaterals should be unifocalized using the described techniques before connecting them across the midline. In older patients where midline tissue approximation is not feasible, an oversized central conduit, created from a pulmonary homograft or synthetic material, may be used (Fig. 35.20).

These techniques are tailored to each patient and are frequently combined, depending on the specific anatomy. Direct tissue-to-tissue anastomoses are achieved by bringing collaterals through the transverse sinus or around the lung hilum, utilizing as much collateral length as possible. Collateral length is a top priority to facilitate tissueto-tissue anastomosis. For instance, if a discrete stenosis is present in the midportion of a collateral, the entire collateral is still used, and the stenosis is relieved through side-to-side reconstruction with another collateral or true PA, or by patch augmentation if necessary. All collaterals, including those supplying dual blood flow to a lung segment along with a true PA, are incorporated into the reconstruction to build the neo- PAs with as much native tissue as possible.

During unifocalization, priority is given to avoiding nonviable conduits in the periphery and achieving unifocalization through native tissue-to-tissue anastomosis. Key concepts include flexibility in reconstruction, aggressive mobilization, maximizing collateral length, and creative rerouting. Pathways for collateral rerouting are developed by opening the subcarinal space through the transverse sinus and by opening the pleura on both sides, posterior to the phrenic nerves in the hilar regions.

*Assessing Adequacy of Unifocalization: Pulmonary Flow Study.* Before proceeding with intracardiac repair, a pulmonary flow study is performed to test the adequacy of the reconstruction. If the reconstruction is sufficient for a complete repair, postrepair right ventricular pressure (RVp) should be less than 60% of systemic pressure, based on long-term follow-up studies. Experienced surgeons often make this assessment based on their preoperative evaluation of the collaterals, PAs, and the technical aspects of unifocalization.

If the repair is not technically perfect or the size of the unifocalized pulmonary arterial supply is insufficient, it is wise to delay complete repair and consider a systemic-to-PA or RV-to-PA shunt. The adequacy of the unifocalization can then be reassessed during follow-up cardiac catheterization. However, if there is uncertainty, it is prudent to perform an objective study of the unifocalized pulmonary arterial system before proceeding with intracardiac repair.

*Cardiac Repair: VSD Closure and RV-to-PA Conduit.* In the majority of patients, blood flow to the unifocalized PAs is provided via an aortic allograft valved conduit from the RV to the central PAs. Following unifocalization, the distal end of the conduit (usually an allograft or porcine valved conduit) is anastomosed to the central PAs. If necessary, allograft tissue from the conduit can be extended distally to augment the central branch PAs.

Once this is completed, the aorta is cross-clamped, and cardioplegia is administered. A longitudinal ventriculotomy is made in the RV infundibulum, and the hypertrophied muscle bundles are resected. For patients undergoing VSD closure, this is performed using an autologous pericardial patch fixed in glutaraldehyde or a Dacron or Gore-Tex patch, with continuous or interrupted pledgeted sutures, following the precautions and considerations addressed earlier for VSD closure (see the technique in Chapter 34). The right atrium is opened to inspect the atrial septum. If an atrial septal defect (ASD) or patent foramen ovale is present, it is partially closed, leaving a small unidirectional interatrial communication for decompression of the right heart in case of postoperative RV dysfunction. In cases with an intact atrial septum, a small one-way interatrial communication is created.

The RV outflow tract reconstruction is completed by anastomosing the proximal conduit to the infundibulotomy (see Figs. 35.15 and 35.16 in the earlier section on TOF/PA, confluent PAs, and duct-dependent pulmonary blood flow [Type A]).

For patients not eligible for complete repair, a systemic-topulmonary shunt is performed (see "Technique of Shunting Operations" in Chapter 34). A central aorta-to-PA shunt or a modified Blalock-Taussig-Thomas shunt is preferred. The size of the shunt depends on the patient's age and weight, although a slightly larger shunt (0.5 to 1 mm larger than in TOF/PS) may be preferred to encourage more blood flow and promote the growth of unifocalized collaterals. If a flow study shows a high mean PA pressure, a larger shunt is recommended. After coming off bypass, if the shunt is deemed too large, it can be partially clipped to achieve optimal flow.

Alternatively, a deliberately restrictive valved RV-PA conduit may provide a more favorable streaming effect (better oxygen saturation for the same pulmonary blood flow), pulsatile flow, improved diastolic blood pressure, and good access for future catheterizations or for any distal pulmonary arterial interventions. A porcine valved conduit (Hancock) can be externally clipped, aiming for the calculated half size plus 2 mm or one size larger if PVR is high. If significant reperfusion injury or pulmonary hemorrhage is evident in the operating room, it may be prudent to leave the sternum open and adjust the shunt size once the lungs recover.

*Completing and Assessing the Repair.* After reconstruction, rewarming, and separation from CPB, the procedure is completed similarly to that of TOF/PS (see Chapter 34). A pressure monitoring catheter with a side hole is placed in the RV or in the conduit distal to the valve, and the RV systolic pressure is measured. The RV/left ventricular (LV) pressure ratio (PRV/LV) is calculated. A ratio of less than 0.5 is preferred, while a higher ratio of up to 0.7 is acceptable after a prolonged bypass run or in cases of reperfusion injury to lung segments.

While the risk of death is acceptable with a PRV/LV ratio of up to 0.9, long-term RV dysfunction becomes a concern. If cardiac output is compromised at a PRV/LV ratio of 0.5 to 0.7, or higher than 0.7, it is recommended to take down the VSD patch and perform a central shunt. Alternatively, fenestrating the VSD patch or banding the conduit may also help mitigate low cardiac output.

In the postoperative period, systemic arterial, pulmonary arterial, and right atrial pressures are continuously monitored. TEE is performed to ensure there are no significant residual defects. Bilateral pleural and mediastinal tube drains are placed, and the sternum is closed. If there is excessive bleeding or ventilation difficulties, the sternum may be left open electively, with the skin incision closed using a Silastic patch. The sternum is then closed on the second or third postoperative day.

A staged unifocalization or a staged unifocalization plus rehabilitation may be advisable under certain circumstances (see later "Special Situations and Controversies").

# **Special Features of Postoperative Care**

In general, the postoperative care of patients with well-repaired PA, VSD, and MAPCAs is similar to that of patients with TOF and PS. However, if the patient develops low cardiac output or if the PRV/LV (pressure ratio of the RV to LV) is increasing, further evaluation is necessary. A simple chest x-ray may reveal the issue. Pleural effusion, collapse, or reperfusion injury may need to be addressed aggressively. A rising PaCO2 is concerning as it often indicates a respiratory problem. Increasing respiratory support with higher peak pressures may be detrimental and can cause further lung injury. Elevated respiratory pressures are often related to pulmonary hemorrhage or reperfusion injury.79 In such cases, a higher positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP), the use of nitric oxide, and accepting higher PaCO2 may be beneficial.

Reperfusion injury is frequently observed in patients with lung segments supplied by severely stenotic collateral arteries and PAs. These segments experience higher flow and pressures after bypass, leading to pulmonary edema, airway secretions, and pleural effusions. This phenomenon can start in the operating room and often persists for two to three days before resolving. Sometimes, bronchoscopic clearance of the airways may be required.

If low cardiac output is attributed to cardiac dysfunction or hypovolemia, the appropriate use of inotropes and fluid administration are indicated. However, it is important not to miss the causes specific to unifocalization patients. If the PRV/LV remains high with low cardiac output and all other issues have been corrected, a thorough evaluation is necessary. Obstruction of the conduit or the reconstructed PAs and collaterals should be assessed via cardiac catheterization and angiography. Any anatomic obstruction should be corrected. With the use of intraoperative pulmonary blood flow studies and a good TEE, such issues are uncommon.

If the elevated pressures are due to high PVR and are not surgically correctable, fenestrating the VSD patch and initiating pharmacologic therapy to reduce the elevated PVR is necessary. However, if the elevated pressures are due to a hypoplastic distal PA bed, removing the VSD patch and providing a systemicto-pulmonary artery shunt is preferable. If the higher PRV/LV is due to a reactive vascular bed, nitric oxide and sedation during the first few postoperative days may be helpful, followed by continued use of pulmonary vasodilators.

Postoperative airway issues are also more common in these patients. Airway compression can result from elevated pressure in conduits, unifocalized collaterals, or a dilated aorta, which can be corrected by aortopexy.52 Tracheomalacia may be worsened or unmasked after surgery, especially in infants and younger patients.

Small airway dysfunction is also more common but tends to improve over time. This is due to extensive dissection, disruption of autonomic nerve supply, and disruption of lymphatics.36,67,80 Careful and controlled pulmonary toilet is very important in these patients, both in the operating room and the postoperative period. Some patients may require repeat bronchoscopy to clear the airways.

Monitoring left atrial (LA) and right atrial (RA) pressures are advisable in these patients. A higher LA pressure (much more than RA pressure) may be due to persistent left-to-right shunting from a residual VSD or collaterals. If these are contributing to low cardiac output, they need to be addressed. Cardiac catheterization and coiling of nonunifocalizable collaterals, as well as surgical or catheter-based closure of the residual VSD, should be performed.

#### **Results**

#### **Early Mortality (Hospital)**

*TOF/PA with Ductal Dependent Pulmonary Blood Flow.* In the last two decades, hospital mortality has decreased for simple TOF with PA (without MAPCAs). Since the pioneering neonatal primary repair of TOF/PA by Castaneda in the 1970s, mortality has decreased from 18.5% to less than 6%, and currently, it is generally less than 3%.81

Early mortality for staged repair of TOF/PA is also lower, although slightly higher than for primary repair82,83.

*PA, VSD with MAPCAs.* In PA, VSD with MAPCAs, early mortality has consistently improved due to the unifocalization procedures, championed by several groups. This has resulted in an acceptable early mortality rate of less than 10%.23,70-72,74 Current reports indicate an early mortality between 0% and 12%.60,61,84,85 Large series with heterogeneous morphology report a mortality rate of less than 6%86; a recent review of this patient group shows early mortality ranging from 0% to 11.5%.87 The largest series in the literature, which included a heterogeneous group of patients, reported an early mortality of 4.9% after the initial surgery and 2.6% after complete repair, with an increasing proportion undergoing complete repair within the first year of age50 (Fig. 35.22).

*Time-Related Survival.* In general, time-related survival after complete repair of patients with TOF/PA and confluent, normal PAs, or after single-stage unifocalization and repair in patients with PA, VSD, and MAPCAs, is comparable. These results are approaching those for TOF with PS. Current timerelated survival is reported at 85% to 92% at 3 to 5 years and 70% to 78% at 15 to 20 years.84,85,87 A large series spanning over 20 years and including a heterogeneous patient group reported time-related survival of 91% at 1 year, 87% at 5 years, and 85% at 10 years. Long-term survival in patients with TOF/ PA without collaterals at 30 years is 78%, compared to 95% in TOF with PS.88

#### **Pulmonary Artery Pressure and Resistance**

Although there is some data on the PRV/LV ratio at follow-up, many studies lack corresponding pulmonary artery pressure (Ppa) and Rp, even from centers with extensive experience. However, data from large studies on unifocalization procedures, particularly from Stanford, show that the PRV/LV ratio at follow-up remains stable compared to perioperative data.

In a selective study of 168 patients between 2002 and 2019, the intraoperative pulmonary artery to aortic pressure (Ppa/ao) ratio was 0.33 (25th and 75th quartiles: 0.28, 0.40), compared to catheterization results at 1 year, which showed a Ppa/ao ratio of 0.41 (0.34, 0.49)96 (Fig. 35.25). However, data on patients who only received rehabilitation without unifocalization showed higher follow-up values.97

Earlier studies suggested that about 50% of a heterogeneous group of patients would have elevated PPA and Rp late after repair, with elevated Rp inversely correlating with the extent of native pulmonary artery arborization.57 However, recent experience with optimized surgical strategies and repair has shown that this correlation is not fixed. At follow-up, the PRV/LV ratio was similar to the intraoperative data. Although most reports from large series lack comprehensive data on all patients, there is reliable data for a subset of patients96,98 (Table 35.3).

# **Indications for Operation**

Proper indications for operation, and their sequencing in the various subsets of patients who have TOF with congenital PA, cannot be identified with certainty. This is not surprising, because the necessary patient-specific (risk-adjusted) comparisons of outcomes after no interventional treatment versus the various types and combinations of procedures are not yet available. Therefore, indications can only be discussed in general terms for the major groups.

#### **Confluent, Nonconfluent, or Absent Pulmonary Arteries and Major Aortopulmonary Collateral Arteries**

Although the natural history is much more variable in this morphologic group, rarely do patients reach adult life in a healthy state if left untreated. Based on early surgical outcomes before 1990, it has been argued by some that surgical intervention is not indicated unless the individual is very symptomatic. More recent surgical results following both staged unifocalization and onestage unifocalization and intracardiac repair demonstrate that repair with low Prv and Ppa can be achieved in a high percentage of patients whether symptomatic or not. Although long-term outcome using these newer techniques is not yet known, the combination of low operative mortality and resultant low Prv suggests that outcomes over the long term are likely, in many cases, to be comparable with those achieved in the more favorable morphologic subset of patients with TOF/PA and ductal-dependent, confluent, normally distributing PAs (see "Results"). The most important principles in achieving results comparable with this more favorable group are incorporating blood supply from all lung segments into the central pulmonary artery system and achieving this in a timely manner such that the microvasculature in each lung segment remains undamaged (see "Technique of Operation").

# **Special Situations and Controversies**

# **Alternatives to One-Stage Unifocalization and Intracardiac Repair**

Approximately 80% to 90% of patients with large MAPCAs can achieve complete bilateral unifocalization through a midline sternotomy in one procedure in early infancy, and of these, approximately two-thirds can undergo intracardiac repair at the same time. Of the one-third of patients who undergo complete unifocalization but in whom Rp is too high to permit immediate intracardiac repair, most achieve repair within 2 years.23

There are various reasons why midline complete unifocalization is contraindicated in approximately 10% to 20% of patients with major MAPCAs. In the presence of native PAs, patients are candidates for a *rehabilitation* approach. A staged unifocalization or a staged unifocalization plus rehabilitation may be indicated when there are significant distal segmental stenoses or if one side of the lung has hypoplastic pulmonary vasculature compared to the contralateral side.

In this situation, a staged approach is preferred, which can be via thoracotomy or sternotomy. A systemic to pulmonary artery shunt is performed to the hypoplastic unifocalized collaterals and PAs to encourage their rehabilitation. This may be only required for one lung or both lungs before proceeding to a complete repair.

In the setting of poorly developed collaterals and absent true central PAs, a lateral thoracotomy with single lung unifocalization connected to an AP shunt may be the procedure of choice. This would be followed by a similar procedure on the contralateral lung at a future time. At a third procedure, a median sternotomy approach would be used to achieve vascular confluence between the left and right lungs, with later intracardiac repair and takedown of the previously placed shunts. Many techniques, using native tissue as well as prosthetic material, have been described to accomplish this type of reconstruction.68,69,71-73,116-118

In other situations, the true central PAs are present, confluent, but extremely hypoplastic in the mediastinum; however, they arborize to all or almost all of the lung segments and communicate to the collateral system extensively within the lung parenchyma. In this situation, there are few if any isolated segments of lung that are supplied by only collateral flow. In this setting, creation in the neonatal or early infancy period of a central native tissue anastomosis between the ascending aorta and blind end of the pulmonary trunk can be performed to promote growth of the central system with communication to most lung segments. Following an interim period of central pulmonary artery growth, a second procedure with ligation of the dual-supply collateral sources at their systemic origin, and subsequent intracardiac repair and placement of an RV-to-pulmonary trunk conduit, can be accomplished.70,119,120

It should be emphasized that there is wide variability among experienced surgeons in the approach to PA, VSD, and large MAPCAs. The staged approach is preferred by some as the primary approach for most patients.71-73,120 One common approach is to perform an initial procedure that provides increased blood flow and pressure into the confluent hypoplastic central artery system whenever there exists confluent central PAs. This can be accomplished through many techniques—for example, by creating the central systemic–pulmonary tissue-to-tissue connection described earlier.119,120 Another approach is to create an RV-to–pulmonary trunk conduit using various tissue or synthetic prostheses, leaving the VSD open.73 A third approach is to create a prosthetic systemic–pulmonary arterial shunt to the pulmonary trunk or one of its branches.

There are several major concerns about all these approaches when they are used unselectively as first-line management, especially when the central PAs have limited arborization. First, they leave collateral flow untouched, allowing time for detrimental changes to occur in the lung vasculature supplied by collaterals, either in the form of overcirculation with development of pulmonary vascular obstructive changes or in the form of stenosis and occlusion leading to loss of lung segments. These negative effects inevitably lead to reduction in healthy lung cross-sectional area, increasing Rp. Another concern is that any surgical procedure is followed by scarring and adhesions, markedly reducing the surgeon's ability to manipulate, reroute, and unifocalize collaterals at subsequent operations. A final concern is that following any shunt or conduit procedure, no matter how small the shunt or conduit is, the flow and pressure transmitted into the distal vasculature of the true PAs are likely to be pathologically elevated when there is a very limited distal bed in association with reduced arborization.

Regardless of the specific surgical approach, most programs with substantial experience with this lesion22,121 believe that unifocalization of large MAPCAs is an important component of overall management; however, this view is not universally acknowledged.122

#### **Percutaneous Balloon Dilation of Stenoses of Right and Left Pulmonary Arteries**

Lock and colleagues first demonstrated the feasibility of this procedure.17,123,124 Intimal disruption with tearing of the media is one mechanism of dilation, with the tears being subsequently filled in by scar tissue.125 In a multiinstitutional study of a heterogeneous group of 156 patients, the largest subset of which had some form of PA, systolic pressure gradient across the stenosis was reduced only 10 to 14 mmHg, on average.126 A single-institution analysis of results in 135 patients indicated that about 60% of the procedures were satisfactory, defining this as a decrease of 20% or more of the peak pressure ratio between RV and aorta.127 Risk of death or other major complication was 1% to 3%. Patient age had no correlation with outcome. Effectiveness may be increased in the future by use of expandable stents.128,129

Effectiveness of balloon dilation can also be assessed by its effect on normalizing the flow distribution in the lungs as measured by nuclear lung perfusion scan, even if Prv is not reduced. Following complete repair, normalization of homogeneous flow to the lungs can be promoted by balloon dilation of lobar and segmental stenoses within the unifocalized pulmonary artery system.23

# **Percutaneous Closure of Large Aortopulmonary Collateral Arteries**

Yamamoto first demonstrated the feasibility of this procedure in 1979,18 and Szarnicki and colleagues accomplished it by embolizing a Gianturco wire coil device in 1981.130 Subsequently, a number of studies have demonstrated the effectiveness and safety of percutaneous closure of large AP collateral arteries as long as proper precautions are taken. When wire coil uniformly coated with thrombogenic polyester strands is used, occlusion by thrombosis usually occurs within about 10 minutes.131 Other devices for promoting thrombosis have been used, including bucrylate adhesive132 and detachable silicone balloons.133,134 With wire coil and proper techniques, complete occlusion is achieved in about 70% of instances and subtotal occlusion in another 25%.135

A large AP collateral artery should never be closed surgically or percutaneously when it connects end to end to distal PAs, and thus is the sole or major source of blood flow to one or more pulmonary segments. Even when blood supply to a pulmonary segment occurs from dual sources, occlusion of the collateral source may not be indicated. For example, if upon review of detailed angiograms, it is determined that the collateral tissue can be used productively in surgical unifocalization, it should not be occluded prematurely. In this case, the collateral should be left open at catheterization and then ligated and divided during surgery, with its tissue used in the unifocalization and pulmonary artery augmentation process. When it is determined that tissue from a dual-supply collateral is not needed in surgical reconstruction and coil occlusion is being considered, it is prudent to occlude the vessel temporarily to be certain that its presence is not necessary for maintaining reasonable Sao2.
