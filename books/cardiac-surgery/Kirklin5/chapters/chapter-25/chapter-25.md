This chapter discusses diseases involving the pulmonary arteries and the systemic veins that are amenable to surgical treatment. Sections I through V review acute massive pulmonary embolism, chronic pulmonary thromboembolic disease (TED), pulmonary artery aneurysm, pulmonary artery dissection (PAD), and pulmonary artery tumors, respectively. Section VI covers acquired diseases of the systemic veins.

## SECTION I: ACUTE MASSIVE PULMONARY EMBOLISM

#### **Definition**

The American Heart Association (AHA) and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) have proposed classifications for pulmonary embolism severity. Acute massive (AHA) or high-risk (ESC) pulmonary embolism is the sudden entrapment of dislodged thrombus in the pulmonary arteries, usually from deep veins of the legs, pelvis, or arms. It is life-threatening and can result in right heart failure, low cardiac output, and sudden death.

#### **Historical Note**

The first pulmonary embolectomy was performed by Trendelenburg in 1908, but long-term survival using his technique was not achieved until 1924. It is prophetic and of great significance that Dr. John Gibbon, who in 1953 performed the first successful operation on a patient totally supported by cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) using a pump-oxygenator, envisioned use of CPB to treat massive pulmonary embolism.

The first successful pulmonary embolectomies performed using CPB were reported by Cooley and colleagues in 1961 and Sharp in 1962.<sup>3,4</sup> The discovery of heparin by McLean<sup>5</sup> and the demonstration of its clinical utility by Murray and colleagues<sup>6</sup> reported in 1937 was a critical parallel advance for the management of this condition.

#### Morphology

Detached venous thrombi pass through the right heart and enter the pulmonary arteries as a single thrombus or as fragmented smaller thrombi. The majority of thrombi lodge in the lower lobes, slightly

more often in the right than left lung. Pulmonary artery pressure rises if greater than 30% to 50% of the total cross-sectional area of the pulmonary vasculature is occluded.<sup>8</sup> Shortly after reaching the lungs, emboli become coated with a layer of platelets and thrombin.<sup>7</sup> Pulmonary arterial obstruction and release of vasoactive agents such as serotonin, adenosine diphosphate, platelet-derived growth factor, and thromboxane by platelets elevate pulmonary vascular resistance (Rp).9 Alveolar dead space increases because of redistribution of blood flow and impairs gas exchange. As right ventricular (RV) afterload increases, RV pressure rises. This may result in RV dilation, myocardial ischemia, and dysfunction. Increased Rp reduces RV stroke volume and left ventricular filling (preload). Reduction in preload and coronary blood flow associated with systemic hypotension markedly reduces left ventricular stroke volume. If a patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect is present, right-to-left shunting of blood and severe hypoxemia may occur, as may paradoxical embolization.

## **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

#### **Symptoms**

Acute massive pulmonary artery embolism can result in dyspnea, tachypnea, tachycardia, diaphoresis, cyanosis, and occasionally loss of consciousness.

#### Signs

The patient may be hypotensive, dyspneic, and cyanotic. There may also be evidence of pulsus paradoxus. <sup>10</sup> Evidence of low cardiac output is present, with weak peripheral pulses and oliguria. Jugular venous pressure is elevated, often with a prominent "a" wave, and neck veins may be distended. Cardiac examination may demonstrate tachycardia, a prominent RV impulse, a loud pulmonary component of the second heart sound, and a gallop rhythm. <sup>10</sup> An ejection or pansystolic murmur is often present that may represent tricuspid valve regurgitation. Rarely, there may be evidence of airway obstruction.

#### **Diagnostic Studies**

The pathway for establishing the diagnosis of acute massive pulmonary embolism will depend on the pre-test probability of massive

pulmonary embolism. The electrocardiogram (ECG) may demonstrate T-wave inversion in the anterior leads, reflecting inferoposterior ischemia from pressure overload, a pseudoinfarction pattern, or an S1Q3T3 pattern.11,12 Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is particularly useful in patients suspected of having pulmonary emboli because it can identify RV pressure overload (Fig. 25.1).13-15 Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) can demonstrate pulmonary artery thrombi and RV pressure overload.16,17 Computed tomography (CT) of the chest with contrast can also detect thromboemboli in the major pulmonary arteries (Fig. 25.2).18-20 Gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can identify pulmonary thromboemboli and RV wall motion abnormalities.21 Contrast pulmonary angiography is a definitive diagnostic study but is infrequently performed in hemodynamically unstable patients.14,22 A normal D-dimer level has a high negative predictive value but a low positive predictive value and consequently is not useful for confirming pulmonary embolism.23-25

#### **Natural History**

In the United States, approximately 100,000 patients are diagnosed with acute pulmonary embolism each year, resulting in thousands of recognized deaths.26,27 Many additional deaths occur yearly because of undiagnosed massive pulmonary embolus mistaken for acute myocardial infarction or ventricular arrhythmia.26 In the International Cooperative Pulmonary Embolism Registry of 2454 consecutive patients presenting with acute pulmonary embolism from 7 countries, 4.2% had massive embolization.28

Untreated massive pulmonary artery embolism, accompanied by hypoxemia and hemodynamic instability, is nearly always fatal. Most deaths occur before effective treatment can be initiated. It is estimated that mortality for obstruction of more than 50% of the pulmonary vasculature approaches 50% and increases to 70% if the patient requires vasopressor therapy. If clinical deterioration continues, mortality approaches 100%.29

#### **Technique of Operation**

#### **Preoperative Preparation**

As soon as massive pulmonary embolism is suspected, high-dose unfractionated heparin should be administered. Most patients should receive a 10,000-unit bolus followed by a continuous infusion of at least 1250 units/hour, with a targeted activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT) of at least 80 seconds.<sup>26</sup>

Maintaining adequate oxygenation and cardiac output before establishing CPB is essential. Endotracheal intubation should be established if hypoxemia is present. If adequate cardiac output cannot be maintained with vasopressors, phosphodiesterase inhibitors, and sodium bicarbonate, or if external cardiac massage is required, CPB should be established by peripheral cannulation (see "Cardiopulmonary Bypass Established by Peripheral Cannulation" in Section III of Chapter 2). If not already given, heparin (300 units · kg<sup>-1</sup>) should be administered as soon as it is determined that operative intervention is indicated. It may be necessary to institute venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) in the intensive care unit (ICU) or emergency department to transport the patient to the operating room. Alternatively, prolonged ECMO support following failed thrombolytic therapy or in cases where patients are unsuitable for operation can be utilized to resolve the sequelae of massive pulmonary embolism.<sup>30</sup> If the patient's condition permits, the usual preparations for establishing CPB are made (see "Preparation for Cardiopulmonary Bypass" in Section III of Chapter 2). If the diagnosis of pulmonary embolism has not been made with certainty before the patient is transported to the operating room, TEE should be performed to establish the diagnosis before the chest is opened. 16,17

#### **Pulmonary Embolectomy**

A midline sternotomy is performed. If peripheral cannulation is not established, cannulae are placed in the aorta and both venae cavae. If the femoral vein has been cannulated, a second venous cannula is positioned in the superior vena cava, and the femoral vein cannula is withdrawn from the right atrium into the inferior vena cava. Alternatively, a long, two-stage cannula can be used. CPB is established with mild hypothermia, and tapes are placed and secured around the superior and inferior venae cavae. The aorta is clamped, and cardioplegic solution infused into the aortic root (see "Methods of Myocardial Management during Cardiac Surgery" in Chapter 3). Alternatively, the heart can be kept beating or fibrillating. The left atrium and left ventricle can be decompressed with a venting catheter inserted into the right superior pulmonary vein.

The pulmonary trunk is incised longitudinally several centimeters from the pulmonary valve. Forceps and suction are used to remove the thrombus. If necessary, the incision can be extended into the left pulmonary artery, and a separate incision can be made in the right pulmonary artery between the superior vena cava and ascending aorta. The use of a sterile fiberoptic bronchoscope to visualize and remove thrombus from secondary and tertiary branches of the pulmonary arteries has been described. The pleural spaces can be incised, and the lungs gently massaged to dislodge smaller thrombi.

Additionally, retrograde perfusion of the pulmonary veins through the opened left atrium can remove additional thromboembolic material from smaller pulmonary arterial branches (along with entrapped air).<sup>33-35</sup> The right atrial and RV cavities are

explored through a right atriotomy to search for and remove residual thrombi. After removing the thrombus, incisions in the pulmonary arteries and right atrium are closed with continuous 5-0 polypropylene suture. After completion of rewarming and evacuation of air from the cardiac chambers, CPB is discontinued. The procedure is completed in the standard manner (see "Completing Cardiopulmonary Bypass" in Section III of Chapter 2). The routine placement of a vena caval filter is not recommended but should be considered in patients with an absolute contraindication to anticoagulation.<sup>23</sup>

#### **Special Features of Postoperative Care**

Postoperative circulatory support with VA-ECMO can benefit patients with persisting severe RV failure after embolectomy.

Anticoagulation with warfarin is recommended for at least 6 months if there is no contraindication. If not already in place, an inferior vena caval filter should be inserted into patients for whom anticoagulant therapy is contraindicated.

#### **Results**

There is reasonable evidence that surgical embolectomy results are improving in the contemporary era. Kilic and colleagues,<sup>36</sup> by using International Classification of Diseases-9 (ICD-9) codes from the Nationwide Inpatient Sample, identified 2709 patients undergoing surgical embolectomy for pulmonary embolism between 1999 and 2008 (although unable to discriminate massive pulmonary embolism from other degrees of severity). The inpatient mortality was 27.2%. A similar analysis using the same data source (and the same limitations) was performed by Algahtani and colleagues.<sup>37</sup> They found an inpatient surgical embolectomy mortality between 2003 and 2009 and 2009 and 2014 of 23.1% and 14%, respectively. Although the reported operative mortality rate is still quite variable, some series have maintained improved outcomes for surgical embolectomy for massive pulmonary embolism.<sup>38-44</sup> A consistent finding in many of these reports is that the operative mortality is substantially greater in patients who are in cardiogenic shock, who have sustained a cardiac arrest, or who are undergoing cardiopulmonary resuscitation on the way to the operating room. The combined use of VA-ECMO and pulmonary embolectomy (surgical or catheter-based) appears to be a strategy that can rescue many of these critically ill patients. 45-52

#### **Indications for Operation**

Indications for pulmonary embolectomy for massive pulmonary embolism should not be considered categorical because the management decision for an individual patient is modulated by programmatic experience and technical resources (surgical vs. catheter-based intervention), the advent of multidisciplinary decision-making using Pulmonary Embolism Response Teams (PERTs),<sup>53</sup> and the lack of clarity regarding the procedural approach with superior clinical outcomes.

## **Special Situations and Controversies**

In massive pulmonary embolism, the initial therapy is anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin. However, this is invariably insufficient therapy and additional strategies are required, including systemic thrombolysis, catheter-directed therapies, or surgical embolectomy.56

## **Thrombolytic Therapy**

Systemic thrombolytic therapy is considered the cornerstone of management of massive pulmonary embolism unless there is a clear contraindication. A meta-analysis by Marti and colleagues,57

which included patients with massive (not exclusively, however) pulmonary embolism, demonstrated a significant reduction (compared with anticoagulation alone) in the combined outcome of mortality and recurrent pulmonary embolism with a 9.9% rate of severe bleeding and a 1.7% rate of intracranial hemorrhage. The current thrombolytic agent of choice is recombinant tissue-type plasminogen activator (rtPA), which is preferred to prolonged infusions of first-generation thrombolytic agents (streptokinase and urokinase). Based on hospital admissions data, there was no difference in 30-day mortality between surgical embolectomy and thrombolytic therapy, although the study did not have the scale of detail to be compelling.58

#### **Catheter-Directed Therapy**

In massive pulmonary embolism, the goal of catheter-directed approaches59 is to rapidly decrease the thrombus burden by pharmacomechanical means to reverse the hemodynamic and gas exchange derangement. The approaches are: (1) catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDL) and (2) catheter-based embolectomy. Selection of a specific approach is determined by patient hemodynamics, thrombus burden, and programmatic/operator experience.

CDL involves catheter-directed injection of a thrombolytic agent directly to the pulmonary artery thrombus, allowing a major reduction in the dose compared with systemic administration, theoretically reducing the risk of bleeding.60 CDL may use a simple infusion catheter or an ultrasound-assisted thrombolysis (USAT) catheter, which has a lumen to deliver the thrombolytic drug and a lumen that emits high-frequency, low-energy ultrasound, theoretically allowing more effective thrombolysis by better penetration of the drug. CDL may also be used with mechanical thrombus fragmentation, aspiration, and maceration.56

Several catheter devices are in use or in trial to accomplish thrombus embolectomy, such as catheters designed to macerate the thrombus; catheters designed to fragment the thrombus,61 allowing its extraction by reverse direction high-speed saline to create a vacuum (rheolytic thrombectomy); and catheters that can be advanced into the thrombus to allow extraction (rotational embolectomy)62 or aspirated through a large-bore suction catheter.

The place of each of these devices has not been established in massive pulmonary embolism. The studies are frequently underpowered, and the patient group usually heterogeneous, including patients with massive and submassive embolism. Furthermore, catheter-directed therapy results are frequently derived from pooled case series and registries.63-65 Superiority of catheter-directed therapy over surgical embolectomy has not been clearly established,66 but there are certainly patients at the highest risk for a surgical embolectomy who would benefit from the less invasive approach.

#### **SECTION II: CHRONIC PULMONARY THROMBOEMBOLIC DISEASE**

#### **Definition**

Chronic pulmonary thromboembolic disease is entrapment of thrombi in pulmonary arteries from a single episode or repeated embolic episodes that subsequently organize, or thrombi that develop in situ in the pulmonary arteries into firm, fibrous tissue that becomes incorporated into the vessel wall. These processes result in variable degrees of pulmonary artery obstruction and consequent pulmonary hypertension when obstruction becomes severe.67,68 Chronic pulmonary TED is estimated to develop in 1% to 5% of all cases of acute pulmonary embolism.69,70

#### **Historical Note**

Chronic pulmonary embolism was suspected by Hart in 1916 and by Molle in 1920. Still, it was not until 1928 that Ljungdahl described two symptomatic patients with chronic obstruction of the pulmonary arteries who ultimately died of right heart failure.71 The first successful embolectomies for recurrent pulmonary embolism were reported by Allison and colleagues in 1958 and by Snyder and colleagues in 1963.72,73 The technique was refined by Cabrol and colleagues, who used a lateral thoracotomy approach to obtain access to distal branches of the pulmonary arteries.74 Subsequently, several small series of patients were reported by Sabiston, Daily, and Dor and their colleagues, who used the lateral thoracotomy approach or CPB with a midline sternotomy.75-77 In 1980, Daily and colleagues reported use of hypothermic circulatory arrest in combination with CPB. This technique was used to eliminate severe back bleeding from the collateral circulation and improve visualization of the pulmonary arteries during endarterectomy.76

#### **Morphology**

The chronic thromboembolic process typically involves the proximal pulmonary arteries from trunk to sublobar levels characterized by fibrous tails, blind pouches, webs, and bands (Fig. 25.5). The distal arterial vasculature remains patent. This forms the basis for surgical treatment of this disorder. The disease can result from a single embolic episode with nonresolution of large thromboemboli or from repeated embolic episodes.78,79 Pulmonary arteries remaining unobstructed are chronically exposed to high flow and, eventually, high pressure. As a result, the proximal patent pulmonary arteries become greatly enlarged, and the distal arterial vasculature develops characteristic changes of pulmonary hypertension. Plexiform lesions in adult lungs diagnostic of primary pulmonary hypertension have been observed in chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH).78

The occlusive process is commonly discrete and central. When the thrombi become fibrotic and endothelialized, they no longer respond to thrombolytic or anticoagulant therapy.

## **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

#### **Symptoms**

Symptoms generally do not develop until months or years after the embolic event.80 Symptoms occur because of pulmonary hypertension and RV failure. Dyspnea with exertion is the most frequent presenting symptom.79 Other symptoms include fatigue, substernal chest pain with exercise, pleuritic pain, and hemoptysis.79,81

#### **Signs**

Pertinent physical findings are related to right heart failure: jugular venous distention, hepatomegaly, ascites, and peripheral edema. The right ventricle may be palpable near the lower left sternal border, and the pulmonic second sound accentuated and split. If right heart failure is severe, a murmur of tricuspid regurgitation is often present.

#### **Diagnostic Studies**

The chest radiograph may demonstrate RV enlargement and prominence of central pulmonary arteries. The ECG commonly shows RV hypertrophy with strain, right axis deviation, ST depression, T-wave inversion in the anterior precordial leads, and (less frequently) right bundle branch block.81 Pulmonary function studies are necessary to exclude restrictive or obstructive pulmonary parenchymal disease as the cause of pulmonary hypertension.

Imaging is critical for making the diagnosis and for surgical decision-making. However, all imaging frequently underestimates the severity of obstructive disease82 due to the process of recanalization. The ventilation/perfusion scan provides functional information that demonstrates ventilation/perfusion mismatches characteristic of CTEPH; a normal V/Q scan essentially rules out CTEPH. CT scanning, magnetic resonance imaging, and conventional pulmonary angiography provide structural information for surgical and balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA) decision-making. Characteristic findings include dilated proximal pulmonary arteries, with obstruction of one or more lobar arteries and appearance of organized thrombi as filling defects, webs, bands, or completely thrombosed vessels.

The dual-energy CT scan blood volume images (iodine maps) can provide useful information regarding lung perfusion. Examples of CTEPH imaging are illustrated in Figs. 25.7, 25.8, and 25.9. Right heart catheterization is performed to measure RV and pulmonary artery pressures and document presence of shunting at the atrial or ventricular level.

Coronary angiography should be performed in patients older than 40 to 45 years or in younger patients with risk factors for coronary artery disease if surgical treatment is contemplated so that obstructive coronary lesions, if present, can be bypassed at the time of pulmonary endarterectomy (PEA).

#### **Natural History**

Patients with CTEPH may remain asymptomatic for months or years. Hemodynamic progression may result from recurrent thromboembolism or in situ pulmonary artery thrombosis. Without intervention, survival is low and proportional to the degree of pulmonary hypertension at time of diagnosis.83,84 In the study of Riedel and colleagues, survival at 5 years was 30% among patients with a mean pulmonary artery pressure exceeding 40 mmHg at time of diagnosis, and only 10% among those with a mean pressure above 50 mmHg.85 In the study of Lewczuk and colleagues, a mean pulmonary artery pressure of 30 mmHg was the threshold for a poor prognosis.86 Among the 13 patients evaluated by Riedel and colleagues, 9 died a mean of 2.8 years after diagnosis of right heart failure; 7 of the 13 had evidence of recurrent embolization.85

It is appropriate to be mindful of this historic natural history data of untreated CTEPH that illustrate the potential lethality of CTEPH. #### **Technique of Operation**

#### **Preoperative Preparation**

Usual preparations for establishing CPB are made (see "Preparation for Cardiopulmonary Bypass" in Section III of Chapter 2). Routine placement of an inferior vena cava filter was recommended in the past for all patients undergoing a PEA, but most expert centers have discontinued this practice. Furthermore, the International CTEPH Registry found no evidence that an IVC filter had a long-term benefit. 92

#### **Pulmonary Endarterectomy**

After inserting appropriate monitoring devices and placing ECG and electroencephalographic electrodes, a median sternotomy is performed. Cannulae are inserted into the ascending aorta and both venae cavae, which are encircled with tapes. Cooling is initiated immediately after CPB is established. A venting catheter is inserted into the left atrium through a purse-string suture in the right superior pulmonary vein. A catheter is inserted into the coronary sinus through a purse-string suture in the right atrial wall for delivery of retrograde cardioplegia (see "Technique of Retrograde Infusion" in Chapter 3). A cannula is also inserted into the ascending aorta for delivery of antegrade cardioplegia and later aspiration of air. The aorta is clamped, and cold cardioplegic solution is infused into the aortic root. Subsequent infusions of cardioplegic solution are given every 15 to 20 minutes, either through the aortic root or retrograde through the coronary sinus (see "Methods of Myocardial Management during Cardiac Surgery" in Chapter 3).

During cooling, the anterior wall of the right pulmonary artery is exposed by retracting the superior vena cava laterally and the aorta medially using a self-retaining retractor, and the superior vena cava is dissected from the right pulmonary artery.

The general technique for establishing hypothermic circulatory arrest in adults is described in detail under "Aortic Arch Replacement" in Chapter 26. During cooling, methylprednisolone (7 mg · kg<sup>-1</sup>) and thiopental (10–15 mg · kg<sup>-1</sup>) are administered to enhance the neuroprotective effect of hypothermia. Mannitol (0.3–0.4 g · kg<sup>-1</sup>) and furosemide (100 mg) are infused to preserve renal function. Hematocrit is maintained in the range of 18% to 22%. When the nasopharyngeal temperature reaches 20°C and the electroencephalogram becomes isoelectric, circulatory arrest is established. The patient is placed in a moderate Trendelenburg position, and some PEA programs pack the head in ice. At this point, 20% to 25% of the patient's

calculated blood volume is withdrawn through the venous tubing into the reservoir of the oxygenator. Additionally, cerebral saturation monitoring may be used to keep the cerebral saturation greater than 35%.

After circulatory arrest is established, and working from the left side of the operating table, the right pulmonary artery is incised between the aorta and superior vena cava (Fig. 25.10),93 extending the incision below the orifice of the right upper lobe branch and into the anterior surface of the artery distally. An endarterectomy plane is established. Establishing the correct plane is important; a plane that is too deep may result in perforation of the vessel, and a plane that is too superficial will result in an inadequate endarterectomy (Fig. 25.11). The core of TED is isolated circumferentially (Fig. 25.12) and removed from the upper lobe and from the remaining portion of the pulmonary artery. Applying gentle traction with PEA double-action forceps on the endarterectomy specimen while sweeping the wall of the pulmonary artery away with a suction dissection will result in progressive removal of the thrombus (Fig. 25.13). The TED core is separated from the proximal portion of the right pulmonary artery and removed. The arteriotomy is closed with a continuous 5-0 or 6-0 polypropylene suture (Fig. 25.14). Alternatively, a patch of autologous pericardium can be used that is sutured into place with a continuous 6-0 polypropylene suture.

Postoperative care is conducted as described in Chapter 5. Mechanical ventilation is used with an Fio2 sufficient to maintain Sao2 greater than 95%. Paco2 is maintained at or below 35 mmHg.

Reperfusion pulmonary edema is occasionally encountered postoperatively and is an important problem in approximately 10% of patients.95 Lung injury usually develops within the first 48 hours and is characterized by hypoxemia and radiographic infiltrates in the areas that have been endarterectomized and reperfused.96 Treatment is generally supportive, using the lowest Fio2 to maintain Sao2 greater than 90% and positive end-expiratory pressures of 5 to 10 cm. Aggressive diuresis is often necessary to remove fluid and reduce the incidence of pulmonary edema.97 Reperfusion pulmonary edema has been demonstrated to be neutrophil-mediated, and treatment with agents that block selectin-mediated adhesion of leukocytes to the endothelium has reduced the prevalence of this complication.96

Hypoxemia is relatively common after PEA due to the shunt created by the ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) mismatch through the endarterectomized lobes. Some patients will require supplemental oxygen, but this requirement disappears by about 3 to 4 weeks postoperatively. A heparin infusion may be commenced when the chest tube output is less than 25 mL/hour for 2 consecutive hours, and warfarin is given on the first postoperative day.

## **Indications for Operation**

It is important that the assessment of operability occurs in an experienced multidisciplinary center for the management of CTEPH,98,100,102,103 and the experience of the team is a major determinant of the outcome for an individual patient. The indications100,103 for PEA are based on the following considerations:

- PEA should be considered in patients who have hemodynamic or ventilatory impairment at rest or exercise
- There should be concordance between the severity of the pulmonary hypertension and the extent of the TED and obstruction
- The extent and level of thromboembolic obstruction should be based on high-quality imaging
- The severity of pulmonary hypertension—patients with a significantly elevated pulmonary vascular resistance, .1500 dynes per second/cm–5, traditionally thought to have a prohibitive operative mortality may still have substantial benefit from PEA.104 This degree of pulmonary vascular resistance is no longer considered a contraindication to PEA
- Patients with chronic thromboembolic disease (CTED)– pulmonary artery pressure is normal at rest but becomes elevated on exercise. These patients should be considered for PEA as they may be quite symptomatic
- Presence of comorbidity
- Presence of coexisting cardiac disease
- Realistic patient expectations regarding the benefit/risk assessment

An important absolute contraindication to PEA is the presence of severe underlying obstructive or restrictive lung disease.83

#### **Special Situations and Controversies**

#### **Massive Intraoperative Endobronchial Hemorrhage**

This is a rare and potentially lethal complication, which is usually due to disruption of the adventitia of a distal pulmonary artery branch, resulting in massive endobronchial bleeding on reperfusion. A variety of strategies have been used including bronchus blockers, fibrin glue injected into the pulmonary artery, a wick of hemostatic material,105 but more recently, the use of ECMO with reversal of anticoagulation seems to have successfully rescued patients from an otherwise fatal complication.106-110 Identifying a breach in the pulmonary artery using the "bubbles technique"111 before reperfusion (filling the pulmonary arteries with saline and looking for bubbles with hand ventilation) may allow intervention before the hemorrhage occurs.

#### **Role of Balloon Pulmonary Angioplasty (BPA) and PEA**

BPA has been used in combination with PEA in patients with operable disease on one side (PEA) and BPA on the other side with inoperable disease.112 BPA should be considered in patients with persistent pulmonary hypertension following a PEA.113,114 PEA after BPA does run the risk that the dissection plane can be distorted or obliterated, increasing the risk of pulmonary artery perforation.103

#### **Reoperation PEA**

CTEPH can recur after a PEA, usually because of anticoagulation failure or non-compliance, initially incomplete PEA, or discontinuation of anticoagulation for medical reasons.115 Reoperation PEA can be performed with low operative mortality and significant symptomatic improvement.115

#### **Anticoagulation after PEA**

Life-long anticoagulation is required after PEA. There is evidence116 that the risk of recurrent venous thromboembolism is significantly higher with the use of direct oral anticoagulants rather than the recommended vitamin K antagonists.

#### **SECTION III: PULMONARY ARTERY ANEURYSM**

#### **Definition**

A true pulmonary artery aneurysm is a focal dilation of all three layers of the pulmonary artery. A pseudoaneurysm lacks all three layers of the arterial wall. This section discusses only aneurysms of the pulmonary trunk and branch pulmonary arteries (proximal pulmonary artery aneurysms) that are amenable to surgical treatment.

The pulmonary artery size that qualifies as aneurysmal has not been clearly defined. The mean diameter of the main pulmonary artery in healthy adults is 25 mm 3 mm, with an upper limit in males of 29 mm and females 27 mm.117 The most common definition of a pulmonary artery aneurysm is dilation greater than 1.5 times the upper limit of normal.118

#### **Historical Note**

The first description of a pulmonary artery aneurysm was by Bristowe in 1860.119 He reported an arteriosclerotic fusiform aneurysm of the pulmonary artery observed at autopsy. In a review of 109,571 autopsies by Deterling and Clagett in 1947, only 8 proximal pulmonary artery aneurysms were identified.120 The first successful repair of an aneurysm of the pulmonary trunk was reported by Williams and colleagues in 1971.121 It was resected and replaced with a woven polyester graft. Aneurysmorrhaphy and patch repair were subsequently used as an alternative method of treatment.122,123

#### **SECTION IV: PULMONARY ARTERY DISSECTION**

#### **Definition**

Pulmonary artery dissection (PAD) is separation of vessel layers of pulmonary arteries, most commonly of the pulmonary trunk, although it can extend into the major branches. It is a rare condition.

#### **Historical Note**

According to Shilkin and colleagues, the first reported case of PAD was by Helmbrecht in 1842.156 Fernando and colleagues157 were able to assemble 150 cases from the literature, and it does appear that the antemortem diagnosis of PAD has substantially increased since 2004. This may be due to increased recognition, although significant publication bias cannot be discounted. Although most cases of PAD are established at postmortem, there are a number of case reports of successful surgical management.142,157-162

#### **Morphology**

PAD originates most commonly in the pulmonary trunk (71% of the 52 patients reviewed by Inayama and colleagues)140 and in the major branches of the pulmonary arteries. It is frequently associated with pulmonary hypertension, including Eisenmenger syndrome, but among patients who have hypertension, only a small percentage have pulmonary arterial hypertension. In the review of Inayama and colleagues, 28 patients had secondary pulmonary hypertension, and congenital cardiac malformations were present in 23 with patent ductus arteriosus being the most common.140 Marfan syndrome was present in only 1 of the 52 patients, although cystic medial degeneration was observed in the pulmonary arteries of 23 of 29 patients with detailed histologic studies. Pulmonary hypertension was present in 20 of these 23 patients.140

In the majority of reported cases (86%), diagnosis of PAD was made at autopsy.140 Rupture of the pulmonary artery into the pericardial cavity or mediastinum or into a pleural space was a frequent finding.

## **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

Presenting symptoms are identical to those observed with acute aortic dissection and include severe chest pain, dyspnea, central cyanosis, and shock. Several days may elapse between onset of pain and rupture.156 The diagnosis may be made by imaging studies including CT (Fig. 25.22),163 MRI, and echocardiography.

## **Natural History**

Prognosis of untreated PAD is extremely poor. Diagnosis has been infrequently made before death, and only a few patients have been successfully managed by surgical intervention.141,142

## **Technique of Operation**

The number of successful repairs of PAD in the literature is small; therefore, a preferred strategy cannot be defined. The technique of operation that seems the most reasonable is identical to that described for treatment of pulmonary artery aneurysm (see "Technique of Operation" in the preceding section and Fig. 25.18). However, successful endovascular procedures have been described.164-166

#### **Results**

It is difficult to determine the outcomes of patients sustaining a PAD because of the small number of reported cases and the accompanying publication bias, the uncertain number of patients from the literature in whom the diagnosis was made postmortem, and the fact that PAD is invariably associated with pulmonary hypertension, substantially increasing the operative risk.

Because of the extremely poor prognosis associated with PAD, operative treatment should be considered for any patient in whom the diagnosis is established. An endovascular procedure should be considered in patients where the operative risk is thought to be prohibitively high.

#### **SECTION V: PULMONARY ARTERY TUMORS**

#### **Definition**

Primary tumors of the pulmonary artery are malignant tumors, most commonly sarcomas, usually arising in the pulmonary trunk and extending into right and left pulmonary arteries. They commonly involve the pulmonary valve and may extend into the right ventricle.171 These tumors, although rare, are being reported with increasing frequency.172-174

#### **Historical Note**

The first report of a sarcoma of the pulmonary trunk was by Mandelstamm in 1923.175 Early surgical treatment consisted of resecting the segment of pulmonary trunk containing tumor, pneumonectomy, or both.171 More recently, resecting and reconstructing the pulmonary arteries using CPB has been used.176-178

## **Morphology**

Sarcomatous tumors arise from the intima of the pulmonary artery, most frequently from the dorsal surface of the pulmonary trunk.179 They rarely extend through the adventitia or invade surrounding structures. Pulmonary metastases are present in up to 60% of patients. Metastases to lymph nodes and other organs are less common.171,180 Microscopic features are highly variable. In a review of 99 pulmonary sarcomas by Lyerly and colleagues, many cell types were represented, including leiomyosarcoma, myxofibrosarcoma, fibrosarcoma, chondrosarcoma, angiosarcoma, osteogenic sarcoma, and liposarcoma.171

Lee and colleagues181 have made the point that in their series of 55 patients with pulmonary artery sarcoma, 91% had bilateral disease, implying that lung resection alone could never be curative.

Clinical presentation resembles that of more commonly occurring conditions such as chronic pulmonary TED. Symptoms

## **Technique of Operation**

The usual operation for this condition is PEA, outlined in section III of this chapter. There may be additional surgical modifications dictated by the pathology and likelihood of clearance of the tumor such as pneumonectomy and main pulmonary artery resection and pulmonary valve replacement. Pneumonectomy alone is very unlikely to be curative. In most circumstances, the therapeutic direction is palliation, but survival may be prolonged with aggressive surgical resection coupled with multimodal adjuvant therapy including targeted radiation and systemic chemo/ immunotherapy.

#### **Results**

Survival after surgery is very dependent on successful clearance of the tumor. In the series of 31 patients of Mussot and colleagues,184 25 of whom underwent PEA (5 including pneumonectomy), 5 undergoing pneumonectomy alone, and in 1 patient, replacement of the right pulmonary artery alone, the 5-year survival was 22%. #### **Indications for Operation**

Because radical surgical resection likely represents the only chance for cure, operation should be considered in patients with no comorbid conditions that would preclude the use of CPB and who have no evidence of distant metastases. Adequate pulmonary reserve should be documented for patients who may require pneumonectomy. PEA can be considered for palliation.

#### **SECTION VI: ACQUIRED DISEASES OF THE SYSTEMIC VEINS**

#### **Definition**

Acquired disease of the systemic veins entails obstruction—partial or complete—of the superior vena cava (SVC) and inferior vena cava (IVC). While SVC syndrome is important and worthy of description, its surgical significance has diminished substantially with the development of endovascular approaches. Left and right brachiocephalic veins, including the jugular-subclavian vein confluence, are major tributaries of the SVC and may be considered collectively with conditions of the SVC. Congenital anomalies of the venae cavae and axillary vein conditions such as effort thrombosis are not considered in this chapter. Obstruction results from extrinsic compression, direct invasion by disease processes, or thrombosis. *SVC syndrome* is the result of venous hypertension in the head, neck, and arms caused by SVC obstruction.

#### **Historical Note**

William Hunter described the first recorded case of SVC syndrome in 1757 in a patient with syphilitic aneurysm of the aorta.191 SVC obstruction was due to compression by the aneurysm. William Osler described SVC compression in his classic text of 1892: "Along the convex border of the ascending part [of the aorta], aneurism frequently develops, and may grow to a large size... In this situation the sac is liable indeed to compress the superior vena cava, causing engorgement of the vessels of the head and arm."192 William Stokes' text of 1853 described SVC obstruction and noted the more frequent occurrence with cancer: "As an indication of intrathoracic tumour, an extensively varicose state of the superficial veins of the neck and thorax is probably less frequent in aneurismal than in cancerous disease... The superior cava may be adherent to the tumour, and become narrowed, not only by pressure, but by adhesion of its internal surfaces."

The first successful bypass operations for SVC obstruction by Klassen and colleagues in 1951 and Bricker and McAfee in 1952 were performed using autologous femoral vein grafts.193,194 In 1965, Hanlon and Danis used other large veins to replace or bypass the SVC, employing variously the femoral, subclavian, and jugular veins.195 In 1962, Benvenuto and colleagues constructed a composite panel graft from pieces of saphenous vein for replacing the SVC.196 A number of conduits had been tried, including autologous, homologous,197 and heterologous vein, aorta, and various synthetic materials. Scherck and colleagues198 concluded that autologous vein grafts of nearly the same size as the SVC were most likely to remain patent. Obtaining such a large autologous vein usually requires removing a large one from elsewhere in the body, with resultant venous drainage problems, or constructing a composite graft from a smaller one.

Synthetic grafts are attractive because of their convenience, availability, and the variety of sizes available. In 1973, Effeney and colleagues reported successful bypass of the SVC using polyester grafts.199 In 1977, Avasthi and Moghissi used a polyester graft interposed between the brachiocephalic vein on the left side and right atrial appendage to bypass the obstructed SVC.200 Thrombosis of polyester grafts limited success of the procedure. Expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) was used successfully as a venous replacement conduit in experimental venous operations in dogs.201-203 Hiratzka and colleagues showed that PTFE and polyester were equally poor venous substitute conduits in the experimental setting, and that they did not approach the effective patency of autologous vein grafts.204 Reichle and colleagues suggested that this was because autologous vein grafts have a living endothelial surface even after initial endothelial desquamation, whereas prosthetic graft inner surfaces are composed of collagen matrix.205 Nevertheless, success using PTFE grafts has been reported. Antiplatelet-adhesive drugs may be of benefit in maintaining patency of PTFE grafts.206 Dartevelle and colleagues reported that 12 of 13 PTFE grafts used to replace the SVC were patent an average of 24 months after operation.207

Composite vein grafts constructed from the saphenous or external jugular veins, in paneled or longitudinal fashion, have been used clinically for SVC bypass or replacement.208,209 In 1974, Chiu and colleagues reported constructing a composite vein graft from the external jugular vein, which was matched to the size of the SVC.210 The donor vein was opened longitudinally and wrapped in spiral fashion around a tubular stent of approximately the same size as the SVC. Vein edges were then sutured together to form the conduit. The graft occluded in the initial three experiments in dogs. After that, however, 10 consecutive grafts remained patent for up to 15 months. This report prompted successful application of this technique in humans by Doty and Baker in 1976.211 Successful percutaneous balloon dilation of the SVC in a child was reported by Rocchini and colleagues in 1982.212 In 1986, Sherry and colleagues reported successful dilation of an SVC stricture caused by pacemaker electrodes in an adult.213 In 1987, Rosch and colleagues used an expandable wire stent to treat SVC obstruction caused by malignant disease that recurred after extensive radiation therapy.214 Endovascular approaches are now so effective that surgical relief of SVC obstruction is largely reserved for an unsuccessful endovascular procedure.

#### **Morphology and Pathogenesis**

#### **Morphology**

*Collateral Circulation.* SVC obstruction stimulates formation of extensive venous collateral circulation (Fig. 25.24). The azygos vein is the only major venous channel that enters the SVC and is the most important collateral pathway. When SVC obstruction is located caudad to a patent azygos vein, there is retrograde flow through the azygos and hemiazygos veins to the lumbar veins below the diaphragm and to the IVC. When obstruction is cephalad to the patent azygos vein, collateral veins in the neck allow blood flow to enter the azygos system and continue directly into the distal SVC below the obstruction. When connection of the azygos vein to the SVC is involved in the obstruction, more complex and varied pathways must develop to drain the upper body. One prominent system consists of the internal thoracic veins, which connect to superior and inferior epigastric veins and subsequently to the IVC by way of the external iliac veins. Lateral thoracic veins drain to thoracoepigastric veins; eventually, blood may enter the femoral veins. Paraspinous veins form a collateral network that connects to the IVC via lumbar veins. The esophageal venous network also can decompress the thorax via the left gastric vein to the portal system. This pathway is not very important unless esophageal varicosities develop, and only rarely are these associated with bleeding into the gastrointestinal tract. Subcutaneous veins are a particularly important means of bringing blood flow from the upper body to below the diaphragm via the IVC.

Typically, the venous pressure proximal to the site of SVC obstruction may reach as high as 20 to 40 mmHg.215 Cerebral venous decompression may be provided through a single internal jugular vein as the veins of the right and left sides of the brain are in continuity through midline venous sinuses.216 Superior and inferior sagittal sinuses drain the cerebral hemispheres to the confluence of sinuses that communicate through transverse and sigmoid sinuses to either internal jugular vein. The cavernous venous sinuses also connect both sides of the brain to either internal jugular vein. Cerebral venous drainage, therefore, may remain adequate.

#### **Pathogenesis**

SVC obstruction may be caused by a spectrum of malignant and benign conditions (Box 25.2). Disease in any adjacent anatomic structures may contribute to SVC syndrome. Historically, for several centuries syphilis and tuberculosis were the major causes of SVC obstruction. The common causes of SVC obstruction have changed over the past 70 years. In 1949, the most common were thoracic malignancy (33%), aortic aneurysm (30%), and chronic granulomatous mediastinitis (19%).217 Up until 1962, approximately 25% of cases were due to benign disease218; between 1969 and 1979, that proportion decreased to 3%.216

Mediastinal malignancies account for nearly two-thirds of cases of SVC syndrome, 85% being due to lung cancer and non-Hodgkin lymphoma. Of the lung cancer cases, small cell cancer and squamous cell cancer are the types most commonly implicated in SVC obstruction, but the higher prevalence of non-small cell lung cancer results in this histologic type being responsible for approximately 50% of malignancy-related cases of SVC obstruction.219 Non-Hodgkin lymphoma accounts for approximately

#### **Clinical Features and Diagnostic Criteria**

#### **Superior Vena Cava Syndrome**

Because *extrinsic compression* usually produces obstruction gradually, collateral circulation develops, the obstruction is usually well tolerated, and the patient has few if any signs and symptoms. If obstruction develops rapidly, as in malignant tumor invasion and in infants and children with central venous catheters, collateral circulation may not have time to develop and adequately decompress upper body veins. The most severe syndrome develops in cases of SVC thrombosis in which obstruction is sudden and collateral venous channels have no time to develop. Thrombosis may also involve major caval tributaries and thus eliminate major collateral pathways. Thrombosis often accompanies SVC obstruction from any cause and compounds the problem because (1) subsequent fibrotic organization of the clot results in permanent SVC stenosis or closure, and (2) thrombosis does not respond to treatment directed at the primary disease process that resulted in the SVC obstruction.

Thoracic lymphatic ducts drain into the subclavian veins and are affected by venous hypertension associated with SVC obstruction. Pulmonary lymphatics may also be secondarily affected, leading to increased lung water and dyspnea. Respiratory insufficiency is frequently associated with acute SVC obstruction and may be difficult to manage. Chylous pleural effusion may result from thoracic lymphatic obstruction.

#### **Symptoms**

Patients with SVC obstruction usually present with a wellestablished syndrome that is easily recognized and unmistakable.228 Only rarely does complete SVC obstruction occur without noticeable signs or symptoms. The typical syndrome consists of swelling of face, neck, and arms; shortness of breath; orthopnea; and cough. Patients may notice tightness of a shirt collar and that their face is flushed and swollen, especially around the eyes. Other symptoms include hoarseness, stridor, tongue swelling, nasal congestion, epistaxis, dysphagia, headache, dizziness, syncope, lethargy, and chest pain. Symptoms are aggravated by bending forward, stooping, or lying down. Many patients become dyspneic when recumbent and must sleep in a chair.

#### **Signs**

The most common signs are dilation and tortuosity of upper body veins, plethora or cyanosis of the face, and swelling of face, neck, or arms.215,220 Other signs include proptosis, glossal edema, rhinorrhea, laryngeal edema, mentation changes, elevated venous and cerebrospinal fluid pressures, and chylous pleural effusion.228 Signs and symptoms suggesting cerebral or laryngeal edema were shown to be of prognostic importance by Lochridge and colleagues.216 Headache, vertigo, visual disturbances, decreased mentation, stupor, somnolence, and convulsions indicate cerebral edema216,229; hoarseness and stridor suggest laryngeal edema.

#### **Clinical Diagnosis**

Clinical diagnosis is usually obvious. Location, degree, and cause of SVC obstruction should be characterized in every case. Some believe that palliation of the intrathoracic malignancy should proceed without delay. Others argue that SVC syndrome is seldom a medical emergency and should be characterized as completely as possible in an orderly fashion so that treatment can be specific. Although tissue diagnosis by biopsy can usually be obtained, in some cases, it may be difficult and even hazardous. Patients seek relief of symptoms of SVC and seldom complain of symptoms related to the etiologic cause of the obstruction. Treatment of SVC syndrome should be accompanied by diagnostic measures and therapy directed at the causative primary disease as well as immediate relief of the symptoms of SVC obstruction.

*Diagnostic Approach.* Confirmation of the clinical diagnosis relies on imaging. Initial chest radiographs are abnormal in 84% of patients and show mediastinal widening in 64% of cases.230 Bronchogenic carcinoma is the most common cause of SVC syndrome and may show a right-sided mass. An anterior mediastinal mass suggests a lymphoma. Contrast-enhanced CT venogram is the critical initial advanced imaging study (Fig. 25.25) 220 and will delineate the degree of SVC obstruction, the etiology of the obstruction, differentiate thrombosis from extrinsic compression, and identify collateral venous circulation.220,231 Digital subtraction venography also provides similar information to CT venography but also assists the interventional radiologist in planning the endovascular procedure including stent sizing and stent deployment landing zones (Fig. 25.26). Using venography in 36 patients, Stanford and Doty defined four patterns of venous circulation useful in planning therapy (Box 25.3).232 Magnetic resonance venography may be an alternative and is equally sensitive and specific compared with conventional venography.231,233

Duplex ultrasound of the upper extremities can provide information about thrombus in the jugular, subclavian, and axillary veins, especially when the SVC obstruction is associated with indwelling leads and lines and help identify access for venography and endovascular intervention.215,231

#### **Natural History**

The natural history of SVC obstruction and accompanying SVC syndrome is variable and depends on etiology of the obstruction, rapidity of onset, extent of the obstructive process, and extent of collateral venous circulation. Collateral venous circulation usually develops rapidly in response to SVC obstruction and is often sufficient to relieve SVC syndrome. The relationship of SVC obstruction to location and patency of the azygos vein has an important effect on natural history.236 When the azygos vein is closed, central thoracic collateral circulation is eliminated. SVC syndrome is worse because venous drainage from the upper body depends on smaller, less reliable venous channels in the chest wall and skin.

Even minimal tumor invasion of the SVC or compression by benign tumor may stimulate formation and propagation of blood clot within the vena cava. Malignant diseases resulting in acute SVC obstruction and thrombosis may not resolve with thrombolytic and radiation therapy. Acute SVC obstruction associated with signs of cerebral or laryngeal edema results in death within 6 weeks, apparently related to SVC syndrome rather than the primary etiology of the caval obstruction.216 The malignant process responds to therapy in a variable manner depending in part on cell type, ultimately determining the patient's fate.

Fibrosis associated with clot resolution and late clot propagation may be accompanied by worsening of symptoms and signs of SVC syndrome, despite adequate venous collateralization. Thus, SVC obstruction may persist even though there appears to be a good response to treatment of the tumor. Some benign causes of SVC obstruction lead to severe and relentless inflammation and fibrosis, resulting in recurrent and extending obstruction.

Clot propagation within the venous system proximal to the primary site of SVC obstruction may lead to progression of SVC syndrome in patients with benign etiology. Clot formation around indwelling catheters is especially prone to extensive thrombosis of the SVC and major thoracic and cervical venous tributaries. Some patients with SVC obstruction may never develop adequate collateral circulation even though the causative process is stabilized or eliminated.

## **Technique of Operation**

In the current era, surgical relief of SVC obstruction is essentially only performed in the setting of a failed endovascular procedure or in the unusual situation that the interventional radiologist deems a patient unsuitable for an endovascular procedure. Surgical access is individualized based on the anatomy and morphology of the obstruction. The series of 38 patients undergoing SVC resection for a variety of benign and malignant disease (not all of whom had SVC obstruction) by Okereke and colleagues237 had access through a median sternotomy (27 cases), thoracotomy (7 cases), clamshell incision (3 cases), and a trap-door incision (1 case). Other incisions that might be considered include a sternotomy with an elongated cervical incision (reversed L shape)238 and a sternotomy with a right-sided neck incision.239 The operation may be performed with or without CPB.

In the era when SVC obstruction was a surgical as opposed to an interventional radiologic procedure, a number of tissue conduits were used including the spiral vein graft,210,211,240 femoral vein graft,194,241 paneled saphenous vein graft (multiple segments sutured side by side to create a conduit),196 saphenous vein grafts,242,243 autologous pericardial tubes,244,245 aortic allografts,198 and SVC allografts.246

In the current era, the most frequently used graft to either bypass or replace the SVC is polytetrafluoroethylene grafts (usually ringed),207,237,238,241,247,248 but other materials have been used including a custom-made bovine pericardial tube (Shelhigh No-React Pericardial Patch; Shelhigh, Millburn, New Jersey)248 and collagen-coated knitted polyester graft.239

#### **Direct Operation on Superior or Inferior Vena Cava**

A direct operation on the SVC or IVC is performed when there is blood clot or tumor partially obstructing the lumen. Indications include removal of thrombus surrounding intraluminal catheters used for parenteral feeding, chemotherapy, or cardiac pacing. In these cases, the thrombus may adhere to the catheter or the intima of the SVC, making nonoperative removal of the mass hazardous or impossible. There may be infection associated with the foreign body or invading the thrombus.

For procedures involving the SVC, a median sternotomy is made. Strategy for inserting the venous cannulae for venous return is based on location of the mass that is to be removed. If the mass is located completely within the SVC, a two-stage venous cannula is inserted through the right atrial wall and passed into the IVC. Masses that completely fill the right atrium require peripheral cannulation (femoral vein, internal jugular vein) (see "Cardiopulmonary Bypass Established by Peripheral Cannulation" in Section III of Chapter 2).

CPB is established, and the patient's body temperature is lowered to 16°C to 20°C (see "Aortic Arch Replacement" under Technique of Operation in Chapter 26). Cardioplegic solution can be administered to arrest the heart after placement of an aortic occlusion clamp. Alternatively, the procedure can be performed using hypothermic fibrillation. When the target temperature is reached, CPB is discontinued. Venous cannulae may be removed to enhance exposure.

The SVC is incised to expose the mass. Endarterectomy technique using a Freer septum elevator may be necessary to remove adherent thrombus. The mass is removed completely, even if a secondary incision is required, and the access incision closed. If a portion of the wall of the vena cava has been excised, the defect can be repaired using a patch of autologous or bovine pericardium to prevent caval stenosis. Air is evacuated from the right heart, CPB is resumed, and rewarming commenced (see "Rewarming" in Section IV of Chapter 2).

IVC extension of a tumor up to the right atrium has been described with renal cell carcinoma,249-254 adrenocortical carcinoma,251,255 and Wilms tumor.249,256-258 IVC extension and obstruction may present with lower extremity edema, varicocele, pulmonary embolism, and Budd-Chiari syndrome.249,259-262 Renal cell carcinoma has venous tumor thrombus in 10% of all cases, and 1% to 4% may extend into the right atrium.263 Mayo Clinic Renal Cell Carcinoma Tumor Thrombosis Level Classification 4264 tumors (extension above the diaphragm) require a surgical approach with a urologic and cardiac surgical team.

Masses in the IVC adjacent to the right atrium are exposed through an incision in the right atrium after establishing hypothermic circulatory arrest. It is sometimes necessary to extend the incision into the IVC. The orifices of the hepatic veins should be thoroughly inspected to ensure that no residual tumor or thrombus remains.

Hypothermic CPB with an interval of circulatory arrest permits safe removal of tumor and thrombus from the retrohepatic segment of the IVC as well as from the right atrium and pulmonary artery.265

#### **Results**

The overall success of management of SVC syndrome in the current era reflects primarily the results of endovascular procedures on the obstructed SVC. However, for the quite small number of reported patients that undergo a surgical approach in the current era, the results appear to be very good. The symptoms and signs of SVC syndrome are relieved immediately. When an operation is performed in patients for malignant obstruction, the natural history of the tumor determines the outcome. Long-term patency of the bypass conduit is critically important but there is no trial/ study/consensus that has produced compelling data to suggest the superiority of one conduit over any others. Therefore, the current use of PTFE grafts (usually externally supported with rings) appears to be a very reasonable approach. Determining the outcome in terms of patency is confounded by the small numbers of patients in current era surgical series, inconsistent follow-up imaging to determine graft patency, the variable use of postoperative anticoagulation and antithrombotic drugs, and for patients with a malignant etiology for SVC syndrome, the competing risk of death from the tumor.

Although not directly analogous, in a series of 72 SVC resections,247 PTFE grafts were used in 28 patients with one postoperative death and one in-hospital graft thrombosis successfully managed with anticoagulation. All surviving 27 patients underwent a CT scan 4 months after surgery, and all grafts were patent. In the study by Okereke and colleagues,237 38 patients underwent SVC resection, and included configurations such as left brachiocephalic vein to SVC. There were 2 perioperative deaths, and 11 patients died of the underlying disease. Twenty patients underwent a CT scan 1 to 113 months (average 24 months) postoperatively with the demonstration of 2 occluded grafts, neither patient having symptoms of SVC obstruction.

The use of either short- or long-term anticoagulation with warfarin, which would seem prudent given the use of prosthetic material in a low pressure-low flow environment, has been inconsistently used so a consensus statement is not possible.

## **Indications for Operation**

In the current era, given the clear efficacy of the endovascular approach, the indications for surgery are a failed endovascular procedure or the unlikely event that an interventional procedure is thought to have a low probability of success.

## **Special Situations and Controversies**

#### **Nonoperative Management**

The management of SVC syndrome should be performed in a multidisciplinary setting, especially for malignant disease, to confirm the diagnosis and the etiology of the obstruction and plan a management strategy. The team should involve oncology, interventional radiology, surgery, and pulmonology representatives. The type and timing of the management approach are dependent on the acuity of the patient's presentation and the underlying etiology of the obstruction. The management options include catheter-based thrombolysis, endovascular procedures, chemotherapy, radiotherapy and surgery, or some combination of options. #### **Injury to Left Brachiocephalic Vein during Sternotomy**

Laceration of the left brachiocephalic vein is a hazard of sternotomy. It occurs rarely during primary sternotomy but is a common threat during repeat sternotomy. The hazard is reduced during reentry by dividing the sternum with an oscillating saw rather than using the standard blade. Once the sternum is divided, tissues adherent to the posterior table of the sternum superiorly are separated sharply or with the electrocautery. Mobilizing the tissues surrounding the brachiocephalic vein will reduce the role of disruption. Wide separation of the sternal edges should be avoided to reduce tension on the vein.

The most common method of managing major injuries is ligation. Usually, left arm or neck swelling is not observed, although later thrombosis of the tributaries of the vein may occur. The consequences of uncontrolled hemorrhage and difficulties in repairing the vein without creating stenosis appear to outweigh the risks of ligation. Repair of the vein is feasible if the disruption is not complete and can be accomplished using autologous or bovine pericardium. If the vein is disrupted at its junction with the SVC such that ligation will compromise flow into the SVC or cause stenosis of the right brachiocephalic vein, repair or SVC bypass should be performed to avoid SVC syndrome.

#### **Budd-Chiari Syndrome**

In 1845, Budd described three cases of hepatic vein thrombosis caused by abscess-induced phlebitis.279 In 1899, Chiari reported another three cases of obliterative phlebitis of large hepatic veins.280 *Budd-Chiari syndrome* (BCS) was thus initially defined as symptomatic occlusion of hepatic veins, but subsequent inclusion of various obliterative changes in the hepatic portion of the IVC and hepatic vein orifices broadened the definition.

BCS is a rare condition characterized by hepatic venous outflow obstruction causing increased hepatic sinusoidal pressure which results in portal hypertension, hepatic congestion and sinusoidal dilatation, hepatomegaly, reduced liver perfusion, and degrees of atrophy and hepatocyte necrosis.281 The level of the obstruction can be located anywhere from the small hepatic venules to the IVC right atrial junction,282 which means that hepatic outflow obstruction due to cardiac disease such as pericardial construction or sinusoidal obstruction syndrome do not fall under the definition of BCS.283 The etiology of primary BCS is related in 60% to 70% of cases to a prothrombotic state, particularly in patients from Western countries.284 The prothrombotic state may be due to acquired conditions such as myeloproliferative malignancies,281,285 antiphospholipid syndrome,286 factor V Leiden,92,287,288 and protein C, S, or antithrombin deficiency.285 Behcet syndrome,289,290 celiac disease, and sarcoidosis have also been implicated.284 Interestingly, the morphology of BCS differs in Asian from Western countries where, in Asian patients, a membranous web-like obstruction of the IVC may be seen, which is considered by some to be congenital, but there may be an association with nutritional depravation.291 Secondary BCS may occur due to hepatic vein compression from a variety of infectious diseases such as hydatid, pyogenic infection, and echinococcosis,284 or benign or malignant tumors including nephroblastoma, renal adenocarcinoma, or leiomyosarcoma of the IVC.285,292-294

The clinical spectrum may range from asymptomatic BCS295 to fulminant BCS with acute liver failure.296

While a surgical approach has been used in the past where the IVC web morphology was the predominant lesion,297,298 endovascular procedures have largely replaced surgery.

The current recommended therapeutic strategy is a step-wise algorithm283 commencing with anticoagulation, angioplasty and stenting where segmental stenoses are present in the IVC, or hepatic veins, and for patients who do not respond to stenting procedures or are not suitable for stenting, the next step is transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) or a surgical mesocaval shunt. For Western patients, angioplasty/stenting is the definitive therapy in less than 10% of patients.299 The final step in the algorithm is liver transplantation for patients unresponsive to the previous steps or with fulminant BCS. The study by Alqahtani and colleagues176 analyzed data extracted from United Network for Organ Sharing to determine the survival of patients undergoing liver transplantation for BCS for decompensated cirrhosis, acute liver failure, or hepatocellular carcinoma. In the era following adoption of Model for End-stage Liver Disease (MELD) score (where adoption of the MELD score based deceased donor liver allocation may have improved survival of liver transplantation for BCS), the 10-year post liver transplant survival was 72.3% for BCS, with comparable survival in patients who had and those who had not undergone a TIPS procedure.
