# Anatomic Considerations in Congenital Heart Disease

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Segmentale Anatomie und Sequenzielle Analyse
* **Sequenzielle segmentale Analyse:** System zur Beschreibung angeborener Herzfehler basierend auf drei kardialen Segmenten (Atrien, Ventrikel, große Arterien). Die Verbindungen zwischen den Segmenten werden unabhängig voneinander klassifiziert.
* **Situs-Bestimmung:** Orientierung der Vorhofohren – *situs solitus* (rechtes Vorhofohr rechts, linkes links), *situs inversus* (Spiegelbild), *rechts Isomerismus* (beide Vorhofohren rechtsmorphologisch, oft Asplenie), *links Isomerismus* (beide linksmorphologisch, oft Polysplenie). Die Bronchialanatomie (eparterial rechts, hyparterial links) ist der zuverlässigste Indikator.
* **D-Loop vs. L-Loop:** Ventrikuläre Topologie – D-Loop (Rechtshand-Topologie) = normale räumliche Beziehung der Ventrikel; L-Loop (Linkshand-Topologie) = invertierte Beziehung.
* **50%-Regel:** Bei überreitender Klappe oder Arterie wird die Zuordnung zu einer Kammer durch den Anteil der circumferentiellen Verbindung bestimmt – >50% = Zuordnung zu dieser Kammer.

### Atrioventrikuläre Verbindungen
* **Biventrikulär-konkordant:** Rechtes Atrium → rechter Ventrikel, linkes Atrium → linker Ventrikel (normal).
* **Biventrikulär-diskordant:** Rechtes Atrium → linker Ventrikel, linkes Atrium → rechter Ventrikel (z.B. bei kongenital korrigierter TGA).
* **Univentrikulär:** Nur ein Ventrikel empfängt die atriale Verbindung. Zwei Subtypen: *Double inlet* (beide Atrien münden in dominanten Ventrikel) und *fehlende AV-Verbindung* (ein Atrium hat muskulären Boden – klassische Trikuspidal- oder Mitralatresie). Der rudimentäre Ventrikel hat komplementäres Trabekelmuster.
* **Imperforate Klappe:** Obwohl undurchlässig, besteht noch eine AV-Verbindung mit hypoplastischem subvalvulärem Apparat.

### Ventrikuloarterielle Verbindungen
* **Konkordant:** Aorta aus linkem Ventrikel, Pulmonalarterie aus rechtem Ventrikel (normal).
* **Diskordant:** Aorta aus rechtem Ventrikel, Pulmonalarterie aus linkem Ventrikel – Essenz der Transposition der großen Arterien (bei konkordanter AV-Verbindung) bzw. kongenital korrigierten TGA (bei diskordanter AV-Verbindung).
* **Double Outlet:** Beide große Arterien entspringen zu >50% ihrer Zirkumferenz aus derselben Kammer (meist rechter Ventrikel). Keine zwingende Notwendigkeit bilateraler Infundibula nach moderner Definition.
* **Single Outlet:** Nur eine große Arterie verbindet mit Ventrikel(n) – *Truncus arteriosus communis* (gemeinsamer Stamm mit gemeinsamer Klappe), *solitary trunk* (nur Aorta, keine atretische Pulmonalarterie identifizierbar), oder Einzelarterie bei Atresie der anderen.

### Räumliche Anomalien
* **Criss-Cross-Heart:** Überkreuzung der atrioventrikulären Einflusstrakte bei erhaltenen atrialen Verbindungen. **Superior-inferior ventricles:** Parallel angeordnete AV-Klappenebenen mit einem Ventrikel oberhalb des anderen.
* **Dextrokardie:** Herz überwiegend im rechten Hemithorax – kann Folge einer Lungenerkrankung (nicht kardial) sein. Getrennte Beschreibung von Herzposition, Apixorientierung und ventrikulärem Loop wird empfohlen.
* **Großarterienbeziehung:** Normalerweise spiralförmiger Verlauf (Pulmonalis anterior zur Aorta). Bei TGA und Double Outlet paralleler Verlauf. D-Transposition = Aorta rechts der Pulmonalis; L-Transposition = Aorta links der Pulmonalis.

### Klappen und Z-Werte
* **Z-Wert:** Anzahl Standardabweichungen vom Mittelwert für eine gegebene Körperoberfläche. Z-Wert ≥ –2 nach Klappenreparatur = normaler Fluss ohne exzessiven Gradienten unter physiologischen Bedingungen.
* **Nomogramme (Fig. 26.9 A–D):** Referenzkurven für Trikuspidalklappe, Pulmonalklappe (RV-PA-Junction), Mitralklappe (lateral, apikal 4-Kammer) und Aortenklappe (LV-aortale Junction) in Abhängigkeit von der Körperoberfläche.

### Leitungssystem bei angeborenen Herzfehlern
* **Perimembranöser VSD:** Penetrierendes und Bündel des His nahe dem posteroinferioren Defektrand.
* **AVSD (Atrioventrikulärer Septumdefekt):** AV-Knoten nach posterior verlagert (Nähe Koronarsinus), langes penetrierendes Bündel auf der Kammerseptumkrista. **Chirurgisch kritisch:** Nähte durch das posteriore überbrückende Segel, mindestens einige Millimeter vom Ansatz entfernt, immer auf der rechtsventrikulären Seite, Nadelausritte ≥ einige Millimeter vom Ventrikelscheitel entfernt.
* **Down-Syndrom:** Höhere Rate an Herzblock nach VSD-Verschluss (2.9% vs. 0.8%; *P* < .0001) – möglicherweise aufgrund höherer AVSD-Assoziation.
* **Ebstein-Anomalie:** AV-Knoten nahe Koronarsinus, rechter Tawara-Schenkel hypoplastisch/deficient, kein Leitungssystem im atrialisierten rechten Ventrikel. Häufig nodoventrikuläre Fasern (Mahaim, Kent) → WPW-Syndrom.
* **Fallot-Tetralogie:** Großer membranöser Flap am posterioren Defektrand – entscheidende Orientierung für Nahttechnik. Rechter Tawara-Schenkel intramural, bedeckt von posteriores Extension der trabecula septomarginalis.
* **Kongenital korrigierte TGA:** **Anteriorer AV-Knoten** – verläuft anterior des pulmonalen Ausflusstrakts. Langer nicht-penetrierender, nicht-bifurzierender Bündel vor Entstehung der Tawara-Schenkel. Bei VSD: Bündel verläuft am superioren Defektrand auf der LV-Seite – Nähte müssen auf der RV-Seite, weg vom superioren Rand.
* **Funktionell univentrikuläres Herz:** Bei konkordanter AV-Verbindung immer posteriore Leitungsbahn. Bei diskordanter Verbindung variabel (anterior, twin node, sling, posterior). Double inlet left ventricle mit rudimentärem rechten Ventrikel → anteriorer AV-Knoten, hohe Vulnerabilität bei Septation.
* **Heterotaxie:** Linksisomerismus → hypoplastischer, abnormal lokalisierter Sinusknoten, gelegentlich Sling der AV-Leitungsbahn. Rechtsisomerismus → bilaterale Sinusknoten. Twin/dual AV-Knoten häufig.

## SECTION I: MORPHOLOGIC APPROACH TO THE CONGENITALLY MALFORMED HEART</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

In the past, embryologic terms were commonly used in the description of congenital heart defects, even without the more complete understanding of cardiac embryology as we have today. Some of these embryology-based names still persist in the current literature, used as stand-alone terms or as synonyms to descriptive terminology.

Several acronyms were used in the 20th century, and some are still used currently in the nomenclature of the congenitally malformed hearts, for historical purposes.

But it was the introduction of the segmental approach by the Van Praaghs in the 1960s and 1970s that allowed the description of any combination of lesions, even the ones never seen before.

The sequential segmental analysis system is based on the observation of the three cardiac segments from a malformed heart (atriums, ventricles, and great arteries), starting with the proper recognition of the anatomical features of the heart components and determination of the alignment between the segments.

A different approach to the segmental analysis was introduced by the European school in the mid-1970s, taking into consideration the way each segment effectively connects with the following and not just the alignments between them (Fig. 26.1).

The most constant anatomical features of each chamber or structure in the normal heart have been described in Chapter 1. As stated initially by the Van Praaghs in the “morphologic method,” one heart chamber or structure should be recognized on its own intrinsic and characteristic morphology and not on other variable structure. A good example is why the pulmonary veins cannot be a landmark of the morphologic left atrium due the possibility of their anomalous connection outside the heart or to the morphologic right atrium.

### Sequential Segmental Analysis: Situs Determination and Connections</summary><div class="details-content">

There are different ways to describe and classify the congenital defects of the cardiovascular system, not specifically right or wrong, simply different. The diverse approaches to the nomenclature of some topics are yet not fully resolved, but consensus is closer than two or three decades ago.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Situs Determination</summary><div class="details-content">

The landmarks of cardiac anatomy are considered as a starting point to describe how each of the cardiac segments connects to the other. Situs or overall organ arrangement determination can rely on some of the cardiac anatomical features, as is the case with the morphology and position of the atrial appendages, or atrial arrangement. The most consistent anatomical feature for atrial morphology is the extent of the trabeculated internal surface, the pectinate muscles. In the morphologic right atrium, it extends from the tip of the appendage to the inferior surface of the atrial wall, next to the opening of the inferior caval vein (*vena cava*).

![](_page_1_Picture_10.jpeg)

**Figure 26.2** A normal heart sectioned through the short axis and above the atrioventricular junction, showing the typical appearance of the atrial appendages, the morphologic right with extensive trabeculations (pectinate muscles) extending to the inferior surface and the morphologic left with trabeculations restricted to its interior. *Ao*, aorta; *PT*, pulmonary trunk.

On the other hand, the morphologic left appendage shows pectinate muscles restricted to its interior, while the rest of the atrial internal surface is smooth (Fig. 26.2).

The commonest form is the usual arrangement or *situs solitus*, with the morphologic right appendage to the right side and the morphologic left to the left side. There are three other possibilities: the mirror-image arrangement of the appendages, or *situs inversus*, and two forms of isomeric appendages, the right and the left *isomerisms*. In each of these isomeric arrangements, the appendages are not lateralized, which means that both appendages have the same characteristics on both sides, being of either right or left morphology.

In cases of *situs solitus* and *situs inversus* (lateralized appendages), the arrangements of the thoracic and abdominal organs inside the body are usually in harmony with the arrangement of atrial appendages; for example, in *situs solitus*, the morphologic right bronchus is right-sided and the morphologic left is left-sided.

In the two forms of isomerism of the atrial appendages, the thoracic organs are also isomeric, as is the case with the bronchial tree and lungs regarding anatomy and number of lobes. The arrangement of abdominal organs is usually highly abnormal, with absence of the spleen (or *asplenia*) in the majority of cases of *right isomerism*, and multiple spleens (or *polysplenia*) in *left isomerism*. However, the association between appendage isomerism with splenic status is far from precise, as right isomerism is found occasionally with the presence of a spleen. Also, there are rare situations with disharmony between the thoracic and abdominal *situs*, with the thoracic organ arrangement not in accordance with the distribution of the abdominal organs.

Bronchial anatomy is a good indicator of the situs and is almost always concordant with the atrial appendage arrangement. The main left bronchus is long and only branches after it has been crossed by the accompanying pulmonary artery, which makes it hyparterial (bronchus below the pulmonary artery). On the other hand, the main right bronchus is short, forming an angle of approximately 90 degrees with the trachea, and its superior branch passes superiorly to its accompanying pulmonary artery, which gives it an eparterial (bronchus above the pulmonary artery) pattern of branching. These bronchial patterns can be observed by x-ray or tomography and serve as a guide for the organ arrangement.

While the morphologists and surgeons are usually able to directly observe the anatomical cardiac landmarks, the images of the chambers obtained by some methods of image may not be so distinctive, and that is why the *situs* is determined by transsectional ultrasonography by the position of the abdominal aorta and major systemic veins relative to the spine. In the usual (*solitus*) and mirror-image (*inversus*) arrangements, the great abdominal vessels, aorta, and inferior vena cava lie on opposite sides of the spine, whereas in isomerism, they lie on the same side. In cases of right isomerism, the aorta lies anterior to the inferior vena cava, and in left isomerism, the aorta is posterior to the vein of the azygos system.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Atrioventricular Connections</summary><div class="details-content">

Description of the way the atria connect to the ventricles is the next step in the segmental analysis, provided that the atrial arrangement and ventricular anatomy have been identified.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### Biventricular Atrioventricular Connections</summary><div class="details-content">

In the normal heart, there are two separate atrioventricular junctions, described as biventricular and concordant atrioventricular connections (Fig. 26.3), with each atrium connected to its appropriate ventricle via an atrioventricular valve. Biventricular and concordant atrioventricular connections may also be present when there is a common atrioventricular junction and common valve. These features—one common valve or two valves—are additionally described as the mode of biventricular atrioventricular connection. An imperforate valve is also a mode of atrioventricular connection. With an imperforate valve, one atrium can still connect with a ventricular cavity. Although there is an imperforate membrane, the connection is still present; a subvalvar apparatus is also present but usually hypoplastic.

![](_page_2_Figure_14.jpeg)

**Figure 26.3** Schematic representation of the modes of biventricular atrioventricular connections: two perforate atrioventricular valves, a common atrioventricular valve, one perforate and one imperforate valve, one straddling and overriding valve. *(Anderson RH, Becker AE. Controversies in the Description of Congenitally Malformed Hearts. In Cardiopulmonary Medicine from Imperial College Press. World Scientific Publishing Co.; 1998. https://doi.org/10.1142/p056)*

Biventricular and discordant atrioventricular connections exist when the morphologic right atrium connects with the morphologic left ventricle and the morphologic left atrium to the morphologic right ventricle, usually via two atrioventricular valves or rarely via a common valve. This type of atrioventricular connection is independent of the *situs*.

When there is isomerism, a biventricular atrioventricular connection can also be described, but by convention, it will not be concordant or discordant but rather mixed (also called *ambiguous*). In such cases, the complete description must include the ventricular topology or loop, which defines the spatial relationships between the ventricles. D-loop or right-hand topology is the anatomical situation when it would be possible to “place” the palmar surface of the right hand on the septal surface of the morphologic right ventricle with the thumb in the inlet and the other fingers toward the outlet. Contrarily, in an L-loop or left-hand topology, it is the left hand that could be “placed” on the septal surface of the morphologic right ventricle in the same way. D-loop or right-hand topology is the finding in a normal heart from an individual with *situs solitus* but can be also found in a person with isomeric atrial appendages.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### Univentricular Atrioventricular Connections</summary><div class="details-content">

This is the situation when only one ventricle connects to the atrial cavities, and that is why the connection is called univentricular rather than biventricular. In most cases, there are two ventricles, one dominant (receiving the connections) and the other rudimentary, lacking an inlet and showing a complementary trabecular pattern to the dominant one. The dominant ventricle may be of left (more commonly) or of right morphology.

The position of the rudimentary chamber may help to determine its morphology, because the morphologic right ventricle occupies an anterior-superior position regardless of its side (right-sided, directly anterior, or left-sided), and the morphologic left ventricle lies at the diaphragmatic aspect of the ventricular mass. Very rarely is there a single ventricular chamber of indeterminate morphology when no rudimentary ventricle can be found (Fig. 26.4).

![](_page_3_Figure_3.jpeg)

**Figure 26.4** Schematic representation of the possibilities of connections between the cardiac chambers and the ventricular patterns in univentricular atrioventricular connections. The upper third of the figure depicts the possible arrangements of atriums: usual arrangement (solitus), mirror image (inversus), and appendage isomerism (right or left). The middle third shows the types of atrioventricular connections: absent right, double inlet, and absent left. Absent connection means that the atrium has a muscular floor, which should not include an imperforated valve. In the inferior third of the figure, we show the patterns of the dominant and the incomplete (rudimentary) ventricles. Rarely a true single ventricle is found, with no detectable rudimentary chamber. *LV*, left ventricle; *Ind V*, indeterminate ventricle; *RV*, right ventricle. *(From Gatzoulis MA, Webb GD, Daubeney PEF, eds. Diagnosis and Management of Adult Congenital Heart Disease. 3rd ed. Elsevier; 2020.)*

This group comprises hearts with both atriums opening into a dominant ventricle (double inlet ventricle) and those with absence of one of the atrioventricular connections (right or left-sided atrium with a complete muscular floor). As previously stated, the morphology of the dominant ventricle is variable.

In one subgroup of hearts with univentricular connections, both atrial cavities open into the dominant ventricle. This type of connection is called double inlet ventricle.

Either two atrioventricular valves or a common valve may be present in double inlet ventricles. When there are two valves, they are better referred to as “right-sided and left-sided atrioventricular valves” rather than as tricuspid and mitral, as their morphology cannot always be defined as typical. In cases showing one atrioventricular valve overriding the ventricular septum, the 50% rule should be applied, defining which ventricular cavity that valve predominantly relates to. The ventricle with both valves opening predominantly is called the dominant one, and the atrioventricular connection is diagnosed as double inlet.

Another subgroup of hearts with univentricular atrioventricular connection show only one atrium connected to one ventricle in the ventricular mass, while the other atrium has a complete muscular floor. This is called absent atrioventricular connection, as the atrium is separated from the ventricular myocardium by the fibrous fat tissue of the base of the heart. Absent connection may be right- or left-sided. Again, the dominant ventricle receiving the atrial connection may be either morphologic right or left, with the rudimentary ventricle with complimentary trabecular pattern. The so-called classical tricuspid and mitral atresia belong to this group (Fig. 26.5).

**Figure 26.5** Frontal view of two malformed hearts sectioned in 4-chamber fashion to show absent right (A) and left (B) atrioventricular connection. The patent valve opens in the dominant ventricle. Note the complete muscular atrial floor. *RA*, right atrium; *LA*, left atrium.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Ventriculoarterial Connections</summary><div class="details-content">

The great arteries may join the ventricular mass in several ways. Their connection to the ventricular cavities as well as their spatial relationships should be described separately and independently, irrespective of the type of atrioventricular connection and ventricular completeness, as is the case when there is a dominant and a rudimentary ventricle.

There are four types of ventriculoarterial connections: concordant, discordant, double outlet, and single outlet.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### Concordant Ventriculoarterial Connections</summary><div class="details-content">

Concordant ventriculoarterial connection occurs when the aorta is connected to the morphologic left ventricle and the pulmonary trunk is connected to the morphologic right ventricle, as is the case in the normal heart. This type of connection may also be described in hearts with one dominant and one rudimentary ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### Discordant Ventriculoarterial Connections</summary><div class="details-content">

In the discordant arrangement, the aorta arises from the morphologic right ventricle (it may be complete or rudimentary), and the pulmonary trunk arises from the left ventricle. It is generally accepted that the discordant ventriculoarterial connection with concordant atrioventricular connection is the essence of transposition of the great arteries (Fig. 26.6), and discordant ventriculoarterial connection with discordant atrioventricular connection is the essence of congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries.

![](_page_4_Figure_4.jpeg)

**Figure 26.6** A heart with complete transposition of the great arteries, characterized by concordant atrioventricular connections in association with discordant ventriculoarterial connections. In (A), there is a view of the right-sided chambers and in (B) of the left-sided chambers. *Ao*, aorta; *PT*, pulmonary trunk; *RA*, right atrium; *LA*, left atrium; *RV*, right ventricle; *LV*, left ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### Double Outlet Ventricles</summary><div class="details-content">

Double outlet ventricles are those that support the greater part of the circumference of both great arteries, irrespective of their spatial relationship and the presence or absence of a subarterial muscular infundibulum.

When one of the great arteries overrides the ventricular septum in the presence of a ventricular septal defect (VSD), the degree of overriding will define its assignment to one ventricle or the other. This definition, however, has not always been a consensus. Controversy still exists regarding the definition of double outlet ventricles because some authors consider that the presence of bilateral infundibulums should be essential. However, the rule of commitment of the great arteries’ circumferences (the 50% rule) to the ventricles has become more accepted. For example, one case of tetralogy of Fallot with the aorta completely supported by the right ventricle can be considered as having a double outlet type of ventriculoarterial connection.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### Single Outlet Ventriculoarterial Connection</summary><div class="details-content">

A heart is considered to have single outlet when only one of the great arteries connects to the ventricular cavity (or cavities) because the other artery is atretic or when there is a common arterial trunk.

A common arterial trunk originates from the base of the heart and usually overrides an outlet VSD, supplying the pulmonary, coronary, and systemic circulations. This common trunk has a common valve with variable number of semilunar leaflets (Fig. 26.7).

![](_page_5_Picture_8.jpeg)

**Figure 26.7** Close detail of the right ventricular outflow tract in a heart with a common arterial trunk (*truncus arteriosus communis*), which is one type of single outlet of the heart. From the same common trunk arises the aorta (Ao), both pulmonary arteries and the coronary arteries. There is a single valve *(asterisk)* which overrides an outlet ventricular septal defect. *RPA*, right pulmonary artery; *LPA*, left pulmonary artery; *RV*, right ventricle.

The second type of single outlet from the heart is the solitary trunk, usually the aorta. In a heart with a solitary trunk, one great artery emerges from the base of the heart, and it is not possible to identify an atretic pulmonary trunk or any intrapericardial supply to the pulmonary circulation.

The other two forms of single outlet from the heart are the single aorta in the presence of an atretic pulmonary trunk or single pulmonary trunk when there is aortic atresia.

It is not always possible to know with certainty if an atretic arterial trunk would have connected to one or the other ventricular cavity, as in most of the cases there is no valvar remnant or membrane. If, on the other hand, an imperforate valve is recognized, the ventricular connection can be established.

### Abnormalities in the Position of the Heart and of the Relationship Between the Ventricles</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Abnormal Position Within the Thorax</summary><div class="details-content">

As described in Chapter 1, two-thirds of the normal heart is located in the left hemithorax, with the apex pointing inferiorly and to the left. To this combination of features, the term *levocardia* is usually applied.

Several terms have been used to describe abnormal position of the heart itself and the orientation of its apex inside the thorax, causing confusion about whether the abnormal position would imply the presence of an associated congenital heart defect. Good examples are terms such as “dextrocardia” (heart positioned on the right side of the chest), “dextroversion” (rightward rotation of the heart), “levoversion” (leftward rotation of the heart), “dextrorotation” (rightward rotation of the heart along its longitudinal axis), and “dextroposition” (shifting of the heart rightward, usually related to external factors). These are sometimes applied to compress different information (position, situs, apex orientation, and ventricular loop) in a single word. Nonuniform definitions of the cited terms have made their understanding difficult.

The location of the heart predominantly in the right hemithorax is not always the result of a cardiac malformation but can be the consequence of collapse or underdevelopment of the right lung or because of abnormal contents within the thoracic cavity, for example, as in diaphragmatic hernia.

So, to avoid confusion, it is better to separately describe the main location of the heart and the orientation of its apex, followed by the sequential segmental analysis that accounts for the connections and ventricular loop.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Ectopia Cordis</summary><div class="details-content">

This term is usually applied for hearts positioned completely or partially outside the thorax (extrathoracic hearts). Defects of the thoracoabdominal wall and cleft of the sternum make possible the exteriorization of the organ. In most cases, the pericardium and the skin are deficient, and the organ can be observed pulsating below the skin in the presence of a cleft sternum. Different types of congenital cardiac defects have been described in ectopic hearts: tetralogy of Fallot, classical tricuspid atresia, etc.

The heart may also be located inside the abdomen, in the thoracoabdominal transition, or rarely, occupying the thoracocervical region.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Abnormal Spatial Relations Between the Ventricles (Criss-Cross, Superior-Inferior Ventricles)</summary><div class="details-content">

In this group of malformed hearts, the ventricles may assume different spatial relations, showing either crossing of the inlets (crisscross), with the atria and venous connections maintaining their position, or a superoinferior relationship, with parallel axes of the atrioventricular junctions. In the criss-cross arrangement, a twisting mechanism originating at the cardiac axis would explain the spiraling of the chambers. On the other hand, a tilting mechanism would explain the superoinferior arrangement of ventricles.

These abnormalities in spatial relations are more frequent in hearts with complete transposition or in congenitally corrected transposition of the great arteries.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Spatial Relationships Between the Great Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

One cannot infer any type of ventricular topology or ventriculoarterial connection when observing the spatial relation between the aorta and pulmonary trunk or of their remnants.

There has been controversy on how to describe the relation between the arterial valves/arterial trunks at their origins from the ventricles. Some prefer to describe the relations in terms of both anterior-posterior and right-left. Others have long used the letters D or L to refer to the aorta being to the right or left relative to the pulmonary trunk; however, the term “D-transposition,” for example, describes a combination of concordant atrioventricular and discordant ventriculoarterial connections and aorta to the right of the pulmonary trunk. By the same token, the term “L-transposition” has been applied to mean a combination of discordant atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial connections, the so-called “corrected transposition of the great arteries.”

Regarding the relation between the arterial trunks, in the normal heart and in those with concordant ventriculoarterial connections, the aorta and pulmonary trunk take a spiraling course as they emerge from the base of the heart, with the pulmonary trunk anterior to the aorta and the aortic arch crossing superior to the pulmonary bifurcation.

The course of the great arteries in complete transposition of the great arteries and in double outlet ventricle is parallel, as they ascend from the base of the heart (Fig. 26.8).

![](_page_6_Picture_15.jpeg)

**Figure 26.8** Longitudinal section of the ventricles and ventricular outflow tracts showing the straight ventricular septum and parallel course of the initial parts of the aorta and of the pulmonary trunk in a heart with complete transposition of the great arteries. There is also a narrowed patent arterial duct (PDA). *Ao*, aorta; *PT*, pulmonary trunk; *RV*, right ventricle; *LV*, left ventricle.

The aortic arch usually crosses over the left main bronchus, but in association with some cardiac defects, it may occupy an anomalous position, being right-sided and showing different patterns of ramification of its main branches.

The position of the descending aorta relative to the vertebral column should also be described in any particular case.

### Atrioventricular and Ventriculoarterial Valves</summary><div class="details-content">

Valve reconstruction is often a component of reconstructive congenital heart surgery. Understanding the adequacy of orifice size is critical to successful valve surgery. In most (but not all) cases, a Z value of –2 or greater following valve repair generally provides normal flow without excessive gradient under physiologic conditions. ## SECTION II: CONDUCTION SYSTEM IN THE CONGENITALLY MALFORMED HEART</summary><div class="details-content">

Conduction disturbances are serious postoperative complications. For the surgeon, in-depth knowledge of atrioventricular conduction and its variability in different malformations is imperative, to avoid heart block, with its long-term implications. Descriptions of the surgically relevant aspects of the conduction system in specific malformations are found in those specific chapters.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Historical Note</summary><div class="details-content">

The first description of the precise anatomy of the conduction system was by Tawara in 1906. Kent suggested that there were multiple pathways crossing the atrioventricular junction. In 1893, Wilhelm His provided the first account of the atrioventricular conduction bundle. Keith and Flack discovered the sinus node. Keith remained skeptical for many years about His’s claims until Tawara’s clarification of the atrioventricular anatomical arrangements.

With respect to specific congenital cardiac malformations, from 1958 to 1963, Lev described the conduction system of common atrioventricular orifice, tetralogy of Fallot, VSD, and corrected transposition. In 1980, Anderson and Becker described the topographic anatomy of the atrioventricular conduction system in the human heart. Davies, Anderson, and Becker described the conduction system in congenital heart disease in 1983. Finally, in 1987, Kurosawa and Becker published a color atlas of the conduction system in congenital heart disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Conduction System in the Normal Heart</summary><div class="details-content">

The topographical anatomy of the atrioventricular conduction tissues in the normal human heart is described in detail in Chapter 1 and elsewhere.

The sinus node is located at the junction of the superior vena cava with the morphologic right atrial appendage. The atrioventricular node is located in the triangle of Koch, which is formed by the tendon of Todaro, which runs in the base of the Eustachian valve guarding the orifice of the inferior vena cava, the membranous septum, and the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve. The area of the triangle of Koch is the atrioventricular septum between the left ventricle and the right atrium.

The right-sided view of the normal heart (Fig. 26.10A) shows the position of the atrioventricular node in the triangle of Koch, the penetrating and branching bundle close to the membranous septum and the right bundle branch, together with the anterior and superior continuation of the main conduction axis, which ends as a “dead-end tract” in the central fibrous body immediately underneath the aortic valve. The dead-end tract is thought to be a remnant of the embryologic anterior atrioventricular bundle. This arrangement of the branching component of the specialized atrioventricular conduction axis is relevant to the development of the conduction axis. Proximally, the right bundle branch is subendocardial but then runs intramyocardially, covered by the trabecula septomarginalis. A view of the same heart from the left side (Fig. 26.10B) shows the close relationship of the penetrating and branching bundle to the aortic valve and the area of fibrous continuity between the mitral and aortic valves. The dead-end tract courses beneath the aortic valve.

### Conduction System in the Congenitally Malformed Heart</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Isolated Ventricular Septal Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

The position of the atrioventricular node in the apex of the triangle of Koch is unaffected by the presence of an isolated VSD. The precise topography of the conduction system, however, depends on the exact type of VSD (see Chapter 33).

**Perimembranous VSDs** are defined as defects that border on the membranous septum. Perimembranous defects may have inlet, outlet, or trabecular extensions. In perimembranous VSDs, there is a close relationship between the penetrating and branching bundles and the posteroinferior margin of the defect.

**Inlet-type VSDs:** The AV node is located in the crux of the triangle of Koch, which comes down posteriorly and away from the anteroseptal commissure of the tricuspid valve and from the aortic valve. The penetrating bundle runs at the base of the fibrous continuity between the mitral and tricuspid valve close to the posteroinferior angle of the defect.

**Doubly committed subarterial defect:** The VSD may be separated from the area of the membranous septum by a distinct bar of muscle. In such instances, the margin of the defect stays remote from the conduction axis.

**Trabecular-type VSDs:** The posteroinferior angle of the defect is modified by a small rim of muscle that runs in a superior direction. However, this muscle is insufficient to safely cover the penetrating and branching bundle. The course of the conduction axis in the usual trabecular-type defect thus closely resembles the situation in perimembranous inlet-type defects.

**Outlet-type defects:** A prominent posterior limb of the trabecula septomarginalis is usually present. It extends to the area of the membranous septum and fuses with the remnant of the interventricular part. Occasionally, a distinct membranous flap is present. Hence, a major part of the main conduction axis remains left-sided and remote from the crest of the defect. The bifurcating bundle comes closest to the inferior margin of the defect, and proper care should be taken in this area not to injure the conduction axis.

**Muscular VSDs:** The conduction axis is remote from the margins of the defect, unless the defect expands to the area of the membranous septum.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Atrioventricular Septal Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

Atrioventricular septal defect is characterized by a deficiency of the muscular atrioventricular septum (i.e., “scooping”) (see Chapter 32). Because the atrioventricular septum is deficient by scooping, the crux of the small triangle of Koch descends away from the aortic valve and the attachment of the anterior bridging leaflet of the common atrioventricular valve. The atrioventricular node is posteriorly displaced and closely related to the orifice of the coronary sinus and the attachments of the posterior bridging leaflet.

The long penetrating bundle and the nonpenetrating, nonbranching bundle are positioned on the crest of the inlet ventricular septum in the base of the connective tissue to which the posterior bridging leaflet is attached. Therefore, sutures for the patch to close the defect should be placed through the posterior bridging leaflet at least a few millimeters away from the attachment of the leaflet around the crux of the triangle of Koch. Then all sutures should be placed on the right ventricular side, and all exits of needle should be away from the ventricular summit by at least a few millimeters. Those careful sutures should be continued until the anterior small papillary muscle supports the center of the posterior bridging leaflet. Beneath this papillary muscle the bifurcating bundle is located and is where the right bundle branch originates.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Down Syndrome</summary><div class="details-content">

Down syndrome is frequently associated with VSD and atrioventricular septal defect. An analysis of the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Congenital Heart Surgery Database revealed that patients with Down syndrome undergoing VSD closure had a higher rate of heart block requiring pacemaker placement in comparison to those without Down syndrome (2.9% vs. 0.8%; *P* < .0001).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Ebstein Anomaly</summary><div class="details-content">

A key feature of Ebstein Anomaly is the atrialized right ventricle (see Chapter 48).

This unique anomaly affects the topography of the conduction system. The AV node is close to the coronary sinus. The right bundle branch is hypoplastic or occasionally deficient. The atrioventricular conduction axis from the penetrating bundle to the right bundle branch is away from the original tricuspid anulus and runs on the right ventricular side of the displaced tricuspid anulus. There is no conduction system in the atrialized right ventricle. Nodoventricular fibers (i.e., Mahaim fibers, fasciculoventricular fibers, or Kent bundle) are frequently seen. Those atrioventricular accessory pathways cause supraventricular tachyarrhythmia or Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW) syndrome.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Tetralogy of Fallot</summary><div class="details-content">

In tetralogy of Fallot, the VSD is always outlet type and may be a perimembranous VSD with outlet extension or muscular outlet VSD (see Chapter 34). Right ventricular hypertension puts pressure on the tricuspid valve, and right ventricular volume reduction results in a well-developed medial papillary muscle that supports the anteroseptal commissure of the tricuspid valve.

A large interventricular part of the membranous septum is present in the posterior margin of the defect, the so-called membranous flap. Proper recognition of this structure is essential for suturing the defect, as the penetrating and branching bundle is positioned to the left side and soon veers away from the inferior margin of the defect. The right bundle branch usually takes a course from the left side of the membranous flap toward the body of the trabecula septomarginalis through the distal area of the medial papillary muscle complex. In this position, it will be intramural, covered by the posterior extension of the trabecula septomarginalis. It surfaces at a more peripheral site along the body of the trabecula septomarginalis.

In tetralogy of Fallot with muscular VSD, the atrioventricular conduction axis usually stays remote from the posteroinferior margins of the VSD, as anticipated.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Double Outlet Right Ventricle</summary><div class="details-content">

Double outlet right ventricle shows variable spatial relationship of the great arteries and variable type of VSD (see Chapter 45). The right ventricle always receives pressure overload. Volume overload of the right ventricle depends on the degree of outflow tract obstruction. The trabecula septomarginalis is always well developed. The atrioventricular node locates in the triangle of Koch as usual. The main conduction axis remains left-sided and usually veers rapidly away from the posteroinferior margin of the VSD.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transposition of the Great Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

In transposition of the great arteries, the AV node and the bundle of His are in a normal position. When present, the type of VSD is variable (see Chapter 44). The spatial relationship of the great arteries is also widely variable: anteroposterior, right oblique, and side-by-side relation. The spatial relationship of the great arteries decides the angle of the bloodstream from the tricuspid valve to the aortic valve in the right ventricle. This angle of the bloodstream affects the location of the medial papillary muscle, which originates from the anterior or posterior extension of the hypertrophic trabecula septomarginalis. Furthermore, the volume load on the right ventricle is variable. The type of the VSD, the spatial relationship of the great arteries, location of the medial papillary muscle, and the extension of the hypertrophic trabecula septomarginalis make the conduction system more susceptible to heart block.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Congenitally Corrected Transposition of the Great Arteries</summary><div class="details-content">

In congenitally corrected transposition, one may anticipate an anterior position of the atrioventricular node, which courses anterior to the pulmonary outflow tract and descends along the anterior part of the outlet septum (see Chapter 47). A long nonpenetrating, nonbranching bundle is present before the left bundle branches appear and the right bundle takes origin. The key to this abnormal position of the conduction tissues is the presence or absence of malalignment between the atrial septum and the underlying inlet part of the ventricular septum. Usually, distinct malalignment prevents a normal posterior node descending onto the ventricular septum. However, when proper alignment is present, one may anticipate a posterior regular node that contacts the ventricular mass. In cases involving straddling mitral valve, a similar anterior position of the AV node and conduction axis may be anticipated. In atrial situs inversus, the posterior AV node extends to the penetrating bundle of His. In presence of a VSD, the bundle passes along the superior margin of the VSD on the morphological LV aspect. Sutures to close the VSD must pass along the right ventricular aspect away from the superior VSD margin. Patients with congenitally corrected TGA are prone to spontaneous development of heart block.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Functionally Univentricular Heart</summary><div class="details-content">

Functionally univentricular hearts have a variable atrioventricular conduction bundle (see Chapter 52).

There are two groups, concordant and discordant atrioventricular connections.

The atrioventricular conduction bundle is always posterior in concordant atrioventricular connection. However, the atrioventricular conduction bundle is variable in discordant atrioventricular connection, such as anterior, twin atrioventricular node, sling, and posterior.

In double inlet left ventricle with rudimentary right ventricle in the left shoulder of the ventricular mass and a discordant arterial connection, the atrioventricular node will be positioned anteriorly. The main conduction bundle will descend along the anterior wall, encircling the orifice of the pulmonary trunk anteriorly as in congenitally corrected transposition. A long nonpenetrating, nonbranching bundle exists before the left bundle branches and the right bundle branch originate. In this position the main conduction axis is highly vulnerable during septation procedure. The bundle will stay on the left side of the septum, although running along the anterior border of the defect. The right bundle branch will cross the septum in superficial position.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Heterotaxy (Isomerism)</summary><div class="details-content">

In left isomerism, the sinus node is hypoplastic and abnormally located and occasionally has a sling of atrioventricular conduction bundle. Right isomerism has bilateral sinus nodes located at both sides of the terminal grooves of morphologic right atrium. The remnant of atrial septum (i.e., the inferior limbic band) and ventricular septum easily align in isomerism. Twin/dual atrioventricular node and sling of atrioventricular conduction bundles are frequently seen in the setting of isomerism.

The morphologies of the ventricles in the setting of isomerism are similar but not the same as nonisomerism. The precise anatomy of the atrioventricular conduction bundle is still obscure in isomerism.

</div></details>
