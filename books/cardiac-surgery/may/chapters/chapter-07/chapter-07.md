### Principles of Extracorporeal Circulation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Definition & The Heart-Lung Machine Concept</summary><div class="details-content">

Many operations performed to correct congenital heart defects require that the flow of blood through the heart, pulmonary arteries or aorta temporarily be stopped; either for purposes of opening one of these structures or in order to stop the heart from beating to allow surgery. In order to safely stop blood flow through the heart, the surgeon must provide an alternative means of circulation for the body and thus will employ a technique called "cardiopulmonary bypass," often abbreviated "CPB" ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01)).

In essence, CPB involves diverting venous blood away from the heart to an apparatus where oxygen is added to the blood and carbon dioxide is removed; the blood is then returned to a systemic artery "downstream" from the heart. Hence the blood "bypasses" the heart and lungs. Cardiopulmonary bypass is instituted by inserting plastic tubes called "cannulae" into the heart chambers or blood vessels of the heart.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Teamwork & Pediatric Perfusion Specifications</summary><div class="details-content">

These cannulae are attached to special plastic tubing connected to the cardiopulmonary bypass pump, often referred to as simply "the pump." Once the cannulae are attached properly, cardiopulmonary bypass commences. This is referred to as being "on pump." By convention, the term "open heart surgery" refers to any cardiac operation that requires the use of CPB (which is often also referred to as the heart-lung machine).

Cardiopulmonary bypass is a complex process that requires specialized equipment and monitors. A team of highly trained professionals called "perfusionists" sets up, runs and maintains the cardiopulmonary bypass machine. Pediatric perfusion requires that the process and mechanisms be tailored to the size and physiology of each child.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Cannulation & Circuit Architecture</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Aortic Cannulation & Purse-String Sutures</summary><div class="details-content">

A typical operation performed to correct congenital heart disease starts with preparation of the heart and main blood vessels for institution of cardiopulmonary bypass. A "purse-string" suture is placed in the surface of the aorta. (A purse-string suture is a circular suture placed in the superficial layer of the blood vessel or heart.) A cannula is inserted into a stab incision in the center of the purse-string.

The strings are then pulled tightly through a rubber sleeve (often called a tourniquet), securing the blood vessel or heart tissue tightly around the cannula. This allows for an air-tight seal around the cannula, so blood cannot leak out and air cannot enter. The first purse-string is placed in the surface of the ascending aorta. Most surgeons place two purse-string sutures here to provide extra security since aortic blood pressure is high.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Venous Drainage: Bi-Caval vs. Single Atrial</summary><div class="details-content">

Next, purse-strings are placed in the superior vena cava and then inferior vena cava, near the entrance of these vessels to the heart. Alternatively, a single right atrial purse-string can be used for certain operations that do not require the atrium be opened ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01)).

The technique of cannulating both vena cavae separately is termed "bi-caval" cannulation. This technique is useful for most congenital heart surgery operations since the right-sided heart structures frequently are opened. When cannulae are placed in the cavae directly, a heavy suture material or umbilical tape is passed around the each vena cava. Both ends of the suture or umbilical tape are brought through a large rubber sleeve and pulled securely to cinch the caval wall around the cannula in the same fashion as previously described for purse-string sutures.

This creates a tight seal between the vessel wall and the cannula, so that air is not entrained into the venous cannulae when the heart is opened. Venous return to the bypass pump is usually a gravity-fed siphon. If air is allowed into the venous return tubing or cannulae, the siphon is broken, and cardiopulmonary bypass must stop.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Venting & Roller vs. Centrifugal Pumps</summary><div class="details-content">

An additional purse-string is placed in the right upper pulmonary vein at its junction with the left atrium. This site is cannulated with a venting cannula, which is threaded into the pulmonary vein, passed into the left atrium and across the mitral valve into the left ventricle ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01)). The left ventricular vent is attached to a bypass machine roller head to provide active suction to keep the left ventricle empty.

The roller heads of the cardiopulmonary bypass machine are circular channels that hold the cardiopulmonary bypass tubing. A rotating "rolling-pin" squeezes the tubing against the channel wall. As the roller head rotates, blood is propelled forward as the "rolling-pin" squeezes blood forward along the tubing. The cardiopulmonary bypass machine has separate roller heads for aortic perfusion, left ventricular vent return and two "pump-suckers." The pump suckers allow the salvage of shed blood from the operative field back to the bypass machine.

The blood from the pump sucker and the left ventricular vent is emptied into a reservoir that sits atop the gas exchange portion of the heart lung machine, which also receives the blood drained from the venous cannulae. Alternatively, a centrifugal pump system can be utilized for aortic perfusion instead of the previously described roller pump. The centrifugal pump head has an impeller propelling the blood, driven by rotating magnets outside the pump housing.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anticoagulation, Gas Exchange & Temperature Control</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Heparin, Protamine & ACT Monitoring</summary><div class="details-content">

Institution of cardiopulmonary bypass requires that precautions are taken to prevent clot formation, which occurs when blood is allowed to contact foreign surfaces. Heparin is used to prevent this, and it is administered both to the patient and into the priming solution in the heart lung machine. Heparin is given following surgical preparation of the heart and its blood vessels, prior to inserting the cannulae.

Following completion of the surgical repair and after CPB has been terminated, a medication called protamine is administered. Protamine reverses the anticoagulation effects of heparin. A test called "activated clotting time" or ACT is used to monitor the effects of heparin and its reversal. A blood heparin assay also is performed, allowing identification of residual heparin in the blood.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Oxygenator Membrane & Heat Exchangers</summary><div class="details-content">

The oxygenator/reservoir of the cardiopulmonary bypass machine is a plastic canister with a collection chamber and oxygenator ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01)). The oxygenator is a specialized device that has many layers of very fine, thin, gas-permeable fibers or a thin membrane. Blood passes through the oxygenator and oxygen passes through the fibers or membrane. Gas exchange occurs on the surface of the fibers or membrane.

Oxygen is added to the blood, and carbon dioxide is removed. The oxygenator of the cardiopulmonary bypass machine functions like the alveoli of the human lung. A heat exchanger also is incorporated into the system to allow the perfusionist to adjust the blood (and body) temperature as needed for the operation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hypothermia & Deep Hypothermic Circulatory Arrest (DHCA)</summary><div class="details-content">

Most operations for congenital heart disease are carried out at moderate hypothermia (26°C to 34°C), but some operations require cooling to profound hypothermia (~ 15 °C). More extreme hypothermia is required for operations that require temporarily turning off the bypass machine for a brief period; this is called "deep hypothermic circulatory arrest" or DHCA.

During DHCA, there is no circulation of blood in the body at all. To minimize the potential for injury to the brain and other vital organs during the period of circulatory arrest, cellular metabolism is reduced to its absolute minimum by extreme cooling of the patient prior to the cessation of pump flow.

New techniques are being employed to minimize risk of brain injury during DHCA. For example, regional flow to the right carotid system can be used to provide the brain with oxygenated blood flow during the arrest period, based on the as-yet unproven hypothesis that some blood flow to the brain is better than no blood flow to the brain, even during profound hypothermia. Long term studies are being developed to determine whether cognitive outcomes are improved with this technique.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Circuit Priming Volume & Bloodless Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

The volume of the cardiopulmonary bypass tubing and oxygenator must be considered when performing open-heart operations on pediatric patients. Since the patient's blood volume effectively becomes the sum of the bypass machine volume and the patient's blood volume, in small children it may be necessary to add blood to the "priming" volume in the bypass circuit to prevent excessive and potentially dangerous dilution of the blood.

Adults and older children have much higher blood volumes; as the ratio of patient blood volume to bypass machine blood volume increases, non-blood prime becomes more feasible. All efforts are made to minimize bypass prime volume to allow for bloodless surgery in smaller and smaller patients.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Myocardial Protection & Weaning Dynamics</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Initiation of CPB & Nonpulsatile Perfusion</summary><div class="details-content">

With successful cannulation of the aorta, vena cavae, and placement of the left ventricular vent, cardiopulmonary bypass can commence. The perfusionist starts the roller pump leading to the aortic cannula and opens the siphon-drain venous return system. Oxygenated blood is actively pumped into the aorta, and venous return to the cardiopulmonary bypass machine is fed by gravity.

When the heart is driving the circulation, the aortic valve opens with each ventricular systole resulting in so-called "pulsatile" perfusion. When on full cardiopulmonary bypass, left ventricular blood is diverted to the bypass machine resulting in the left ventricle beating nearly empty, therefore the aortic valve does not open, and aortic blood pressure becomes constant (provided by the bypass pump) rather than pulsatile (as provided by the heart).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Aortic Cross-Clamping & Cardioplegia Delivery</summary><div class="details-content">

Most open-heart surgeries require that the heart be completely stopped or "arrested." In order to achieve safe cardiac arrest, a "cross-clamp" is placed across the aorta between the aortic valve and the site of insertion of the aortic cannula, but distal to the origin of the coronary arteries ([Fig. 7-1](#fig-07-01)). A small cannula is placed in the aortic root proximal to the aortic cross-clamp with another purse-string and is used to inject a cold, high-potassium solution (called cardioplegia) into the aortic root. Since the aortic cross clamp prevents the solution from going into the systemic circulation, and a competent aortic valve prevents the solution from entering the left ventricle, the cardioplegia has nowhere else to go but into the coronary arteries where it is distributed to the heart muscle evenly. Because of the high potassium concentration in the cardioplegia, cardiac electrical and mechanical activity ceases, and the cold temperature of the solution reduces cardiac metabolism to minimal levels.

Because the effect of the cardioplegia is temporary, it must be re-dosed at regular intervals during the operation. When aortic valve disease renders the aortic valve incompetent, an alternative technique for cardioplegia delivery can be employed. So-called "retrograde" cardioplegia can be given via a cannula placed in the coronary sinus, which normally drains coronary venous blood back to the heart. The coronary sinus ostium is located in the right atrium. The retrograde cannula is threaded into the mouth of the coronary sinus and advanced until a seal is created between the coronary sinus lumen and the outside of the tube. When retrograde cardioplegia is given, the cardioplegia runs "backwards" into the myocardium, via the coronary vein instead of the coronary arteries. This solution then drains into the aortic root by way of the coronary artery ostia.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Topical Cooling & Reperfusion Pay-Back Time</summary><div class="details-content">

During the cardiac arrest phase of the operation, the myocardium is kept very cool. Sterile saline ice slush is sometimes placed around the heart to further insure complete cooling of the heart. When the heart muscle is cool, myocardial oxygen demand is very low. This allows for extended periods of cold cardiac arrest. It is during this phase of the operation that complex intracardiac operations can be performed.

At normothermia, deprivation of cardiac oxygen supply will result in rapid myocardial cell injury and even death. When cold cardioplegia and cardiac arrest is employed, myocardial integrity is maintained safely for hours. Following completion of the intracardiac phase of the operation, the heart is re-perfused by removing the aortic cross-clamp.

With removal of the aortic cross-clamp, blood from the cardiopulmonary bypass machine is allowed to perfuse the coronary arteries. Blood supply to the myocardium is resumed, and the heart soon starts to beat normally. A period of "pay-back time" ensues during which the metabolic stores of the heart muscle are re-charged. This pay-back time allows the heart to regain normal function, by beating while empty (since the left ventricular vent keeps the blood removed from the heart). This pay-back time is usually approximately equivalent to half of the total "cross-clamp" time.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rewarming, Partial Bypass & Weaning</summary><div class="details-content">

Following intracardiac repair and during the pay-back period, the perfusionist actively warms the blood as it passes through the bypass circuit and the body temperature slowly returns to normal. During this phase of the operation, the snares placed around the venous return cannulae are removed. Some blood now is allowed to enter the heart around the venous return cannulae. This process is called "partial cardiopulmonary bypass." The heart is allowed to process a small amount of blood in preparation for separation from bypass.

Following complete rewarming of the body and blood, and completion of the pay-back process, vasoactive infusions that may be required for support of postoperative hemodynamics are started. Separation from cardiopulmonary bypass support is carried out by slowly restricting the venous return to the bypass machine, allowing more and more blood to be pumped by the heart. The roller pump that pumps blood into the aorta gradually is slowed. Eventually, venous return to the bypass machine is reduced to zero and aortic perfusion by the arterial roller pump is stopped. The patient is now "off bypass." The hemodynamic state is optimized with the use of vasoactive infusions and manipulation of cardiac preload and afterload.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Modified Ultrafiltration (MUF) & Systemic Inflammation</summary><div class="details-content">

After the completion of CPB, many centers employ a technique known as "modified ultra-filtration" (MUF) to salvage blood from the heart lung machine, hemoconcentrate the patient's blood, and possibly remove harmful low molecular weight cytokines. MUF is performed immediately following separation from cardiopulmonary bypass. Blood remaining in the bypass reservoir and tubing is drawn through a special filter that allows removal of fluid and small molecules resulting in a significant increase in hematocrit. After filtration, the blood is returned to the patient via one of the venous cannulae. Simultaneously, blood is drawn backward from the aortic cannula, filtered and returned to the patient as well. The MUF process takes about 10 to 15 minutes, and it is common to see gradual improvement in hemodynamics as the process progresses.

Myocardial performance following open-heart surgery is dependent upon many factors, but is particularly influenced by the duration of cross-clamping, the adequacy of "myocardial protection" during the cross-clamp interval (by cardioplegia and cooling), and the adequacy of the actual surgical repair. Thus, the efforts of the surgical team are directed at achieving the shortest cross clamp time, the best myocardial protection and the best technical operation to optimize the function of the heart.

Cardiopulmonary bypass and deep hypothermic cardiac arrest have allowed successful repairs of complex cardiac anomalies. Despite this, certain adverse effects occur with any use of extracorporeal circulation (circulation provided by mechanisms external to the body). During cardiopulmonary bypass, the blood is exposed to synthetic surfaces, which provoke the release of substances called "inflammatory mediators," which in the body cause tissue edema and dysfunction. This process is reversible, but may significantly impair organ performance. Continuing improvement in bypass technology have helped to minimize its deleterious effects, and continuing research offers the hope that cardiopulmonary bypass will continue to grow safer and safer.

</div></details>

### Indications and System Mechanics</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Myocardial Dysfunction & Clinical Indications</summary><div class="details-content">

Life threatening myocardial dysfunction can occur as a consequence of primary cardiomyopathic processes as well as following complex cardiac operations. Intrinsic to the pediatric population are some unique challenges to mechanical support including the wide range of patient size from neonate to adult. In addition, complex anatomy such as single ventricle variants, dextrocardia, and anomalies of systemic venous return such as left superior vena cava must be addressed.

In general, support strategies can be divided into two categories: extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) commonly used for short-term support and ventricular support devices used for more long-term support including bridge to transplantation. All modes of mechanical support are associated with risks including stroke, infection, bleeding, thromboembolism, air embolism, artificial device failure and death.

ECMO was originally developed to treat neonatal respiratory insufficiency. It was quickly applied to infants with myocardial dysfunction following cardiac surgery ([Fig. 7-2](#fig-07-02)). Indication for ECMO includes acute life-threatening low cardiac output due to any number of causes that have failed medical therapy including: myocardial dysfunction, arrhythmias and systemic to pulmonary artery shunt occlusion. In addition, ECMO support can be applied to patients with acute pulmonary insufficiency due to meconium aspiration, congenital diaphragmatic hernia and other causes.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Circuit Components & Safety Systems</summary><div class="details-content">

ECMO supports end-organ function until pulmonary and/or myocardial function improves. Among the advantages of ECMO are its ability to conform to atypical anatomy and the presence of specialized care teams that are familiar with the ECMO circuit who are able to provide standardized care. The disadvantages of ECMO include the need for anticoagulation with the associated risks of bleeding and thromboembolism and short duration of support. Although there are notable exceptions, the duration of support possible with ECMO is typically about 2 weeks.

ECMO is a closed cardiopulmonary bypass system. Venous drainage is accomplished with a cannula placed in a large vein, such as the jugular or femoral vein or directly in the right atrium ([Fig. 7-2](#fig-07-02)). The blood is then pumped through a membrane oxygenator with heat exchanger to permit temperature regulation. Blood returns to the patient via a cannula placed in a large artery, such as carotid or femoral or directly into the aorta. In the patient with myocardial dysfunction in the immediate postoperative period, cardiac cannulation is typical; venous drainage is via a cannula in the right atrium and arterial inflow via a cannula in the aorta. In other patients undergoing ECMO support, peripheral cannulation is commonly used.

For neonates and infants this is typically accomplished using the common carotid artery and internal jugular vein. For older patients the femoral vessels are more commonly used. Unlike conventional cardiopulmonary bypass there is no venous reservoir and the patient acts as the reservoir. Excessive negative venous pressure could result in air entrainment into the circuit. In order to prevent air embolism, a safety mechanism called a "bladder box" is included which temporarily suspends pump function if the inlet pressure becomes dangerously low.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Ventricular Afterload & Rescue Modalities (ECPR)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### LV Distention & Decompression Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

ECMO increases afterload to the left (systemic) ventricle. In the face of severe myocardial impairment, the systemic ventricle can become distended which impairs myocardial blood flow and will prevent myocardial recovery.

In cases of cardiac cannulation, as is typically used in the postoperative period, a vent or drainage cannula can be placed into the systemic ventricle. This cannula can be connected to the venous limb of the ECMO circuit. In cases of peripheral cannulation, left sided decompression can be accomplished via an atrial septal defect (ASD) created in the cath lab. Should left atrial drainage via the ASD prove inadequate, a vent can be introduced via peripheral venous cannulation and directed into the left (systemic ventricle). This cannula is then connected to the venous limb of the ECMO circuit.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (ECPR)</summary><div class="details-content">

ECMO has more recently been applied in a rescue modality where cannulation is accomplished while the patient is being resuscitated. A smaller simplified circuit that has low priming volume is used.

It is fully assembled and ready to prime or is pre-primed with crystalloid solution. This modality is used to allow for transition from CPR resuscitation to ECMO as quickly as possible. A traditional ECMO setup requires 30 to 45 minutes to assemble while the simplified circuit is ready for immediate use.

</div></details>

</div></details>

### Principles of Pediatric Ventricular Support</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### VAD Fundamentals & Physiological Advantages</summary><div class="details-content">

A ventricular assist device (VAD) is a blood pump that can augment or replace the function of a failing ventricle. Unlike ECMO the lungs provide gas exchange and an oxygenator is not necessary. In the adult population, VADs have been used for many years and provide circulatory support with improvement in end-organ function.

VADs are used as a bridge to transplant, a bridge to recovery or in select circumstances in adults, as destination therapy. The pumps come in two general varieties, displacement pumps that produce pulsatile flow and axial pumps that provide continuous flow.

Pediatric ventricular assist technology has been slower to develop due to the complexities of miniaturizing these devices. The main advantage to the use of VADs is the potential for longer-term use. This permits recovery of previously impaired organ function, reduces left atrial filling pressure and reduces pulmonary vascular resistance. The result is a better cardiac transplant candidate and a less complicated post-transplant course.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Economic Advantages & Pediatric Morbidity Profile</summary><div class="details-content">

In addition, significant economic advantage exists with the use of a VAD since the devices are less personnel intensive and ICU stay is not necessarily required.

Thromboembolic complications of pediatric assist devices are, however, significant and exceed those of similar adult devices from which they modeled. As a consequence of the high complication rate and potential for long-term survival with transplantation, pediatric assist devices are not suitable as destination therapy.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Assist Device Systems & The Berlin Heart EXCOR</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### The DeBakey VAD Child (Axial Flow)</summary><div class="details-content">

The only FDA approved ventricular assist device for children is the DeBakey VAD Child (MicroMed Technology, Inc., Houston, TX). It uses an axial flow pump, and is modified from a device designed for adults.

The DeBakey VAD Child was given exemption by the FDA for humanitarian use as a bridge to transplantation for children. The device is totally implantable, and is approved for use in patients aged from 5 to 16 years, and with body surface areas greater than 0.7 m² and less than 1.5 m². This size range excludes smaller patients. Other axial flow pumps in which there is some experience in children include the HeartMate II and Jarvik 2000.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Displacement Pumps & Clinical Experience</summary><div class="details-content">

Displacement pumps that have been used in children include: the Berlin Heart EXCOR, the Medos HIA, the Thoratec Ventricular Assist System, HeartMate pneumatic VAD, Abiomed BVS 5000 and Abiomed AB 5000 Circulatory Assist Systems.

The Berlin Heart EXCOR has been implanted in more than 100 patients in North America with duration of VAD support exceeding 200 days in some cases ([Fig. 7-3](#fig-07-03)). In May of 2007 FDA granted conditional approval for a prospective study to evaluate the Berlin Heart EXCOR in the pediatric age group.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pneumatic Chamber & Diaphragm Mechanics</summary><div class="details-content">

The Berlin Heart EXCOR is a displacement pump that comes in a variety of sizes making it suitable for use in patients of all sizes including infants ([Fig. 7-3](#fig-07-03)). This device utilizes an inflow cannula that leads to a valved pumping device outside the body and a return cannula. The pump mechanism is driven by an external compressor and suction source.

Blood enters the pumping unit through a one way valve. Inside the pumping unit there is a blood chamber separated from the pressure/suction chamber by a flexible diaphragm. As blood enters the VAD the diaphragm relaxes as suction is applied to the pressure/suction chamber.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulsatile Ejection Cycle & Evolution of Tech</summary><div class="details-content">

When the blood chamber is full, compressed CO2 is allowed to enter the pressure/suction chamber pushing (displacing) the diaphragm towards the blood side. Blood is then ejected through another one-way valve creating a "systole". Flow is pulsatile thus mimicking normal blood flow.

The field of ventricular assist and mechanical support is constantly changing as novel new devices and techniques are developed. No current device is without its risks. With careful attention to all aspects of device management and selection, good outcomes are achievable.

</div></details>
