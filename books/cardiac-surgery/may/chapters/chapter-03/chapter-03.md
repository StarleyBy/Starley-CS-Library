# RVOT Obstruction and Pulmonary Vasculature

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Pathophysiological Spectrum of Right-Sided Obstruction
* **Right Ventricular Pressure Overload & Hypertrophy:** Fixed or dynamic obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT)—at subinfundibular, infundibular, valvar, supravalvar, or branch pulmonary artery levels—imposes severe systolic pressure afterload on the right ventricle. This induces compensatory concentric right ventricular hypertrophy, decreased diastolic compliance, elevated right ventricular end-diastolic pressure (RVEDP), elevated central venous pressure, and secondary tricuspid regurgitation.
* **Cyanosis & Dynamic Infundibular Spasm (Tet Spells):** In the presence of an unrestrictive ventricular septal defect (Tetralogy of Fallot), the magnitude and direction of intracardiac shunting are governed by the ratio of RVOT impedance to systemic vascular resistance ($SVR$). Endogenous catecholamine surges, agitation, hypovolemia, or crying cause acute infundibular muscular spasm, precipitating sudden right-to-left shunting, profound arterial desaturation, and hypercyanotic crises ("tet spells").
* **Ductal-Dependent Pulmonary Blood Flow:** In severe forms of RVOT obstruction (critical pulmonary stenosis, pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum, and pulmonary atresia with VSD without collaterals), pulmonary perfusion relies strictly on retrograde flow through a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA). Immediate continuous intravenous infusion of prostaglandin E1 ($PGE_1$, 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min) is lifesaving to prevent fatal hypoxemia upon spontaneous ductal closure.
* **Aortopulmonary Collateral Dynamics (MAPCAs):** In pulmonary atresia with VSD and discontinuous or hypoplastic native pulmonary arteries, lung arborization is supplied by major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs) arising from the descending thoracic aorta. These exhibit complex arborization patterns, dual supply zones, and variable intrinsic stenoses.
* **Right Ventricular-Dependent Coronary Circulation (RVDCC):** In pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum and severe RV hypoplasia, suprasystemic RV cavitary pressures force blood into persistent myocardial sinusoids communicating directly with coronary arteries. In the presence of proximal coronary artery stenoses, myocardial perfusion depends entirely on high RV cavity pressure; surgical decompression of the right ventricle in this setting precipitates catastrophic myocardial infarction and death.

### Clinical Syndromes & Anatomic Spectra
* **Isolated Pulmonary Valve Stenosis:** Dome-shaped dysplastic or fused semilunar leaflets with a small central orifice; typically amenable to percutaneous balloon pulmonary valvuloplasty, reserving open surgical valvotomy or transannular patch reconstruction for dysplastic or refractory variants.
* **Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF):** Classically defined by anterocephalad deviation of the outlet (infundibular) septum resulting in:
  1. Ventricular septal defect (large, non-restrictive malalignment defect);
  2. Subvalvar and valvar right ventricular outflow tract obstruction;
  3. Concentric right ventricular hypertrophy;
  4. Aortic override across the ventricular septum.
* **Tetralogy of Fallot with Absent Pulmonary Valve Syndrome:** Marked hypoplasia or complete agenesis of pulmonary valve leaflets resulting in massive free pulmonary regurgitation, associated with huge aneurysmal dilatation of the main and branch pulmonary arteries that severely compresses the tracheobronchial tree, causing dynamic central airway obstruction, atelectasis, and respiratory failure.
* **Pulmonary Atresia with Ventricular Septal Defect (PA/VSD):** Extreme variant of Tetralogy of Fallot characterized by total anatomical discontinuity between the right ventricular outflow tract and pulmonary arteries, with pulmonary perfusion derived from a PDA or multiple MAPCAs.
* **Pulmonary Atresia with Intact Ventricular Septum (PA/IVS):** Complete membranous or muscular atresia of the pulmonary valve with a closed ventricular septum; manifests with variable hypoplasia of the tricuspid valve and right ventricular cavity, obligatory right-to-left interatrial shunting, and potential RVDCC.
* **Pulmonary Artery Sling & Tracheal Stenosis:** Aberrant origin of the left pulmonary artery from the posterior aspect of the right pulmonary artery, coursing between the trachea and esophagus to the left lung hilum. Associated in 25–50% of cases with complete cartilaginous tracheal rings ("stovepipe" trachea) causing critical fixed airway narrowing.

### Surgical Principles & Reconstructive Standards
* **Total Repair of Tetralogy of Fallot:** Median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass with aortic cross-clamping. Right transatrial-transpulmonary approach preferred to avoid extensive ventriculotomy:
  * Resection of obstructive parietal and septal infundibular muscle bands;
  * Closure of the non-restrictive VSD using synthetic (Dacron) or glutaraldehyde-treated autologous pericardial patch, placing sutures away from the penetrating bundle of His on the right ventricular aspect;
  * Pulmonary valvotomy or valve-sparing annular enlargement; if the pulmonary valve annulus Z-score is $<-2$ to $-3$, a transannular patch (TAP) of pericardium or PTFE is utilized to widely decompress the RVOT.
* **Absent Pulmonary Valve Reconstruction:** Downsizing and reduction arterioplasty/plication of dilated pulmonary arteries; LeCompte maneuver (translocating the pulmonary artery bifurcation anterior to the ascending aorta) to relieve extrinsic tracheobronchial compression; RV-to-PA reconstruction utilizing an allograft valved conduit or monocusp patch.
* **Unifocalization for PA/VSD with MAPCAs:** Systematic surgical mobilization and recruitment of collateral arteries into unified central neo-pulmonary arteries, followed by RV-to-PA conduit placement and VSD closure (single-stage vs. staged).
* **PA/IVS Staging Strategy:**
  * *Biventricular Pathway (adequate RV/TV, non-RVDCC):* Surgical pulmonary valvotomy / transannular patch RVOT decompression supplemented by a systemic-to-pulmonary shunt (modified Blalock-Taussig shunt) to promote right ventricular cavity remodeling and growth.
  * *Univentricular Pathway (severe RV hypoplasia or RVDCC):* Avoidance of RV decompression; initial systemic-to-pulmonary shunt followed by staged cavopulmonary connections (Glenn and Fontan).
* **Pulmonary Sling Correction & Slide Tracheoplasty:** Complete detachment of the aberrant left pulmonary artery with anterior reimplantation into the main pulmonary trunk. Concomitant long-segment tracheal stenosis is repaired using slide tracheoplasty (transecting the trachea at the midpoint of narrowing and sliding beveled anterior and posterior segments together), doubling cross-sectional luminal area while halving the shortened segment.

### Postoperative Considerations & Pitfalls
* **Right Ventricular Restrictive Physiology:** Early postoperative diastolic dysfunction of the hypertrophied and ventriculotomized right ventricle; manifests as low cardiac output, hepatomegaly, elevated central venous pressure ($CVP$), and prominent end-diastolic forward flow in the pulmonary artery on echocardiography. Requires maintenance of higher preload ($CVP\text{ 12–16 mmHg}$), preserved atrioventricular synchrony, and inotropic support with milrinone.
* **Ventilatory Optimization & Pulmonary Afterload:** Hypoxia, hypercarbia, and acidosis dramatically increase pulmonary vascular resistance ($PVR$), worsening right ventricular failure and increasing right-to-left shunting. Maintained on mild respiratory alkalosis with deep sedation; inhaled nitric oxide (iNO) utilized if pulmonary arterial hypertension or elevated PVR is encountered.
* **Conduction Disturbances:** Risk of right bundle branch block (RBBB, almost universal after infundibular resection/ventriculotomy), bifascicular block (RBBB + left anterior hemiblock), and transient or permanent complete AV block; dual-chamber temporary epicardial pacing wires are essential.
* **Prolonged Pleural Effusions:** Common after transannular patch repair or extensive MAPCA unifocalization due to high systemic venous pressures, lymphovascular disruption, and systemic inflammatory response; managed with prolonged pleural drainage, low-fat or medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) diets, and diuresis.
* **Airway Malacia & Prolonged Intubation:** Airway collapse may persist for weeks or months following surgical decompression of vascular slings or absent pulmonary valve syndrome due to intrinsic cartilaginous weakness (tracheobronchomalacia); requires careful positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) management and serial bedside bronchoscopy.

## Pulmonary Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Congenital pulmonary stenosis is a form of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction that may present as valvar, subvalvar or supravalvar stenosis.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hypertrophy and Ventricular Dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

Obstruction to the egress of blood from the right ventricle to the pulmonary artery results in right ventricular hypertrophy and can eventually lead to right ventricular dysfunction. Exercise intolerance and arrhythmias may occur in advanced cases.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-03-01"></span>![](fig-03-01.png)

**FIGURE 3-1** Pulmonary Stenosis. Diagram of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction by a stenotic pulmonary valve with marked concentric right ventricular hypertrophy (1), and surgical relief via right ventriculotomy or pulmonary arteriotomy with commissurotomy and outflow patch angioplasty (2).

Relief of isolated pulmonary valve stenosis can sometimes be accomplished percutaneously with balloon valvuloplasty. When the percutaneous technique is not possible, surgical intervention is required ([Fig. 3-1](#fig-03-01)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Surgical Approach & Resection</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiopulmonary bypass is necessary for the repair of pulmonary stenosis not amenable to percutaneous technique. Approach to surgical relief of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction may be via right atriotomy, right ventriculotomy, or pulmonary arteriotomy ([Fig. 3-1](#fig-03-01)).

Subvalvar right ventricular outflow tract obstruction is relieved by resection of obstructive tissue. Valvar pulmonary stenosis is relieved by pulmonary valve commissurotomy. Repair of supravalvar pulmonary stenosis often requires patch angioplasty.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transannular Patching vs. Valve Preservation</summary><div class="details-content">

Severe forms of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction sometimes require transannular patch angioplasty. Preservation of pulmonary valve function is attempted in all cases, even if mild residual obstruction persists.

Aortic cross-clamp time and cardiopulmonary bypass time required for repair are usually short. Transesophageal echocardiography is utilized to help assess adequacy of repair.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of pulmonary stenosis is usually benign. Invasive monitors utilized following surgery include arterial and central venous catheters. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic Management & Inotropic Support</summary><div class="details-content">

Milrinone may be used for hemodynamic management. More extensive vasoactive support is rarely required. Excessive bleeding and cardiac arrhythmias are rarely observed postoperative complications.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pleural Drainage & Hospital Stay</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative pleural effusion is occasionally encountered following relief of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction. Length of hospital stay required following surgery averages 5–7 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Tetralogy of Fallot</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Tetralogy of Fallot occurs when there is underdevelopment of the right ventricular infundibulum or conus. Ventricular septal defect, right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, right ventricular hypertrophy and overriding of the aorta are the four abnormalities classically described.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Shunt Dynamics and Hypercyanotic Spells</summary><div class="details-content">

Tetralogy of Fallot comprises a spectrum of severity. As the severity of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction increases, right-to-left shunting of blood occurs through the VSD, resulting in cyanosis.

When severe right ventricular outflow tract obstruction and hypertrophy exist, hypercontractile episodes, often associated with agitation, give rise to severe right-to-left shunting of blood and hypercyanosis. These episodes are referred to as "tet spells."

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-03-02"></span>![](fig-03-02.png)

**FIGURE 3-2** Tetralogy of Fallot. Schema of classic anatomy with malalignment VSD, infundibular/valvar stenosis, overriding aorta, and right ventricular hypertrophy (1), and complete surgical repair with infundibular muscle resection, patch closure of the VSD, and RVOT reconstruction with transannular patch angioplasty (2).

Timing of complete repair of Tetralogy of Fallot varies. Early palliation for hypercyanotic spells or severe cyanosis can be provided by a modified Blalock-Taussig shunt ([Fig. 3-2](#fig-03-02)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Exposure & Muscle Resection</summary><div class="details-content">

Total repair of Tetralogy of Fallot requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping. Repair of this lesion can be approached via either a right atriotomy or a right ventriculotomy, depending on the degree and length of right ventricular outflow tract obstruction ([Fig. 3-2](#fig-03-02)).

Right ventricular obstruction is relieved first with resection of obstructive muscle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### VSD Closure & Outflow Tract Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

The ventricular septal defect is then closed with a patch of autologous pericardium or synthetic material. Pulmonary arterioplasty or pulmonary valvotomy is performed next, depending upon the individual needs of the patient.

The integrity of the pulmonary valve annulus is spared whenever possible to maintain pulmonary valve competence. A patch across the pulmonary valve annulus (a transannular patch) is often required in order to adequately relieve right ventricular outflow tract obstruction.

Following adequate rewarming, the patient is weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass. Transesophageal echocardiography is utilized to help assess adequacy of repair. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required to complete repair are usually moderate in length.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of Tetralogy of Fallot can be variable and depends upon the individual anatomy and surgical course.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Invasive Monitoring & Right Ventricular Dysfunction</summary><div class="details-content">

Invasive monitors utilized following surgery include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter can be used to aid in assessment of cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone and/or nitroprusside. Afterload reduction should be used judiciously since systemic hypotension can exacerbate right ventricular dysfunction.

Some degree of right ventricular failure is nearly universal following repair of Tetralogy of Fallot. Significant resection of right ventricular muscle, coupled with pulmonary insufficiency resulting from transannular patch repair can lead to early postoperative hemodynamic instability.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic Interdependence & Preload Optimization</summary><div class="details-content">

Since left ventricular output is dependent upon adequate transpulmonary blood flow and adequate left ventricular filling, satisfactory right ventricular function is an important determinant of postoperative hemodynamic stability.

Maintaining adequate right ventricular function depends upon atrioventricular synchrony, adequate preload, and maintenance of low pulmonary vascular resistance. Inotropic support is commonly required.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Arrhythmias, Pleural Effusions & Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Bleeding is a potential postoperative complication and can occur in patients who have undergone extensive pulmonary artery patch angioplasty. Atrioventricular conduction abnormalities are occasionally encountered since ventricular septal defect closure requires manipulation near atrioventricular conduction tissue. Atrioventricular pacing capability should be readily available.

Pleural effusion is a common postoperative complication. Prolonged chest tube drainage may be required. Arterial oxygen saturation following repair should be normal.

Intracardiac pressures might reflect a higher than normal right ventricular diastolic pressure secondary to pulmonary insufficiency. In addition, central venous pressure may be elevated, indicating right ventricular failure. Intracardiac pressures usually return to normal as right ventricular performance improves. Length of hospital stay following repair of Tetralogy of Fallot is variable, but averages 1–2 weeks.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Tetralogy of Fallot with Absent Pulmonary Valve</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Tetralogy of Fallot with absent pulmonary valve refers to a rare form of Tetralogy of Fallot in which there is complete absence of the pulmonary valve with resultant severe pulmonary regurgitation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Aneurysmal Pulmonary Arteries & Tracheobronchial Compression</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with this syndrome typically exhibit massive aneurysmal dilatation of the central pulmonary arteries, which in turn leads to compression and obstruction of the large central airways.

The embryology of the syndrome is interesting in that there is felt to be no ductus arteriosus in utero, which is felt to exacerbate the prenatal stress on the wall of the central pulmonary arteries.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Respiratory Compromise & Cyanosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinical symptoms are typically those of respiratory distress, often complicated by recurrent pneumonia, although some children also may be cyanotic if the pulmonary annulus and infundibulum are small.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-03-03"></span>![](fig-03-03.png)

**FIGURE 3-3** Tetralogy of Fallot with Absent Pulmonary Valve. Anatomy showing absence of pulmonary valve leaflets, massive aneurysmal dilatation of central pulmonary arteries compressing the bronchi, and ventricular septal defect (1), with surgical repair including reduction arterioplasty, LeCompte maneuver, VSD patch closure, and valved conduit reconstruction (2).

Timing of surgical intervention is dictated by the severity of respiratory insufficiency. In severe cases, neonatal intervention may be required, possibly including extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) if the patient cannot be effectively ventilated. In patients with less severe disease, elective surgery can be postponed for several months ([Fig. 3-3](#fig-03-03)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Resection, Patching & Branch Downsizing</summary><div class="details-content">

Repair of Tetralogy of Fallot with absent pulmonary valve is performed through median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping ([Fig. 3-3](#fig-03-03)). Repair requires resection of any sub-annular obstruction within the right ventricle.

The ventricular septal defect then is closed with a patch. The branch pulmonary arteries are downsized with plication and/or resection of part of the pulmonary artery wall.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Airway Decompression via LeCompte Maneuver</summary><div class="details-content">

In order to minimize airway compression, the pulmonary artery bifurcation can be relocated anterior to the aorta (the LeCompte maneuver), typically in combination with plication or reduction arterioplasty ([Fig. 3-3](#fig-03-03)).

In some cases, the main pulmonary artery is resected and replaced with an allograft valved conduit. In other cases a transannular patch with a monocusp pulmonary valve is inserted. In the event that significant right ventricular dysfunction is anticipated early after surgery, a small atrial septal defect is left open or created if one is not present.

Following weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, transesophageal echocardiography is utilized to assess adequacy of repair. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required to complete the repair usually are moderate in length.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of Tetralogy of Fallot with absent pulmonary valve is variable and depends chiefly on the degree of preoperative airway compromise and the degree of hemodynamic support required preoperatively.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intensive Hemodynamic Support & ECMO Backup</summary><div class="details-content">

Invasive monitors utilized following surgery include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter can be used to help assess adequacy of cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, norepinephrine and milrinone. Postoperative utilization of ECMO for hemodynamic support sometimes is required.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Airway Malacia & Prolonged Mechanical Ventilation</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative complications can include bleeding, especially when extensive pulmonary artery suture lines are required. Atrioventricular conduction abnormalities occasionally are encountered since ventricular septal defect closure requires the placement of sutures very near the conduction system. Temporary pacing capability should be readily available.

Persistent respiratory insufficiency may occur, necessitating prolonged mechanical ventilation. It must be realized that although compression of the airway will have been relieved at the time of surgery, significant airway malacia will likely remain and take weeks or even months to resolve completely. Pleural effusions sometimes are encountered, and may require prolonged pleural drainage.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intracardiac Pressures & Hospital Course</summary><div class="details-content">

Systemic arterial oxygen saturation should be nearly normal following repair of Tetralogy of Fallot with absent pulmonary valve. Left atrial pressure should be normal; right atrial pressure might be slightly elevated in the early postoperative period. This is due to temporarily decreased right ventricular performance, common following surgery involving the right ventricular outflow tract (particularly if resection of right ventricular muscle has been necessary).

Filling pressures should approximate normal by the time of hospital discharge. Length of hospital stay expected following surgery is quite variable and depends upon severity of illness at presentation and relative smoothness of the postoperative course.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Pulmonary Atresia with Ventricular Septal Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect is characterized by underdevelopment of the right ventricular outflow tract with atresia of the pulmonary valve, a large VSD and overriding of the aorta.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Blood Supply: Ductus vs. MAPCAs</summary><div class="details-content">

There is a wide spectrum of severity dependent on the development of the pulmonary arteries. When a large patent ductus arteriosus is associated, pulmonary atresia with VSD is managed as a severe form of Tetralogy of Fallot. In this subtype, the pulmonary arteries are usually normal in size and distribute blood to all segments of the lungs.

When a small patent ductus arteriosus is present, or when there is no patent ductus arteriosus, pulmonary artery blood supply is provided partially or entirely by major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs). Intermediate forms of this cardiac anomaly exist and are approached individually depending upon the specific anatomy.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-03-04"></span>![](fig-03-04.png)

**FIGURE 3-4** Pulmonary Atresia with Ventricular Septal Defect. Schema of absent pulmonary valve connection with large VSD and systemic arterial supply to the lungs (1), and surgical unifocalization with RV-to-PA conduit placement and VSD closure (2).

The surgical technique employed to repair pulmonary atresia with ventricular septal defect with MAPCA dependent pulmonary blood supply is variable, and depends upon individual anatomy and surgeon preference ([Fig. 3-4](#fig-03-04)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Goals of Unifocalization & Staging</summary><div class="details-content">

The vast anatomic variability seen in pulmonary atresia with VSD makes the surgical approach patient specific. The ultimate goal is complete repair, achieved by constructing central pulmonary arteries from the MAPCAs, closing the VSD and establishing continuity between the right ventricle and reconstructed pulmonary arteries ([Fig. 3-4](#fig-03-04)).

Staged approach to repair, by performing sequential aortopulmonary collateral artery unifocalization, or recruitment procedures is preferred by some surgeons. Alternatively, early single stage total repair is sometimes performed.

Regardless of the approach chosen for repair of this lesion, total repair with VSD closure should proceed as early as the patient's pulmonary anatomy will allow.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of pulmonary atresia with VSD and MAPCAs may vary, and depends upon individual anatomy and surgical course.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Monitoring & Bleeding Control</summary><div class="details-content">

Invasive monitors utilized postoperatively include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter may be used to monitor cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone and/or nitroprusside.

Excessive postoperative bleeding can occur when extensive pulmonary artery angioplasty is required for repair.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rhythm, Conduction & Hospital Stay</summary><div class="details-content">

Atrioventricular conduction abnormalities are occasionally encountered since ventricular septal defect closure requires manipulation near atrioventricular conduction tissue. Atrioventricular pacing capability should be readily available.

Arterial oxygen saturation and intracardiac pressures should be normal following surgery. Length of hospital stay required following repair is variable, but 1–2 weeks is average.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Pulmonary Atresia with Intact Ventricular Septum</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum is characterized by complete atresia of the pulmonary valve with hypoplasia of the right ventricle and tricuspid valve. The pulmonary arteries are usually normal in size.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Right Ventricular-Dependent Coronary Circulation (RVDCC)</summary><div class="details-content">

With severe right ventricular hypoplasia, coronary artery abnormalities can occur. Right ventricular sinusoids are sometimes present and connect to the coronary arteries.

In the presence of right ventricle to coronary artery connections with proximal coronary artery stenoses, myocardial perfusion is dependent upon the right ventricle.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Obligatory Atrial Shunting & Ductal Dependency</summary><div class="details-content">

A large atrial septal defect is present in nearly all cases, and pulmonary blood supply is provided by a patent ductus arteriosus. Consequently, obligatory right to left shunting occurs at the atrial level.

Ductal closure in the neonate with pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum results in severe hypoxia. Prostaglandin E1 infusion is used to maintain patency of the ductus arteriosus prior to surgical intervention. Prostaglandin E1 infusion in these patients is life saving and permits stabilization prior to surgical intervention.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-03-05"></span>![](fig-03-05.png)

**FIGURE 3-5** Pulmonary Atresia with Intact Ventricular Septum. Anatomy of complete pulmonary valve atresia with hypoplastic right ventricle and intact septum (1), and surgical options: systemic-to-pulmonary shunt alone for RV-dependent coronary circulation, or combined with RVOT decompression/valvotomy for non-dependent biventricular recruitment (2).

Surgical intervention proposed for patients with pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum must be individualized depending upon the patient's specific anatomy ([Fig. 3-5](#fig-03-05)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Management in the Presence of RVDCC</summary><div class="details-content">

When myocardial blood supply is determined to be dependent upon the right ventricle (right ventricular to coronary artery sinusoids with proximal coronary artery stenosis) right ventricular decompression will result in coronary hypoperfusion and myocardial ischemia. For these patients, systemic to pulmonary artery shunting is the first surgical procedure ([Fig. 3-5](#fig-03-05)).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Biventricular vs. Univentricular Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

When coronary artery perfusion is not dependent upon the right ventricle, right ventricular decompression with pulmonic valvotomy or right ventricular outflow tract reconstruction and systemic to pulmonary artery shunting is the surgical approach of choice.

Systemic to pulmonary artery shunting is usually required in this subset of patients since a very poorly compliant, hypertrophied right ventricle is not usually capable of providing adequate pulmonary blood flow. Right to left atrial level shunting often persists following right ventricular outflow tract reconstruction for the same reason.

The ultimate goal when planning surgical intervention for patients with pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum is for adequate right ventricular development to occur, allowing ultimate biventricular repair. Severe hypoplasia of the tricuspid valve or right ventricular cavity may preclude biventricular repair. In these cases, a staged univentricular approach is taken.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

Hemodynamic instability is sometimes encountered following right ventricular reconstruction and systemic to pulmonary artery shunting. Right ventricular performance is often depressed immediately following reconstruction.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Multi-Catheter Monitoring & Inotropes</summary><div class="details-content">

Invasive monitors utilized following surgery usually include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Moderate to high dose inotropic support is often required during the early postoperative period. Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone and nitroprusside.

Intravenous infusions of fentanyl and a neuromuscular blocking agent are often utilized.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Compliance Evolution & Hospital Stay</summary><div class="details-content">

Hemodynamic performance gradually improves as right ventricular compliance improves during the first few postoperative days. Bleeding is an uncommon complication encountered following repair of this lesion. Cardiac rhythm disturbance is rarely observed.

Delayed sternal closure is sometimes required. Length of hospital stay required following right ventricular reconstruction is variable, but 1–2 weeks is average.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Pulmonary Artery Sling and Tracheal Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary artery sling occurs when there is failure of development of the left sixth aortic arch during gestation. When this occurs, the left pulmonary artery arises as a branch from the right pulmonary artery rather than as a branch from the main pulmonary artery.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Vascular Anomalies and Airway Compression</summary><div class="details-content">

The result is a left pulmonary artery that courses posteriorly between the trachea and esophagus before reaching the hilum of the left lung. Symptoms are usually due to tracheal compression and include wheezing and stridor. Presentation usually occurs in infancy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Associated Complete Tracheal Rings</summary><div class="details-content">

Tracheal stenosis is commonly associated with pulmonary artery sling, with complete tracheal rings occurring in 25% to 50% of cases. Complete tracheal rings are characterized by full cartilaginous rings around the trachea, rather than "C" shaped tracheal cartilages with a membranous posterior portion as seen in the normal trachea.

A long segment of complete tracheal rings, sometimes referred to as "stovepipe" trachea, can result in severe stenosis and a narrow tracheal lumen.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Diagnostic Modalities</summary><div class="details-content">

Classically the diagnosis of pulmonary artery sling was made by barium esophagography where a pulsatile anterior indentation of the esophagus is observed. Today diagnosis is made by echocardiography, magnetic resonance imaging and spiral computed tomography.

Magnetic resonance imaging and CT scan permit complete delineation of the vascular anatomy and are helpful in determining the presence or absence of tracheal stenosis. If tracheal stenosis is suspected, bronchoscopy should be performed at the time of surgery to determine the length and degree of tracheal involvement.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-03-06"></span>![](fig-03-06.png)

**FIGURE 3-6** Pulmonary Artery Sling and Tracheal Stenosis. Schema of aberrant left pulmonary artery arising from the right pulmonary artery and passing between trachea and esophagus (1), and surgical repair with transection and anterior reimplantation of the LPA into the main pulmonary artery combined with slide tracheoplasty (2).

Repair of pulmonary artery sling is accomplished through a median sternotomy ([Fig. 3-6](#fig-03-06)). If there are no additional intracardiac defects or significant tracheal narrowing, repair can be accomplished without the use of cardiopulmonary bypass.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Vascular Detachment & Anterior Reimplantation</summary><div class="details-content">

Using partial occlusion clamps, the left pulmonary artery is detached from the right pulmonary artery, the origin oversewn and the left pulmonary artery implanted end-to-side into the main pulmonary artery ([Fig. 3-6](#fig-03-06)).

If there are associated intracardiac defects or if significant tracheal stenosis is present, then cardiopulmonary bypass is used. When performed with cardiopulmonary bypass, aortic cross-clamping is not required unless repair of intracardiac defects is necessary.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Tracheal Reconstruction Techniques</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### Resection with End-to-End Anastomosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Tracheal reconstruction is performed when moderate to severe tracheal stenosis is present and the patient is symptomatic. Two techniques for intervention on the trachea are generally used: resection with end-to-end anastomosis and slide tracheoplasty. For both techniques the trachea is mobilized posteriorly and anteriorly keeping the lateral blood supply intact.

With short segment stenosis, the narrowed area can be excised and the trachea reapproximated using absorbable suture.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

##### Slide Tracheoplasty Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

For more extensive tracheal stenosis, a slide tracheoplasty is preferred ([Fig. 3-6](#fig-03-06)).

The trachea is transected at the point of maximal stenosis. The trachea is then incised along its length above and below in such a way as to allow overlapping of the segments. This overlapping or slide technique maximizes the caliber of the trachea while minimizing the tension on the reconstruction.

Reconstruction is accomplished with absorbable monofilament sutures. Following repair, the patient is reintubated and separated from cardiopulmonary bypass. Bronchoscopic visualization of the tracheal lumen is performed to assess adequacy of repair.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of isolated pulmonary artery sling is usually uncomplicated. When associated defects are addressed, the postoperative course for those lesions would apply.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Airway Issues & Bronchoscopic Surveillance</summary><div class="details-content">

Generally, the presence of symptomatic preoperative tracheal stenosis is predictive of a longer postoperative course and airway issues dominate the picture. When tracheal reconstruction is performed, frequent assessment of the airway with bronchoscopy may be necessary.

Invasive monitors utilized postoperatively include arterial and central venous catheters. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions might include milrinone and occasionally low-dose epinephrine.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recovery & Hospital Stay</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac arrhythmias are rare, persistent postoperative bleeding is uncommon and cardiovascular recovery is usually uncomplicated. Arterial and central venous pressures and systemic saturations should be normal.

Hospital stay following uncomplicated pulmonary sling repair averages 3 days to 1 week. Hospital stay will likely be longer following repair of tracheal stenosis, and is predicted by the extent of the stenosis and the severity of preoperative symptoms.

</div></details>
