## Section 1: Anomalous Origin of the Left Coronary Artery (ALCAPA)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomical Substrate & Figure 6-1</summary><div class="details-content">

<a id="fig-06-01"></a>
![Fig. 6-1: Anomalous Left Coronary Artery from the Pulmonary Artery](fig-06-01.png)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Coronary Steal & Myocardial Ischemia</summary><div class="details-content">

Anomalous left coronary artery is a rare congenital cardiac anomaly in which the left coronary artery arises from the pulmonary artery ([Fig. 6-1](#fig-06-01)). The exact site of origin varies, but most frequently the origin is from the proximal main pulmonary artery.

During the early postnatal period, elevated pulmonary vascular resistance results in antegrade perfusion of the anomalous coronary artery with desaturated blood. As pulmonary vascular resistance drops during the first few weeks of life, blood shunts retrograde from the anomalous coronary artery into the pulmonary artery.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Left Ventricular Dilatation & Mitral Insufficiency</summary><div class="details-content">

This abnormal blood flow pattern results in decreased perfusion of the left coronary artery system. Decreased myocardial blood supply leads to left ventricular ischemia and left ventricular failure ensues.

Left ventricular dilatation results in mitral insufficiency. Anomalous origin of the left coronary artery must be ruled out in any patient who presents with severe left ventricular dilatation and mitral insufficiency in the first few months of life.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Two-Coronary System & Direct Reimplantation</summary><div class="details-content">

The goal of surgical repair of anomalous origin of the left coronary artery is to reestablish a two coronary artery system. Cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross clamping are required for repair of this defect.

Technique for repair varies, but reimplantation of the anomalous coronary artery into the aortic root is performed whenever possible (not shown).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### The Takeuchi Intrapulmonary Baffle</summary><div class="details-content">

Another innovative technique utilized for repair of anomalous left coronary artery is depicted here ([Fig. 6-1](#fig-06-01)). In this technique, an aortopulmonary window is created, and a flap utilizing the anterior wall of the pulmonary artery is made.

The flap of anterior pulmonary artery wall is then sutured into place along the posterior pulmonary artery, creating a tunnel, which directs aortic blood flow to the orifice of the anomalous coronary ostium. The deficiency left in the anterior wall of the pulmonary artery is then repaired with a patch.

Alternatively, a patch of synthetic material or cryopreserved homograft may be used to create the baffle. Transesophageal echocardiography is utilized to help assess adequacy of repair. Aortic cross-clamp time and cardiopulmonary bypass time required for this procedure are moderate in length.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic Instability & Mechanical Circulatory Support</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of anomalous left coronary artery is often difficult. Ventricular dysfunction and mitral insufficiency prior to surgery often results in hemodynamic instability in the early postoperative period.

Invasive monitors commonly utilized include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter is utilized to allow monitoring of cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone and/or nitroprusside. Infusions of fentanyl and a neuromuscular blocking agent are utilized for sedation. Postoperative ventricular support in the form of extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is sometimes required in severe cases of depressed cardiac output. Following repair of anomalous left coronary artery, gradual improvement in ventricular function is the rule.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Arrhythmias, Pressures & Hospital Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative arrhythmias are occasionally encountered following this repair. Excessive bleeding is an uncommon complication.

With improvement in ventricular function and resolution of left atrial dilatation, intracardiac pressures and oxygen saturations should return to normal. Length of hospital stay is variable depending upon preoperative myocardial function and postoperative course. 10 days to 3 weeks is average.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Section 2: Cor Triatriatum</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomical Substrate & Figure 6-2</summary><div class="details-content">

<a id="fig-06-02"></a>
![Fig. 6-2: Cor Triatriatum](fig-06-02.png)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Fibromuscular Membrane & Chambers</summary><div class="details-content">

A very rare congenital cardiac anomaly, cor triatriatum occurs when there is failure of the normal development of the heart where the pulmonary veins enter the left atrium ([Fig. 6-2](#fig-06-02)). In the normal heart, pulmonary venous return of fully oxygenated blood enters the left atrium by way of the pulmonary veins.

Patients with cor triatriatum have a fibromuscular membrane that divides the left atrium into two parts. The pulmonary venous return enters the left atrium proximal to the membrane. The mitral valve and left atrial appendage occur distal or downstream to the membrane.

A communication or multiple small communications allow pulmonary venous return to enter the proximal left atrium and pass through the mitral valve to the left ventricle. In some variations of cor triatriatum, a patent foramen ovale or atrial septal defect occurs between the proximal left atrium (receiving pulmonary venous return) and the right atrium, usually with an additional communication between the right atrium and the distal chamber.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Venous Hypertension & Clinical Spectra</summary><div class="details-content">

Presentation of the patient with cor triatriatum is variable. When pulmonary venous return to the mitral valve is severely obstructed by a fibromuscular membrane, presentation is similar to that of mitral stenosis or obstructed total anomalous pulmonary venous return.

Pulmonary venous hypertension, pulmonary artery hypertension and signs and symptoms of congestive heart failure and depressed cardiac output prevail. These patients present with pallor, diminished peripheral pulses, tachypnea, feeding difficulties, poor weight gain and pulmonary edema.

In contrast, patients with lesser degrees of obstruction may present later in life or go undiagnosed for decades. Early severe obstruction predicts poorer prognosis. In patients with an atrial septal defect that communicates with the proximal chamber left-to-right shunting occurs, and these patients may have normal pulmonary venous pressure due to the decompression of the proximal left atrial chamber into the right atrium. Presentation can occur later and signs and symptoms are that of pulmonary overcirculation and left-to-right shunting.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Excision of the Obstructive Membrane</summary><div class="details-content">

Repair of cor triatriatum is performed soon after diagnosis in cases where pulmonary hypertension and depressed cardiac output are present. Elective repair can be performed later in life in patients whose symptoms are less severe.

Repair is carried out via median sternotomy using cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping. The obstructive membrane is resected via right atriotomy, working through an atrial septal defect, or via left atriotomy ([Fig. 6-2](#fig-06-02)).

Any associated defects also are repaired at this time. Following adequate rewarming, the patient is weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass. Transesophageal echocardiography is performed to help assess the adequacy of repair. Aortic cross-clamp time usually is short.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Hypertension Crises & Monitoring</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following elective repair of cor triatriatum without obstruction to pulmonary venous return is usually benign. In contrast, the postoperative course following repair of cor triatriatum with obstruction to pulmonary venous return is more complex and will mimic that of patients after repair of obstructed total anomalous pulmonary venous return.

Preoperative pulmonary artery hypertension and elevated pulmonary vascular resistance often predict postoperative pulmonary artery hypertension. Acute episodes of systemic and even suprasystemic pulmonary artery pressure in the postoperative period can result in cardiovascular instability.

Invasive monitors utilized following surgery include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter placed in the pulmonary artery allows monitoring of pulmonary artery pressure and, indirectly, cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near infrared spectroscopy) regional oximetry, and traditional pulse oximetry. Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone and/or nitroprusside.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Critical Care, Inhaled Nitric Oxide & Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Excessive postoperative bleeding is an uncommon complication. Cardiac rhythm disturbances can occur following surgery. Atrioventricular conduction disturbances, atrial tachydysrhythmias and sinus bradycardia sometimes are encountered.

Elevated pulmonary vascular resistance with decreased cardiac output is a common serious postoperative complication. Sedation and inhaled nitric oxide are treatment modalities utilized to manage pulmonary hypertensive crisis. ECMO support is sometimes required during the early postoperative period.

Infusions of fentanyl, supplemented by benzodiazepines, usually are employed for sedation. Neuromuscular blockade also is occasionally employed in the early post-operative period if there is significant hemodynamic instability. Pulmonary vascular resistance and pressure usually decrease gradually following repair, and extubation often is possible within days to 1 week after surgery. Length of hospital stay following repair averages 10 days to 3 weeks.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Section 3: Ebstein's Malformation of the Tricuspid Valve</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomical Substrate & Figure 6-3</summary><div class="details-content">

<a id="fig-06-03"></a>
![Fig. 6-3: Ebstein's Malformation of the Tricuspid Valve](fig-06-03.png)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Leaflet Displacement & RV Atrialization</summary><div class="details-content">

Ebstein's malformation is a rare congenital anomaly characterized by downward displacement of the posterior and septal leaflets of the tricuspid valve combined with an enlarged sail-like anterior leaflet ([Fig. 6-3](#fig-06-03)). The portion of the right ventricle on the atrial side of the inferiorly displaced tricuspid valve is abnormally thinned.

This thinning is known as "atrialization". Anatomic severity of the Ebstein's tricuspid valve, right atrium and right ventricle is variable. Commonly, the tricuspid valve is insufficient and the right atrium is dilated. Tricuspid insufficiency is occasionally combined with stenosis. Right ventricular and right atrial dysfunction results in cyanosis and right ventricular failure. Surgical intervention is undertaken when symptoms develop.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Critical Neonatal Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

Neonatal presentation of Ebstein's malformation represents a difficult and unique challenge. The neonate with Ebstein's malformation presents with cyanosis due right ventricular dysfunction.

Patency of the ductus arteriosus is usually required to provide adequate pulmonary blood flow. The pulmonary valve, although normally formed, does not open due to the inability of the Ebstein's right ventricle to generate pressure in excess of the pulmonary artery pressure. Systemic venous return crosses an atrial septal defect and mixes with pulmonary venous return, resulting in cyanosis.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Postnatal Reconstruction vs. Neonatal Starns Palliation</summary><div class="details-content">

The ideal surgical approach to repair of Ebstein's malformation of the tricuspid valve is to create a normally functioning tricuspid valve and close any atrial communication, resulting in separation of the pulmonary and systemic circulations. Various surgical repair techniques have been performed for post-natal presentation of Ebstein's malformation.

The atrialized portion of the right ventricle is plicated, the tricuspid valve and/or annulus is reconstructed, and the atrial septal defect is closed. In addition, redundant atrial wall is usually resected prior to atriotomy closure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### The Starns Single Ventricle Conversion</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical approach to repair of Ebstein's malformation presenting during the neonatal period is individualized, but an innovative approach to severe forms of this anomaly has been described by Starns et al. This approach, like the others described above, requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping.

The tricuspid valve orifice is closed utilizing a patch of cryopreserved homograft, pericardium or synthetic material. Care is taken when placing sutures near the coronary sinus ostium since atrioventricular conduction pathways are located in this area. Unrestricted flow across the atrial septal defect is insured by resecting the atrial septum.

Next, the redundant atrialized right ventricular tissue can be plicated and the dilated atrial wall resected. The patent ductus arteriosus is divided, and pulmonary blood flow is provided by placement of a systemic to pulmonary artery shunt. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required to complete repair are usually moderate in length. Subsequent surgical intervention would include bidirectional cavopulmonary shunting and eventually, Fontan completion.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Monitoring & Inotropic Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following neonatal palliation of Ebstein's malformation can be variable. Invasive monitors utilized following surgery include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter is utilized to help assess cardiac output.

Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry. Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone, nitroprusside and sometimes phenoxybenzamine and norepinephrine. Infusions of fentanyl and a neuromuscular blocking agent are commonly utilized following neonatal palliation of Ebstein's malformation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Arrhythmias, Conduction System & Hospital Stay</summary><div class="details-content">

Arrhythmia is a common complication encountered in patients with Ebstein's malformation. Preoperative atrial and ventricular dilatation as well as right ventricular dysfunction predispose these patients to arrythmias.

Postoperative conduction disturbance is a potential complication since the surgical repair requires placement of sutures near atrioventricular conduction pathways. Atrioventricular pacing equipment should be readily available. Bleeding is an uncommon complication. Following acute recovery, the usual neonatal postoperative course would apply. Hospital stay required following repair averages 2 to 3 weeks.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Section 4: High SVC Partial Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Drainage</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomical Substrate & Figure 6-4</summary><div class="details-content">

<a id="fig-06-04"></a>
![Fig. 6-4: High SVC Partial Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Drainage (Warden Procedure)](fig-06-04.png)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Atrial-Level Shunt & Right Heart Volume Overload</summary><div class="details-content">

Anomalous pulmonary venous drainage occurs when the pulmonary veins drain into systemic veins or into the right atrium rather than the left atrium ([Fig. 6-4](#fig-06-04)). The anomalous pulmonary venous drainage can be total, involving all of the pulmonary venous return, or partial, involving some of the pulmonary venous return.

High SVC partial anomalous pulmonary venous drainage refers to drainage of some or all of the right sided pulmonary veins to the SVC remote from the right atrium. This abnormality results in left-to-right shunting of blood at the atrial level as well as right ventricular and right atrial dilatation.

With prolonged dilatation, right ventricular dysfunction and atrial arrhythmias can occur. In addition, long standing pulmonary overcirculation occurring as a result of this anomaly can eventually lead to pulmonary vascular obstructive disease.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Conventional Baffling vs. SVC Obstruction Risks</summary><div class="details-content">

Repair of high SVC partial anomalous pulmonary venous return requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping.

The conventional approach to repair of this lesion is similar to the technique of repair described for sinus venosus atrial septal defect in which the anomalous pulmonary venous drainage is redirected with a patch, through an atrial septal defect into the left atrium (see Sinus Venosus Atrial Septal Defect). A patch, or baffle placed in the superior vena cava, however, could potentially result in obstruction of the superior vena cava or the anomalous pulmonary vein.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### The Warden Procedure Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

With an alternative technique, the SVC is divided just above the entrance of the anomalous pulmonary vein ([Fig. 6-4](#fig-06-04)). Next, the lower SVC stump is oversewn, and the entire mouth of the SVC is redirected, with an intracardiac baffle, through an atrial septal defect created by the surgeon.

The upper SVC is then anastomosed to the right atrial appendage to allow upper body systemic venous return to enter the right atrium ([Fig. 6-4](#fig-06-04)). With this technique, the potential for SVC obstruction due to scarring around the intra-caval baffle is avoided.

In addition, postoperative atrial arrhythmias are minimized since suture lines are remote from the area of the SA node. Aortic cross clamp time and cardiopulmonary bypass time required to repair this defect are usually short to moderate in length.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamics & Upper Body Venous Drainage</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of high SVC partial anomalous pulmonary venous drainage is usually benign. Invasive monitors utilized include central venous and arterial catheters.

Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry. Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management are rarely required.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rhythm, Anastomotic Patency & Hospital Recovery</summary><div class="details-content">

Atrial arrhythmias are a potential complication following this repair. Temporary cardiac pacing capability should be readily available. Stenosis of the SVC-right atrial anastomosis is a rare complication, but would likely be manifest as swelling of the head and upper body.

Bleeding is an unusual complication since suture lines are exposed to low right heart pressure. Length of hospital stay required for recovery averages 4 to 6 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Section 5: Scimitar Syndrome</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomical Substrate & Figure 6-5</summary><div class="details-content">

<a id="fig-06-05"></a>
![Fig. 6-5: Scimitar Syndrome](fig-06-05.png)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### The "Turkish Sword" & Associated Malformations</summary><div class="details-content">

Scimitar syndrome is a rare congenital anomaly in which there is an anomalous pulmonary vein draining most or all of the right lung, which connects to the inferior vena cava, adjacent to the cavo-atrial junction ([Fig. 6-5](#fig-06-05)). The name of the syndrome is derived from the curved appearance of the anomalous vein on chest X-ray, which resembles a "Turkish sword" or scimitar.

An anomalous arterial supply to the affected lung arising from the descending aorta or one of its branches is commonly associated with scimitar syndrome, as are intra-cardiac defects, particularly secundum atrial septal defects. Hypoplasia of the right lung, with consequent rightward displacement of the mediastinum is also occasionally seen.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Infantile vs. Adult Clinical Presentations</summary><div class="details-content">

Depending on the degree of left to right intracardiac shunting and right lung hypoplasia, the patient with Scimitar syndrome may present with recurrent respiratory infections and mild exertional dyspnea (older children and adults), or with severe disease in the neonatal period or early infancy.

The latter presentation is characterized by cyanosis, respiratory distress, congestive heart failure and pulmonary artery hypertension.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Systemic Collateral Ligation & Re-routing Approaches</summary><div class="details-content">

Achievement of unobstructed drainage of pulmonary venous return to the left atrium is the goal of surgical intervention for patients with Scimitar syndrome. In addition, the anomalous systemic arterial supply to the abnormal pulmonary lobe(s) is ligated or divided ([Fig. 6-5](#fig-06-05)). Associated intracardiac defects also are addressed at this time.

Surgical approach may vary depending upon the patient's individual anatomy and associated defects. When isolated Scimitar syndrome is not associated with intracardiac defects, right thoracotomy with direct reimplantation of the anomalous pulmonary vein to the left atrium often can be performed without the need for cardiopulmonary bypass.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intracardiac Baffle & Pneumonectomy Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

When associated with intracardiac defects, or when direct anastomosis of the anomalous pulmonary vein to the left atrium is not possible, cardiopulmonary bypass is utilized. Right thoracotomy or median sternotomy can be used. The Scimitar vein is anastomosed to the right atrium, and an intracardiac baffle or patch is constructed to redirect the anomalous venous drainage across an atrial septal defect to the left atrium ([Fig. 6-5](#fig-06-05)).

Alternatively, when the native insertion of the anomalous pulmonary vein is near the mouth of the inferior vena cava or into the right atrium directly, an intracardiac baffle as described above is all that is required. Any additional intracardiac defects are addressed at this time. Cardiopulmonary bypass time required to complete repair is usually short if femoral venous cannulation is employed for inferior vena caval drainage. In small patients, however, a brief period of deep hypothermic circulatory arrest may be required during the portion of the repair at the inferior vena caval-atrial junction.

In cases where there is marked pulmonary parenchymal change and/or severe hypoplasia of the right lung, right pneumonectomy represents a reasonable option. Pneumonectomy eliminates the left to right shunt and anomalous arterial supply.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Severity Stratification & Hemodynamic Monitoring</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of Scimitar syndrome is related to the degree of congestive heart failure, respiratory compromise and pulmonary artery hypertension encountered preoperatively. Older patients who present with mild symptoms will likely have an uncomplicated recovery. Infants presenting with severe pulmonary artery hypertension are likely to have a more involved and prolonged postoperative course.

Invasive monitors include arterial and central venous catheters. Intracardiac lines may be utilized in patients who present with severe symptoms and pulmonary artery hypertension. Non invasive monitors might include NIRS (near infrared spectroscopy) regional oximetry, and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusion requirement varies. Patients who present with severe symptoms and pulmonary artery hypertension might require epinephrine and milrinone. Inhaled nitric oxide may be necessary as well. Older patients who present with fewer symptoms rarely require vasoactive infusions.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Phrenic Nerve, Thoracic Duct & Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative complications can include phrenic nerve injury since the operative field is adjacent to the right phrenic nerve. Postoperative bleeding is uncommon. Thoracic duct injury is a rarely encountered complication.

Prolonged mechanical ventilation is a potential complication for the infant who presents with severe symptoms. Cardiac rhythm disturbance is uncommon, but more likely in patients who have repair of associated intracardiac defects. Intracardiac pressures should be normal following surgery. Patients who present with pulmonary artery hypertension often will have persistence of this for some time postoperatively.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Section 6: Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection (TAPVC)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomical Substrate & Figures 6-6 to 6-8</summary><div class="details-content">

<a id="fig-06-06"></a>
![Fig. 6-6: Supracardiac Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection](fig-06-06.png)

<a id="fig-06-07"></a>
![Fig. 6-7: Intracardiac Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection](fig-06-07.png)

<a id="fig-06-08"></a>
![Fig. 6-8: Infracardiac Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection](fig-06-08.png)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Classification & Common Confluence</summary><div class="details-content">

Anomalous pulmonary venous drainage is defined as drainage of the pulmonary veins into systemic veins or into the right atrium rather than the left atrium. The anomalous pulmonary venous drainage can be total, involving all of the pulmonary venous return, or partial, involving some of the pulmonary venous return. When anomalous drainage of the pulmonary veins is total, three main subtypes exist.

Supracardiac TAPVC is present when all of the pulmonary venous return enters a common confluence located posterior to the left atrium ([Fig. 6-6](#fig-06-06)). Blood drains from the pulmonary venous confluence to the vertical vein, or cardinal vein into the innominate vein where it subsequently enters the superior vena cava to mix with systemic venous return. The left atrium receives mixed systemic and pulmonary venous return via an atrial septal defect. Obstruction can occur anywhere along this path.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intracardiac & Infracardiac Variants</summary><div class="details-content">

Intracardiac TAPVC is present when the pulmonary venous return enters a confluence as described above, which empties into the coronary sinus ([Fig. 6-7](#fig-06-07)). Coronary venous return, pulmonary venous return and systemic venous return empty into the right atrium. The left atrium receives mixed systemic and pulmonary venous return via an atrial septal defect.

Infracardiac TAPVC is present when the pulmonary venous confluence drains inferiorly to the ductus venosus which empties into the inferior vena cava ([Fig. 6-8](#fig-06-08)). The right atrium receives mixed systemic and pulmonary venous return. The left atrium is supplied by the right atrium via an atrial septal defect. Obstruction can occur anywhere along this path.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Obstructed Pulmonary Venous Drainage & Urgency</summary><div class="details-content">

Repair of TAPVC is performed early in life. When obstruction is present, pulmonary artery hypertension results.

Pulmonary congestion and cardiovascular collapse may ensue. Urgent surgical repair is essential in this subset of patients.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Supracardiac Anastomosis & Vertical Vein Ligation</summary><div class="details-content">

Repair of TAPVC requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping. Temporary hypothermic circulatory arrest is frequently utilized to provide the surgeon with a bloodless field. The pulmonary venous confluence is mobilized and the right atrium is opened.

With repair of Supracardiac TAPVC, the posterior left atrial wall and anterior pulmonary venous confluence are opened widely and anastomosed together ([Fig. 6-6](#fig-06-06)). The vertical vein is then ligated. Next, the atrial septum is reconstructed with a patch in such a way as to enlarge the left atrial chamber size.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intracardiac Unroofing & Infracardiac Reconstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

With repair of Intracardiac TAPVC, the coronary sinus is "unroofed" by incising across the coronary sinus ostium into the left atrium ([Fig. 6-7](#fig-06-07)). The atrial septum is then reconstructed with a patch allowing the pulmonary venous return to enter the left atrium directly.

With repair of Infracardiac TAPVC, the posterior left atrial wall and anterior pulmonary venous confluence are opened widely and anastomosed together ([Fig. 6-8](#fig-06-08)). The vein originally draining pulmonary venous blood to the ductus venosus is ligated. The atrial septum is next reconstructed with a patch in such a way as to enlarge the left atrial chamber size.

Delayed sternal closure is sometimes required, especially in infants with obstruction of pulmonary venous drainage preoperatively. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required to complete repair are usually moderate in length. Temporary circulatory arrest time, if required is usually less than 1 hour.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Obstructed vs. Unobstructed Clinical Course</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following elective repair of unobstructed TAPVC is usually benign. In contrast, the postoperative course following repair of obstructed TAPVC is usually more complex.

Preoperative pulmonary artery hypertension and elevated pulmonary vascular resistance often leads to postoperative pulmonary artery hypertension. Acute episodes of systemic or suprasystemic level pulmonary artery pressure in the postoperative period can result in cardiovascular instability.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Invasive Monitoring & Pulmonary Hypertension Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Invasive monitors utilized following surgery include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter placed in the pulmonary artery allows monitoring of pulmonary artery pressure and cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone and/or nitroprusside. Excessive postoperative bleeding is an uncommon complication. Cardiac rhythm disturbances can occur following surgery. Atrioventricular conduction disturbances, atrial tachydysrhythmias and sinus bradycardia are sometimes encountered.

Elevated pulmonary vascular resistance with decreased cardiac output is a common serious postoperative complication. Sedation and inhaled nitric oxide are treatment modalities utilized to manage pulmonary hypertensive crisis. ECMO support is sometimes required during the early postoperative period. Infusions of fentanyl and a neuromuscular blocking agent are usually required for sedation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Extubation Timeline & Hospital Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary vascular resistance and pressure usually decrease gradually following repair, and extubation is often possible 5 days to 1 week after surgery.

Length of hospital stay following repair averages 10 days to 3 weeks.
