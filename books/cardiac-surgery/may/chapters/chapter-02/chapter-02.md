# LVOT Obstruction and Aortic Anomalies

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Pathophysiological Spectrum of Left-Sided Obstruction
* **Left Ventricular Outflow Tract (LVOT) Gradient & Remodeling:** Fixed or dynamic obstruction at subvalvar, valvar, supravalvar, or aortic arch levels creates significant pressure overload on the developing left ventricle. This triggers concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, reduced diastolic ventricular compliance, elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP), subendocardial ischemia, and an increased risk of malignant ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death.
* **Ductal-Dependent Systemic Perfusion:** Severe neonatal obstructive lesions—such as critical aortic stenosis, severe coarctation of the aorta, and interrupted aortic arch—depend entirely upon right-to-left shunting across a patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) to maintain systemic, coronary, or lower-body organ perfusion. Spontaneous functional closure of the ductus arteriosus in the first days of life triggers acute cardiogenic shock, severe metabolic acidosis, and multiorgan failure.
* **Prostaglandin E1 Stabilization:** Immediate continuous intravenous infusion of prostaglandin E1 ($PGE_1$, 0.05–0.1 mcg/kg/min) is lifesaving in ductal-dependent neonatal left-sided obstruction to reopen or maintain ductal patency and restore systemic circulation prior to surgical or transcatheter intervention.
* **Concomitant Anomalies & Shone Complex:** Obstruction frequently occurs at multiple levels simultaneously (supravalvar mitral ring, parachute mitral valve, subaortic stenosis, and coarctation of the aorta), requiring comprehensive multi-level hemodynamic evaluation.

### Anatomic Forms & Clinical Classification
* **Critical Aortic Stenosis:** Severe valvar obstruction in neonates with pinhole unicuspid or dysplastic bicuspid valve architecture; associated with marked LV dysfunction, low cardiac output, and endocardial fibroelastosis (EFE).
* **Subaortic Stenosis:** Discrete fibromuscular membrane or diffuse tunnel-like fibromuscular narrowing of the LVOT; often develops secondarily or progresses after prior repair of VSD, AV canal, or interrupted aortic arch.
* **Supravalvar Aortic Stenosis:** Discrete hourglass narrowing or diffuse hypoplasia of the ascending aorta beginning at the sinotubular junction; strongly associated with Williams-Beuren syndrome (7q11.23 microdeletion) and elastin gene abnormalities, frequently involving coronary ostial stenosis.
* **Aortic Annular Hypoplasia:** Small aortic valve annulus that cannot accommodate an age-appropriate prosthetic valve, mandating annular enlargement techniques (Konno, Ross-Konno, Manouguian, or Nicks).
* **Coarctation of the Aorta:** Discrete shelf-like narrowing of the aortic media and intima at the junction of the aortic isthmus and ductus arteriosus; classically presents with upper extremity hypertension, diminished or absent femoral pulses, and upper-to-lower extremity systolic pressure gradient $\ge 20\text{ mmHg}$.
* **Interrupted Aortic Arch (IAA):** Complete anatomical and luminal discontinuity between the ascending and descending aorta:
  * *Type A (30–40%):* Interruption distal to the left subclavian artery.
  * *Type B (50–60%):* Interruption between the left common carotid and left subclavian arteries; highly associated with 22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge syndrome), aberrant right subclavian artery, and conoventricular VSD.
  * *Type C (< 5%):* Interruption between the innominate and left common carotid arteries.
* **Vascular Ring (Double Aortic Arch):** Complete persistence of both right and left fourth embryonic aortic arches encircling the trachea and esophagus; right arch is typically dominant and posterior, while the left arch is smaller, anterior, and gives rise to the ductus/ligamentum arteriosum, causing stridor, barking cough, and dysphagia.

### Surgical Techniques & Procedural Standards
* **Aortic Valve Preservation & Reconstruction:** In neonates, surgical aortic valvotomy via median sternotomy or percutaneous balloon aortic valvuloplasty is palliative, aiming to relieve obstruction while minimizing aortic regurgitation.
* **The Ross Procedure (Pulmonary Autograft):** Complete aortic root replacement utilizing the patient's own pulmonary valve autograft with coronary ostial reimplantation. Offers normal hemodynamics, freedom from systemic anticoagulation, and somatic growth potential; the right ventricular outflow tract is reconstructed with a cryopreserved pulmonary homograft.
* **The Konno Procedure (Aortoventriculoplasty):** Anterior incision through the aortic annulus extending deeply into the muscular ventricular septum to enlarge a hypoplastic LVOT; the enlarged LVOT is closed with a prosthetic or autograft root, and the created septal defect is patched.
* **Subaortic Membrane Resection:** Transaortic exposure with careful enucleation of the fibromuscular ridge and conservative septal myectomy; extreme caution is required to prevent perforation of the anterior mitral leaflet and damage to the underlying bundle of His.
* **Supravalvar Stenosis Repair:** Symmetrical multi-sinus reconstruction (Brom or Doty three-patch technique) or interdigitating aortoplasty extending into the non-coronary and right coronary sinuses of Valsalva to restore normal sinotubular geometry without distorting coronary ostia.
* **Coarctation Repair Techniques:** Left posterolateral thoracotomy approach:
  * *Resection with Extended End-to-End Anastomosis:* Standard procedure of choice, excising all abnormal ductal tissue and opening the underside of the transverse aortic arch to relieve associated arch hypoplasia.
  * *Subclavian Flap Angioplasty (Waldhausen):* Division and longitudinal opening of the left subclavian artery turned down across the coarctation shelf; sacrifices the left subclavian vessel.
  * *Subclavian Translocation:* Transposition of the left subclavian origin distal to the coarctation site.
* **Interrupted Arch Reconstruction:** Single-stage complete repair in neonates via median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA) or selective antegrade cerebral perfusion (SACP). Direct extensive end-to-side anastomosis between the mobilized descending aorta and ascending arch, accompanied by VSD patch closure.
* **Vascular Ring Division:** Left posterolateral thoracotomy without bypass; division and oversewing of the non-dominant (anterior/left) arch and division of the ligamentum arteriosum, supplemented by aortopexy to the sternum when severe tracheomalacia is present.

### Critical Postoperative Management & Pitfalls
* **Paradoxical Postcoarctectomy Hypertension:** Reactive sympathetic hyperactivity and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis activation following aortic declamping; managed aggressively with continuous sodium nitroprusside or esmolol infusions, followed by oral beta-blockers or ACE inhibitors to prevent postcoarctectomy syndrome (mesenteric arteritis, abdominal pain, ileus, and bowel infarction).
* **Conduction System Trauma:** Suture-line edema or surgical incision into the ventricular septum (Konno, VSD closure in IAA, deep subaortic myectomy) carries significant risk of transient or permanent complete atrioventricular block; temporary epicardial pacing wires and external dual-chamber pacing capability are mandatory.
* **Nerve Protection:** High vulnerability of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve (vocal cord paresis, hoarseness) and left phrenic nerve (diaphragmatic plication risk) during dissection of the aortic isthmus, ductus, and vascular rings.
* **Spinal Cord Ischemia:** Prolonged aortic cross-clamping during coarctation repair carries a risk of spinal cord hypoperfusion and paraplegia; mitigated by minimizing clamp time, maintaining proximal blood pressure, and preserving intercostal collateral circulation.
* **Left Ventricular Diastolic Dysfunction:** Severe post-relief LV stiffness requires inotropic support (milrinone, epinephrine) and cautious volume administration to avoid pulmonary venous congestion and elevated left atrial pressure.

## Critical Aortic Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Critical aortic stenosis is a severe form of congenital aortic stenosis that presents in the neonatal period. Cardiovascular symptoms become acute as the patent ductus arteriosus closes and the left ventricle is required to supply the entire systemic circulation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Valve Morphology and Degree of Obstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

The severity of symptoms depends upon the degree of obstruction. The aortic valve may be bicuspid or unicuspid. In unicuspid valves, the orifice is eccentric with a single fused commissure, whereas bicuspid valves often present with thickened, dysplastic raphes and restricted leaflet excursion.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Left Ventricular Dysfunction & Endocardial Fibroelastosis</summary><div class="details-content">

Abnormalities of the left ventricle can occur and include dilatation, depressed function and endocardial fibroelastosis. Cardiovascular collapse may occur in patients with severe obstruction. Early surgical intervention is required.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-02-01"></span>![](fig-02-01.png)

**FIGURE 2-1** Critical Aortic Stenosis. Diagram of neonatal left ventricular outflow tract obstruction by a stenotic, dysplastic aortic valve with marked concentric hypertrophy (1), followed by surgical commissurotomy under direct vision with selective incising of fused commissures (2).

The goal of therapy in patients with critical aortic stenosis is to relieve obstruction of the flow of blood through the aortic valve without causing aortic valve insufficiency ([Fig. 2-1](#fig-02-01)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transcatheter vs. Surgical Options</summary><div class="details-content">

Percutaneous balloon valvotomy can be effective in relieving valve obstruction in these patients. The surgical approach to this lesion is via median sternotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass or temporary inflow occlusion.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Direct-Vision Commissurotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

The aortic valve is visualized and incised at the commissure(s) ([Fig. 2-1](#fig-02-01)). Unicuspid or bicuspid valves are commonly encountered. Precise commissurotomy may be difficult due to the abnormal nature of aortic valve development in these patients. Care is taken to avoid aortic insufficiency as this is poorly tolerated.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following relief of critical aortic stenosis depends upon the degree of left ventricular dysfunction present preoperatively as well as the relative success of the procedure.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Monitoring & Inotropic Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

Invasive monitors utilized following repair of critical aortic stenosis include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, nitroprusside and milrinone.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recovery & Long-Term Valve Surveillance</summary><div class="details-content">

With recovery of left ventricular function following repair, prognosis is good. Aortic valve replacement may be required later in life. Length of hospital stay is variable, but 1–3 weeks is average.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Subaortic Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Rare in infancy, subaortic stenosis may present as distinct fibromuscular stenosis, or as hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM). When present in infancy, associated left heart obstructive lesions, such as coarctation of the aorta, or interrupted aortic arch may coexist.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Mechanical Obstruction & Myocardial Hypertrophy</summary><div class="details-content">

Obstruction to the egress of blood from the left ventricle results in left ventricular hypertrophy and its sequelae, including increased potential for arrhythmia and sudden death.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Indications for Surgical Intervention</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical intervention is indicated when the left ventricular outflow tract obstruction is moderate to severe, or when progressive aortic insufficiency develops secondary to high-velocity jet trauma on the aortic valve leaflets.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-02-02"></span>![](fig-02-02.png)

**FIGURE 2-2** Subaortic Stenosis. Diagram showing discrete subaortic fibromuscular membrane obstructing the left ventricular outflow tract beneath the aortic valve (1), and transaortic resection of the membrane with preservation of the aortic and mitral valve leaflets and conduction system (2).

Surgical repair of subaortic stenosis requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping ([Fig. 2-2](#fig-02-02)). The aorta is opened just above the aortic valve, and the aortic valve leaflets are retracted to expose the obstructive tissue below the valve.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transaortic Resection & Tissue Preservation</summary><div class="details-content">

Care is taken to excise as much obstructive tissue as possible without damaging the mitral valve apparatus, the atrioventricular conduction tissue or the aortic valve leaflets ([Fig. 2-2](#fig-02-02)).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Assessment of Relief</summary><div class="details-content">

Transesophageal echocardiography and pressure measurement comparison are utilized to help assess the adequacy of repair. Aortic cross-clamp time and cardiopulmonary bypass time required to complete repair are usually short.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following resection of subaortic stenosis is usually uncomplicated. Invasive monitors utilized following surgery include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic & Antihypertensive Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic support are usually focused upon control of systemic hypertension. Nitroprusside and/or esmolol are often used in the early postoperative period. Captopril or enalapril are usually started later in the postoperative course.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Conduction Disturbances & Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Conduction abnormalities including complete heart block can occur following surgery. Atrioventricular pacing should be readily available. Excessive bleeding is an uncommon postoperative complication. Length of hospital stay required following resection of subaortic stenosis averages 5–7 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Supravalvar Aortic Stenosis</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

As the name suggests supravalvar aortic stenosis indicates a narrowing of the junction between the aortic valve and the ascending aorta. This is an uncommon form of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction and is encountered in patients with Williams syndrome or as a familial form.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hypertrophy and Coronary Perfusion Risks</summary><div class="details-content">

Obstruction to the egress of blood from the left ventricle to the systemic circulation results in left ventricular hypertrophy and its sequelae, including left ventricular dysfunction, ischemia and risk for sudden death. In addition, the coronary arteries arise proximal to the obstruction, exposing them to high systolic pressures and predisposing to premature atherosclerosis or ostial stenosis.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Surgical Indications</summary><div class="details-content">

Surgical intervention is indicated when the pressure gradient across the area of narrowing is large, or when signs of myocardial ischemia are detected.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-02-03"></span>![](fig-02-03.png)

**FIGURE 2-3** Supravalvar Aortic Stenosis. Schema demonstrating hourglass narrowing of the ascending aorta at the sinotubular junction (1), counter-incisions extending into the aortic sinuses of Valsalva with excision of the stenotic ridge (2), and interdigitating reconstruction (3).

Repair of supravalvar aortic stenosis requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping ([Fig. 2-3](#fig-02-03)). The aorta is incised into each sinus of Valsalva.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Sinus Reconstruction & Interdigitation</summary><div class="details-content">

Counter-incisions are made in the aorta above the area of obstruction, and the stenotic segment is removed ([Fig. 2-3](#fig-02-03)). The two segments of aorta are interdigitated, and anastomosed with monofilament suture.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pressure Gradients and TEE</summary><div class="details-content">

Following weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, pressures in the left ventricle and peripheral artery are compared and transesophageal echocardiography is performed to assess the adequacy of repair. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required for completion of repair are usually short to moderate in length.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of supravalvar aortic stenosis is usually benign. Invasive monitors utilized include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Systemic Afterload & Antihypertensive Therapy</summary><div class="details-content">

Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry. Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management are usually aimed at reduction of systemic vascular resistance.

Excessive bleeding is an uncommon postoperative complication. Arterial oxygen saturation and intracardiac pressures following surgery should be normal. Systemic hypertension is often encountered during the postoperative period. Anti-hypertensive medications are usually required to control this, and might include a beta-blocker and/or captopril or enalapril.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hospital Course</summary><div class="details-content">

Length of hospital stay required following repair averages 5–7 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## The Ross Procedure (Pulmonary Autograft)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic valve stenosis causes progressive left ventricular hypertrophy with associated sequelae of decreased ventricular function and myocardial ischemia. These factors place the patient at increased risk for sudden death.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Insufficiency and Ventricular Dilation</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic valve insufficiency results in left ventricular dilatation, decreased cardiac output and can eventually lead to congestive heart failure. Exercise intolerance and exertional dyspnea can occur in patients with severe forms of aortic valve disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Autograft Growth Potential & Anticoagulation Freedom</summary><div class="details-content">

The Ross procedure is an innovative technique that utilizes the patient's own pulmonary valve for aortic valve replacement. A cryopreserved pulmonary valve homograft is used to replace the patient's native pulmonary valve. Intermediate follow-up studies indicate that the pulmonary autograft grows, making the Ross procedure the aortic valve replacement technique of choice for small children and infants.

Further, anticoagulation with coumadin is not required following the Ross procedure. In contrast to traditional mechanical aortic valve replacement, however, the Ross procedure is a more extensive operation. In addition, later replacement of the pulmonary valve homograft is generally required due to growth of the patient or degeneration of the homograft.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-02-04"></span>![](fig-02-04.png)

**FIGURE 2-4** The Ross Procedure. Aortic valve replacement with pulmonary autograft. Harvest of the native pulmonary root as an autograft with coronary button excision, implantation of the neo-aortic root with coronary reimplantation (1), and reconstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract with a pulmonary homograft (2).

Aortic valve replacement requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping ([Fig. 2-4](#fig-02-04)). The aorta is opened and the aortic valve is inspected. Once suitability for the Ross procedure has been determined, the pulmonary root autograft is excised.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Autograft Implantation & Coronary Buttons</summary><div class="details-content">

As the aortic root is excised, the coronary artery ostia are excised as buttons with adjacent aortic wall tissue. In children, implantation of the autograft is generally performed as a root replacement ([Fig. 2-4](#fig-02-04)).

The proximal pulmonary autograft is secured in the position of the native aortic root. The coronary arteries are next implanted, and the distal end of the autograft is anastomosed to the ascending aorta.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Homograft</summary><div class="details-content">

A cryopreserved, valved pulmonary homograft is inserted in the position of the original pulmonary root ([Fig. 2-4](#fig-02-04)). Transesophageal echocardiography is utilized to assess adequacy of the repair. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required to complete repair are moderate to long.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following the Ross aortic valve replacement procedure can be variable. This is an extensive operation and inotropic support is frequently required.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic & Bleeding Surveillance</summary><div class="details-content">

Invasive monitors utilized following surgery include arterial, central venous, and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter is utilized to assess cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone, nitroprusside and/or nitroglycerin. Postoperative bleeding is a potential complication since there are numerous external cardiac suture lines exposed to systemic pressure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rhythm, Diastolic Function & Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Cardiac arrhythmias occasionally occur following repair. Temporary atrioventricular pacing capability should be readily available.

Postoperative intracardiac pressures should be normal. Slightly elevated left atrial pressure may occur secondary to left ventricular hypertrophy and poor left ventricular compliance. Arterial oxygen saturation should be normal. Length of hospital stay following the Ross procedure averages one week to 10 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## The Konno Procedure (Aortoventriculoplasty)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic stenosis causes progressive left ventricular hypertrophy with associated sequelae of decreased ventricular function and myocardial ischemia. These factors place the patient at increased risk for sudden death. Exercise intolerance and exertional dyspnea can occur in patients with severe forms of aortic valve disease.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Annular Hypoplasia and Subvalvar Obstruction</summary><div class="details-content">

When obstruction to the outflow of blood from the left ventricle to the aorta includes aortic valve annular hypoplasia and subvalvar obstruction, traditional aortic valve replacement may not be possible.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Ventricular Septal Extension Principle</summary><div class="details-content">

In such cases, enlargement of the aortic valve annulus can be carried out by extending an anterior incision through the annulus of the aortic valve into the ventricular septum. Aortic root replacement can then be performed with a cryopreserved, valved aortic homograft, a pulmonary autograft or a mechanical aortic valve prosthesis.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-02-05"></span>![](fig-02-05.png)

**FIGURE 2-5** The Konno Procedure. Aortic root replacement with ventricular septal extension. Excision of the native aortic root with coronary buttons, anterior incision through the aortic annulus extending into the ventricular septum (1), insertion of a neo-aortic root prosthesis or autograft enlarging the LVOT (2), and coronary button reimplantation (3, 4).

Aortic root replacement with extension into the ventricular septum requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping ([Fig. 2-5](#fig-02-05)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Root Excision & Ventricular Septotomy</summary><div class="details-content">

The native aortic root is excised, with careful attention to excision of the coronary arteries as buttons with adjacent aortic wall. Next, the left ventricular outflow tract is opened widely by incising into the ventricular septum ([Fig. 2-5](#fig-02-05)).

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Graft Implantation & Coronary Reimplantation</summary><div class="details-content">

A cryopreserved, valved aortic homograft, pulmonary autograft or mechanical aortic valve prosthesis is inserted into the newly opened left ventricular outflow tract. When a homograft or autograft is used, the neo-aortic root is stitched to the native aortic annulus, and the coronary artery buttons are implanted into the base of the graft. The distal end of the graft is then sutured to the distal native aorta.

Transesophageal echocardiography and intracardiac pressure measurements are performed to evaluate the adequacy of repair. Aortic cross-clamp time and cardiopulmonary bypass time required to complete repair are moderate to long.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following the Konno aortic root replacement procedure is variable. Invasive monitors utilized include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter may be utilized to monitor cardiac output.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic Instability & Suture Line Bleeding</summary><div class="details-content">

Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry. Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone, nitroprusside and/or nitroglycerin.

This is an extensive operation. Hemodynamic instability in the early postoperative period is sometimes encountered. Excessive postoperative bleeding is a potential complication since the surgical site includes suture lines exposed to systemic pressure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Conduction Block & Recovery</summary><div class="details-content">

Abnormalities of atrioventricular conduction can occur due to manipulation or incisions at the site of ventricular septal extension. Atrioventricular pacing capability should be readily available.

Postoperative intracardiac pressures and arterial oxygen saturation should be normal. Length of hospital stay following repair averages 7–10 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Coarctation of the Aorta</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortic coarctation is a congenital narrowing of the aorta, usually distal to the origin of the left subclavian artery, opposite the area of the ductus arteriosus at the aortic isthmus. Varying degrees of aortic arch hypoplasia may coexist.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Ductal Dependency and Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

In the newborn, the presence of a patent ductus arteriosus will often allow adequate lower body perfusion. Presentation may vary from acute decompensation at the time of ductal closure in the infant, to upper extremity hypertension in the older child or adult. Identification of elevated pressures in the upper versus lower extremities is useful in making the diagnosis of aortic coarctation.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-02-06"></span>![](fig-02-06.png)

**FIGURE 2-6** Coarctation of the Aorta. Anatomy of juxtaductal aortic coarctation with upper-to-lower body gradient, and principal surgical repair modalities: left subclavian flap angioplasty (1), resection with end-to-end anastomosis (2), and left subclavian translocation angioplasty (3).

Repair of aortic coarctation is usually performed via a left thoracotomy without cardiopulmonary bypass ([Fig. 2-6](#fig-02-06)). There are a number of techniques available to repair the coarctation, each with its own advantages and disadvantages.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 1. Left Subclavian Flap Angioplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

In this technique, the left subclavian artery is isolated and divided distally ([Fig. 2-6](#fig-02-06)). The vessel is then opened longitudinally with extension of the incision on to the aorta. The subclavian artery flap is then folded down over the area of aortic narrowing and sutured into place.

Subclavian patch angioplasty has a number of advantages over other techniques. This technique is relatively simple, it allows for the use of the patient's own tissue, it has a low recurrence rate, and it provides excellent relief of the obstruction. A relative disadvantage is that the subclavian artery is sacrificed in the subclavian flap angioplasty technique. Transient decreased perfusion to the left arm has been observed in older patients who have undergone repair using this technique, but limb threatening ischemia is rare. Associated aortic arch hypoplasia is not addressed with this technique.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 2. Resection with End-to-End Anastomosis</summary><div class="details-content">

With resection and end-to-end anastomosis, the aorta is isolated and the aortic isthmus and ductal tissue are resected ([Fig. 2-6](#fig-02-06)). The distal aortic arch is incised along its inferior side, the lower aorta is incised along its lateral side, and the two are sewn together. The advantages of end-to-end technique are that the subclavian artery is not sacrificed, and complete relief of obstruction is easily obtained. The end-to-end aortoplasty technique is the procedure of choice for many surgeons today, especially in patients beyond the newborn period.

A variation of the classic end-to-end repair is the extended end-to-end technique. This technique is similar to the classic end-to-end repair of coarctation of the aorta discussed above, but differs in that the aortic arch is incised more proximally along its lesser curvature, and the lower aorta is incised further along its posterior-lateral aspect. The two ends are then brought together and anastomosed. The advantage of this variation is that relief of aortic arch hypoplasia is possible since the aortic arch is more widely opened, and therefore, obstruction is more readily relieved. Extended end-to-end repair may prove to be the operation of choice since aortic arch hypoplasia is now thought to be more prevalent than previously recognized.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 3. Subclavian Translocation</summary><div class="details-content">

With the subclavian artery translocation technique, the subclavian artery is isolated and transected at its origin ([Fig. 2-6](#fig-02-06)). A longitudinal incision is made along the medial aspect of the subclavian artery and also along the anterior aspect of the aorta. The two segments are then anastomosed as depicted in the diagram.

The advantages of this repair technique are similar to the advantages in the subclavian flap angioplasty technique, but since the subclavian artery is reimplanted, the vessel is not sacrificed. A disadvantage of this technique is that the aorta and subclavian artery must be extensively mobilized and tension on the suture line may make recurrence more likely.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### 4. Synthetic Patch Angioplasty</summary><div class="details-content">

With synthetic patch angioplasty, the aorta is isolated, the site of coarctation is opened with proximal and distal extension of the incisions, and a patch of synthetic material or homograft is sutured into place.

This method of repair has the advantage of being technically simple, relatively quick, offers a low rate of recurrence, and provides excellent relief of the obstruction. The use of prosthetic material has been associated with late aneurysm formation. It is thought that newer materials may decrease the potential for this complication.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of coarctation of the aorta is usually uncomplicated. Invasive monitors commonly utilized include arterial and central venous catheters.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Paradoxical Hypertension & Mesenteric Protection</summary><div class="details-content">

Systemic hypertension is often encountered in the postoperative period, and usually responds to nitroprusside or intravenous beta blockers. Uncontrolled rebound hypertension may result in ileus or, more rarely, bowel necrosis.

Later in the hospital stay, intravenous anti-hypertensive medications are switched to orally administered beta blockers and/or afterload reducing agents such as captopril or enalapril.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Neurologic Complications & Hospital Stay</summary><div class="details-content">

As with patent ductus arteriosus surgery, the recurrent laryngeal branch of the vagus nerve is near the operative site and occasional hoarseness is encountered following surgery. This is usually transient and resolves spontaneously.

Paraplegia is a rare postoperative complication, and is related to spinal cord ischemia during repair. Postoperative bleeding requiring reoperation is rare. Length of hospital stay required following repair of coarctation of the aorta averages 4–7 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Interrupted Aortic Arch</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Interrupted aortic arch is characterized by interruption of the aortic arch distal to the left subclavian artery (Type A), proximal to the left subclavian artery (Type B), or between the innominate artery and the left common carotid artery (Type C).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Collateralization, Perfusion & Ductal Closure</summary><div class="details-content">

Blood supply to arterial distributions proximal to the interruption is provided by the ascending aorta. Blood supply to arterial distributions distal to the interruption is provided by a patent ductus arteriosus. Arterial oxygen saturation proximal to the interruption is normal, while the arterial oxygen saturation distal to the interruption is lower than normal.

Closure of the patent ductus arteriosus in patients with interrupted aortic arch results in lower body hypoperfusion and acidosis. Infusion of prostaglandin E1 to maintain ductal patency is an important component of resuscitation and preoperative stabilization.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Associated Defects & Surgical Urgency</summary><div class="details-content">

Ventricular septal defect is a frequent association, especially with Types B and C. Surgical correction of this anomaly is performed during the neonatal period.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-02-07"></span>![](fig-02-07.png)

**FIGURE 2-7** Interrupted Aortic Arch. Anatomic classification into Type A (interruption distal to left subclavian), Type B (interruption between left carotid and left subclavian), and Type C (interruption between innominate and left carotid), with Type B presenting with associated VSD (1–4), and complete single-stage neonatal repair via direct aortic anastomosis and VSD patch closure (bottom).

Type A interrupted aortic arch without VSD or with a small VSD is usually repaired much like coarctation of the aorta with end-to-end anastomosis ([Fig. 2-7](#fig-02-07)). As the site of interruption moves more proximally, the patient is more likely to have a non-restrictive VSD and a one stage repair of the aortic arch and intracardiac anomalies is performed.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Dual-Cannulation Bypass & Circulatory Arrest</summary><div class="details-content">

Repair of type B and C interrupted aortic arch requires cardiopulmonary bypass, with deep hypothermia and circulatory arrest. Since there is interruption of the aorta, a unique cardiopulmonary bypass technique is utilized. The arterial limb of the cardiopulmonary bypass circuit is divided into two arms. Perfusion of the proximal aorta is accomplished with one arm, and perfusion of the distal aorta is accomplished with the other arm via cannulation of the patent ductus arteriosus.

Once total body hypothermia has been attained, cardiopulmonary bypass is discontinued and the cannulae are removed.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Direct Arch Reconstruction & Concomitant Repair</summary><div class="details-content">

Direct anastomosis of the upper aortic and lower aortic segments is usually possible ([Fig. 2-7](#fig-02-07)). With recannulation of the aorta in the traditional way, cardiopulmonary bypass is resumed. Associated defects such as ventricular septal defect and atrial septal defect are now repaired.

Transesophageal echocardiography, proximal and distal aortic pressure measurements and oxygen saturation comparison are utilized to confirm adequacy of repair. Delayed sternal closure is sometimes required following this repair.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of type B or C interrupted aortic arch with ventricular septal defect can be variable. This operation represents a major undertaking for a neonate. Preoperative pulmonary overcirculation or recent resuscitation from ductal closure could contribute to hemodynamic instability in the postoperative period.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Multi-Catheter Monitoring & Vasoactive Support</summary><div class="details-content">

Invasive monitors utilized following repair include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter placed in the pulmonary artery is utilized to monitor pulmonary artery pressure and cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone, nitroprusside or phenoxybenzamine, and norepinephrine. Sedation with intravenous infusions of fentanyl and a neuromuscular blocking agent is often required to reduce pulmonary artery hypertensive episodes. Inhaled nitric oxide has proven useful in the management of pulmonary artery hypertension.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Conduction, Hemostasis & Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative bleeding is a rare complication, but may occur since the surgical repair involves suture lines exposed to systemic level pressure. Atrioventricular conduction disturbance is a potential complication since the surgical procedure involves working near the atrioventricular conduction tissue. Temporary pacing capability should be readily available.

Postoperative arterial oxygen saturation and intracardiac pressures should be normal. Length of hospital stay following repair of interrupted aortic arch is variable, but two weeks or more is average.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Vascular Ring (Double Aortic Arch)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Vascular ring is an anomaly of aortic arch development in which the trachea and esophagus are completely encircled by vascular structures. The most common types of vascular ring involve a dominant right aortic arch with a persistent left ductus arteriosus or ligamentum arteriosum.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Arch Anatomy & Tracheoesophageal Compression</summary><div class="details-content">

Double aortic arch, as the name implies, occurs when there are two aortic arches. The posterior, right aortic arch gives rise to the right common carotid artery and the right subclavian artery. The anterior, left aortic arch gives rise to the left common carotid artery and the left subclavian artery.

Double aortic arch encircles the trachea and the esophagus and can cause compression of either structure. Barium swallow study is usually diagnostic.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-02-08"></span>![](fig-02-08.png)

**FIGURE 2-8** Vascular Ring: Double Aortic Arch. Congenital vascular ring encircling the trachea and esophagus (1), surgical relief through a left thoracotomy with division and oversewing of the smaller non-dominant anterior arch, and division of the ligamentum arteriosum (2).

Repair of double aortic arch is performed through a posterior lateral thoracotomy ([Fig. 2-8](#fig-02-08)). Cardiopulmonary bypass is not required for repair of this anomaly.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Arch Division & Ligamentum Resection</summary><div class="details-content">

The aortic arch anatomy is first identified. The non-dominant aortic arch is divided and oversewn taking care to avoid compromise of blood flow to the branches arising from the aortic arches ([Fig. 2-8](#fig-02-08)).

The ligamentum arteriosus is divided in all cases. Many surgeons perform aortopexy, with anterior suspension of the left aortic arch segment to the posterior aspect of the sternum.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of double aortic arch type vascular ring is usually benign. Invasive monitors utilized include an arterial catheter and rarely, a central venous catheter.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Respiratory Dynamics & Tracheomalacia</summary><div class="details-content">

Vasoactive infusions are rarely required for postoperative hemodynamic management. Phrenic nerve or recurrent laryngeal nerve injury is rarely encountered. Bleeding is an uncommon postoperative complication.

Tracheomalacia may coexist and can cause respiratory symptoms. Length of hospital stay required following repair of double aortic arch averages 3–6 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>
