# Septal Defects and Left-to-Right Shunts

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Hemodynamic Foundations & Shunt Physiology
* **Determinants of Left-to-Right Shunting:** The magnitude of intracardiac and extracardiac shunting depends strictly on the anatomic cross-sectional area of the defect, the diastolic compliance differential between the right and left ventricles (for atrial defects), and the systolic/diastolic pressure gradient and relative vascular resistances of the systemic and pulmonary circulations (for ventricular and arterial-level communications).
* **Pulmonary Overcirculation & Remodeling:** Unrestricted left-to-right shunting causes volume and pressure overload of downstream chambers and pulmonary vascular beds, progressively leading to endothelial dysfunction, medial hypertrophy, pulmonary arterial hypertension, and eventually irreversible pulmonary vascular obstructive disease (Eisenmenger syndrome).
* **Aortic Runoff Phenomenon:** Large communications at the great arterial level (patent ductus arteriosus, aortopulmonary window) result in severe diastolic runoff from the aorta into the lower-resistance pulmonary bed, markedly widening pulse pressure, depressing diastolic perfusion pressure, and causing critical systemic organ hypoperfusion (coronary ischemia, mesenteric ischemia, and necrotizing enterocolitis in neonates).

### Clinical Syndromes & Anatomic Spectra
* **Secundum Atrial Septal Defect:** Defect within the fossa ovalis; leads to diastolic volume overload, right atrial and right ventricular dilatation, tricuspid regurgitation, late atrial flutter/fibrillation, and right heart failure if uncorrected.
* **Sinus Venosus Atrial Septal Defect:** Communication located adjacent to the cavoatrial junction near the orifice of the superior vena cava; almost universally associated with partial anomalous pulmonary venous drainage (PAPVD) of the right upper and middle lobe pulmonary veins into the SVC or high right atrium.
* **Atrioventricular Septal Defect (AV Canal):** Spectrum resulting from developmental failure of the superior and inferior endocardial cushions. Characterized by an *ostium primum* atrial septal defect, deficient inlet ventricular septum, abnormal atrioventricular junction with a common annulus, and cleft left AV valve. Strong genetic linkage to trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), who exhibit rapid progression of pulmonary vascular obstructive disease.
* **Ventricular Septal Defect:** Anatomic subdivision into four zones: perimembranous (most common, bordering the tricuspid septal leaflet and central fibrous body), inlet (beneath the AV valves), outlet/infundibular (beneath the semilunar valves), and muscular (trabecular, apical, marginal). Large non-restrictive defects present with biventricular work overload, left ventricular dilation, failure to thrive, and congestive heart failure in early infancy.
* **Patent Ductus Arteriosus:** Persistence of fetal vessel connecting the proximal descending aorta just opposite the left subclavian artery to the origin of the left pulmonary artery. Crucial contributor to morbidity in premature neonates.
* **Aortopulmonary (A-P) Window:** Direct communication between the ascending aorta and main pulmonary trunk in the presence of two separate, normally formed semilunar valves; mimics the physiology of a massive non-restrictive VSD or truncus arteriosus with early congestive failure.

### Surgical Principles & Conduction Anatomy
* **Secundum ASD Closure:** Primary suture repair for smaller defects using continuous monofilament suture; patch closure (autologous pericardium or synthetic polyester/PTFE) for large fenestrations or deficient rim margins.
* **Sinus Venosus ASD & PAPVD Baffling:** Requires an intracardiac baffle (pericardium or Dacron) sewn to channel the anomalous right pulmonary venous return through the septal defect into the left atrium while maintaining unobstructed, non-stenotic SVC blood transit into the right atrium.
* **AV Canal Reconstruction:** Closure of inlet VSD using synthetic patch (Dacron) incorporating the bridging AV valve leaflets at the predetermined division line; meticulous closure of the zone of apposition (cleft) in the left AV valve leaflet with interrupted sutures to eliminate left AV valve regurgitation; closure of the primum ASD with a second pericardial or synthetic patch.
* **Conduction System Preservation in AVSD:** The atrioventricular node is displaced postero-inferiorly toward the ostium of the coronary sinus, and the bundle of His courses along the postero-inferior margin and crest of the inlet ventricular septum. Sutures must be kept superficial and placed along the right atrial side of the coronary sinus to prevent permanent complete heart block.
* **VSD Patch Closure:** Standard right atriotomy through the tricuspid valve. Sutures placed along the postero-inferior rim of perimembranous defects must be kept superficial on the right ventricular aspect away from the crest of the septum to protect the penetrating bundle of His.
* **PDA Ligation & Division:** Left posterolateral muscle-sparing thoracotomy without cardiopulmonary bypass. The left vagus nerve and its recurrent laryngeal branch must be identified and protected as the recurrent nerve loops underneath the ductus.
* **A-P Window Transaortic Patch Repair:** Transverse or longitudinal aortotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping; synthetic or pericardial patch closure isolating the aortic and pulmonary lumens while safeguarding the left main coronary ostium and right pulmonary artery orifice.

### Postoperative Management & Critical Pitfalls
* **Pulmonary Hypertensive Crises:** High-risk in AV canal, large VSDs, and A-P windows with preoperative hyperkinetic hypertension. Managed with deep sedation, fentanyl infusion, neuromuscular blockade, hyperventilation (avoiding hypercarbia and acidosis), and selective pulmonary vasodilators (inhaled nitric oxide at 10–20 ppm).
* **Inotropic & Afterload Management:** Milrinone (phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor) for inodilatation to lower systemic and pulmonary vascular resistance without excessive myocardial oxygen expenditure; cautious titration of epinephrine for low cardiac output syndrome; sodium nitroprusside for pure systemic afterload reduction.
* **Arrhythmias & Conduction Surveillance:** High vigilance for transient or permanent complete AV block, nodal rhythms, and junctional ectopic tachycardia (JET) following AV canal or VSD repair due to mechanical traction and suture-line edema near the conduction bundle. Temporary epicardial atrioventricular pacing capability is mandatory.
* **Residual Shunt & Regurgitation Monitoring:** Routine intraoperative and early postoperative transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) to confirm complete defect closure, exclude residual shunts, evaluate AV valve competency, and rule out systemic or pulmonary venous obstruction.
* **Hospital Convalescence:** Uncomplicated secundum ASD and PDA recoveries average 2–5 days; complete AVSD, complex VSD, and A-P window repairs typically require 5–14 days depending on pulmonary recovery and inotropic weaning.

## Secundum Atrial Septal Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Atrial septal defect is a common congenital heart defect that results in left-to-right shunting of blood. The defect occurs within the region of the fossa ovalis in the center of the interatrial septum, resulting from deficiency or excessive resorption of the septum primum or incomplete development of the septum secundum.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic Consequences of Diastolic Compliance</summary><div class="details-content">

The amount of blood shunting across a secundum atrial septal defect is dependent upon the size of the defect and the relative compliances of the left and right ventricles during diastole. Because the right ventricle is normally more compliant and thin-walled than the left ventricle, blood preferentially shunts from the left atrium to the right atrium throughout the cardiac cycle, particularly during ventricular diastole.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Long-Term Cardiopulmonary Impact</summary><div class="details-content">

Left-to-right shunting of blood results in excessive blood flow to the right ventricle and pulmonary artery. Right ventricular and right atrial dilatation results. With prolonged dilatation, right ventricular dysfunction and atrial arrhythmias can occur. In addition, long-standing pulmonary overcirculation can eventually lead to pulmonary vascular obstructive disease.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-01-01"></span>![](fig-01-01.png)

**FIGURE 1-1** Secundum Atrial Septal Defect. Diagram of normal intracardiac anatomy, isolated secundum defect with left-to-right atrial shunting (1), primary closure with running suture (2), and patch repair utilizing pericardium or synthetic material (3).

Closure of secundum atrial septal defect requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping. The right atrium is opened, the defect is visualized, and it is closed primarily utilizing running monofilament suture.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Primary vs. Patch Closure Selection</summary><div class="details-content">

Especially large secundum atrial septal defects are closed with a patch of pericardium or synthetic material ([Fig. 1-1](#fig-01-01)). Aortic cross-clamp time and cardiopulmonary bypass time required to complete repair are short. Patients who undergo this operation should be extubated early in the postoperative course.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following ASD closure is usually benign. Invasive monitors utilized include arterial and central venous catheters. Intracardiac monitoring catheters and vasoactive infusions are rarely required for hemodynamic management. Length of hospital stay following surgery averages 4–6 days.

</div></details>

## Sinus Venosus Atrial Septal Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Sinus venosus atrial septal defect is a congenital cardiac anomaly in which there is an abnormal communication between the left and right atria located near the mouth of the superior vena cava. This defect is often associated with partial anomalous pulmonary venous drainage of the right upper pulmonary veins.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Shunt Characteristics and Volume Overload</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with sinus venosus atrial septal defect have left-to-right shunting of blood at the atrial level. Left-to-right atrial level shunting results in right ventricular and right atrial dilatation. With prolonged dilatation, right ventricular dysfunction and atrial arrhythmias can occur.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Protection & Timing of Surgery</summary><div class="details-content">

In addition, long-standing pulmonary overcirculation can eventually lead to pulmonary vascular obstructive disease. Repair is undertaken in early childhood in order to avoid these potential complications.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-01-02"></span>![](fig-01-02.png)

**FIGURE 1-2** Sinus Venosus Atrial Septal Defect. Anatomy demonstrating abnormal communication high in the atrial septum adjacent to the superior vena cava orifice with associated anomalous right upper pulmonary venous drainage (1), and surgical reconstruction using an intracardiac baffle/patch redirecting pulmonary venous return into the left atrium (2).

Repair of sinus venosus atrial septal defect requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping. The right atrium is opened and the defect is identified ([Fig. 1-2](#fig-01-02)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intracardiac Baffling of Anomalous Pulmonary Veins</summary><div class="details-content">

A patch of Dacron or pericardium is utilized to close the atrial septal defect, redirecting the mouth of the anomalous pulmonary vein(s) through the atrial septal defect ([Fig. 1-2](#fig-01-02)). This patch acts as a closure for the ASD and as a baffle to redirect the anomalous pulmonary venous drainage to the left atrium. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required to complete repair are usually short.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following closure of sinus venosus atrial septal defect is usually benign. Invasive monitors utilized include arterial and central venous lines.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Monitoring & Hospital Course</summary><div class="details-content">

Vasoactive infusions are rarely required for hemodynamic management. Extubation is usually accomplished early in the postoperative course. Hospital stay required following repair averages 4–6 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Atrioventricular Septal Defect (A-V Canal)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Atrioventricular canal is the result of failure of normal development of the endocardial cushion. In patients with atrioventricular canal, the lower part of the atrial septum and the upper part of the ventricular septum are deficient. Further, the atrioventricular valves fail to separate into the mitral and tricuspid valves and a common atrioventricular valve persists.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Dual-Level Shunting & Valvular Competency</summary><div class="details-content">

Patients with atrioventricular canal have left-to-right shunting of blood at the atrial level and at the ventricular level. Insufficiency of the common atrioventricular valve may occur.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Vascular Obstructive Disease & Trisomy 21</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary vascular obstructive disease secondary to the large left-to-right shunt can occur early in these patients. Surgical intervention is undertaken early in infancy in order to avoid this complication. Atrioventricular canal is often associated with trisomy twenty-one.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-01-03"></span>![](fig-01-03.png)

**FIGURE 1-3** Atrioventricular Canal (Atrioventricular Septal Defect). Normal cardiac anatomy and complete AV canal with deficient atrial and ventricular septa and a common AV valve (1), followed by surgical repair with patch closure of septal defects, division of the common valve, and closure of the left AV valve cleft (2).

Repair of atrioventricular canal requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping. The heart is opened via a right atriotomy. Interrupted sutures are placed along the crest of the ventricular septum ([Fig. 1-3](#fig-01-03)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Ventricular Patch & Valve Leaflet Division</summary><div class="details-content">

The ventricular septal defect is closed with a patch of Dacron incorporating the atrioventricular valve tissue where the surgeon determines division should occur between the tricuspid side of the valve and the mitral side. A cleft in the mitral valve leaflet is closed utilizing interrupted sutures.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Atrial Patch & Conduction Tissue Avoidance</summary><div class="details-content">

The atrial septal defect is next closed utilizing a synthetic patch secured with sutures along the junction of the ventricular septal defect patch and the division of the common atrioventricular valve. Care is taken to secure the atrial septal defect patch in such a way as to avoid deep sutures near the coronary sinus, which could result in interruption of atrioventricular conduction pathways.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intraoperative Verification</summary><div class="details-content">

Transesophageal echocardiography is utilized to help assess adequacy of repair. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required to repair this defect are usually moderate in length.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following repair of atrioventricular canal can be variable. Invasive monitors utilized include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter is useful for monitoring pulmonary artery pressure and cardiac output. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic Support & Pulmonary Hypertension Crises</summary><div class="details-content">

Vasoactive infusions might include epinephrine, milrinone and/or nitroprusside. Sedation is usually required to manage early postoperative pulmonary hypertension. Inhaled nitric oxide has become very useful for the management of pulmonary artery hypertensive episodes. These patients will require frequent assessment and careful bedside monitoring.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Conduction Disturbances & Postoperative Bleeding</summary><div class="details-content">

Besides pulmonary hypertension, another important postoperative consideration is cardiac rhythm. Since the repair of atrioventricular canal requires manipulation very close to atrioventricular conduction tissue, one must be prepared to utilize temporary pacing in the postoperative period. Heart block and junctional ectopic tachycardia are occasionally encountered.

Edema around the ventricular septal defect sutures can cause acute development of transient heart block, requiring temporary pacing. Postoperative arterial oxygen saturation should be normal. Excessive postoperative bleeding is rare. Length of hospital stay required following repair of atrioventricular canal averages 1–2 weeks.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Ventricular Septal Defect</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

The ventricular septum of the heart is divided into four regions: the inlet (the area beneath the atrioventricular valves), the outlet (the area that gives rise to the great vessels), the perimembranous septum (formed at the junction of the inlet and outlet, adjacent to the tricuspid valve) and the muscular septum (which includes the muscular continuity of the left and right ventricles).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Anatomical Subtypes & Shunt Dynamics</summary><div class="details-content">

Defects in the interventricular septum can occur in any of these four general areas. The most common type of VSD is the perimembranous VSD. Ventricular septal defects result in left-to-right shunting of blood.

The amount of left-to-right shunting of blood in patients with ventricular septal defect depends upon the size of the defect, the ventricular pressure differential and the relative resistances of the pulmonary and systemic circulations.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Volume Overload & Pulmonary Vascular Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

Isolated non-restrictive VSD results in pulmonary overcirculation, left ventricular overload and can lead to left ventricular dilatation and congestive heart failure. In addition, pulmonary vascular obstructive disease can occur.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-01-04"></span>![](fig-01-04.png)

**FIGURE 1-4** Ventricular Septal Defect. Schema of normal heart, isolated non-restrictive ventricular septal defect with left-to-right ventricular shunt (1), and patch closure through a right atriotomy avoiding conduction pathways (2).

Repair of ventricular septal defect requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping. Most ventricular septal defects are repaired via right atriotomy and are visualized through the tricuspid valve ([Fig. 1-4](#fig-01-04)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Patch Placement & Conduction Preservation</summary><div class="details-content">

A patch of autologous pericardium or synthetic material is used to close the defect. Care is taken to avoid atrioventricular conduction tissue as this can result in loss of normal atrioventricular conduction postoperatively.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evaluation of Shunt Elimination</summary><div class="details-content">

Transesophageal echocardiography is utilized to help assess adequacy of repair. Comparison of mixed venous and pulmonary arterial oxygen saturation is performed to rule out residual ventricular septal defect. Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required for repair of ventricular septal defects are usually short to moderate in length.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following isolated VSD closure is usually uncomplicated. Invasive monitors utilized include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. A pulmonary artery pressure monitoring catheter is sometimes utilized when preoperative pulmonary artery hypertension exists. Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pharmacologic & Ventilatory Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone and/or nitroprusside. When surgical intervention is delayed in patients with non-restrictive VSD, long-standing pulmonary artery hypertension can result in a more complex postoperative course. Sedation with infusions of fentanyl and a neuromuscular blocking agent is instituted to manage pulmonary artery hypertension. Inhaled nitric oxide has proven useful in the reduction of pulmonary vascular resistance and pressure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Rhythm Management & Hospital Convalescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Atrioventricular conduction abnormalities are occasionally encountered following repair of ventricular septal defects. Edema around the VSD sutures can cause acute development of transient heart block, requiring temporary pacing. Atrioventricular pacing capability should be readily available. Excessive postoperative bleeding is rare. Length of hospital stay required following repair averages 5–7 days.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Patent Ductus Arteriosus</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

The ductus arteriosus is part of the normal fetal circulatory system. This vessel connects the aorta and the pulmonary artery. Prior to birth the ductus arteriosus allows for antegrade flow from the right ventricle to the aorta. Following birth the ductus arteriosus normally closes.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Shunt Physiology & Aortic Runoff</summary><div class="details-content">

If the ductus remains open, blood will shunt from the aorta into the pulmonary artery due to the decrease in pulmonary vascular resistance. The amount of left-to-right shunting depends upon the size of the PDA and the relative resistances of the systemic and pulmonary circulations.

Left-to-right shunting of blood caused by patency of the ductus arteriosus results in increased pulmonary artery blood flow as well as left atrial and left ventricular overload. Extensive aortic runoff, with low aortic diastolic pressure secondary to a large patent ductus arteriosus can result in systemic organ hypoperfusion.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Vascular Consequences</summary><div class="details-content">

Pulmonary vascular obstructive disease may occur, sometimes as early as one year of life.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-01-05"></span>![](fig-01-05.png)

**FIGURE 1-5** Patent Ductus Arteriosus. Schematic of normal heart, persistent patent ductus arteriosus connecting the proximal descending aorta to the left pulmonary artery (1), and surgical closure via left thoracotomy with ligation and division (2).

Surgical technique may vary, but closure of isolated patent ductus arteriosus is most often performed through a left posterior thoracotomy ([Fig. 1-5](#fig-01-05)). The PDA is isolated and either ligated or divided.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recurrent Laryngeal Nerve Protection</summary><div class="details-content">

Special care must be exercised in order to avoid damage to the recurrent laryngeal branch of the vagus nerve which courses around the patent ductus. A chest tube may be placed prior to chest closure.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

The postoperative course following closure of isolated patent ductus arteriosus is usually benign. Length of hospital stay following repair may be as short as two days.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Premature Neonates & Hemodynamic Management</summary><div class="details-content">

Longer hospital stay is sometimes required in patients who present as premature infants or who are in significant congestive heart failure preoperatively. Invasive monitors and vasoactive infusions are rarely required for postoperative management.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

## Aortopulmonary Window</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Anatomy and Pathophysiology</summary><div class="details-content">

Aortopulmonary window is a rare congenital heart defect in which there is communication between the ascending aorta and the main pulmonary artery above two distinct semilunar valves. This defect may vary in size, but usually results in a large left-to-right shunt.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Shunt Magnitude & Clinical Presentation</summary><div class="details-content">

The amount of shunting that occurs in patients with aortopulmonary window depends upon the size of the aortopulmonary window and the relative vascular resistances in the systemic and pulmonary circulations. This left-to-right shunt results in increased pulmonary artery blood flow, left atrial and left ventricular volume overload and right ventricular pressure overload.

Clinical presentation is similar to that of a patient with a large ventricular septal defect or patent ductus arteriosus. Patients present with congestive heart failure and decreased cardiac output.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pulmonary Vascular Risk & Surgical Urgency</summary><div class="details-content">

If pulmonary vascular resistance is high, patients are at risk for early development of pulmonary vascular obstructive disease. Repair should be performed as early as possible once the diagnosis is made in order to avoid these complications.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Surgical Technique</summary><div class="details-content">

<span id="fig-01-06"></span>![](fig-01-06.png)

**FIGURE 1-6** Aortopulmonary (A-P) Window. Anatomy of communication between the ascending aorta and pulmonary trunk above two separate semilunar valves (1), and surgical repair using cardiopulmonary bypass with transaortic patch closure (2).

Repair of aortopulmonary window requires cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamping ([Fig. 1-6](#fig-01-06)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transaortic Patch Repair</summary><div class="details-content">

The aorta or main pulmonary artery is opened, the aortopulmonary window defect is visualized, and a patch of synthetic material or pericardium is sutured in place to close the defect ([Fig. 1-6](#fig-01-06)). Cardiopulmonary bypass time and aortic cross-clamp time required to repair this defect are short to moderate in length.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Postoperative Considerations</summary><div class="details-content">

Preoperative pulmonary overcirculation and congestive heart failure make these patients susceptible to pulmonary hypertensive episodes. Invasive monitors utilized following repair of aortopulmonary window defect include arterial, central venous and left atrial catheters. An oximetric catheter placed in the pulmonary artery is utilized to monitor pulmonary artery pressure and systemic cardiac output.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hemodynamic & Ventilatory Strategy</summary><div class="details-content">

Non-invasive monitors might include NIRS (near-infrared spectroscopy) probes to assess adequacy of regional and global perfusion and traditional pulse oximetry. Sedation is usually required, and inhaled nitric oxide may be necessary to manage pulmonary vascular hypertensive episodes. Vasoactive infusions required for hemodynamic management might include epinephrine, milrinone and/or nitroprusside.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Recovery & Hospital Stay</summary><div class="details-content">

Postoperative arterial oxygen saturation and intracardiac pressures should be normal. Excessive bleeding and atrioventricular conduction abnormalities are uncommon complications. Hospital stay following this repair averages one week to 10 days.

</div></details>
