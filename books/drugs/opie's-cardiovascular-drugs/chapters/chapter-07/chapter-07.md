# 7. Drugs Targeting Inflammation

##### Inflammatory responses encompass a series of host defense and repair mechanisms. Inflammatory processes fight off microbial and viral invaders. They aid the repair of injured tissues. Thus, the operation of inflammatory pathways proves essential to survival of the individual and of the species. Yet, these very processes that can protect from infection or injury, when unleashed inappropriately, or if the inflammatory response fails to resolve, can cause disease. The aging of the population favors the development of chronic diseases. The control of communicable diseases by sanitation, vaccination, antibiotic treatment, and other societal measures permits more individuals to survive to the point of developing chronic diseases of many organ systems. Inflammatory pathways contribute to many of the chronic conditions that currently challenge successful aging. Inflammation contributes to the pathogenesis of diseases as diverse as forms of dementia, the arthritides, chronic lung and kidney diseases, and of course cardiovascular conditions. Thus, understanding and combatting inflammation holds a key for the prevention and treatment of many chronic conditions associated with aging. Moreover, recognition has increased that oncogenesis, and the growth, invasion, and metastasis of many cancers hijack inflammatory pathways.

##### Given the critical role of inflammation in host defenses and tissue homeostasis, interventions that modulate inflammatory processes require careful targeting and modulation, lest adverse consequences outweigh potential benefits. While antiinflammatory strategies have long contributed to the management of forms of arthritis and airways disease, the advent of strategies that modulate inflammation have just begun to show promise in cardiovascular conditions. Given the delicate balance required to intervene successfully to modulate therapeutically inflammation, a background in the basics of inflammation biology provides a framework for this undertaking.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Evolutionary Foundations and Dual Immunological Arms
* **Innate Immunity:** Ancient, rapidly mobilized defense network conserved across phylogeny (e.g., starfish, limulus crab); acts within minutes to hours via germline-encoded pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) without immunological memory. Key cellular effector: mononuclear phagocytes (monocytes and macrophages).
* **Adaptive Immunity:** Evolutionarily late defense mechanism requiring antigen presentation, clonal selection, and somatic maturation ("education"). Orchestrated by T and B lymphocytes; provides fine antigen specificity and durable immunological memory.
* **T-Lymphocyte Functional Subsets:**
  * **Th1 Cells:** Driven by IFN-γ; powerfully activate macrophages, enhance cellular cytotoxicity, and promote atheroma progression.
  * **Th2 Cells:** Secrete IL-4 and IL-10; predominantly mediate humoral and allergic responses, exerting counter-regulatory dampening effects on vascular inflammation.
  * **Regulatory T Cells (Tregs):** Characterized by FoxP3 expression; secrete TGF-β and IL-10; actively suppress effector T-cell activation, quell plaque inflammation, and stimulate reparative tissue fibrosis.
* **B-Lymphocyte Dichotomy:**
  * **B1 Lymphocytes:** T-cell independent subset that constitutively secretes "natural" IgM antibodies (e.g., anti-oxidized phospholipid IgM), conferring atheroprotective effects.
  * **B2 Lymphocytes:** Classical adaptive subset producing high-affinity IgG antibodies; potentiate chronic adaptive inflammation and accelerate vascular lesion growth.
* **Reciprocal Immune Crosstalk:** Dendritic cells bridge innate and adaptive arms via MHC antigen presentation to T-cell receptors; reciprocally, Th1-derived IFN-γ triggers potent macrophage activation, while Th2/Treg cytokines suppress innate phagocyte responses.

### Cardiovascular Triggers and Upstream Inflammatory Drivers
* **Atherogenic Lipoproteins:** Unmodified, native LDL does not cause macrophage lipid overload due to strict feedback down-regulation of the classical LDL receptor; atherogenic foam cell transformation requires scavenger receptor-mediated uptake (CD36, SR-A) of modified LDL (oxidized or glycated).
* **PCSK9 Inhibition Paradigm:** Monoclonal antibodies targeting PCSK9 robustly lower LDL cholesterol and reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) without lowering circulating biomarkers of inflammation (hs-CRP), confirming that LDL particle reduction operates in parallel with, rather than through, inflammation suppression.
* **Hemodynamic and Neurohumoral Stress:** High concentrations of angiotensin II stimulate vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) to produce IL-6; however, anti-inflammatory therapies fail to reduce blood pressure, and traditional NSAIDs increase blood pressure via renal prostaglandin inhibition.
* **Failure of Antioxidant Strategies:** Comprehensive randomized trials of antioxidant vitamins (vitamins E, C, beta-carotene) and inhibitors of lipoprotein oxidation failed to demonstrate clinical benefit, disproving oxidative stress as an actionable standalone therapeutic target.
* **Infectious Hypothesis and Antibiotic Trials:** Extensive seroepidemiologic links between pathogens (*Chlamydia pneumoniae*, cytomegalovirus) and atherosclerosis prompted large clinical trials with macrolides and fluoroquinolones (WIZARD, ACES, PROVE-IT); all demonstrated neutral cardiovascular outcomes, demonstrating that pathogen-directed antibacterial therapy does not prevent atherothrombosis.
* **Gut Microbiome and TMAO:** Intestinal microbiota metabolize dietary phosphatidylcholine and L-carnitine into trimethylamine (TMA), oxidized by hepatic flavin monooxygenases to trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a circulating pro-atherogenic and prothrombotic metabolite.
* **DAMPs and Post-Infarction Remote Signaling:** Ischemic myocardial necrosis releases damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs; mitochondrial and nuclear DNA) that activate IRF3 and TLR cascades; systemic sympathetic activation (β3-adrenergic signaling) mobilizes hematopoietic stem cells from bone marrow to the spleen, multiplying monocytes that enter preexisting distant atheromas ("echo in plaque").
* **Adipose Tissue Compartments:** Visceral and ectopic perivascular adipose depots exhibit marked infiltration of proinflammatory macrophages and T cells, secreting TNF-α and IL-6 to drive systemic insulin resistance and adventitial "outside-in" vascular signaling.

### Cardiac Allograft Immunobiology and Immunosuppression
* **Allograft Rejection Spectra:**
  * **Hyperacute Rejection:** Occurs within minutes to hours; driven by preformed donor-specific anti-HLA or ABO antibodies triggering complement activation and microvascular thrombosis; largely eliminated by prospective cross-matching.
  * **Acute Cellular Rejection:** Mediated by alloreactive host CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes infiltrating graft parenchyma, leading to direct cytolysis.
  * **Chronic Rejection & CAV:** Characterized by cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV)—diffuse, concentric, accelerated fibrointimal hyperplasia of epicardial and intramyocardial coronaries driven by combined cell-mediated and antibody-mediated injury.
* **Clinical Immunosuppressive Armamentarium:**
  * **Induction Biologics:** Antithymocyte globulin (ATG; polyclonal T-cell depletion) and basiliximab (anti-CD25 / IL-2Rα monoclonal antibody) provide immediate post-transplant immunosuppression, sparing early corticosteroid and calcineurin inhibitor burdens.
  * **Calcineurin Inhibitors (Cyclosporine, Tacrolimus):** Block calcineurin-dependent NFAT dephosphorylation, shutting down IL-2 transcription. Major toxicities: nephrotoxicity, hypertension, tremor, and post-transplant diabetes (NODAT).
  * **Antimetabolites (Azathioprine, Mycophenolate Mofetil [MMF]):** Block *de novo* purine biosynthesis (MMF inhibits IMPDH); selectively suppress lymphocyte clonal expansion. Toxicities: leukopenia, pure red cell aplasia, severe gastrointestinal toxicity.
  * **mTOR Inhibitors (Sirolimus, Everolimus):** Inhibit mTORC1, blocking IL-2 downstream signaling and arresting cell cycle at G1-S phase; simultaneously suppress vascular smooth muscle proliferation, uniquely mitigating the progression of CAV.
  * **Corticosteroids:** Provide broad transrepression of NF-κB and AP-1, suppressing cytokine transcription; essential for pulse treatment of acute cellular rejection.

### Pathobiology of Myocarditis and Checkpoint Inhibitor Toxicity
* **Viral Myocarditis Biphasic Mechanism:** Phase 1 involves direct viral cytolysis (Coxsackievirus B3) and protective NK-cell infiltration; Phase 2 (peaking at 7–14 days) involves infiltration by CD3+ T cells and CD68+ macrophages targeting viral antigens and newly exposed cryptic autoantigens (cardiac myosin-derived peptides), triggering progressive autoimmune dilated cardiomyopathy.
* **Clinical Trials in Myocarditis:** The Myocarditis Treatment Trial demonstrated no benefit of empiric immunosuppression in unselected lymphocytic myocarditis; conversely, in viral genome-negative myocarditis and giant cell myocarditis, intensive immunosuppression significantly prolongs survival.
* **Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor (ICI) Myocarditis:**
  * **Mechanism:** Monoclonal antibodies targeting PD-1 (nivolumab, pembrolizumab), PD-L1 (atezolizumab), or CTLA-4 (ipilimumab) unleash high-avidity autoreactive T-cell clones against cardiac self-antigens.
  * **Clinical Severity:** Occurs in < 1% of patients but carries ~ 50% incidence of major adverse cardiac events (cardiogenic shock, refractory VT/VF, complete AV block) and 30%–50% mortality.
  * **Management Protocol:** Immediate permanent discontinuation of ICI, continuous telemetry in cardiac ICU, immediate pulse IV methylprednisolone (1000 mg/day for 3 days) followed by slow oral taper, and early escalation to abatacept (CTLA-4-Ig agonist), tofacitinib, or alemtuzumab in steroid-refractory cases.

### Clonal Hematopoiesis of Indeterminate Potential (CHIP)
* **Biological Definition:** Acquired somatic mutations in hematopoietic stem cells driving clonal expansion of mutant myeloid progeny in peripheral blood, without overt hematologic malignancy (< 1%/year progression).
* **Prevalence and Risk:** Present in > 10% of individuals older than 70 years; represents a potent, newly discovered, independent cardiovascular risk factor conferring a 1.5- to 2-fold increased risk of coronary events and heart failure.
* **Genetic Drivers:** Most common mutations involve *DNMT3A*, *TET2*, and *ASXL1*; mutant macrophages exhibit hyperactive NLRP3 inflammasome priming and exaggerated IL-1β / IL-6 secretion, accelerating atherogenesis.

### Central Hubs of Inflammatory Signaling
* **NF-κB Transcriptional Master Hub:** Nuclear factor kappa B orchestrates transcription of adhesion molecules, chemokines, and cytokine precursors; direct pharmacological inhibition remains clinically unfeasible due to critical roles in host defense and tumor surveillance (apoptosis resistance).
* **NLRP3 Inflammasome Architecture:** A tripartite intracellular multiprotein complex (NLRP3 sensor, ASC adaptor, procaspase-1) that responds to two distinct signals: Signal 1 (priming via TLR/NF-κB) and Signal 2 (activation via cholesterol crystals, uric acid, ROS, or K+ efflux).
* **Caspase-1 Enzymatic Cleavage:** Active caspase-1 processes inactive 31-kDa precursors of IL-1β and IL-18 into mature, biologically active 17-kDa cytokines; IL-1β binds IL-1R1 to establish an auto-inductive positive feedback loop.
* **Kruppel-Like Factors (KLFs) and Statin Pleiotropy:** Laminar shear stress induces KLF2, driving transcriptional repression of endothelial adhesion molecules and thrombotic factors; HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (statins) independently induce KLF2 and inhibit small G protein isoprenylation (Rho, Ras), exerting lipid-independent anti-inflammatory benefits.
* **Epigenetic Readers and BET Proteins:** Bromodomain and extraterminal domain (BET) proteins recognize acetylated histones and assemble transcriptional machinery for inflammatory genes; the BET inhibitor apabetalone was evaluated in the BETonMACE trial in diabetic post-ACS patients, yielding neutral overall MACE reduction.

### Downstream Cellular and Molecular Mediators
* **Leukocyte Adhesion Cascades:**
  * **Rolling:** Mediated by endothelial E-selectin and P-selectin binding to leukocyte carbohydrates (PSGL-1).
  * **Firm Adhesion & Arrest:** Mediated by endothelial immunoglobulin superfamily members (ICAM-1, VCAM-1) binding to leukocyte integrins (LFA-1, VLA-4).
  * **Clinical Inhibitor Trials:** Direct inhibitors of selectins and integrins failed to improve outcomes in ischemia-reperfusion injury and clinical atherothrombosis.
* **Phospholipases and Lipid Mediators:**
  * **Secretory PLA2 & Lp-PLA2:** Responsible for generating lysophosphatidylcholine and oxidized free fatty acids. Clinical trials of varespladib (sPLA2 inhibitor) and darapladib (Lp-PLA2 inhibitor in SOLID-TIMI 52 and STABILITY) showed no reduction in cardiovascular events.
  * **Eicosanoid Sweet Spot:** Low-dose aspirin (75–100 mg/day) achieves selective, irreversible inhibition of platelet COX-1 (thromboxane A2 suppression) and suppresses inflammatory degranulation without fully ablating endothelial prostacyclin (PGI2); selective COX-2 inhibitors increase cardiovascular hazard.
  * **Specialized Pro-Resolving Mediators (SPMs):** Arachidonic acid and omega-3 metabolites (resolvins, protectins, maresins) stimulate resolution of inflammation, macrophage efferocytosis, and non-phlogistic clearance of cellular debris without compromising antimicrobial host defense.

### Pivotal Targeted Anti-Inflammatory Clinical Trials
* **CANTOS (Canakinumab Anti-inflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study):**
  * **Population & Design:** 10,061 stable post-MI patients with persistent residual inflammatory risk (baseline hs-CRP ≥ 2 mg/L despite optimal statin therapy); randomized to canakinumab (anti-IL-1β mAb; 50, 150, or 300 mg subcutaneously every 3 months) vs. placebo. Median follow-up: 3.7 years.
  * **Primary Endpoint:** Canakinumab 150 mg Q3M achieved a statistically significant 15% reduction in relative risk of MACE (nonfatal MI, nonfatal stroke, or cardiovascular death; HR 0.85, p = 0.021) without altering LDL cholesterol or blood pressure.
  * **On-Treatment Responders:** Patients who achieved on-treatment hs-CRP < 2 mg/L experienced a 31% reduction in cardiovascular mortality (HR 0.69, p = 0.0004) and a 31% reduction in all-cause mortality (HR 0.69, p = 0.0001).
  * **Safety Balance:** A slight but significant increase in fatal infections and sepsis occurred (~ 0.31 vs. 0.18 events per 100 person-years), fully counterbalanced by a dramatic dose-dependent reduction in incident and fatal lung cancer (up to 77% reduction in lung cancer mortality).
  * **Heart Failure Substudy:** Canakinumab produced a dose-dependent reduction in hospitalizations for heart failure and heart failure-related composite outcomes.
* **CIRT (Cardiovascular Inflammation Reduction Trial):**
  * **Population & Design:** 4,786 patients with prior MI or multivessel CAD plus type 2 diabetes or metabolic syndrome; randomized to low-dose weekly oral methotrexate (target 15–20 mg/week) vs. placebo.
  * **Outcome:** Completely neutral; methotrexate did not reduce MACE, nor did it reduce circulating levels of IL-1β, IL-6, or hs-CRP. Unlike CANTOS, CIRT did not select patients based on elevated baseline hs-CRP, demonstrating that non-targeted anti-inflammatory therapy lacking downstream IL-1/IL-6 pathway inhibition is ineffective.
* **Anti-TNF-α Disasters in Heart Failure:** Large-scale randomized trials of infliximab (ATTACH) and etanercept (RENAISSANCE, RECOVER) demonstrated neutral or worsened clinical outcomes, including increased mortality and hospitalizations in heart failure, establishing TNF-α blockade as harmful in cardiovascular disease.

### Future Horizons: The Interleukin-6 Axis and Precision Medicine
* **The IL-1β → IL-6 → CRP Inflammatory Conduit:** Mendelian randomization studies unequivocally validate genetically determined downregulation of the IL-6 receptor pathway with reduced coronary heart disease risk, establishing causal pathogenic involvement.
* **Therapeutic Targets Along the Conduit:**
  * **Small-Molecule NLRP3 Inhibitors:** Target upstream inflammasome assembly without suppressing basal IL-1α or alternative host defense pathways.
  * **Interleukin-6 Antagonism:** Ziltivekimab (anti-IL-6 ligand mAb) markedly suppresses hs-CRP and fibrinogen in chronic kidney disease patients (RESCUE trial; phase 3 ZEUS trial ongoing); suppresses hepatic synthesis of prothrombotic substrates (fibrinogen, PAI-1).
* **Biomarker-Guided Stratification:** Translating cardiovascular immunotherapy into routine clinical practice requires precision targeting: identifying patients with "residual inflammatory risk" (hs-CRP ≥ 2 mg/L with controlled LDL-C) to maximize clinical efficacy while mitigating infection risk.

## 1. Basic Concepts of Cardiovascular Inflammation and Immunity</summary><div class="details-content">

Inflammatory responses encompass a series of host defense and repair mechanisms that have evolved across millions of years. Interventions that therapeutically modulate inflammation require an appreciation of the fundamental biological distinctions and continuous reciprocal interactions between the innate and adaptive branches of immunity.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Evolutionary Foundations: Innate vs. Adaptive Immunity</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### The Ancient Defense Network: Rapid Innate Immunity</summary><div class="details-content">

Inflammatory pathways encompass two major sets of responses that have evolved in an interdependent manner ([Fig. 7.1](#fig-07-01)). The innate immune response arose early in evolution. Marine invertebrates such as the starfish and limulus crab have innate immune responses. The innate pathways mobilize rapidly. They do not require prior exposure to an antigen, nor do they "learn" or undergo clonal selection and expansion upon reexposure to an offending agent.

Innate immunity relies on germline-encoded pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and scavenger receptors, which detect invariant structural motifs found on pathogens or stressed host tissues. In acute bacterial infections, the rapidly mobilized innate immune system responds rapidly, causing fever and a series of emergency measures that serve to defend the host and neutralize invaders.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Evolutionary Emergence of Adaptive Immunity</summary><div class="details-content">

In contrast, the adaptive immune response arose much later in evolution. Most arms of adaptive immunity require "education." Upon first exposure to an antigen, the adaptive immune system mounts a primary response. This initial response requires processing and presentation of the antigen and clonal expansion of the specific effector cells that recognize that particular molecular target.

On subsequent reencounter with the antigen, the adaptive immune response shows memory, mounting a much more rapid and intense secondary response. Adaptive immunity provides the host with exquisite molecular specificity and lasting immunological memory, conferring permanent protection against many recurrent pathogens.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Cellular Architecture and Molecular Crosstalk</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Cellular Mediators: Mononuclear Phagocytes, T and B Lymphocytes</summary><div class="details-content">

While the mononuclear phagocyte exemplifies the main cellular mediator of innate immunity, lymphocytes give rise to the adaptive immune response. T lymphocytes comprise the afferent limb of cellular immunity. Several important subclasses of T lymphocytes subserve specific functions:

* **T Helper 1 (Th1) Cells:** Characterized by secretion of a signature cytokine immune interferon or interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), promote adaptive immune responses and cellular cytotoxicity.
* **T Helper 2 (Th2) Cells:** Tend to mediate allergic responses and humoral immunity; Th2 cells secrete interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) as signature cytokines.
* **Regulatory T Cells (Tregs):** Secrete transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) and IL-10, can calm immune responses, suppress effector clones, and promote tissue repair through fibrosis.

B lymphocytes mediate humoral immunity and give rise to plasma cells that produce large quantities of antibodies. In an exception to the rule that adaptive immune responses require "education," B1 lymphocytes secrete "natural" antibodies, some of which can mitigate experimental atherosclerosis. B2 lymphocytes tend to aggravate adaptive immune responses and promote vascular inflammation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Regulatory Networks and Innate-Adaptive Reciprocal Crosstalk</summary><div class="details-content">

Although innate immunity evolved before the adaptive response, adaptive immune factors regulate innate immunity. Innate immune cells such as the dendritic cell (a relative of the mononuclear phagocytes) serve to present antigens to the T cell, initiating the adaptive immune response. IFN-γ elaborated by Th1 cells can activate macrophages strongly. Cytokines derived from Th2 cells such as IL-10 can dampen innate immune responses mediated by macrophages.

These examples provide an illustration of the complex crosstalk underway in any moment in complex organisms ([Fig. 7.1](#fig-07-01)). The inflammatory status of an individual can vary immensely and depends on an intricate balance of proinflammatory, antiinflammatory, and proresolving pathways.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Acute vs. Chronic Inflammatory Dynamics in the Cardiovascular System</summary><div class="details-content">

In acute bacterial infections, the rapidly mobilized innate immune system responds rapidly, causing fever and a series of emergency measures that serve to defend the host. Such acute inflammatory responses usually resolve promptly upon elimination of the stimulus.

On the other end of the spectrum, the chronic conditions that plague the cardiovascular system often involve much more muted, smoldering, and lower grade inflammation. The symptoms and signs that accompany acute inflammation (rubor, calor, tumor, dolor, et functio laesa) usually do not announce smoldering chronic cardiovascular inflammation. Rather, laboratory markers often provide the only manifestations of chronic low-level inflammation.

</div></details>

## 2. Triggers and Upstream Stimuli of Cardiovascular Inflammation</summary><div class="details-content">

A number of stimuli can initiate inflammatory responses in the context of cardiovascular disease ([Fig. 7.2](#fig-07-02)). These proximal triggers include metabolic derangements, hemodynamic shear forces, microbial antigens, cellular injury products, and adipokine imbalances.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Atherogenic Lipoproteins and Vascular Inflammation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Native vs. Modified Low-Density Lipoproteins and Foam Cell Formation</summary><div class="details-content">

Atherosclerosis represents the best-studied chronic inflammatory condition of the cardiovascular system. Although low-density lipoprotein (LDL) doubtless plays a pivotal permissive role in atherosclerosis, it does not appear to exert most of its proatherogenic actions primarily by instigating inflammation. While macrophages of the innate immune system accumulate lipid and become foam cells when exposed to excessive concentrations of atherogenic lipoproteins, unmodified LDL itself does not lead to lipid overload.

The LDL receptor responds exquisitely to intercellular cholesterol concentrations. Hence, loading cells with cholesterol requires uptake of cholesterol-containing lipoproteins via receptors besides the classical LDL receptor. Such scavenger receptors tend to recognize modified LDL particles that have undergone oxidation or glycation. Foam cell formation may thus depend less on native LDL than on cholesterol derived from other atherogenic lipoproteins.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Immunogenicity of Lipoproteins and PCSK9 Paradigm</summary><div class="details-content">

Although initial evidence pointed to oxidized LDL as a possible antigen triggering the adaptive immune response in the context of atherosclerosis, more recent data suggest that the T cells recognize native LDL more readily than modified LDL.

Finally, an intervention that lowers LDL by augmenting activity of the LDL receptor, inhibitors of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9), can mitigate atherosclerotic events without decreasing biomarkers of inflammation. This observation underscores the contention that LDL itself has modest proinflammatory properties at best.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Hemodynamic Stress and Oxidative Modification Pathways</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hypertension and Angiotensin II Signaling</summary><div class="details-content">

Hypertension and mediators of high blood pressure can involve both adaptive and innate immunity. High concentrations of angiotensin II, a prototypical vasoconstrictor hormone implicated in the pathogenesis of many forms of hypertension, can elicit the production of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-6 from various cell types involved in atherosclerosis, including smooth muscle cells.

Considerable experimental evidence supports the involvement of adaptive immunity in hypertension. Yet, antiinflammatory drugs tend not to improve blood pressure in hypertensive individuals. Indeed, nonsteroidal antiinflammatory agents tend to increase blood pressure modestly. These findings argue against inflammation as a major contributor to chronic forms of hypertension.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Oxidative Stress and the Failure of Antioxidant Therapies</summary><div class="details-content">

Many have invoked oxidative stress, and oxidatively modified lipoproteins in particular as instigators of both innate and adaptive immune responses in the context of atherosclerosis. Yet, all antioxidant vitamins tested, and a number of inhibitors of oxidative pathways including the production of oxidized LDL, have failed to reduce cardiovascular events in rigorously conducted clinical trials.

Once again, despite considerable preclinical evidence and the results of observational studies, oxidative stress and lipoprotein oxidation have not shown promise as therapeutic targets. These observations demote the clinical relevance of oxidative pathways as instigators of immune and inflammatory responses.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Infectious and Environmental Contributors</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Infectious Hypotheses and Clinical Antibiotic Trials</summary><div class="details-content">

A large body of experimental and observational epidemiologic literature support associations between various infectious agents and atherosclerosis. While viral myocarditis indubitably represents an example of an inflammatory trigger for cardiovascular disease, targeting infectious agents has not proven actionable in general in atherosclerosis. While bacteria and viruses can stimulate both innate and adaptive immunity, and microbial products can serve as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), rigorously conducted and appropriately sized antibiotic intervention studies have failed to show reduced cardiovascular events.

These studies have used several classes of antibiotic agents that target microorganisms implicated by experimental and seroepidemiologic studies in atherosclerosis, e.g., *Chlamydia pneumoniae*. The types of agent used in the larger randomized clinical trials include macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) and fluoroquinolones (e.g., gatifloxacin). While various viruses, notably Herpesviridae, can inhabit many human tissues including atheromata, rigorous evidence implicating viral agents as triggers to innate and adaptive immunity in usual forms of human atherosclerosis has not emerged. Experimentally, a herpes virus can cause an atherosclerotic-like disease in avian species (Marek disease), and cytomegalovirus can also enhance arterial disease in rodents.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Gut Microbiome and Atherogenic Metabolites (TMAO)</summary><div class="details-content">

Considerable recent interest has highlighted the potential of the microbiome as a contributor to cardiovascular disease. The clinical extrapolation of intriguing experimental results showing the ability of intestinal microbiota to produce metabolites that putatively potentiate atherosclerosis, such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), to humans still requires reinforcement.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Tissue Injury and Adiposity as Inflammatory Amplifiers</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs) and Remote Myocardial Signaling</summary><div class="details-content">

Tissue damage can instigate innate immune responses through the production of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) ([Fig. 7.1](#fig-07-01)). Experimental evidence substantiates the possibility that DAMPs elaborated from the infarcted myocardium can augment innate immune responses. For example, dying cardiac myocytes can release DNA that can initiate an inflammatory response through a pathway mediated by interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3).

Myocardial infarction can mobilize systemic inflammatory responses that can augment remote inflammatory responses, including in preexisting atherosclerotic lesions. These pathways involve mobilization of macrophages and their activation in response to tissue injury ([Fig. 7.3](#fig-07-03)). These examples show how ischemic damage to myocardium produced by preexisting atherosclerotic plaques can elicit and amplify immune responses. Yet, these pathways likely participate in the potentiation of inflammatory responses to preexisting disease, rather than proving pathogenic in initiation of primary atherosclerotic plaques.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Visceral and Perivascular Adipose Tissue in Systemic Inflammation</summary><div class="details-content">

A good deal of recent work has firmly established that adipose tissue can contribute to inflammatory states. In particular, ectopic fat deposition around the viscera, associated with the android fat distribution (male or "apple" pattern), associates with markers of innate immune activation such as C-reactive protein (CRP). Such ectopic depots of adipose tissue team with inflammatory cells including macrophages and T lymphocytes. Ectopic adipose tissue elaborates proinflammatory mediators such as tumor necrosis factor that can mediate insulin resistance. Perivascular adipose tissue may participate in local "outside in" inflammatory signaling that can potentiate vascular disease.

Thus, weight loss either pharmacologic or through bariatric surgery might mitigate inflammation in cardiovascular diseases. The use of pharmacologic agents in this regard has proved quite challenging due to adverse or off-target effects. For example, certain classes of weight loss drugs can produce pulmonary hypertension or valvular heart disease. The thiazolidinediones that activate peroxisome proliferation activation receptor gamma (PPAR-γ) may cause a redistribution of adipose tissue away from the visceral depot, but also can cause fluid retention and exacerbate heart failure. These limitations have frustrated pharmacologic management of adiposity and as antiinflammatory strategies in cardiovascular disease.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 3. Cardiac Transplantation Immunobiology and Allograft Vasculopathy</summary><div class="details-content">

Exposure to foreign tissues, such as transplanted organs, leads to potent activation of the immune response ([Fig. 7.4](#fig-07-04)). Such adaptive immune reactions to foreign tissues, known as the allogeneic immune response, clearly contribute to rejection of solid organ transplants. This is one arena where therapies that mitigate immune and inflammatory responses have proven of daily applicability in the practice of cardiovascular medicine.

<span id="fig-07-04"></span>![](_page_8_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7.4** Multiple mechanisms in the pathogenesis of transplantation-associated arteriosclerosis. Each of the depicted immune and nonimmune mechanisms may pertain to variable extents in individual patients. In addition to these mechanisms, the risk factors for usual atherogenesis (dyslipidemia, smoking, hypertension) may also operate in the transplant recipient, and graft denervation may contribute.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Mechanisms of Allograft Rejection</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Allogeneic Immune Response and Rejection Spectra: Hyperacute, Acute, and Chronic</summary><div class="details-content">

Allograft rejection can be hyperacute, acute, or chronic. Preformed antibodies against donor determinants mediate hyperacute rejection, a process that occurs within minutes to hours after transplantation. This form of allograft rejection has become relatively rare due to the practice of prospective cross-matching between donor tissues and recipient serum.

Acute cellular rejection, mediated primarily by T cells that recognize foreign major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, can occur weeks to months after transplantation. Both CD4+ helper T cells and CD8+ cytolytic T cells participate in acute cellular rejection. Activated CD4+ T cells secrete cytokines that recruit and activate effector cells such as macrophages, while CD8+ cytolytic T cells mediate direct cytotoxic injury to donor cardiac myocytes. Antibody-mediated rejection, involving donor-specific antibodies directed against human leukocyte antigens (HLA) or non-HLA antigens on donor endothelium, also contributes significantly to acute graft dysfunction.

Chronic rejection of cardiac allografts manifests predominantly as cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV). CAV represents an accelerated form of arteriosclerosis that affects the coronary arteries of the transplanted heart. Unlike typical coronary atherosclerosis, which tends to be focal and eccentric, CAV typically causes diffuse, concentric, and longitudinal luminal narrowing of the entire coronary arterial tree.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Pathogenesis of Cardiac Allograft Vasculopathy (CAV)</summary><div class="details-content">

Both immune and nonimmune factors participate in the pathogenesis of CAV ([Fig. 7.4](#fig-07-04)). Immune factors include cell-mediated and humoral responses directed against donor vascular endothelium, episodes of acute rejection, and ischemia-reperfusion injury sustained during organ harvest and preservation. Nonimmune factors that contribute to CAV include traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cytomegalovirus infection, and graft denervation.

Endothelial injury caused by these insults initiates a cascade of vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and extracellular matrix elaboration, culminating in progressive intimal hyperplasia and luminal obliteration.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clinical Immunosuppressive Pharmacology in Cardiac Transplantation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Induction vs. Maintenance Immunosuppressive Strategies</summary><div class="details-content">

Transplant immunosuppression can be divided into induction and maintenance therapies ([Table 7.1](#table-07-01)). Induction therapies, including antithymocyte globulin or basiliximab, may be given for a limited duration, shortly after transplant, to allow earlier reduction of steroid dosage or to delay introduction of calcineurin inhibitors, thereby mitigating their nephrotoxic effects. Maintenance immunosuppression typically involves a multidrug regimen comprising a calcineurin inhibitor (cyclosporine or tacrolimus), an antimetabolite (azathioprine or mycophenolate mofetil), and corticosteroids.

Inhibitors of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), such as sirolimus and everolimus, have also demonstrated utility in maintenance regimens, particularly due to their unique ability to inhibit vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation and retard the progression of CAV.

</div></details>

## 4. Pathobiology and Management of Myocarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

For reasons that remain unclear, self-antigens can occasionally trigger the immune response leading to myocarditis. Cardiac infiltration by inflammatory cells resulting in myocardial necrosis characterizes the myocarditides. Myocarditis can have many etiologies including viral (Coxsackievirus B, parvovirus B19, adenovirus), autoimmune diseases (systemic lupus erythematosus, sarcoidosis, giant cell myocarditis), or toxic exposures.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Viral and Autoimmune Pathogenesis of Myocardial Injury</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Biphasic Immune Dynamics in Viral Myocarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

Knowledge regarding the immunobiology of myocarditis emerged from two types of animal experiments: viral myocarditis caused by infection of mice with Coxsackievirus B3 and experimental autoimmune myocarditis caused by immunization of mice with cardiac myosin or a myocarditogenic peptide derived from cardiac myosin; and transgenic mice with cardiac expression of ovalbumin in the myocardium in mice with ovalbumin responsive T cells.

In viral myocarditis, myocardial injury results from a direct viral cytopathic effect as well as activation of the host cellular immune response. During acute infection, natural killer (NK) cells infiltrate the myocardium and play a critical role in the early host response by preventing viral replication. A second wave of infiltrating leukocytes, composed primarily of CD3+ T cells that colocalize with CD68+ macrophages, peaks 7–14 days after viral infection.

They destroy infected myocytes to promote viral clearance, and the ensuing damage exposes cryptogenic intracellular antigens, such as myosin-derived peptides, that generate an autoimmune cardiac-specific response leading to chronic inflammation, fibrosis, and progression to dilated cardiomyopathy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Autoimmune Mechanisms and Cryptic Epitope Spreading</summary><div class="details-content">

Similarly, in other forms of autoimmune myocarditis, unknown triggers, combined with specific host genetic factors, activate the innate immune response leading to myocyte damage.

This primary necrosis results in exposure of cryptic antigens that are recognized by autoimmune T-cell clones, leading to sustained activation of the adaptive immune response, chronic epitope spreading, and progressive myocardial damage.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Evidence-Based Immunosuppression and Checkpoint Inhibitor Myocarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Immunosuppressive Trials in Lymphocytic and Giant Cell Myocarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

The therapeutic approach to myocarditis has relied on immunosuppressive therapy as a way to target proinflammatory mediators of disease. The Myocarditis Treatment Trial failed to show a benefit of corticosteroids, in combination with cyclosporine or azathioprine, in patients with histologically proven lymphocytic myocarditis and left ventricular dysfunction compared to placebo. The failure of immunosuppressive therapy in this study may have resulted from suppression of the beneficial effects of the innate immune response on viral replication.

Another study evaluated the effects of corticosteroids with azathioprine in patients with histologically proven myocarditis and chronic heart failure with no evidence of myocardial viral genomes. In this study, immunosuppression resulted in a significant improvement in left ventricular function and volumes compared to placebo.

Clinically, treatment of myocarditis does not routinely use immunosuppression, save for patients with giant cell myocarditis where registry data indicate that the use of immunosuppression improved mean survival from 3 to 12.3 months. However, only 11% of patients survived without transplantation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor (ICI)-Induced Myocarditis</summary><div class="details-content">

Recently, the rising use of immune checkpoint inhibitors for the treatment of solid tumors has also given rise to T-cell–mediated myocarditis in < 1% of patients. Despite treatment with high-dose immunosuppressive therapy, almost half of these patients suffer major adverse cardiovascular events including cardiovascular death, cardiogenic shock, cardiac arrest, and hemodynamically significant complete heart block.

The clinical management of immune checkpoint inhibitor myocarditis requires heightened clinical suspicion, urgent diagnostic confirmation with cardiac biomarkers and advanced imaging, and immediate implementation of aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

## 5. Novel Upstream Inflammatory Triggers: Complement and Clonal Hematopoiesis</summary><div class="details-content">

Emerging research has identified additional upstream triggers that interface directly with cardiovascular inflammation, including humoral cascades of innate immunity and age-acquired somatic mutations in bone marrow stem cells.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Complement Cascade in Vascular Injury</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Role of Complement in Vasculitis and Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury</summary><div class="details-content">

Another arm of innate immunity, the complement system, also participates in certain vasculitides. A large body of experimental work has implicated complement activation in ischemia-reperfusion injury.

Yet, anticomplement strategies have not proven productive in the clinical management of either vasculitis or reperfusion injury in humans.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Clonal Hematopoiesis of Indeterminate Potential (CHIP)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Somatic Bone Marrow Mutations and Cardiovascular Risk</summary><div class="details-content">

A novel aspect of inflammation has emerged from studies of clonal hematopoiesis ([Fig. 7.5](#fig-07-05)). With age, humans often host clones of myeloid cells, classical participants in innate immunity, that bear mutations in genes implicated in driving acute leukemia. Over 10% of individuals over 70 will harbor such clones of mutant leukocytes.

The development of acute leukemia generally requires successive accumulation in the same clone of two or three mutations in leukemia driver genes. Thus, most individuals with clonal hematopoiesis will never develop leukemia. Yet, these individuals have a striking enrichment in cardiovascular risk. This condition is called clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential (CHIP). As in the case of monoclonal gammopathy of unknown significance (MGUS), most individuals with CHIP will never develop a hematologic malignancy.

**FIGURE 7.5** Clonal hematopoiesis: a common, powerful, and recently recognized risk factor for atherothrombotic and heart failure events. Acquired mutations in bone marrow stem cells can give rise to a clone of circulating mutant leukocytes (top panel) in peripheral blood (middle panel). Individuals who harbor these clones with somatic mutations in blood have a heightened risk of atherothrombotic and heart failure events (lower panel, left). </div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Inflammatory Mechanistic Links and Therapeutic Perspectives in CHIP</summary><div class="details-content">

Only a small subset of the forty-odd well-characterized driving mutations for leukemia cause CHIP and associate with enhanced cardiovascular risk. The most common mutations that cause CHIP associate with enhanced inflammation in mice engineered to bear CHIP mutations.

Hence, CHIP represents a potent and common but only very recently recognized cardiovascular risk factor, totally independent of classical risk factors. As pharmacologic interventions exist that can target the pathways activated by CHIP mutations, management of CHIP may prove susceptible to antiinflammatory therapies in the future.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 6. Central Hubs of Inflammatory Signaling</summary><div class="details-content">

Many of the triggers to innate immune responses delineated above activate central hubs of inflammatory signaling that represent potential therapeutic targets ([Fig. 7.2](#fig-07-02)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Nuclear Factor Kappa B (NF-κB) Signaling Architecture</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Transcriptional Amplification Cascades and Proteasome Regulation</summary><div class="details-content">

A transcription factor known as nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB), first recognized in B lymphocytes, orchestrates the expression of many proinflammatory cytokines and other inflammatory mediators. Proinflammatory cytokines themselves activate this transcriptional control system providing an amplification mechanism. PAMPs and DAMPs through TLRs can also activate NF-κB.

The details of NF-κB activation and signaling have undergone extensive analysis identifying numerous potential therapeutic targets. Inhibitors of the proteasome can augment concentrations of the inhibitor of NF-κB (IκB). While experimental studies have shown efficacy of targeting NF-κB activation in muting experimental inflammation, drugs of practical utility in the treatment of cardiovascular disease have not emerged from this well-understood pathway. Indeed, as NF-κB activation may protect cells from apoptosis, survival of malignantly transformed cells might increase under circumstances of NF-κB inactivation, causing the unwanted effect of augmenting tumor growth.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The NLRP3 Inflammasome: Master Sensor of Metabolic Danger</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Supramolecular Assembly and Caspase-1-Mediated Cytokine Maturation</summary><div class="details-content">

Another intracellular multi-molecular structure, the inflammasome, consists of subunits that can sense various kinds of danger signals including certain PAMPs and DAMPs, especially crystalline structures such as silica, monosodium urate, or cholesterol monohydrate. The danger-sensing moieties of inflammasomes ultimately activate an enzyme, caspase-1, that can process the inactive precursors of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 to their biologically active forms ([Fig. 7.6](#fig-07-06)).

While various types of inflammasomes act as hubs of inflammatory signaling, the nod-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome has received particular attention because of its role in generating the primordial proinflammatory cytokine, IL-1β.

<span id="fig-07-06"></span>![](_page_15_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7.6** The inflammasome interleukin (IL) IL-18, IL-1β, IL-6 pathway. The nod-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, an intracellular supramolecular structure senses various danger signals that ultimately activate the IL-1β convertase caspase-1. This converting enzyme processes the inactive precursors of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and IL-18 to their biologically active mature forms. IL-1 can induce its own gene expression, an auto-induction amplification loop. IL-18 or IL-1β can induce the production of copious IL-6 from various cell types, thus amplifying pro-inflammatory signaling. IL-6 signals hepatocytes to boost synthesis of the acute phase reactants C-reactive protein, fibrinogen, and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Therapeutic Inhibition of the NLRP3 Inflammasome Cascade</summary><div class="details-content">

Targeting the inflammasome has given rise to new potential antiinflammatory strategies in development currently. By inhibiting the assembly or activation of this supramolecular hub, pharmacotherapy can selectively blunt the maturation of IL-1β and IL-18 without necessarily disabling other arms of innate immunity.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Kruppel-Like Factors (KLFs) and Statin Pleiotropy</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Laminar Shear Stress, KLF2 Induction, and Endothelial Quiescence</summary><div class="details-content">

Another central hub in inflammatory signaling of relevance to cardiovascular disease, the Kruppel-like factors (KLFs) operate as transcription factors. Activation of KLF2 by laminar shear stress transcriptionally regulates a cassette of genes implicated in protection against atherosclerosis and blood clot formation and stability.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Statin-Mediated Anti-Inflammatory Signaling and Isoprenoid Pathway Inhibition</summary><div class="details-content">

Of direct relevance to cardiovascular pharmacology, the HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, statins, also induce this transcription factor. Thus, unlike the PCSK9 inhibitors, statins both lower LDL and can exert an independent antiinflammatory effect through the mediation of KLFs. Statins can also inhibit the prenylation of small G proteins involved in intracellular signaling.

Thus, statins can have "pleiotropic" antiinflammatory effects that do not depend on LDL lowering through at least two sets of molecular mechanisms. This dual action of the statins, LDL lowering and an antiinflammatory action, may account for the marked success of this class of agents in forestalling cardiovascular disease both in primary and secondary prevention.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Epigenetic Regulation of Vascular Inflammation</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Chromatin Remodeling: Histone Acetylation and Methylation</summary><div class="details-content">

Epigenetic regulation can influence the transcription of genes involved in cardiovascular disease pathogenesis, including those implicated in inflammation. Modifications of histones, proteins around which strands of DNA coil in nucleosomes, or of DNA itself mediate epigenetic control of gene transcription.

Methylation and acetylation comprise the moieties that signal such epigenetic regulation. Various enzymes participate in the "writing" and "erasing" of these covalent modifications of chromatin components. "Readers" of these epigenetic modifications provide links to components of transcriptional complexes that regulate the expression of genes.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Bromodomain and Extraterminal (BET) Inhibitors in Clinical Cardiovascular Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

In particular, one family of epigenetic "readers" has become a target of pharmacologic agents under evaluation as cardiovascular therapeutics that target in part inflammatory pathways. Bromodomain and extraterminal domain (BET) proteins bind to acetylated lysine residues in histones and promote the assembly of transcriptional complexes that enhance the expression of numerous genes involved in cardiovascular inflammation.

A recent clinical trial has evaluated the ability of an inhibitor of BET proteins to improve outcomes in diabetic patients with coronary artery disease. This study did not show a significant reduction in major adverse cardiac events in diabetic individuals post acute coronary syndrome.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 7. Downstream Cellular and Molecular Mediators</summary><div class="details-content">

The central hubs of inflammatory signaling delineated above influence the expression of a variety of mediators of inflammation and immunity implicated in cardiovascular disease ([Fig. 7.2](#fig-07-02)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Leukocyte Recruitment and Vascular Adhesion Cascades</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Selectins, Immunoglobulin Superfamily (ICAM-1, VCAM-1), and Rolling-Adhesion Dynamics</summary><div class="details-content">

A key event in most inflammatory processes and immune responses is the recruitment of leukocytes. A variety of endothelial surface molecules that mediate adhesive interactions with various classes of leukocytes participate in the recruitment of the inflammatory cells ([Fig. 7.7](#fig-07-07)).

Molecules that bear homology to lectins known as the selectins cause leukocytes to associate with the surface of activated endothelial cells that express these adhesion molecules. Selectins cause a rolling interaction of the leukocytes. Members of the immunoglobulin superfamily such as intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) or vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 (VCAM-1) mediate firmer and less transitory adhesion of leukocytes to the endothelial surface.

The expression of leukocyte adhesion molecules can regulate the type of leukocyte recruited to a site of inflammation or immune response. For example, E-selectin interacts particularly with polymorphonuclear leukocytes implicated in acute inflammatory responses. VCAM-1 interacts with monocytes, the precursor of many tissue macrophages, and T lymphocytes that participate in more chronic adaptive immune responses in tissues.

<span id="fig-07-07"></span>![](_page_18_Figure_1.jpeg)

**FIGURE 7.7** Local leukocyte and smooth muscle cell functions in the atherosclerotic plaque. Accumulation of lipoprotein particles in the intima (yellow spheres). The modification of these lipoproteins is depicted by the darker color. Modifications include oxidation and glycation. Oxidative stress, including products found in modified lipoproteins, can induce local cytokine elaboration (green spheres). The cytokines thus induced increase expression of adhesion molecules (blue stalks on endothelial surface) for leukocytes that cause their attachment and chemoattractant molecules that direct their migration into the intima. Blood monocytes, on entering the artery wall in response to chemoattractant cytokines such as monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1), encounter stimuli such as macrophage colony-stimulating factor that can augment their expression of scavenger receptors. Scavenger receptors mediate the uptake of modified lipoprotein particles and promote the development of foam cells. Macrophage foam cells are a source of mediators, such as additional cytokines and effector molecules such as hypochlorous acid, superoxide anion (O2), and matrix metalloproteinases. In this manner, the fatty streak can evolve into a fibrofatty lesion. In later stages, calcification can occur and fibrosis continues, sometimes accompanied by SMC death (including programmed cell death or apoptosis), yielding a relatively acellular fibrous capsule surrounding a lipid-rich core that also may contain dying or dead cells and their detritus. IL, Interleukin; LDL, low-density lipoprotein.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Chemokine Signatures and Directed Transendothelial Migration</summary><div class="details-content">

Once bound to the endothelium, leukocytes receive signals that direct their migration to within tissues. Protein mediators of this directed migration or chemoattraction include a series of protein mediators known as chemokines. Small molecules including certain lipid mediators may also promote the migration of leukocytes within tissues.

Subsets of the numerous chemokine families can govern the type of leukocyte recruited. The chemokines provide a kind of "zip code" for signaling accumulation of acute versus chronic cellular mediators of inflammation and immunity.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Clinical Trials of Adhesion Molecule and Chemokine Inhibitors</summary><div class="details-content">

Despite the molecular characterization of leukocyte adhesion molecules and chemoattractant cytokines and an abundance of in vitro and experimental animal data that demonstrates their function, inhibitors of these adhesive pathways have not borne fruit in terms of clinical application.

Leukocyte adhesion inhibitors have failed to improve ischemia reperfusion injury despite numerous preclinical studies suggesting efficacy. An inhibitor of the adhesion molecule P-selectin also failed to improve cardiovascular outcomes. Given the complexity and promise of potential specificity of interference in leukocyte accumulation pathways, targeting these mediators may yet prove beneficial in some circumstances.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Modified Lipids, Phospholipases, and Foam Cell Biology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Scavenger Receptor Activation and Foam Cell Cytotoxicity</summary><div class="details-content">

Constituents of oxidized lipoproteins, notably oxidized phospholipids and reactive oxygen or reactive nitrogen species themselves, have undergone intensive investigation as potential targets in the treatment of atherosclerosis.

These modified lipid species promote vascular inflammation by triggering scavenger receptors, activating inflammatory signaling cascades, and promoting the accumulation of cytotoxic lipid breakdown products in the vascular intima.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Lipoprotein-Associated Phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2) and Phospholipase Trials</summary><div class="details-content">

Various classes of phospholipases that generate proinflammatory constituents of oxidized lipoproteins have undergone investigation as therapeutic targets.

Rigorous clinical studies however have not substantiated improvement in cardiovascular outcomes with inhibitors of a soluble phospholipase (varespladib) or a lipoprotein associated phospholipase A2 (Lp-PLA2). Darapladib, an inhibitor of Lp-PLA2, did not improve outcomes in individuals with either acute or chronic atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in the SOLID and STABILITY trials.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Extracellular Matrix Remodeling and Proteinases</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Matrix Metalloproteinases (MMPs) in Arterial Ectasia and Plaque Rupture</summary><div class="details-content">

A variety of different classes of proteinases may participate in cardiovascular disease. An extensive preclinical literature supports roles for matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) in remodeling of the extracellular matrix in both arteries and the myocardium. Compensatory enlargement of arteries during atherogenesis likely involves the action of MMPs that degrade constituents of the arterial extracellular matrix such as elastin and interstitial collagen.

Penetration of medial smooth muscle cells into the intima to form an atheroma likewise requires lysis of extracellular matrix components mediated by MMPs. Arterial ectasia, giving rise to aortic aneurysms in the extreme, also involves degradation of extracellular matrix macromolecules. MMPs and various cathepsins implicated in elastin breakdown likely contribute to all of these situations that involve macrovascular remodeling. These various families of proteinases generally depend on inflammatory mediators to augment their expression and activation. Thus, these proteases are central participants in inflammatory reactions.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Cathepsins and Proteinases in Post-Infarction Ventricular Remodeling</summary><div class="details-content">

Formation of microvessels, angiogenesis, also involves proteinase-mediated remodeling of the extracellular matrix. Remodeling of the ventricular myocardium following ischemic injury also involves breakdown of extracellular matrix constituents.

A large literature implicates proteinases including the MMPs and certain cathepsins in the geometrical remodeling, repair, and healing of myocardial infarction. Despite the convincing preclinical data and experimental promise, no cardiovascular therapy that targets matrix-degrading proteinases has demonstrated efficacy in clinical trials.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Bioactive Lipid Mediators: Prostanoids, Leukotrienes, and Resolvins</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Eicosanoids, Cyclooxygenase Inhibition, and the Aspirin Paradigm</summary><div class="details-content">

Small molecule lipid mediators of inflammation, particularly the eicosanoids, have served successfully as targets for cardiovascular intervention. Most notably, aspirin, an inhibitor of cyclooxygenases, can improve cardiovascular outcomes in acute coronary syndromes and secondary prevention. Although the doses of aspirin that confer cardiovascular benefit may not quell systemic inflammation, they may act as antiinflammatory agents secondarily by inhibiting platelet activation. Platelets contain preformed proinflammatory mediators that can potentiate inflammatory processes locally upon their degranulation.

Thus, low-dose aspirin may exert some of its benefit through an antiinflammatory action secondary to its inhibition of platelet activation. The selective cyclooxygenase-2 inhibitors have not only not shown an ability to improve cardiovascular outcomes but in some cases have shown signals of cardiovascular hazard. Thus, aspirin treatment particularly in low doses appears to have achieved a "sweet spot" in inhibition of prostanoid production. Recent evidence from large-scale clinical trials, however, indicates that the bleeding risk counterbalances clinical benefit of low-dose aspirin in most primary prevention indications.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Leukotrienes and 5-Lipoxygenase Pathway Inhibition</summary><div class="details-content">

Another class of small low-molecular-weight lipids can participate in cardiovascular inflammation—the leukotrienes, enzymes such as 5-lipoxygenase, and its activator FLAP—have undergone investigation in cardiovascular clinical trials.

Despite the theoretical and experimental considerations that led to targeting of leukotrienes, inhibitors of the enzymes that govern the production of this class of mediators have not shown benefit in clinical trials.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Specialized Pro-Resolving Mediators (SPMs): Resolvins and Maresins</summary><div class="details-content">

Among the metabolites of arachidonic acid, several families of mediators of resolution of inflammation have emerged. These complex structures known as resolvins or maresins can mitigate inflammation without impairing host defenses. These classes of proresolving mediators are known as specific proresolving mediators, or SPMs. The elegant chemistry and assessment of protective functions in vivo have led to experiments that target atherosclerosis. Indeed, experimental evidence that SPMs can mitigate experimental atherosclerosis has emerged.

Whether achievable concentrations of these proresolving mediators will permit boosting of resolution of inflammatory processes in the cardiovascular system requires further study. The concept of promoting resolution without impairing host defenses or tumor surveillance or reducing defenses against infection has considerable appeal.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Therapeutic Modulation of Adaptive Immunity</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### B-Cell Targeted Therapies and Atheroprotective Natural Antibodies</summary><div class="details-content">

The participation of adaptive immunity in atherosclerosis and certain forms of myocardial disease has received considerable experimental support buttressed by human observations. These considerations have led to several attempts to modulate adaptive immunity therapeutically in the context of cardiovascular disease. Elimination of B cells with a therapeutic antibody that targets cluster of differentiation molecule 20 (CD20) can benefit certain vasculitides.

As natural antibodies secreted by B1 lymphocytes can mitigate experimental atherogenesis, a number of attempts to vaccinate with modified lipoproteins that elicit these natural antibodies are in various stages of clinical development. The concept of a vaccine for atherosclerosis has considerable attraction. While one clinical trial that evaluated a biomarker, fluorodeoxyglucose uptake, did not show benefit (GLACIER), several teams are striving to implement vaccination strategies in the clinic that could prove beneficial in mitigating atherosclerotic complications.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Low-Dose Interleukin-2 and Regulatory T-Cell Expansion</summary><div class="details-content">

Another clever and intriguing approach to manipulating adaptive immunity involves the administration of low doses of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-2 to individuals at risk for cardiovascular complications. High doses of IL-2, used in treatment of certain malignancies, can trigger a capillary leak syndrome and have proven hazardous from a cardiovascular perspective.

Yet, low doses of IL-2 can skew the adaptive immune response toward an activation of regulatory T cells that can mitigate inflammation, largely through the production of TGF-β. Pilot clinical studies evaluating this ingenious approach are currently underway.

</div></details>

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 8. Targeted Anti-Inflammatory Clinical Trials in Cardiovascular Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

Clinical investigation of targeted anti-inflammatory agents in cardiovascular disease has provided landmark insights into the pathobiology of atherothrombosis and myocardial failure.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The CANTOS Trial: Proof of Concept for Targeted Cytokine Inhibition</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Canakinumab Mechanism and Study Design</summary><div class="details-content">

One place where targeted antiinflammatory therapy has shown clinical benefit in a rigorous and adequately powered clinical trial is targeting of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-1β. The Canakinumab Antiinflammatory Thrombosis Outcomes Study (CANTOS) targeted IL-1β with a monoclonal antibody, canakinumab, that very specifically inhibits IL-1β.

The study selected individuals who were in the stable phase following an acute coronary syndrome and who showed an indication of persistent inflammation despite receiving statins and other evidence-based secondary prevention treatments, as indicated by an elevation in high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) above the approximate population median (≥ 2 mg/L). The median duration of treatment in the trial was 3.7 years.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Clinical Outcomes: MACE Reduction, hs-CRP Responders, and Mortality Benefit</summary><div class="details-content">

Administration of canakinumab 150 mg subcutaneously every 3 months yielded a statistically significant 15% reduction in relative risk of the primary composite endpoint of myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death.

In an on-treatment analysis, individuals who achieved a greater than median response to canakinumab as judged by CRP reduction (< 2 mg/L) showed an over 30% decrease in cardiovascular and total mortality ([Fig. 7.8](#fig-07-08)). Thus, CANTOS provided the first large-scale rigorous clinical trial evidence that targeting inflammation could provide a cardiovascular benefit.

<span id="fig-07-08"></span>![](_page_23_Figure_1.jpeg)

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Safety Balance: Infections vs. Cancer Mortality Reduction</summary><div class="details-content">

In CANTOS, as expected, there was a statistically significant albeit slight increase in infections, some of them fatal. While the type of infections and identification of those susceptible to infections during treatment with canakinumab should permit mitigation of this risk, this result illustrates the need to calibrate carefully antiinflammatory interventions when attempting to treat cardiovascular disease and carefully monitoring the benefit/risk ratio.

Although a slight increase in fatal infections occurred in CANTOS, a striking reduction in cancer mortality counterbalanced this risk. This observation underscores the commonality of inflammatory mechanisms shared by cancer and cardiovascular disease.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Alternate Cytokine and Targeted Interventions</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Interleukin-1 Receptor Antagonism: The MRC-ILA and Acute Coronary Syndromes</summary><div class="details-content">

Other studies targeting IL-1 and allied proinflammatory cytokines have shown evidence of benefit. The MRC IL-1 trial used anakinra, an inhibitor of the IL-1 receptor that blocks responses to both the IL-1α and β isoforms, and reduced the area under the curve of the hs-CRP response in patients undergoing acute coronary syndromes.

Anakinra administration has yielded positive signals in heart failure and experimentally as well. Other studies have targeted the proinflammatory cytokine IL-6 in patients undergoing acute coronary syndromes and shown a decrease in the area under the curve of CRP particularly in those who underwent percutaneous coronary intervention. As there are numerous proinflammatory cytokines, their exploration as therapeutic targets in cardiovascular disease merits further investigation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Interleukin-6 Receptor and Ligand Blockade</summary><div class="details-content">

Targeting interleukin-6 downstream of IL-1β has gained tremendous traction following the validation provided by CANTOS.

By suppressing both leukocyte-derived and vascular wall IL-6, direct neutralizing agents suppress downstream acute phase reactant generation while blunting prothrombotic vascular activation.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Tumor Necrosis Factor-Alpha (TNF-α) Neutralization Hazards in Heart Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

A large body of experimental literature and pilot clinical observations suggest that targeting the proinflammatory tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) could benefit individuals with heart failure. The large-scale clinical trials that evaluated this proposition did not show clinical benefit, and indeed suggested higher hazard rate of an anti-TNF strategy. Thus, TNF neutralization, which has received wide acceptance in rheumatology and gastroenterology, does not appear a promising strategy for reducing cardiovascular risk.

A prespecified secondary analysis of CANTOS did show a reduction in heart failure events and hospitalization for heart failure in patients receiving canakinumab. Thus CANTOS also provided a proof of principle of the efficacy of anticytokine therapy in heart failure.

Most therapies that have proven beneficial in treating patients with heart failure involve neurohumoral blockade. Hypotension and impaired renal function provide serious clinical limitations to ever increased neurohumoral blockade in our heart failure patients. Therefore, the concept of targeting inflammation that does not lower blood pressure or impair renal functions provides a therapy worthy of further exploration to complement the standard approaches of diuresis and blockade of the sympathetic nervous system and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system in patients with heart failure.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

## 9. Conclusions, Challenges, and Future Perspectives</summary><div class="details-content">

The plethora of proinflammatory cytokines, in the context of the success of CANTOS, suggests that selective cytokine inhibition warrants further investigation.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### Non-Targeted Anti-Inflammatory Trials: CIRT and Methotrexate</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Low-Dose Methotrexate in the Cardiovascular Inflammation Reduction Trial (CIRT)</summary><div class="details-content">

Methotrexate, an antiinflammatory intervention that has revolutionized the treatment of rheumatoid arthritis and allied disorders, has an uncertain mechanism of antiinflammatory effect and did not show benefit in a large-scale cardiovascular outcomes trial, the Cardiovascular Inflammation Reduction Trial (CIRT). This impeccably conducted trial yielded a null result despite a robust body of observational and preclinical data indicating its efficacy.

The population enrolled in CIRT did not have baseline elevation in inflammation as would be indicated by above median CRP, a distinct difference from CANTOS. Moreover, the low-dose weekly methotrexate employed in CIRT did not lower CRP or IL-6, again in contrast to the CANTOS findings.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### The Interleukin-1β to Interleukin-6 Inflammatory Axis as a Therapeutic Frontier</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Upstream Inflammasome vs. Downstream Cytokine Neutralization</summary><div class="details-content">

Taken together, the results of CANTOS and CIRT point to the IL-1β pathway as an attractive target for further attempts to target inflammation in cardiovascular disease. The inflammasome activates pro-IL-1β to its active form. It also processes the precursor of IL-18 to the active cytokine.

Some data suggest that the IL-1α isoform may also undergo activation by caspase-1, the enzyme component of the inflammasome that responds to the danger signals sensed by its other constituent proteins. IL-1β strongly induces IL-6. IL-6 signaling participates causally in cardiovascular disease as shown by recent concordant human genetic studies using Mendelian randomization approaches.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Dual Anti-Inflammatory and Antithrombotic Benefits of IL-6 Attenuation</summary><div class="details-content">

IL-6 can signal both through a classical pathway that involves activating the acute phase response in the hepatocyte through binding of the transmembrane form of the IL-6 receptor, and through trans-signaling mediated by the soluble IL-6 receptor ([Fig. 7.9](#fig-07-09)). Strategies exist for neutralizing the cytokine IL-6 and its receptor, thus providing the ability to antagonize selectively classical or trans IL-6 signaling. Thus, IL-6 stands out as a logical candidate for further investigation as a target for antiinflammatory therapy in cardiovascular disease.

As the acute-phase response boosts production of fibrinogen, the precursor of clots, and of plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), a major inhibitor of endogenous fibrinolysis, inhibiting classical signaling of IL-6, and limiting the acute phase response would not only quell inflammation but might also reduce thrombus formation and favor fibrinolysis ([Fig. 7.6](#fig-07-06), [Fig. 7.9](#fig-07-09)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Small-Molecule NLRP3 Inhibitors and Direct Cytokine Antagonists</summary><div class="details-content">

IL-18 has multiple proinflammatory properties. In addition to its classical receptor, IL-18 can signal through a sodium-chloride co-transporter. Interference with IL-18 signaling can reduce experimental atherosclerosis. Thus, targeting IL-18 also merits consideration and further exploration of antiinflammatory strategies to treat cardiovascular disease. As the NLRP3 inflammasome generates both active IL-1β and active IL-18, targeting of the inflammasome could prove beneficial in cardiovascular disease as well.

Although one might be concerned about upstream inhibition of IL-1β and IL-18 maturation, alternate pathways can also generate active forms of IL-1β. In contrast to CANTOS, which targeted the cytokine itself, inhibition of the inflammasome might theoretically interfere less with host defenses than canakinumab and avoid some of the unwanted infectious consequences of IL-1β inhibition. Several small molecule inhibitors of the inflammasome have begun clinical development. Mining the inflammasome-IL-1β, IL-18, to IL-6 to acute phase response pathways holds considerable promise for further exploration as antiinflammatory therapies in atherosclerosis ([Fig. 7.2](#fig-07-02), [Fig. 7.6](#fig-07-06), and [Fig. 7.9](#fig-07-09)).

</div></details>

### Biomarker-Guided Precision Cardiovascular Medicine</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Patient Selection via High-Sensitivity C-Reactive Protein (hs-CRP)</summary><div class="details-content">

As illustrated in CANTOS, using biomarkers for selective targeting of antiinflammatory therapies provides a scheme for targeting antiinflammatory therapies. As we strive to realize the promise of personalized medicine, the identification of subsets of individuals particularly likely to respond to a given intervention based on biomarker status could prove promising.

The development of biomarkers of inflammation and their validation could also aid with pharmaceutical development. The use of biomarkers could help select appropriate doses for clinical trials.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Target Engagement and Pharmacodynamic Monitoring</summary><div class="details-content">

Biomarker studies could also validate target engagement. Finally, the use of biomarkers and surrogate endpoints could provide early glimpses of clinical efficacy to aid the allocation of resources for large-scale clinical trials to the most promising agents at doses likely to provide benefit.

### Summary Paradigm of Cardiovascular Immunotherapy</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Balancing Host Defense, Cancer Immunosurveillance, and Atheroprotection</summary><div class="details-content">

The fundamental biology of inflammation and a wealth of experimental data have highlighted inflammation and the immune response as a potential series of therapeutic targets beyond the traditional pharmaceutical approaches to treating cardiovascular conditions.

Multiple examples described above have demonstrated that interventions that have shown promise experimentally and in small-scale clinical investigations have often proved disappointing in appropriately powered randomized clinical trials. Accordingly, we must apply what we have learned while moving forward in expanding the results of CANTOS to identify further and ever safer approaches to targeting inflammation and immunity in cardiovascular disease.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Strategic Vision for Anti-Inflammatory Therapeutics</summary><div class="details-content">

Given the wealth of the fundamental scientific underpinnings of this approach and the multitude of potential targets discussed in this chapter, it is likely that advances in therapeutics will realize the promise of targeted antiinflammatory therapies as a reality in cardiovascular therapeutics in the future.
