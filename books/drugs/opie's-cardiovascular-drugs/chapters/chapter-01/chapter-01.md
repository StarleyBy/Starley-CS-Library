# 1. Drugs for Ischemic Heart Disease

##### The contemporary management of patients with ischemic heart disease demands a sound understanding of the pathophysiologic precipitants of both angina pectoris and myocardial ischemia from which the principles of pharmacotherapy can be applied and tailored to the specific causes underlying these perturbations of myocardial oxygen supply and demand. This chapter details several broad classes of drug therapies directed at both symptom relief and ameliorating the consequences of reduced coronary blood flow and myocardial supply-demand imbalances for which specific treatments are targeted, including the traditional agents (β-blockers, nitrates, calcium channel blockers) as well as newer, non-traditional antianginal agents such as ranolazine as well as agents (ivabradine, nicorandil, and trimetazidine) that are not available for use in the US, but are in use internationally. These drugs are discussed comprehensively for both acute and chronic coronary syndromes, with particular attention to drug selection, dosing considerations, drug interactions, and common side effects that may influence treatment considerations.

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Pathophysiology of Angina and Myocardial Oxygen Balance
* **Determinants of Myocardial Oxygen Consumption ($MVO_2$):** $MVO_2$ is governed by four major mechanical and metabolic determinants: heart rate, myocardial contractility (inotropic state), left ventricular peak systolic wall stress (afterload), and left ventricular end-diastolic wall stress/volume (preload). Antianginal drugs act by reducing one or more of these determinants or by augmenting coronary perfusion to ischemic tissue.
* **Coronary Blood Flow and Perfusion Gradient:** Coronary perfusion of the left ventricular subendocardium occurs almost exclusively during diastole. When diastolic perfusion pressure falls or left ventricular end-diastolic pressure (LVEDP) rises, the transmyocardial pressure gradient declines, precipitating subendocardial ischemia. Tachycardia markedly shortens diastolic filling time, worsening supply-demand mismatch.

### β-Adrenergic Receptor Antagonists (β-Blockers)
* **Mechanism of Action & Signal Transduction:** Competitive inhibition of cardiac $\beta_1$-adrenergic receptors blocks stimulatory G protein ($G_s$) activation of adenylyl cyclase, attenuating cyclic AMP (cAMP) generation and protein kinase A (PKA) activation. This prevents voltage-gated L-type calcium channel phosphorylation, reducing cytosolic calcium influx during systole (negative inotropic, chronotropic, and dromotropic effects) while accelerating calcium reuptake into the sarcoplasmic reticulum during diastole.
* **Oxygen-Conserving Metabolic Shift:** β-blockade shifts myocardial substrate utilization from oxygen-inefficient free fatty acid oxidation toward oxygen-conserving glucose oxidation, reducing the oxygen cost of ATP production during acute and chronic ischemia.
* **Prolongation of Diastole:** By lowering heart rate, β-blockers lengthen diastolic perfusion time, significantly improving nutritive subendocardial coronary blood flow during exercise and stress.
* **Classification by Generations:** First-generation agents (propranolol, nadolol, timolol) are nonselective ($\beta_1 + \beta_2$); second-generation agents (metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol) are $\beta_1$-cardioselective at standard doses; third-generation agents possess vasodilating properties via $\alpha_1$-adrenergic blockade (carvedilol, labetalol) or endothelial nitric oxide (NO) generation via $\beta_3$-receptor stimulation (nebivolol).
* **Pharmacokinetic Differences:** Lipophilic agents (propranolol, metoprolol, carvedilol) undergo extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism (bioavailability 20–50%), readily cross the blood-brain barrier (risk of sleep disturbance, vivid dreams, depression), and require dosage reductions in hepatic failure. Hydrophilic agents (atenolol, nadolol, sotalol) are excreted unchanged by the kidneys, have minimal brain penetration, longer elimination half-lives (6–24 h), and require dose adjustments in renal impairment.
* **Ultrashort-Acting β-Blockade:** Esmolol is an intravenous $\beta_1$-selective blocker with an elimination half-life of only 9 minutes due to rapid hydrolysis by red blood cell esterases; ideal for unstable hemodynamics, perioperative ischemia, and acute supraventricular tachycardias.
* **Clinical Indications in CAD:** First-line therapy for exertional angina, unstable angina, and silent ischemia. Proven to reduce mortality, recurrent MI, and sudden cardiac death when initiated following acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and continued long-term. Also cornerstone therapy in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF: carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol).
* **Contraindications & Cautions:** Absolute contraindications include symptomatic bradycardia (<50 bpm), high-degree AV block (second- or third-degree without a pacemaker), cardiogenic shock, and overt decompensated heart failure. Severe active bronchospastic asthma is an absolute contraindication for nonselective agents. Vasospastic (variant or Prinzmetal) angina is a relative contraindication (unopposed $\alpha$-vasoconstriction can precipitate coronary spasm).
* **Withdrawal (Rebound) Phenomenon:** Chronic β-blocker exposure upregulates surface adrenoceptors. Abrupt cessation triggers a hyperadrenergic rebound state with unstable angina, myocardial infarction, ventricular tachyarrhythmias, or severe hypertension. Tapering over 10–14 days is mandatory prior to elective discontinuation.
* **Overdose Management:** Manifests as severe bradycardia, electromechanical dissociation, cardiogenic shock, and hypoglycemia. Intravenous glucagon (5–10 mg IV bolus followed by 2–5 mg/h infusion) is the first-line antidote, stimulating adenylyl cyclase directly via non-adrenergic glucagon receptors to increase cAMP. High-dose insulin euglycemia (HIE) and intravenous lipid emulsion are used in refractory cases.

### Nitrates and Nitrite Formulations
* **Cellular Mechanism of Action:** Organic nitrates are prodrugs that undergo denitration via mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH2) and other enzymes to yield nitric oxide ($NO^\bullet$). Nitric oxide stimulates soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC) to form cyclic GMP (cGMP), activating protein kinase G (PKG). PKG dephosphorylates myosin light chains and lowers cytosolic $Ca^{2+}$, causing profound smooth muscle relaxation.
* **Hemodynamic Profile:** At low-to-moderate doses, nitrates produce selective systemic venodilation, pooling blood in peripheral venous capacitance beds. This reduces venous return, ventricular end-diastolic volume, LVEDP, and ventricular wall stress (preload reduction), markedly lowering $MVO_2$. At higher doses, systemic arteriolar dilation occurs, lowering afterload.
* **Coronary Effects:** Nitrates dilate large conductance epicardial coronary arteries and coronary collaterals without causing coronary arteriolar "steal", facilitating collateral redistribution toward underperfused subendocardial zones.
* **Nitrate Formulations:** Sublingual nitroglycerin (0.3–0.6 mg) or lingual spray terminates acute anginal attacks within 1–3 minutes; isosorbide dinitrate (ISDN) and isosorbide mononitrate (ISMN) are oral agents for chronic angina prophylaxis; intravenous nitroglycerin is used in acute coronary syndromes and acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema.
* **True Nitrate Tolerance (Tachyphylaxis):** Continuous round-the-clock exposure to nitrates causes blunting of hemodynamic and antianginal efficacy within 24–48 hours, driven by ALDH2 oxidation by reactive oxygen species (superoxide, peroxynitrite) and neurohormonal pseudotolerance (renin-angiotensin-aldosterone activation). Prevention requires an asymmetric dosing schedule that incorporates a daily 10- to 12-hour nitrate-free interval.
* **Lethal Interaction with PDE-5 Inhibitors:** Concomitant administration of nitrates with phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE-5) inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil, avanafil) causes catastrophic, refractory systemic hypotension and cardiovascular collapse. Coadministration is strictly contraindicated: a minimum waiting period of 24 hours after sildenafil/vardenafil and 48 hours after tadalafil is required before any nitrate can be safely administered.

### Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)
* **Mechanisms of Action:** CCBs selectively inhibit inward calcium entry through voltage-sensitive L-type calcium channels in cardiac myocytes, specialized conduction tissues (SA and AV nodes), and vascular smooth muscle cells. This decreases intracellular calcium availability, inhibiting calmodulin-dependent myosin light chain kinase and causing vasodilation and reduced contractility.
* **Classification:** Divided into two distinct functional groups: Non-Dihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem) with prominent cardiac and nodal depressant activity (heart rate-lowering CCBs); and Dihydropyridines (DHPs: nifedipine, amlodipine, felodipine) with high vascular selectivity and minimal direct effect on cardiac conduction.
* **Verapamil:** A phenylalkylamine that binds to the intracellular mouth of the L-type channel pore. Exhibits potent negative inotropic, chronotropic, and dromotropic actions. Highly effective in terminating AV nodal re-entrant tachycardias, controlling ventricular rate in atrial fibrillation, and relieving effort or vasospastic angina. Main side effects include constipation (10–15%) and peripheral edema. Contraindicated in severe systolic heart failure, sick sinus syndrome, high-degree AV block, and Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome with atrial fibrillation.
* **Diltiazem:** A benzothiazepine with a balanced pharmacological profile between verapamil and dihydropyridines. Modulates SA and AV nodal conduction with less negative inotropy and less constipation than verapamil. Widely used for exertional angina, supraventricular arrhythmias, and hypertension.
* **Dihydropyridines (DHPs):** Short-acting nifedipine capsules provoke rapid, uncontrolled vasodilation with intense reflex sympathetic activation and tachycardia, which may worsen ischemia (contraindicated in acute CAD without β-blockade). Long-acting formulations (nifedipine GITS) and amlodipine provide smooth, 24-hour arteriolar vasodilation without reflex tachycardia. Amlodipine has an elimination half-life of 35–50 hours, allowing consistent once-daily dosing. The primary adverse effect of DHPs is dependent pretibial ankle edema resulting from precapillary arteriolar vasodilation.
* **CCB Contraindications:** DHPs are contraindicated in severe aortic stenosis and hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM) because peripheral vasodilation exacerbates dynamic left ventricular outflow tract gradients. Non-DHPs are contraindicated in advanced conduction disease and decompensated systolic heart failure.

### Non-Traditional & Novel Antianginal Agents
* **Ranolazine:** Selectively inhibits the late inward sodium current ($I_{Na,late}$) during myocardial ischemia. By attenuating intracellular sodium accumulation, ranolazine prevents calcium overload via the reverse mode of the sodium-calcium exchanger ($NCX$), thereby improving diastolic ventricular relaxation and microvascular perfusion without altering heart rate, inotropy, or blood pressure. Proven antianginal efficacy in the MARISA, CARISA, and MERLIN-TIMI 36 trials. Additionally produces significant reductions in HbA1c in patients with diabetes mellitus.
* **Ivabradine:** A selective inhibitor of hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated ($HCN$) channels carrying the pacemaker $I_f$ current in the sinoatrial node. Reduces resting and exercise heart rates without reducing ventricular inotropy, affecting blood pressure, or delaying AV conduction. Indicated in stable angina and systolic heart failure with resting heart rate $\ge 70$ bpm. Adverse effects include visual symptoms (luminous phenomena / phosphenes) in ~ 3% of patients due to retinal $I_h$ channel blockade.
* **Trimetazidine:** A metabolic cytoprotective agent that selectively inhibits mitochondrial 3-ketoacyl-CoA thiolase, partially inhibiting fatty acid oxidation (pFOX) and shifting substrate utilization toward aerobic glucose metabolism. Generates more ATP per mole of oxygen consumed, preserving cellular energetics during ischemia without hemodynamic effects. Contraindicated in Parkinson disease and motor disorders.
* **Nicorandil:** A dual-mechanism agent functioning as both an ATP-sensitive potassium channel ($K_{ATP}$) opener (inducing coronary and systemic arteriolar vasodilation) and a nitrate-like $NO$ donor (inducing venodilation and preload reduction). Mimics ischemic preconditioning; reduced major cardiovascular events in the IONA trial.

### Stepwise Combination Therapy Principles
* **Synergistic Double Therapy:** Combining a $\beta$-blocker with a long-acting dihydropyridine CCB (e.g., metoprolol or bisoprolol plus amlodipine) provides complementary antianginal efficacy: the $\beta$-blocker blunts any reflex sympathetic activation, while the CCB counteracts β-blocker-mediated coronary and peripheral vasoconstriction.
* **Prohibited Combination:** Concomitant administration of intravenous verapamil and a $\beta$-blocker is strictly contraindicated due to the severe risk of high-degree AV block, profound myocardial depression, and asystole. Oral combinations of verapamil and β-blockers require extreme caution.
* **Triple Therapy:** In patients with refractory symptoms, addition of an eccentric-dosed nitrate, ranolazine, or ivabradine (if in sinus rhythm with HR $\ge 70$ bpm) to baseline $\beta$-blocker + DHP CCB therapy provides symptomatic control without escalating hemodynamic depression.

#### Approved Clinical Indications for β-Blockers</summary><div class="details-content">

β-adrenergic receptor antagonist agents remain a therapeutic mainstay in the management of ischemic heart disease with the exception of variant angina or myocardial ischemia due to coronary vasospasm. β-blockade is still widely regarded as standard therapy in cardiology professional society guidelines for exertional angina, unstable angina, and for variable threshold angina (or mixed angina), particularly where increases in heart rate and/or blood pressure (BP) (including the rate-pressure product rise that occurs during exercise or stress) results in an increase in myocardial oxygen consumption.

β-blockers have an important role in reducing mortality when used as secondary prevention after acute myocardial infarction (MI), though outcomes data are lacking to support a beneficial role of β-blockers in ischemic heart disease patients without prior MI. And while β-blockers exert a markedly beneficial effect on outcomes in patients with heart failure, particularly in those with reduced EF, and have an important role as antiarrhythmic agents and to control the ventricular rate in chronic atrial fibrillation, as well as to adjunctively treat hypertension, the therapeutic applications of β-blockers in these other disease states will not be discussed in this chapter. Established and approved indications for β-blockers in the United States are shown in [Table 1.1](#table-01-01).

The extraordinary complexity of the β-adrenergic signaling system probably evolved millions of years ago when rapid activation was required for hunting and resisting animals, with the need for rapid inactivation during the period of rest and recovery. These mechanisms are now analyzed.

#### The β1-Adrenoceptor, Gs-Protein and Adenylyl Cyclase</summary><div class="details-content">

The $\beta_1$-adrenoceptor and signal transduction. Situated on the cardiac sarcolemma, the $\beta_1$-receptor is part of the adenylyl (= adenyl) cyclase system ([Fig. 1.1](#fig-01-01)) and is one of the group of G protein-coupled receptors. The G protein system links the receptor to adenylyl cyclase (AC) when the G protein is in the stimulatory configuration ($G_s$, also called $G_{\alpha s}$). The link is interrupted by the inhibitory form ($G_i$ or $G_{\alpha i}$), the formation of which results from muscarinic stimulation following vagal activation. When activated, AC produces cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) from adenosine triphosphate (ATP).

The intracellular second messenger of $\beta_1$-stimulation is cAMP; among its actions is the "opening" of calcium channels to increase the rate and force of myocardial contraction (the positive inotropic effect) and increased reuptake of cytosolic calcium into the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR; relaxing or lusitropic effect, see [Fig. 1.1](#fig-01-01)). In the sinus node the pacemaker current is increased (positive chronotropic effect), and the rate of conduction is accelerated (positive dromotropic effect). The effect of a given β-blocking agent depends on the way it is absorbed, the binding to plasma proteins, the generation of metabolites, and the extent to which it inhibits the $\beta$-receptor (lock-and-key fit).

<span id="fig-01-01"></span>![](_page_4_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1-1** β-Adrenergic signal transduction pathways involved in positive inotropic and lusitropic (enhanced relaxation) cardiac effects. Interaction of the agonist with the $\beta_1$-receptor activates adenylyl cyclase (AC) via the stimulatory G-protein ($G_s$). The second messenger, cyclic AMP (cAMP), activates protein kinase A (PKA) to phosphorylate (P) voltage-gated L-type calcium channels, increasing $Ca^{2+}$ entry and triggering calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) via ryanodine receptors. Lusitropic effect is mediated by phosphorylation of phospholamban (PL), accelerating calcium uptake into the SR.

#### β2- and β3-Adrenoceptor Subtypes & Vasodilatory Pathways</summary><div class="details-content">

**β**<sub>2</sub>-receptors. The β-receptors classically are divided into the $\beta_1$-receptors found in heart muscle and the $\beta_2$-receptors of bronchial and vascular smooth muscle. If the β-blocking drug selectively interacts better with the $\beta_1$-than the $\beta_2$-receptors, then such a $\beta_1$-selective blocker is less likely to interact with the $\beta_2$-receptors in the bronchial tree, thereby giving a degree of protection from the tendency of non-selective β-blockers to cause pulmonary complications.

**β**<sub>3</sub>-receptors. Endothelial $\beta_3$-receptors mediate the vasodilation induced by nitric oxide in response to the vasodilating β-blocker nebivolol (see [Fig. 1.2](#fig-01-02)). Vasodilatory β-blockers tend to decrease the cardiac output less as systemic vascular resistance falls. Vasodilatory mechanisms include $\alpha$-blockade (carvedilol), formation of nitric oxide (nebivolol and carvedilol), and intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA, as in pindolol).

**FIGURE 1-2** Vasodilatory mechanisms and hemodynamic consequences of third-generation β-blockers. Mechanisms include $\alpha_1$-adrenergic blockade (carvedilol, labetalol), endothelial nitric oxide (NO) generation via $\beta_3$-adrenoceptors (nebivolol), and intrinsic sympathomimetic activity (ISA, pindolol). Peripheral vasodilation counteracts systemic vasoconstriction, preserving cardiac output despite heart rate reduction.

#### Intracellular Calcium Handling & Lusitropic Cardiac Effects</summary><div class="details-content">

Secondary effects of β-receptor blockade. During physiologic $\beta$-adrenergic stimulation, the increased contractile activity resulting from the greater and faster rise of cytosolic calcium ([Fig. 1.3](#fig-01-03)) is coupled to increased breakdown of ATP by the myosin adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase). The increased rate of relaxation is linked to increased activity of the sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium uptake pump. Thus, the uptake of calcium is enhanced with a more rapid rate of fall of cytosolic calcium, thereby accelerating relaxation.

Increased cAMP also increases the phosphorylation of troponin-I, so that the interaction between the myosin heads and actin ends more rapidly. Therefore, the $\beta$-blocked heart not only beats more slowly by inhibition of the depolarizing currents in the sinoatrial (SA) node but has a decreased force of contraction and decreased rate of relaxation. Metabolically, $\beta$-blockade switches the heart from using oxygen-wasting fatty acids toward oxygen-conserving glucose. All these oxygen-conserving properties are of special importance in the therapy of ischemic heart disease. Inhibition of lipolysis in adipose tissue explains why gain of body mass may be a side effect of chronic $\beta$-blocker therapy.

**FIGURE 1-3** The cardiac β-adrenergic receptor-adenylyl cyclase cascade and modulatory pathways. Stimulatory $G_s$ coupling promotes cAMP generation, which is broken down by phosphodiesterase (PDE-3). Inhibitory inputs via muscarinic receptors and inhibitory G-protein ($G_i$) attenuate adenylyl cyclase activity. In advanced heart failure, $\beta_2$-adrenoceptor coupling may partially switch to $G_i$, serving a protective counter-regulatory role against adrenergic toxicity.

### 1.2 Cardiovascular & Hemodynamic Effects</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Effects on Heart Rate, Contractility and AV Conduction</summary><div class="details-content">

β-blockers were originally designed by the 1988 Nobel Laureate Sir James Black to oppose the cardiac actions of catecholamines. In emotional stress, exercise, cold, and other conditions releasing catecholamines, β-blockers attenuate increases in heart rate and contractility, thereby reducing the rate-pressure product and reducing myocardial oxygen demand ($MVO_2$). They also slow SA nodal pacemaking, prolong AV nodal conduction velocity, and increase the AV nodal effective refractory period ([Fig. 1.4](#fig-01-04)).

By slowing the resting heart rate and particularly blunting exercise-induced tachycardia, β-blockers substantially prolong the duration of diastole, which is the sole phase of the cardiac cycle during which coronary perfusion of the left ventricular subendocardium takes place. Consequently, total coronary perfusion time and subendocardial blood flow are preserved or augmented despite systemic negative inotropy.

Blockade produces negative chronotropic effects at the sinoatrial (SA) node, negative dromotropic effects at the atrioventricular (AV) node, and negative inotropic effects in ventricular myocardium, collectively decreasing myocardial oxygen consumption ($MVO_2$). Major pharmacodynamic drug interactions (calcium channel blockers, digitalis, antiarrhythmics) are shown on the right.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Coronary Hemodynamics & Subendocardial Perfusion Redistribution</summary><div class="details-content">

Effects on coronary flow and myocardial perfusion. Enhanced $\beta$-adrenergic stimulation, as in exercise, leads to $\beta$-mediated coronary vasodilation. The signaling system in vascular smooth muscle again involves the formation of cAMP, but whereas the latter agent increases cytosolic calcium in the heart, it paradoxically decreases calcium levels in vascular muscle cells (see [Fig. 1.6](#fig-01-06)). Thus, during exercise, the heart pumps faster and more forcefully while coronary flow is augmented to meet the increased demand imposed by the increment in external workload.

Conversely, while $\beta$-blockade might be expected to cause vasoconstriction by leaving unopposed $\alpha$-adrenergic vasoconstriction, in practice the dominant effect in ischemic myocardium is favorable redistribution of perfusion. Because non-ischemic beds undergo autoregulatory vasoconstriction in response to reduced metabolic demand, coronary perfusion pressure is maintained, diverting nutrient blood flow toward critically ischemic, hypoperfused subendocardial zones ([Fig. 1.5](#fig-01-05)).

<span id="fig-01-05"></span>![](_page_9_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1-5** Effects of β-blockade on the ischemic heart. Bradycardia extends diastolic filling and perfusion time, augmenting subendocardial blood flow, while reduced contractility and heart rate lower myocardial oxygen demand. In vasospastic angina, unopposed $\alpha$-adrenergic vasoconstriction may paradoxically worsen coronary spasm.

### 1.3 Pharmacokinetic Properties of β-Blockers</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Bioavailability, Half-Lives & Routes of Elimination</summary><div class="details-content">

Plasma half-lives. Esmolol, given intravenously, has the shortest of all half-lives at only 9 minutes. Esmolol may therefore be preferable in unstable angina and threatened infarction when hemodynamic changes may call for rapid withdrawal of β-blockade. Intermediate-acting agents like metoprolol and propranolol require twice- or thrice-daily dosing unless formulated as extended-release preparations, whereas nadolol has a long elimination half-life of 20 to 24 hours ([Fig. 1.7](#fig-01-07)).

Protein binding and lipophilicity. Highly lipophilic β-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol, carvedilol) readily cross the blood-brain barrier, causing central nervous system effects such as vivid dreams, insomnia, fatigue, and depression. In contrast, hydrophilic β-blockers (atenolol, nadolol) have negligible central penetration and are cleared unchanged by the kidneys ([Table 1.2](#table-01-02)).

<span id="fig-01-07"></span>![](_page_15_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1-7** Comparative pharmacokinetic routes of elimination for β-blockers. Hydrophilic agents (atenolol, nadolol, sotalol) are excreted unchanged by the kidneys and have low brain penetration. Highly lipophilic agents (propranolol, metoprolol, carvedilol) undergo extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism and cross the blood-brain barrier. Agents with balanced elimination (bisoprolol, pindolol) utilize dual hepatic and renal clearance pathways.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Hepatic Metabolism & Cytochrome P450 Drug Interactions</summary><div class="details-content">

Pharmacokinetic drug interactions and hepatic metabolism. β-blockers metabolized by the cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP2D6 (particularly metoprolol, carvedilol, and propranolol) exhibit extensive first-pass hepatic extraction with substantial interindividual variation in bioavailability. Strong CYP2D6 inhibitors such as fluoxetine, paroxetine, quinidine, and propafenone significantly elevate plasma β-blocker concentrations, precipitating severe bradycardia and hypotension.

Conversely, hepatic enzyme inducers (rifampin, phenobarbital, phenytoin, and cigarette smoking) accelerate hepatic clearance, dramatically lowering circulating concentrations and antianginal efficacy. Renal clearance of atenolol, nadolol, and sotalol depends entirely on glomerular filtration, requiring dose reduction in patients with renal failure.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 1.4 Clinical Indications in Ischemic Heart Disease</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Effort Angina, Exercise Capacity & Combination Antiischemic Regimens</summary><div class="details-content">

Symptomatic reversible myocardial ischemia (effort angina). β-blockers remain first-line therapy for chronic stable exertional angina in clinical guidelines worldwide. By reducing resting and exercise heart rates and myocardial contractility, they increase the threshold for ischemic ST-segment depression and prolong total treadmill exercise time ([Fig. 1.8](#fig-01-08)).

In patients with variable-threshold angina or mixed exertional/vasospastic ischemia, monotherapy with nonselective β-blockers may provoke coronary vasospasm via unopposed $\alpha$-adrenergic constriction. In such patients, combination therapy with calcium channel blockers or long-acting nitrates is strongly recommended ([Table 1.3](#table-01-03)). Silent ischemia is also effectively suppressed by titrated β-blocker therapy.

**FIGURE 1-8** The ischemic cascade in exertional angina. Perfusion mismatch leads sequentially to metabolic abnormalities (lactate production), diastolic dysfunction, systolic wall motion abnormalities, electrocardiographic ST-segment shifts, and finally angina pectoris, followed by delayed mechanical stunning upon reperfusion.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Acute Coronary Syndromes & Early Triage</summary><div class="details-content">

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Acute coronary syndrome is an all-inclusive term that encompasses unstable angina, non-ST elevation MI (NSTEMI), and ST-elevation MI (STEMI). Early risk stratification and triage determine the urgency of invasive versus medical therapy ([Fig. 1.9](#fig-01-09)). In ACS without persistent ST elevation, early oral β-blockade relieves ischemic chest pain, stabilizes hemodynamics, and reduces progression to transmural infarction.

However, early high-dose intravenous β-blockade in hemodynamically unstable patients is hazardous. Intravenous administration should be reserved for hypertensive emergencies or refractory tachyarrhythmias in the absence of signs of heart failure or cardiogenic shock.

<span id="fig-01-09"></span>![](_page_19_Figure_2.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1-9** Triage and management principles in non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndromes (NSTE-ACS). Risk stratification directs urgent vs early invasive strategies, antithrombotic therapies (aspirin, P2Y12 inhibitors, parenteral anticoagulation), and prompt initiation of oral β-blockade once hemodynamic stability is established.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Acute ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction & Reperfusion Era</summary><div class="details-content">

Early ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). In the modern reperfusion era (primary PCI and fibrinolysis), the routine use of immediate intravenous β-blockade has been challenged by the large-scale COMMIT/CCS-2 trial (45,852 patients). Immediate IV metoprolol followed by high-dose oral metoprolol reduced reinfarction and ventricular fibrillation, but this benefit was entirely offset by a significant increase in cardiogenic shock, especially on days 0 to 1 in patients with hemodynamic compromise, heart failure, or heart rates <60 bpm or systolic BP <100 mmHg.

Consequently, contemporary guidelines mandate that oral β-blockers be initiated within the first 24 hours only after the patient is hemodynamically stable, without signs of heart failure, low cardiac output, or risk factors for cardiogenic shock.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Post-Infarction Secondary Prevention & Lack of Stable Angina Outcome Trials</summary><div class="details-content">

Post-infarction secondary prevention. Solid clinical trial evidence from the pre-reperfusion and early reperfusion eras demonstrates that long-term oral β-blockade (metoprolol succinate, carvedilol, bisoprolol, timolol, or propranolol) reduces total mortality, sudden cardiac death, and recurrent infarction by 20% to 25% after acute MI.

In contrast, for patients with stable CAD without prior MI or left ventricular systolic dysfunction, contemporary observational registries (e.g., the REACH registry) have questioned whether β-blockers improve cardiovascular mortality, emphasizing that their primary indication in stable CAD remains antianginal symptom relief rather than mortality reduction.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Other Cardiac & Noncardiac Indications for β-Blockade</summary><div class="details-content">

Other cardiac and noncardiac indications. Beyond ischemic heart disease, β-blockers have established roles in managing supraventricular tachycardias, atrial fibrillation rate control, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM), and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).

Noncardiac indications include migraine prophylaxis (propranolol, timolol), essential tremor (propranolol), portal hypertension and prevention of bleeding from esophageal varices (propranolol, nadolol via splanchnic vasoconstriction), thyrotoxicosis symptom relief, and perioperative management in high-risk vascular surgery, subject to careful titration to avoid POISE-trial-related excess stroke and hypotension.

</div></details>

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 1.5 Classification & Specific β-Adrenergic Antagonists</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### β1-Cardioselectivity, ISA & Drug Generations</summary><div class="details-content">

β-blocker generations and selectivity. First-generation agents (propranolol, nadolol, timolol, sotalol) are nonselective, blocking both $\beta_1$ and $\beta_2$ receptors. Second-generation agents (metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol, acebutolol, esmolol) are cardioselective, predominantly antagonizing $\beta_1$ receptors at therapeutic dosages, though selectivity is dose-dependent and lost at higher doses ([Fig. 1.10](#fig-01-10)).

Third-generation agents possess vasodilatory properties through $\alpha_1$-adrenoceptor antagonism (carvedilol, labetalol) or endothelial nitric oxide release mediated by $\beta_3$-adrenoceptors (nebivolol). In chronic heart failure, excess adrenergic signaling is toxic to cardiomyocytes, and third-generation vasodilating β-blockers provide profound reverse-remodeling and survival benefits ([Fig. 1.11](#fig-01-11)).

<span id="fig-01-10"></span>

**FIGURE 1-10** Conceptual comparison of $\beta_1$ versus $\beta_2$ adrenergic selectivity. $\beta_1$-selective blockade achieves bradycardia, negative inotropy, and blood pressure reduction with significantly less bronchospasm, peripheral vasoconstriction, or metabolic disturbance compared with nonselective agents.

<span id="fig-01-11"></span>![](_page_26_Figure_3.jpeg)

**FIGURE 1-11** Downregulation and uncoupling of cardiac β-adrenergic receptors in advanced heart failure. Chronic intense adrenergic stimulation activates G-protein receptor kinases (GRK-2), causing $\beta_1$-receptor internalization and uncoupling from adenylyl cyclase, resulting in depleted intracellular cAMP levels and contractile failure.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Specific Oral β-Blockers in Clinical Practice</summary><div class="details-content">

Specific β-blockers used in clinical practice. The choice of agent depends on pharmacokinetic profile, half-life, lipophilicity, and organ clearance ([Table 1.4](#table-01-04)). Propranolol remains the prototype nonselective drug. Metoprolol is available as tartrate (immediate-release, dosed twice daily) and succinate (extended-release, once daily; proven mortality reduction in HFrEF in MERIT-HF).

Bisoprolol is highly $\beta_1$-selective with balanced 50:50 renal and hepatic clearance. Carvedilol provides combined $\beta_1$-, $\beta_2$-, and $\alpha_1$-blockade with potent antioxidant properties. Nebivolol has the highest $\beta_1$-selectivity among approved agents and enhances endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Intravenous β-Blockade in Acute Care: Esmolol & Labetalol</summary><div class="details-content">

Ultrashort-acting intravenous β-blockade: Esmolol. Esmolol is an ultrashort-acting $\beta_1$-selective antagonist metabolized by red blood cell esterases, independent of renal or hepatic function, with an elimination half-life of only 9 minutes. It is administered as an IV bolus (0.5 mg/kg over 1 minute) followed by maintenance infusion (0.05 to 0.3 mg/kg/min).

Esmolol is ideal for acute aortic dissection, perioperative hypertension, unstable tachycardia, and acute ischemia where prompt titration or rapid reversal within 10 to 20 minutes is required. Labetalol is also used intravenously (20 to 80 mg boluses or continuous infusion) in acute hypertensive urgencies and aortic emergencies.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 1.6 Adverse Effects, Concomitant Diseases & Overdose Management</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Impact of Concomitant Diseases on β-Blocker Selection</summary><div class="details-content">

Concomitant diseases and drug selection. In patients with asthma or severe reactive airway disease, nonselective β-blockers are contraindicated; highly selective $\beta_1$-blockers or vasodilating agents may be used with extreme caution if clinically indispensable. In peripheral artery disease or Raynaud phenomenon, nonselective agents may worsen vasospasm due to unopposed $\alpha$-constriction; vasodilating agents (carvedilol, nebivolol) are preferred.

In diabetes mellitus, β-blockers may mask symptoms of hypoglycemia (tachycardia, tremor, though diaphoresis is preserved) and impair glycogenolysis. Traditional β-blockers (atenolol, metoprolol) slightly increase insulin resistance and risk of new-onset diabetes, whereas carvedilol and nebivolol have neutral or favorable glycemic and lipid profiles.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Side Effects, Contraindications & Organ System Cautions</summary><div class="details-content">

Side effects, contraindications, and organ system cautions. Common side effects include bradycardia, hypotension, cold extremities, fatigue, and sleep disturbances ([Fig. 1.12](#fig-01-12)). Abrupt cessation of chronic β-blocker therapy can precipitate a severe rebound withdrawal syndrome characterized by tachycardia, severe angina, hypertensive crisis, acute MI, or sudden death due to receptor upregulation.

Absolute contraindications include severe sinus bradycardia, high-degree AV block (second- or third-degree) without pacemaker, cardiogenic shock, decompensated acute heart failure, and severe active bronchial asthma ([Table 1.5](#table-01-05)).

#### Management of β-Blocker Overdose & Glucagon Rescue</summary><div class="details-content">

Overdose of β-blockers and glucagon rescue. Massive β-blocker overdose causes refractory bradycardia, severe hypotension, low cardiac output, conduction delays, and cardiogenic shock. Lipophilic agents like propranolol also cause seizures, coma, and respiratory depression.

Therapy involves IV atropine (0.5 to 1 mg) for bradycardia, followed by intravenous glucagon as the antidote of choice (5 to 10 mg IV bolus over 1 minute, followed by continuous infusion of 1 to 5 mg/h). Glucagon stimulates adenylyl cyclase via a non-adrenergic pathway bypassing $\beta$-receptors, restoring intracellular cAMP and myocardial contractility. High-dose insulin euglycemia therapy and intravenous lipid emulsion are valuable adjuncts in refractory cases.

Their therapeutic index is optimized by individualized drug selection, gradual uptitration to target resting heart rates of 55 to 60 beats per minute, avoidance of abrupt cessation, and vigilant screening for contraindications ([Table 1.6](#table-01-06)).

## 2. Nitrates and Nitrite Drugs</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 2.1 Mechanisms of Action & Vascular Physiology</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Coronary and Peripheral Vasodilation, Venodilation & Myocardial Oxygen Balance</summary><div class="details-content">

This section focuses on the antianginal and hemodynamic actions of organic nitrates and nitrites. Organic nitrates are prodrugs that serve as exogenous sources of nitric oxide (NO), mimicking the biological actions of endogenous endothelium-derived relaxing factor (EDRF). Their primary hemodynamic effect is systemic venodilation, which increases venous capacitance, reduces systemic venous return to the heart, and markedly decreases left ventricular end-diastolic volume and filling pressure (preload).

This reduction in ventricular chamber dimensions and wall tension directly decreases myocardial oxygen demand ($MVO_2$) according to the law of Laplace. In addition, nitrates dilate epicardial coronary conductance arteries, relieve dynamic coronary vasospasm, and promote collateral blood flow toward ischemic subendocardial territories without causing coronary steal ([Fig. 1.13](#fig-01-13), [Fig. 1.14](#fig-01-14)).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Cellular NO-cGMP Pathway, ALDH-2 Bioactivation & Peroxynitrite</summary><div class="details-content">

Cellular NO-cGMP cascade and mitochondrial ALDH-2 bioactivation. At the cellular level, nitroglycerin (glyceryl trinitrate, GTN) undergoes enzymatic bioactivation in vascular smooth muscle cells primarily by mitochondrial aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH-2), whereas higher-potency nitrates or high concentrations may utilize microsomal enzymes. Bioactivation releases nitric oxide or an S-nitrosothiol intermediate, which activates soluble guanylyl cyclase (sGC) ([Fig. 1.15](#fig-01-15)).

Activated sGC catalyzes the synthesis of cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) from GTP. Elevated cGMP activates protein kinase G (PKG), which phosphorylates several target proteins, leading to dephosphorylation of myosin light chains, inhibition of calcium influx via L-type channels, stimulation of SERCA calcium reuptake, and activation of large-conductance calcium-activated potassium channels. The resulting decrease in cytosolic free calcium produces smooth muscle relaxation ([Fig. 1.16](#fig-01-16)). Sustained nitrate exposure leads to reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation, peroxynitrite generation, and ALDH-2 inactivation, precipitating clinical tolerance.

#### Short-Acting Nitrates for Acute Anginal Attacks & Prophylaxis</summary><div class="details-content">

Short-acting nitrates for acute exertional angina and prophylaxis. Sublingual nitroglycerin (0.3 to 0.6 mg tablets or 0.4 mg per metered spray) remains the cornerstone therapy for prompt termination of acute anginal attacks and for situational prophylaxis taken 5 to 10 minutes prior to activities known to provoke angina ([Table 1.7](#table-01-07)).

Because sublingual nitroglycerin bypasses first-pass hepatic metabolism, therapeutic plasma levels are achieved within 1 to 2 minutes, with peak hemodynamic action at 3 to 5 minutes and duration of effect lasting 20 to 30 minutes. Patients must be instructed to sit down before administration to prevent orthostatic syncope. If chest pain does not subside after one dose, the dose may be repeated every 5 minutes up to a maximum of 3 doses; persistent discomfort after 10 to 15 minutes mandates immediate emergency medical evaluation for suspected acute coronary syndrome.

#### Long-Acting Nitrates & Interval Therapy to Avoid Tolerance</summary><div class="details-content">

Long-acting nitrates and interval therapy to prevent tolerance. Long-acting oral formulations include isosorbide dinitrate (ISDN) and isosorbide mononitrate (ISMN). ISDN undergoes extensive hepatic metabolism to active metabolites (IS-5-MN and IS-2-MN) with an elimination half-life of ~ 5 hours. In contrast, ISMN exhibits 100% oral bioavailability and is not subject to first-pass hepatic clearance, resulting in predictable plasma levels and a half-life of 5 hours.

Continuous around-the-clock administration of oral or transdermal nitrates leads to rapid loss of antianginal efficacy (complete nitrate tolerance within 24 to 48 hours). To maintain therapeutic responsiveness, an eccentric asymmetric dosing schedule providing a daily nitrate-free or low-nitrate interval of 10 to 14 hours is mandatory ([Table 1.8](#table-01-08)). For immediate-release ISMN, 20 mg is taken twice daily at 8:00 AM and 3:00 PM; for extended-release ISMN, 30 to 120 mg is taken once daily in the morning.

#### Intravenous Nitroglycerin in Acute Coronary Syndromes & Heart Failure</summary><div class="details-content">

Nitrates in acute coronary syndromes and acute heart failure. In unstable angina, NSTEMI, and STEMI, intravenous nitroglycerin is widely utilized for acute relief of ongoing ischemic discomfort, management of associated severe hypertension, and reduction of pulmonary congestion in acute heart failure. Large randomized clinical trials (ISIS-4, GISSI-3) demonstrated that routine long-term nitrate therapy does not reduce all-cause mortality in acute MI; therefore, therapy is targeted to symptom control and hemodynamic optimization.

Intravenous nitroglycerin is initiated at 5 µg/min using non-adsorptive polyethylene infusion tubing, titrating upward by 5 to 10 µg/min every 3 to 5 minutes until ischemic pain resolves or mean arterial pressure decreases by 10% in normotensive patients (or 25% in hypertensive patients), maintaining systolic BP >90 mmHg and heart rate between 50 and 100 bpm. High infusion rates (>100 to 200 µg/min) rapidly induce tolerance and should be avoided.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Combined Nitrate-Hydralazine Therapy in HFrEF & Special Populations</summary><div class="details-content">

Combined nitrate-hydralazine therapy in HFrEF and special populations. The fixed-dose combination of isosorbide dinitrate (20 to 40 mg three times daily) plus hydralazine (37.5 to 75 mg three times daily) provides synergistic venodilation and arterial afterload reduction.

Hydralazine additionally acts as a powerful vascular antioxidant, inhibiting membrane-bound NADPH oxidase and preventing superoxide-mediated oxidation of nitric oxide, thereby preserving nitrate sensitivity. In the landmark A-HeFT trial, this combination demonstrated a 43% reduction in mortality, a 33% reduction in heart failure hospitalizations, and significant improvement in quality of life in self-identified African American patients with NYHA class III-IV HFrEF on standard neurohormonal blockade.

</div></details>

### 2.3 Side Effects, Tolerance & Drug Interactions</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Adverse Effects, Orthostatic Hypotension & Contraindications</summary><div class="details-content">

Nitrate precautions, adverse effects, and contraindications. Throbbing headache resulting from cerebral meningeal vasodilation is the most common adverse effect (up to 50% of patients), which typically attenuates after 1 to 2 weeks of continued therapy and responds to acetaminophen. Postural hypotension, dizziness, and reflex tachycardia occur secondary to excessive venodilation and arterial unloading ([Table 1.9](#table-01-09)).

Nitrates are strictly contraindicated in right ventricular infarction because right ventricular preload is critical to maintaining cardiac output; venodilation in this setting can cause catastrophic hypotension and shock. Other contraindications include severe aortic stenosis, hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (where cavity obliteration worsens), severe baseline hypotension (systolic BP <90 mmHg), marked bradycardia (<50 bpm), and elevated intracranial pressure.

#### Life-Threatening Interaction with PDE-5 Inhibitors</summary><div class="details-content">

Lethal interaction with phosphodiesterase type 5 (PDE-5) inhibitors. Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil) prevent the breakdown of cGMP. Because nitrates stimulate cGMP production via soluble guanylyl cyclase, concurrent administration causes massive, uncontrolled accumulation of intracellular cGMP, precipitating profound vasodilation, refractory hypotension, coronary hypoperfusion, myocardial infarction, and cardiovascular collapse ([Fig. 1.17](#fig-01-17)).

Co-administration of nitrates and PDE-5 inhibitors is absolutely contraindicated. Nitrates must not be administered within 24 hours of receiving sildenafil or vardenafil, or within 48 hours of receiving tadalafil (due to its prolonged elimination half-life of 17.5 hours).

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Nitrate Tolerance Mechanisms, Pseudotolerance & Nitrate Rebound</summary><div class="details-content">

Mechanisms of true nitrate tolerance, pseudotolerance, and nitrate rebound. True vascular tolerance develops within 24 to 48 hours of continuous, unremitting nitrate administration. Mechanisms include mitochondrial oxidative stress with superoxide generation, endothelial dysfunction, uncoupling of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), and oxidation/inactivation of ALDH-2, resulting in impaired bioactivation of nitrates to NO.

Pseudotolerance refers to counter-regulatory neurohormonal activation, characterized by secondary increases in renin-angiotensin-aldosterone axis activity, sympathetic tone, and plasma volume expansion with renal sodium retention. Nitrate rebound describes the abrupt recurrence or worsening of angina and ischemic episodes during the nitrate-free interval, especially in the early morning hours, which can be blunted by background β-blockade or calcium channel blocker therapy.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Resistance to Endogenous Nitric Oxide</summary><div class="details-content">

Endogenous nitric oxide resistance and endothelial dysfunction. Certain clinical populations exhibit blunted responsiveness to both endogenous and exogenous nitrates (NO resistance). This phenomenon is common in patients with advanced atherosclerosis, diabetes mellitus, hypercholesterolemia, and cigarette smoking, where vascular superoxide production ($O_2^{\bullet-}$ ) rapidly scavenges NO to form toxic peroxynitrite ($ONOO^-$ ), destroying the vasodilator capacity of nitric oxide.

</div></details>

#### Combination Antianginal Strategies & Step-Care Algorithm</summary><div class="details-content">

Combination antianginal therapy and step-care algorithm. Optimal medical therapy of chronic stable angina requires rational combination regimens. Combining β-blockers with long-acting nitrates produces complementary hemodynamic benefits: β-blockers blunt the reflex tachycardia and inotropic surge provoked by nitrates, while nitrates counteract the increase in left ventricular end-diastolic volume caused by β-blocker-mediated bradycardia ([Table 1.10](#table-01-10)).

A structured step-care algorithm begins with short-acting nitroglycerin as needed plus a standard β-blocker (Step 1), uptitrating the β-blocker to target resting heart rate. If angina persists, a dihydropyridine CCB is added (Step 2). For refractory symptoms, a long-acting nitrate with eccentric dosing or ranolazine is incorporated (Step 3).

## Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs)</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 3.1 Cellular Mechanisms, Molecular Anatomy & Classification</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### L-Type vs T-Type Calcium Channels & Cellular Comparison with β-Blockade</summary><div class="details-content">

Calcium channel blockers (CCBs, also termed calcium antagonists) act primarily by inhibiting voltage-gated inward calcium influx across the sarcolemma of cardiac myocytes and vascular smooth muscle cells. The dominant target is the high-voltage-activated L-type ("long-lasting") calcium channel ($Ca_v1.2$), which governs myocardial contractility, vascular tone, and sinoatrial (SA) and atrioventricular (AV) nodal action potential generation ([Fig. 1.18](#fig-01-18)). T-type ("transient") channels ($Ca_v3.1, Ca_v3.2$) activate at more negative voltages and contribute to pacemaker depolarization and renal microvascular regulation.

At the cellular level, both β-blockers and CCBs ultimately decrease cytosolic free calcium, but they do so through fundamentally divergent molecular mechanisms ([Fig. 1.19](#fig-01-19)). β-blockers inhibit the sympathetic-adenylyl cyclase cascade, thereby preventing protein kinase A-mediated phosphorylation of L-type calcium channels. In contrast, CCBs directly bind to and plug the pore-forming $\alpha_1$ subunit of the L-type channel from within the lipid bilayer, independently of adrenergic tone.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Subunit α1 Anatomy, Molecular Binding Sites & Tissue Specificity</summary><div class="details-content">

Subunit $\alpha_1$ anatomy and molecular binding sites. The pore-forming $\alpha_1$ subunit of the L-type calcium channel contains distinct, allosterically interacting receptor sites for the three major chemical classes of CCBs ([Fig. 1.20](#fig-01-20), [Fig. 1.21](#fig-01-21)). Dihydropyridines (DHPs, e.g., nifedipine, amlodipine) bind to the transmembrane segments of domains III and IV; phenylalkylamines (verapamil) bind to the cytoplasmic intracellular mouth of the pore; and benzothiazepines (diltiazem) interact with an adjacent extracellular domain ([Table 1.11](#table-01-11)).

Tissue specificity dictates clinical behavior. Dihydropyridines exhibit profound vascular selectivity, producing potent arteriolar vasodilation with negligible direct negative chronotropic or inotropic effects at therapeutic concentrations. In contrast, non-dihydropyridines (verapamil and diltiazem) possess equipotent cardiac and vascular activity, exerting prominent negative chronotropic, dromotropic, and inotropic actions ([Table 1.12](#table-01-12)).

### 3.2 Non-Dihydropyridines: Verapamil & Diltiazem</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Verapamil: Electrophysiology, Nodal Conduction & Antianginal Action</summary><div class="details-content">

Verapamil: Electrophysiology, nodal conduction and antianginal action. Verapamil exhibits marked frequency-dependent ("use-dependent") block, binding with greatest affinity to open and inactivated calcium channels during rapid depolarization. Consequently, verapamil slows sinoatrial nodal pacemaking (antagonizing the $I_f$ current and calcium clock) and markedly prolongs AV nodal conduction time and refractory period ([Fig. 1.22](#fig-01-22), [Fig. 1.23](#fig-01-23), [Fig. 1.24](#fig-01-24)).

In chronic stable angina, verapamil reduces myocardial oxygen consumption ($MVO_2$) by decreasing heart rate, blood pressure, and left ventricular contractility, while dilating epicardial coronary arteries. In clinical trials, verapamil improves exercise tolerance, delays the onset of angina, and suppresses silent ischemia with efficacy comparable to atenolol or propranolol. Standard oral maintenance doses range from 180 to 480 mg daily, administered as extended-release tablets once or twice daily.

#### Verapamil: Side Effects, Contraindications & Drug Interactions</summary><div class="details-content">

Verapamil: Adverse effects, contraindications, and drug interactions. The most characteristic non-cardiovascular side effect of verapamil is severe, dose-dependent constipation (occurring in up to 10% to 15% of elderly patients), caused by inhibition of calcium-dependent colonic peristalsis ([Table 1.13](#table-01-13)). Other side effects include bradycardia, high-degree AV block, hypotension, and ankle edema.

Verapamil is absolutely contraindicated in patients with second- or third-degree AV block, severe sinus node dysfunction (sick sinus syndrome), severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction ($LVEF <35\%$), and in patients with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome who develop atrial fibrillation, because blocking the AV node accelerates conduction down the accessory pathway, precipitating ventricular fibrillation ([Fig. 1.25](#fig-01-25), [Fig. 1.26](#fig-01-26), [Table 1.14](#table-01-14)). Furthermore, verapamil is a potent inhibitor of both CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein, dramatically elevating blood levels of digoxin, simvastatin, and cyclosporine.

#### Diltiazem: Hemodynamics, Clinical Role, Dosing & Safety Profile</summary><div class="details-content">

Diltiazem: Hemodynamics, clinical indications, dosing, and safety. Diltiazem occupies an intermediate pharmacological profile between verapamil and nifedipine, producing balanced peripheral and coronary arteriolar vasodilation combined with moderate negative chronotropic and dromotropic actions, but with less direct negative inotropy and much lower incidence of constipation than verapamil.

Diltiazem is widely used for exertional angina, coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal variant angina), rate control in atrial fibrillation, and systemic hypertension. In post-MI patients without pulmonary congestion or reduced EF, the MDPIT trial demonstrated that diltiazem reduced recurrent cardiac events, although it increased mortality in patients with heart failure. Typical oral dosing of diltiazem extended-release ranges from 120 to 360 mg once daily.

### 3.3 Dihydropyridines: Nifedipine, Amlodipine & Newer Generations</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

#### Nifedipine: Vasodilation, Formulations, Indications & Contraindications</summary><div class="details-content">

Nifedipine: Formulations, arteriolar vasodilation, and clinical role. Nifedipine is the prototype dihydropyridine, acting as a potent selective peripheral and coronary arteriolar vasodilator with minimal direct myocardial depressant action at clinical doses ([Fig. 1.27](#fig-01-27)). Early short-acting liquid-filled capsule formulations caused precipitous arterial hypotension followed by intense reflex sympathetic activation, provoking tachycardia, neurohormonal surge, and increased risk of reinfarction and death in acute coronary syndromes ([Fig. 1.28](#fig-01-28)).

Consequently, short-acting nifedipine is obsolete and contraindicated in coronary disease. Modern therapy utilizes once-daily extended-release formulations (GITS, gastrointestinal therapeutic system, 30 to 90 mg once daily), which provide zero-order drug delivery over 24 hours without peak-to-trough fluctuations or reflex tachycardia ([Table 1.15](#table-01-15)). In the ACTION trial (7,665 patients with stable angina), long-acting nifedipine was safe and significantly reduced the need for coronary interventions and heart failure admissions.

#### Amlodipine: Pharmacokinetics, Landmark Outcome Trials & Clinical Role</summary><div class="details-content">

Amlodipine: Pharmacokinetics, major outcome trials, and clinical role. Amlodipine is the definitive second-generation dihydropyridine, characterized by high oral bioavailability (60% to 65%), high volume of distribution, slow hepatic clearance, and an extraordinary elimination half-life of 35 to 50 hours, allowing once-daily morning administration with consistent 24-hour hemodynamic protection ([Table 1.16](#table-01-16)).

In the CAMELOT trial (1,991 patients with CAD and normal BP), amlodipine significantly reduced adverse cardiovascular events (hospitalization for angina and coronary revascularization) by 31% compared to placebo, while intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) showed halting of coronary atheroma progression. In the massive ALLHAT hypertension trial (33,357 patients), amlodipine was equivalent to chlorthalidone in preventing coronary heart disease death and nonfatal MI. Furthermore, in the PRAISE trials, amlodipine proved safely neutral in patients with severe non-ischemic cardiomyopathy.

#### Felodipine, Third-Generation DHPs & Comparative Hemodynamics with β-Blockade</summary><div class="details-content">

Felodipine, third-generation DHPs, and comparative hemodynamics with β-blockers. Felodipine extended-release (2.5 to 10 mg once daily) exhibits exceptionally high vascular-to-cardiac selectivity (greater than 100:1) with proven antianginal and antihypertensive efficacy. Third-generation dihydropyridines (lercanidipine, lacidipine) are highly lipophilic molecules that integrate into the phospholipid membrane bilayer, forming a drug depot that slowly diffuses to the channel receptor, providing ultra-smooth vasodilation with significantly reduced peripheral ankle edema.

Comparative hemodynamics show that while DHP CCBs reduce afterload and improve coronary flow, combining a DHP with a β-blocker produces optimal clinical synergy: the β-blocker prevents any reflex heart rate rise, while the DHP prevents excessive negative inotropy and coronary vasoconstriction ([Fig. 1.29](#fig-01-29)).

## 4. Newer (Nontraditional) Antianginal Agents</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 4.1 Late Inward Sodium Current Inhibition: Ranolazine</summary><div class="details-content">

Ranolazine is the prototype inhibitor of the late inward sodium current ($I_{Na,late}$) in cardiac myocytes ([Fig. 1.30](#fig-01-30)). During myocardial ischemia, pathological phosphorylation and oxidative modification of voltage-gated cardiac sodium channels ($Nav1.5$) cause a persistent, non-inactivating late sodium influx. Intracellular sodium accumulation drives the reverse mode of the sodium-calcium exchanger ($NCX$), leading to pathological cytosolic and mitochondrial calcium overload.

This calcium overload causes incomplete diastolic myocardial relaxation, elevates left ventricular diastolic wall stiffness and filling pressures, and mechanically compresses the subendocardial coronary microcirculation, creating a vicious cycle of supply-demand mismatch. By selectively inhibiting $I_{Na,late}$, ranolazine prevents calcium overload, reduces diastolic wall tension, and restores subendocardial nutritive blood flow without producing hemodynamic depression (zero effect on heart rate, blood pressure, or inotropy).

In the pivotal MARISA, CARISA, and ERICA clinical trials, ranolazine significantly prolonged exercise duration, delayed time to 1-mm ST depression, and reduced weekly anginal frequency in patients with symptomatic chronic angina refractory to maximal doses of β-blockers, CCBs, or nitrates. Standard dosing is 500 to 1000 mg twice daily. Ranolazine is metabolized by CYP3A4 and causes mild, concentration-dependent prolongation of the QTc interval (typically 2 to 5 ms), but does not induce torsades de pointes or ventricular arrhythmias.

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 4.2 Sinoatrial Pacemaker Current (If) Inhibition: Ivabradine</summary><div class="details-content">

Ivabradine is a direct and selective inhibitor of the hyperpolarization-activated cyclic nucleotide-gated (HCN) funny pacemaker current ($I_f$) in the sinoatrial node. By slowing the diastolic depolarization slope, ivabradine produces pure, dose-dependent heart rate reduction without altering myocardial contractility, ventricular repolarization, intracardiac conduction velocity, or peripheral vascular resistance.

In patients with stable coronary artery disease and left ventricular systolic dysfunction, heart rate reduction with ivabradine substantially reduces myocardial oxygen consumption ($MVO_2$) and prolongs coronary diastolic perfusion time. In clinical trials (ASSOCIATE, INITIATIVE), ivabradine demonstrated antianginal efficacy non-inferior to atenolol and amlodipine. It is recommended for patients in normal sinus rhythm with resting heart rate $\ge 70$ bpm who have persistent symptoms despite β-blocker therapy or who are intolerant of β-blockers.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 4.3 Metabolic Modulators & Potassium Channel Openers: Trimetazidine, Perhexiline & Nicorandil</summary><div class="details-content">

Trimetazidine and perhexiline: Metabolic antianginals. Trimetazidine is a selective inhibitor of mitochondrial long-chain 3-ketoacyl coenzyme A thiolase (3-KAT). By selectively suppressing free fatty acid (FFA) $\beta$-oxidation in ischemic cardiomyocytes, trimetazidine shifts substrate utilization toward glucose oxidation. Because aerobic glucose metabolism generates substantially more ATP molecules per atom of oxygen consumed than fatty acid oxidation, trimetazidine preserves intracellular high-energy phosphate stores, maintains cellular homeostasis, and reduces acidosis during ischemia without exerting any hemodynamic effects.

Perhexiline acts upstream by inhibiting carnitine palmitoyltransferase-1 (CPT-1), similarly shifting fuel metabolism toward glucose. Because of its narrow therapeutic window and risk of severe peripheral neuropathy and hepatotoxicity in slow CYP2D6 metabolizers, perhexiline requires therapeutic drug monitoring. Nicorandil exerts a unique dual antianginal mechanism: a nitrate-like moiety stimulates soluble guanylyl cyclase to generate cGMP, while its nicotinamide ester group opens ATP-sensitive potassium channels ($K_{ATP}$) in vascular smooth muscle and mitochondrial inner membranes, mimicking the cardioprotective phenotype of ischemic preconditioning.

</div></details>

</div></details>

## 5. Summary & Comparative Antianginal Algorithms</summary><div class="details-content">

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 5.1 Comparative Clinical Synthesis of Major Antianginal Classes</summary><div class="details-content">

Integrated clinical synthesis of antianginal pharmacotherapy. The contemporary management of chronic stable angina pectoris and acute coronary syndromes is grounded in the complementary pharmacological actions of three traditional drug classes (nitrates, β-blockers, and calcium channel blockers) along with modern metabolic and late inward sodium current inhibitors.

1. **Nitrates:** Act primarily via systemic venodilation and epicardial coronary vasodilation mediated by the cellular NO-cGMP pathway. By reducing left ventricular filling pressures and wall tension, they lower myocardial oxygen demand ($MVO_2$) and redistribute perfusion to ischemic subendocardium. Short-acting sublingual formulations remain first-line for acute attack termination, while long-acting formulations require daily 10- to 14-hour nitrate-free intervals to avoid tolerance and peroxynitrite-induced ALDH-2 inactivation.

2. **β-Blockers:** Represent standard first-line cardioprotective therapy for exertional angina, reducing heart rate, contractility, and systolic blood pressure. Prolongation of diastole optimizes subendocardial perfusion time. In post-myocardial infarction patients and those with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), evidence-based β-blockers (metoprolol succinate, carvedilol, bisoprolol) confer substantial long-term survival advantages.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 5.2 Calcium Antagonists, Dihydropyridines & Second-Line Metabolic Therapies</summary><div class="details-content">

3. **Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs):** Exhibit functional dichotomy based on molecular binding sites on the L-type channel $\alpha_1$ subunit:

* **Non-Dihydropyridines (Verapamil, Diltiazem):** Exert balanced cardiac and vascular actions, lowering heart rate, depressing AV nodal conduction, and reducing contractility. They represent premier alternatives when β-blockers are contraindicated (e.g., severe reactive bronchospasm) or when supraventricular rate control is needed, but are contraindicated in severe left ventricular systolic dysfunction.

* **Dihydropyridines (Amlodipine, Nifedipine ER, Felodipine):** Function as potent selective arteriolar vasodilators with negligible direct myocardial depressant activity. They are safe in concomitant asthma, conduction disturbances, and peripheral artery disease, and provide optimal hemodynamic synergy when combined with β-blockers, eliminating reflex sympathetic activation.

4. **Novel Agents:** Ranolazine selectively inhibits the late inward sodium current ($I_{Na,late}$), relieving diastolic wall stiffness and microvascular compression without altering heart rate or blood pressure. Ivabradine provides pure heart rate reduction via sinoatrial $I_f$ inhibition in patients in sinus rhythm with HR $\ge 70$ bpm. Trimetazidine and nicorandil optimize cardiomyocyte substrate energetics and ischemic preconditioning.

</div></details>

<details class="med-details"><summary>

### 5.3 Integrated Clinical Practice Algorithm for Chronic Coronary Syndromes</summary><div class="details-content">

Structured step-care clinical algorithm for chronic stable angina:

* **Step 1 (First-Line Monotherapy):** Initiate short-acting sublingual nitroglycerin as needed plus a cardioselective β-blocker (e.g., bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate), titrating to a target resting heart rate of 55 to 60 beats per minute. If β-blockers are contraindicated or poorly tolerated, initiate a rate-lowering non-DHP CCB (verapamil ER or diltiazem ER) in patients with preserved EF, or a long-acting DHP (amlodipine) if heart rate is already low.

* **Step 2 (First-Line Combination Therapy):** If angina or ischemic symptoms persist despite optimal β-blocker monotherapy, add a long-acting dihydropyridine CCB (amlodipine 5 to 10 mg once daily or nifedipine GITS 30 to 60 mg once daily) for synergistic arterial unloading and coronary vasodilation.

* **Step 3 (Triple Therapy & Novel Adjuncts):** In refractory exertional angina, add a long-acting nitrate with an asymmetric dosing schedule (e.g., isosorbide mononitrate extended-release 30 to 120 mg every morning) or add ranolazine (500 to 1000 mg twice daily). For patients in sinus rhythm with resting HR $\ge 70$ bpm despite maximal tolerated β-blockade, add ivabradine (5 to 7.5 mg twice daily). Prompt coronary angiography and revascularization (PCI or CABG) must be pursued in high-risk patients or those with medically refractory ischemia.
