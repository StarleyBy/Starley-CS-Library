## Chapter 1: Surgical Approaches to the Heart</summary>

### Mediastinal Anatomy and Surgical Access
* **Median sternotomy:** The most frequent surgical approach; involves midline incision from suprasternal notch to xiphoid process, splitting the sternum to expose the pericardial sac between pleural cavities.
* **Thymus gland:** Particularly prominent in infants; wraps over anterior and lateral pericardium at the base of the heart; supplied by internal thoracic and inferior thyroid arteries; drains via fragile veins into left brachiocephalic vein — undue traction can damage this major vessel.
* **Anterior mediastinum:** The retrosternal space is devoid of vital structures, allowing safe blunt dissection to join superior and inferior incisions.

### Phrenic and Vagus Nerves — Surgical Hazards
* **Phrenic nerve vulnerability:** Most liable to injury during median sternotomy (not immediately evident); passes close to superior caval vein, persistent left caval vein, and internal thoracic artery near thoracic inlet.
* **Phrenic nerve injury mechanisms:** Direct trauma during vessel takedown, avulsion of pericardiophrenic artery, pericardial patch harvesting, pericardiectomy, or injudicious use of cooling agents within pericardial cavity → phrenic paralysis or paresis.
* **Vagus nerve:** Passes posterior to lung hilum; left recurrent laryngeal branch arises near arterial ligament/duct, passes around inferior border, then ascends on medial aortic wall adjacent to esophagus.
* **Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury:** Caused by excessive traction on vagus nerve as it courses along left subclavian artery, or direct trauma near ligamentum arteriosum.

### Left Lateral Thoracotomy
* **Standard incision:** Fourth intercostal space; posterior extent through triangular bloodless space between latissimus dorsi, trapezius, and teres major muscles (floor = sixth intercostal space).
* **Muscle-sparing technique:** Preserves latissimus dorsi and serratus anterior; partial division of latissimus dorsi may be needed for greater exposure; serratus anterior rarely requires division.
* **Intercostal neurovascular bundle:** Protected beneath lower margin of the fourth rib.
* **Intrapericardial access:** Gained anterior to phrenic nerve; left lateral lobe of thymus overlies pericardial sac and aortic arch.
* **Aortic isthmus approach:** Lung retracted anteriorly, parietal pleura divided posterior to vagus nerve to expose descending thoracic aorta.

### Left-Sided Vascular Structures
* **Left recurrent laryngeal nerve:** Passes around inferior border of arterial ligament (or patent ductus arteriosus), then ascends to larynx on medial aspect of posterior aortic wall adjacent to esophagus.
* **Superior intercostal vein:** Crosses aorta, insinuates between phrenic and vagus nerves; rarely of surgical significance but frequently divided to provide access to aorta.
* **Thoracic duct:** Ascends through area, drains into junction of left subclavian and internal jugular veins; accessory lymph channels can be troublesome during dissection of left subclavian artery origin.

### Right Lateral Thoracotomy
* **Interspace selection:** Fifth interspace for approaching the heart; fourth interspace for right-sided great vessels.
* **Pericardial access:** Incision anterior to phrenic nerve, often requiring retraction of right thymic lobe.
* **Azygos vein:** Divided near junction with superior caval vein to reach right pulmonary artery and adjacent mediastinal structures.
* **Right recurrent laryngeal nerve:** Originates from right vagus, curls around posteroinferior wall of right subclavian artery (origin of brachiocephalic trunk) before ascending into neck.
* **Ansa subclavia:** Subclavian sympathetic loop encircling right subclavian origin; damage produces Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis, enophthalmos).

### Anterior Thoracotomy
* **Application:** Occasionally used for congenital malformations; same basic anatomical rules apply as for lateral approaches.

## Chapter 2: Anatomy of the Cardiac Chambers</summary>

### Pericardial Cavity and Sinuses
* **Pericardial layers:** Fibrous pericardium (tough outer sac) + serous pericardium (parietal layer lines fibrous sac, visceral layer = epicardium on heart surface).
* **Transverse sinus:** Occupies inner curvature of heart; anterior boundary = posterior surface of great arteries; posterior boundary = right pulmonary artery and roof of left atrium; permits passage of clamp behind great vessels.
* **Oblique sinus:** Blind-ending cavity behind left atrium; upper boundary = reflection between upper pulmonary veins; right/left borders = reflections around pulmonary veins and inferior caval vein.
* **Pericardial fold between SVC and right upper pulmonary vein:** Incision provides access to superior left atrium and right pulmonary artery; also incised when placing snare around SVC.

### External Cardiac Anatomy
* **Right atrial appendage:** Blunt triangular shape with broad junction to atrial cavity.
* **Left atrial appendage:** Tubular structure with narrow junction; found at left border of pulmonary trunk.
* **Juxtaposition:** Both appendages on same side of arterial pedicle — anomaly associated with additional intracardiac malformations.
* **Persistent left superior caval vein:** Crosses anterior to left pulmonary artery, extends down posterior left atrium through left atrioventricular groove to coronary sinus orifice.
* **Ventricular surfaces:** Three-sided pyramid with inferior diaphragmatic, anterior sternocostal, and posterior pulmonary surfaces; acute margin (sharp) between sternocostal and diaphragmatic surfaces; obtuse marginal areas supplied by circumflex obtuse marginal branches.

### Right Atrial Anatomy — Surgical Landmarks
* **Terminal groove (sulcus terminalis):** Marks junction between right atrial appendage and systemic venous sinus; sinus node located subepicardially within this groove.
* **Sinus node location:** Usually lateral and inferior to superior cavoatrial junction (cigar-shaped); in ~10% extends across crest in horseshoe fashion.
* **Artery to sinus node:** Branch of right coronary artery (55%) or circumflex artery (45%); may cross crest of right appendage, course retrocavally, or form arterial circle around cavoatrial junction.
* **Waterston's (Sondergaard's) groove:** Deeper groove posterior/parallel to terminal groove between right atrium and right pulmonary veins; incision provides access to left atrium.
* **True atrial septum:** Confined to floor and anteroinferior margin of oval fossa; superior and posteroinferior rims are infoldings, not true septum.
* **Triangle of Koch:** Bounded by tendon of Todaro, septal leaflet of tricuspid valve attachment, and coronary sinus orifice; contains atrioventricular node.
* **Tendon of Todaro:** Fibrous structure formed by junction of Eustachian valve (IVC valve) and Thebesian valve (coronary sinus valve); inserts into atrioventricular component of membranous septum.
* **Atrioventricular muscular sandwich:** Atrial and ventricular musculature separated by fibrofatty tissue from inferior atrioventricular groove; no true muscular atrioventricular septum.

### Internodal Conduction — Clinical Implications
* **No specialized internodal tracts:** All atrial myocardium (except nodes) has working phenotype; no insulated conduction tracts within atrial walls.
* **Preferential pathways:** Terminal crest, superior rim of oval fossa, Eustachian ridge, superior interatrial fold serve as preferential routes due to geometry.
* **Key to avoiding postoperative arrhythmias:** Fastidious preservation of sinus node, AV node, and their arterial supply — not concern over nonexistent specialized tracts.

### Central Fibrous Body
* **Components:** Membranous septum + hinges of AV valves + fibrous components of aortic root in continuity; right fibrous trigone (rightward margin of aortic-mitral continuity) joins membranous septum.
* **Gerbode defect:** Direct shunt from left ventricular outflow tract to right atrium through atrioventricular component of membranous septum.

### Left Atrial Anatomy — Surgical Access
* **Left atrial appendage:** Narrow junction with atrial body (no terminal groove); mouth lies left of mitral orifice.
* **Waterston's groove approach:** Incision posterior to right lateral aspect of interatrial groove → directly into left atrium; extension via pericardial fold between SVC and right pulmonary artery.
* **Superior approach:** Retract aorta anteriorly and left → trough between atrial appendages → incision through floor enters left atrium.
* **Risk to sinus node artery:** May course through interatrial groove or roof of left atrium (especially when originating from circumflex).

### Right Ventricular Anatomy
* **Three-component model:** Inlet (tricuspid valve + tension apparatus), apical trabecular (coarse trabeculations, thin apex vulnerable to perforation), outlet (infundibulum, complete muscular sleeve).
* **Tricuspid valve hallmark:** Direct cordal attachments of septal leaflet to septum.
* **Supraventricular crest:** Muscular shelf between tricuspid and pulmonary orifices; largely infolded inner heart curve (ventriculoinfundibular fold); only medial portion is true outlet septum.
* **Free-standing infundibular sleeve:** Permits pulmonary valve autograft harvest for Ross procedure.
* **Septomarginal trabeculation (septal band):** Anterosuperior and posteroinferior limbs clasp septal attachment of crest; moderator band extends from septomarginal trabeculation to anterior papillary muscle.
* **Septoparietal trabeculations:** Variable trabeculations from anterior margin of septomarginal trabeculation.
* **Morphologic discriminator:** Coarse right ventricular apical trabeculations vs. fine left ventricular trabeculations.

### Left Ventricular Anatomy
* **Mitral valve leaflets:** Aortic leaflet (short, squat, in fibrous continuity with aortic valve) and mural leaflet (narrower, more extensive attachment, posteroinferior).
* **Papillary muscles:** Anteroinferior and posterosuperior positions (not anteroseptal/posterolateral — terms reflect incorrect apex-on orientation).
* **No direct septal attachments:** Deep posteroinferior diverticulum of subaortic outflow tract displaces aortic leaflet away from muscular septum.
* **Left ventricular apex:** Surprisingly thin myocardium — risk of perforation by catheters/electrodes/drainage tubes, especially with hypothermia-stiffened catheters during coronary surgery.
* **Left bundle branch:** Descends from membranous septum beneath zone of apposition between right and non-coronary aortic leaflets; divides into three interconnected fascicles.

### Aorta
* **Sinutubular junction:** Distal extremity of aortic sinuses at line of free edge opening of aortic leaflets.
* **Arch branches:** Brachiocephalic, left common carotid, left subclavian arteries.
* **Bronchial arteries:** Arise from underside of arch; troublesome in aortic coarctation.
* **Intercostal arteries:** Lower nine pairs; critical collaterals in coarctation (rib notching); dorsal branches supply spinal cord — protect from occlusion.
* **Semilunar attachment:** Leaflet hinges cross ventriculoarterial junction → crescents of ventricular muscle in aortic sinuses, triangles of arterial tissue in ventricular base.

### Pulmonary Arteries
* **Pulmonary trunk:** <5 cm in adult; completely intrapericardial; covered with serous pericardium except where abuts aorta.
* **Arterial ligament:** Extends from aorta to pulmonary trunk bifurcation.
* **Ligament of Marshall:** Fold of serous pericardium between left pulmonary artery and left superior pulmonary vein; contains remnant of left superior caval vein.
* **Right pulmonary artery:** Longer, traverses beneath aortic arch and behind SVC; often branches before reaching lateral wall of SVC — large upper lobar branch may be mistaken for main right pulmonary artery.

## Chapter 3: Surgical anatomy of the valves of the heart</summary>

### Valvar Complexes: Fundamental Concepts

* **Atrioventricular vs. arterial valves:** AV valves (mitral, tricuspid) guard the atrioventricular junctions; arterial valves (aortic, pulmonary) guard the ventriculoarterial junctions. The distinction determines their structural design and surgical approach.

* **AV valvar complex components:** Annulus, leaflets, tendinous cords, papillary muscles, and supporting ventricular musculature — all must function in harmony to achieve competence. The complex tension apparatus withstands full ventricular systolic pressure.

* **Arterial valvar complex components:** Leaflets (semilunar in shape), supporting arterial sinuses, and fibrous interleaflet triangles. The complex extends from the virtual ring at the nadir of leaflet hinges to the sinutubular junction.

* **Semilunar hinges:** In arterial valves, leaflets are hinged in crown-like (semilunar) fashion, with attachments crossing the anatomical ventriculoarterial junctions — fundamentally different from the true annular suspension of AV valves.

* **Ventriculoarterial junction (anatomical ring):** The circular junction where myocardial walls meet fibroelastic walls of the sinuses. Best seen in pulmonary root after leaflet removal. One of two true anatomical rings in each arterial root.

* **Sinutubular junction:** The distal attachment point of semilunar hinges; the other obvious anatomical ring within arterial roots. Forms the peripheral attachment of commissures.

* **Virtual annulus (echocardiographic):** Formed by joining the nadirs (most proximal parts) of valvar hinges. Not a true anatomical structure, but increasingly accepted by surgeons as representing the valvar annulus. Measured as the entrance diameter of the arterial root.

* **Terminological controversy:** Surgeons often describe leaflet remnants as the "annulus"; echocardiographers measure the virtual ring. Growing consensus suggests surgeons adopt the virtual ring as the annulus to avoid confusion.

* **Commissure definitions:** Anatomically, a line of junction between adjacent structures with two ends. In cardiac valves, traditionally used to mean the end-point of a zone of apposition. Arterial valves and tricuspid valve have three zones of apposition; mitral valve has a solitary zone.

### Position and Support Within the Heart

* **Fibrous continuity:** Three of the four valves have leaflets in fibrous continuity within the short-axis cylinder of the heart. Descriptions of an extensive fibrous skeleton are "grossly exaggerated."

* **Pulmonary valve support:** Exclusively by ventricular muscle — specifically the free-standing right ventricular infundibulum. No direct fibrous relationship with other valves.

* **Valve orientation (attitudinally correct):** Pulmonary valve — most anterior and superior; tricuspid valve — most inferior; mitral valve — most posterior; aortic valve — centrally located, rightward and posterior to pulmonary valve.

* **Aortic-mitral curtain:** Direct fibrous continuity between the aortic leaflet of the mitral valve and two leaflets of the aortic valve. Forms the roof of the left ventricle.

* **Fibrous trigones:** Right and left thickenings at the ends of the aortic-mitral fibrous continuity. Anchor the aortic-mitral valvar unit to ventricular musculature across the short axis of the left ventricle.

* **Central fibrous body:** Conjoined structure of the right fibrous trigone and the membranous septum — the strongest part of fibrous support in the cardiac base.

* **Interleaflet fibrous triangles:** Fill gaps between semilunar hinges of aortic leaflets. Extend to the sinutubular junction. The triangle between non-coronary and right coronary leaflets is included in the central fibrous body.

* **Tendon of Todaro:** Fibrous cord extending through right atrial wall, attaching to the right aspect of the membranous septum. Forms the atrial boundary of the triangle of Koch. Marks the attachment to the central fibrous body.

* **Penetrating bundle of His:** The solitary normal area of atrioventricular muscular continuity. Pierces the strongest part of the fibrous skeleton through the atrioventricular component of the membranous septum.

* **Absence of true arterial "rings":** No collagenous ring supports arterial valve leaflets. Semilunar attachments cross the anatomical ventriculoarterial junction. Two true uniplanar rings exist in each root (ventriculoarterial junction and sinutubular junction), but neither is confluent with leaflet attachments.

### Atrioventricular Valves: Common Features

* **Valvar complex concept:** Annulus, leaflets, tendinous cords, papillary muscles, and supporting ventricular musculature must function in concert. Lesion of any component can result in regurgitation.

* **Leaflet structure:** Spongy atrial layer and fibrous ventricular layer. Atrial myocardium inserts between endocardium and spongy layer. Blood vessels found only where muscular fronds present.

* **Continuous skirt:** Leaflets form a continuous skirt hanging from the atrioventricular junction. No consensus exists on number of leaflets per valve (tricuspid: 3 vs. 2; mitral: 2 vs. 4 vs. 6).

* **Commissural definition preferred:** Authors define leaflets by zones of apposition, not by variable fan-shaped cords. Peripheral extents of apposition zones become commissures.

* **Chordal classification (simplified):** Free-edge cords (if cut → regurgitation), rough-zone cords (including prominent strut cords in mitral valve), and basal cords (from ventricular wall). Detailed Toronto classification has little surgical utility.

* **Mechanisms of prolapse:** Lack of uniform cordal support to free edge, combined with cordal lengthening, contributes to leaflet prolapse — one of the most common congenital lesions.

### Mitral Valve Specifics

* **Two major leaflets:** Aortic leaflet (attached to 1/3 of annulus, trapezoidal/semicircular) and mural leaflet (attached to 2/3 of annulus, long rectangular with variable scallops).

* **Attitudinally correct orientation:** Papillary muscles positioned inferoanteriorly and superoposteriorly (not posteroseptal and anterolateral). Angle exists between valve opening axis and inlet component of muscular ventricular septum.

* **Mural leaflet variability:** Variable number of slits acting as pleats for snug closure. Carpentier classification (P1–P3, A1–A3) widely used but artificial. Four, five, or more components possible.

* **Papillary muscles:** Paired prominent structures, neither arising from septum (unlike tricuspid). Located at junction of apical and middle thirds of parietal ventricular wall.

* **Conduction vulnerability:** Area related to right fibrous trigone and central fibrous body contains AV node and penetrating bundle. AV node lies 5–10 mm from atrial attachment of medial scallop of mural leaflet.

* **Coronary relationships (right dominant — 90%):** Circumflex artery related only to lateral scallop; coronary sinus from below and right.

* **Coronary relationships (left dominant — 10%):** Entire mural leaflet attachment intimately related to circumflex artery, including AV nodal branch.

### Tricuspid Valve Specifics

* **Three major leaflets (by closure pattern):** Septal, anterosuperior, and inferior (mural — usually incorrectly called posterior).

* **Septal leaflet:** Supported between inconstant inferior papillary muscle and constant medial papillary muscle (muscle of Lancisi/papillary muscle of conus). Attachment crosses membranous septum, dividing it into AV and interventricular components.

* **Distinguishing feature:** Multiple cords from free edge attach directly to septum — mitral valve lacks any septal attachments.

* **Anterosuperior leaflet:** Descends from ventriculoinfundibular fold, supported by cords from medial papillary muscle. May have muscle extensions from papillary muscles or fold.

* **Inferior leaflet:** Most variable, attached to parietal part of AV junction, supported by basal cords.

* **Parietal attachments:** Encircled by right coronary artery in AV groove. Well-formed collagenous annulus rare; fibrofatty tissue separates atrial and ventricular myocardium.

### Arterial Valves: Basic Arrangement

* **Simpler design:** No complex tension apparatus. Three semilunar leaflets open into arterial sinuses during systole, collapse during diastole, held closed by hydrostatic pressure.

* **Histology:** Fibrous core with endothelial lining. Nodule of Arantius (fibrous thickening at central portion of free edge) appears with age.

* **Line of closure:** Some distance from free edge toward semilunar attachment. Perforations beyond closure line are normal and do not affect function.

* **Semilunar hinges:** Half-moon shape; overall seating forms a coronet (crown-like). Attachments at apex of zones of apposition significantly higher than at midportion.

* **Two true rings in arterial root:** Sinutubular junction (plane of commissure attachment) and anatomical ventriculoarterial junction (where fibrous walls attach to ventricular supporting structures). Neither is confluent with semilunar attachments.

* **Virtual ring (echocardiographic annulus):** Formed by joining nadirs of valvar hinges. No anatomical counterpart; represents entrance diameter of arterial root. Consensus moving toward accepting this as valvar annulus.

* **Mercedes-Benz sign:** Sinuses arranged in clover-like fashion permitting leaflet closure.

### Aortic Valve Specifics

* **Leaflet nomenclature (by coronary origin):** Right coronary, left coronary, and non-coronary (more accurately "non-adjacent" as rarely a coronary may arise from it).

* **Muscular vs. fibrous support:** Right and left coronary leaflets have predominantly muscular origin from left ventricular wall. Non-adjacent leaflet has entirely fibrous origin, incorporated in fibrous skeleton.

* **Interleaflet triangles:** Fibrous triangles fill gaps between semilunar hinges. Triangle between non-coronary and right coronary leaflets included in central fibrous body; between non-coronary and left coronary confluent with aortic-mitral curtain.

* **Surgical danger areas:** Ascending part of non-coronary leaflet directly above AV node; zone of apposition with right coronary leaflet above penetrating bundle and membranous septum.

* **Konno procedure basis:** Zone of apposition between right and left coronary leaflets opposite corresponding pulmonary zone. Incision through RV outflow tract leads directly into subaortic region.

* **Lateral part of left coronary leaflet:** Only part of aortic valve not intimately related to another cardiac chamber; related externally to free pericardial space.

### Pulmonary Valve Specifics

* **Exclusively muscular attachments:** To right ventricular infundibulum. No fibrous continuity with other valves.

* **Nomenclature (by relationship to aortic valve):** Right adjacent, left adjacent, and non-adjacent leaflets. Two leaflets adjacent to septum are always adjacent to two pulmonary leaflets.

* **Free-standing infundibular sleeve:** Zone of apposition between two adjacent leaflets attached to sleeve, not to muscular outlet septum as traditionally thought.

* **Ventriculoinfundibular fold:** Separates hinges of AV and arterial valves.

* **Ross procedure relevance:** Valve can be liberated with its infundibular sleeve without damaging vital structures. Left adjacent leaflet closely related to first septal perforating artery — preservation key to success.

### Atrioventricular Conduction Axis

* **Penetrating bundle:** Only muscular communication between atrial and ventricular muscle masses. Penetrates membranous septal component of central fibrous body. Intimately related to mitral, tricuspid, and aortic valves.

* **Triangle of Koch:** Contains AV node and atrial zones of transitional cells. Inferior extent is junctional attachment of septal leaflet of tricuspid valve.

* **Penetration point:** Anteroinferior end of zone of apposition between mitral leaflets. AV node lies 5–10 mm from atrial attachment of medial scallop of mural leaflet.

* **Branching bundle:** Begins on crest of muscular ventricular septum or left ventricular aspect, immediately beneath zone of apposition between non-adjacent and right coronary aortic leaflets. Height of interleaflet fibrous triangle separates valvar attachments from conduction axis.

* **Left bundle branch:** Fans out as continuous sheet on smooth left septal surface beneath zone of apposition, splitting into three divisions toward apex.

* **Right bundle branch:** Emerges on right ventricular aspect in area of medial papillary muscle, runs within septomarginal trabeculation.

* **Surgical safety:** Branching segment can be imagined as line joining apex of triangle of Koch to medial papillary muscle. Entire axis within 5 mm of right ventricular septal surface.

### Coronary Circulation Vulnerability

* **Coronary sinus:** Great cardiac vein runs in left AV groove, becomes coronary sinus at oblique vein confluence. Approaches within 5–15 mm of medial attachment of mural leaflet. Deep sutures during mitral replacement may damage sinus → troublesome bleeding.

* **Left coronary artery main stem:** Branches above left fibrous trigone.

* **Anterior interventricular artery:** Septal perforating branches extend into septum beneath left-facing pulmonary leaflet — can be damaged by extensive dissection.

* **Circumflex artery (right dominant — 90%):** Related only to area around lateral scallop of mural leaflet.

* **Circumflex artery (left dominant — 10%):** Entire course intimately related to entire mural leaflet, including AV nodal branch.

* **Right coronary artery:** Runs circumferential course around tricuspid valve attachments in AV groove, on epicardial aspect of ventriculoinfundibular fold. Can be damaged by deep sutures.

* **AV nodal artery:** When right dominant — prominent U-loop beneath floor of coronary sinus gives off AV nodal artery at apex of loop. When left dominant — arises from circumflex artery. Related to inferior aspect of annular attachment of septal leaflet of tricuspid valve.

## Chapter 4: Surgical anatomy of the coronary circulation</summary>

### Coronary Arteries: General Anatomy
* **Aortic sinuses:** Three sinuses within the aortic root, two give rise to coronary arteries: right coronary sinus (no. 1 by Leiden convention) and left coronary sinus (no. 2). The third is the non-coronary sinus.
* **Leiden convention:** The surgeon stands, figuratively, in the non-coronary sinus and looks toward the pulmonary trunk. Of the sinuses adjacent to the pulmonary trunk, the right-hand sinus is no. 1 (gives rise to RCA), the left-hand sinus is no. 2 (gives rise to left main stem). This convention holds irrespective of arterial trunk relationships.
* **Sinutubular junction:** Coronary arteries usually arise beneath (distal to) this transition between the aortic root and ascending aorta. Origin >1 cm distal to the sinutubular junction occurs in almost one-twentieth of normal hearts and is considered abnormal in adults.
* **Ectopic orifice:** The arterial opening may be deviated toward the ventricle (deep within the aortic sinus) or toward the aortic arch (outside the sinus). An oblique course through the aortic wall with origin from an inappropriate sinus is known as anomalous aortic origin of a coronary artery (AAOCA).

### Anomalous Aortic Origin of a Coronary Artery
* **Variants:** RCA from left coronary sinus (1 in 500 individuals) or left main from right coronary sinus (1 in 2000). The proximal artery crosses the zone of apposition between adjacent leaflets (intramural course).
* **Risk:** Potential for luminal narrowing, disturbances in myocardial perfusion, sudden cardiac death.
* **Solitary coronary orifice:** Usually within the right coronary sinus. The left artery may pass in front of the pulmonary trunk, between it and the aorta, or behind the arterial pedicle. Interarterial course between the great trunks is most significant.
* **Three interarterial course variants:** (1) Between aorta and pulmonary trunk at sinutubular junction level; (2) Deep within the ventricular septum (greatest compression); (3) Within the tissue plane between the pulmonary infundibular sleeve and the aortic root (below the hinges of pulmonary valvar leaflets).

### Right Coronary Artery (RCA)
* **Course:** Emerges from right coronary sinus (no. 1), enters right atrioventricular groove, encircles the tricuspid orifice.
* **Inferior interventricular artery (formerly posterior descending):** In approximately nine-tenths of cases, RCA gives rise to this artery at the crux. CT confirms the artery is inferior and interventricular.
* **Right dominance:** The artery continues beyond the crux, supplying downgoing branches to the diaphragmatic surface of the left ventricle.
* **Sinus node artery:** In just over half of cases, arises from proximal RCA. May arise more distally, coursing over the lateral margin of the right atrial appendage to reach the terminal groove — major surgical significance for standard atriotomy.
* **Infundibular branches:** May arise by separate orifices within the right aortic sinus.

### Left Coronary Artery (LCA)
* **Left main artery:** Short (<1 cm), arises from left coronary sinus (no. 2), enters left margin of transverse sinus behind the pulmonary trunk and beneath the left atrial appendage.
* **Bifurcation:** Divides into anterior interventricular (descending) and circumflex branches. Trifurcation (with intermediate branch to obtuse margin) occurs in some hearts.
* **Anterior interventricular artery (LAD):** Runs inferiorly within the anterosuperior interventricular groove. Gives off diagonal branches to the pulmonary surface of the left ventricle and septal perforating branches. The first septal perforating branch is at major risk when the pulmonary valve is removed for use as a homograft.
* **Circumflex branch (LCx):** Passes backward in relation to the mitral orifice. Left dominance (LCx gives rise to inferior interventricular artery at the crux) occurs in only about one-tenth of cases. In almost half of normal individuals, the circumflex artery gives rise to the sinus node artery.

### Arterial Supply to the Conduction Tissues
* **AV nodal artery:** Arises from the dominant coronary artery at the crux, usually from a U-turn beneath the floor of the coronary sinus. Passes toward the central fibrous body within the fibrofatty plane. In some hearts, perforates the fibrous AV junction to supply part of the branching AV bundle.
* **Septal perforating arteries from LAD:** Always supply the anterior parts of the ventricular bundle branches. Occasionally supply the greater part of the inferior ventricular conduction tissues.

### Myocardial Bridges
* The myocardium may form a bridge over segments of a coronary artery. Role in coronary artery disease is unclear, but bridges can impede surgical isolation of the artery.

### Coronary Veins
* **Coronary sinus:** Drains most myocardial venous blood into the right atrium between the sinus septum and the sub-Eustachian sinus.
* **Great cardiac vein:** Runs alongside the anterior interventricular artery, becomes the coronary sinus as it encircles the mitral orifice.
* **Tributaries:** Middle cardiac vein (ascends with the inferior interventricular artery), small cardiac vein (encircles the tricuspid orifice with the RCA). Occasionally drain directly to the right atrium.
* **Thebesian valve:** Guards the coronary sinus orifice; rarely may be imperforate.
* **Vieussens’ valve:** Prominent valve in the great cardiac vein where it turns around the obtuse margin of the left ventricle; some consider it marks the transition to the coronary sinus.
* **Direct drainage:** Smaller anterior and smallest cardiac veins drain directly to the right atrial cavity.

### Cardiac Lymphatics
* **Lymphatic networks:** Superficial, myocardial, and subendocardial. Surgical significance is limited.
* **Thoracic duct:** Originates from the cysterna chyli (L2), enters the thorax through the aortic hiatus, runs in the right paravertebral gutter to Th4, crosses the midline, runs in the left paravertebral gutter beneath the aortic arch, curves over the arch between the left common carotid and subclavian arteries, terminates in the left subclavian or internal jugular vein.
* **Clinical significance:** Ligation may be needed in patients with lymphatic drainage problems after conversion to the Fontan circulation.

## Chapter 5: Surgical anatomy of the conduction system</summary>

### Landmarks to the Atrioventricular Conduction Axis
* **Triangle of Koch:** The apex of this triangle marks the penetration of the atrioventricular conduction axis through the fibrous insulating plane. The apex is the point where the tendon of Todaro inserts into the central fibrous body.
* **Bundle of His:** Just inferior to the apex of the triangle of Koch, the AV node components gather and enter the insulating tissues of the fibrous body. Once insulated from atrial myocardium, the axis becomes the penetrating AV bundle (bundle of His).
* **Membranous septum:** As the axis passes through fibrous tissue and divides, the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve crosses the membranous septum, dividing it into atrioventricular and interventricular components.
* **Branching bundle:** The axis reaches the crest of the muscular septum immediately beneath the fibrous membranous septum. The left bundle branches cascade down the smooth left surface of the muscular ventricular septum.
* **Right bundle branch:** Burrows intramyocardially to reach the right side of the septum, surfacing beneath the medial papillary muscle.
* **Dead-end tract:** The axis continues beyond the origin of the right bundle branch, entering the aortic root — a rudimentary structure that may be a substrate for outflow tract tachycardias.
* **Clinical landmark:** The site of the conduction axis can be determined by a line from the apex of the triangle of Koch to the medial papillary muscle.

### Ventricular Pre-Excitation
* **Definition:** All or part of the ventricular myocardium is excited earlier than expected if the impulse had reached the ventricles via the normal AV conduction system.
* **Accessory pathways (atrio-Hisian tracts):** Exist between the atrium and the AV bundle — not amenable to surgical division.
* **Mahaim (paraspecific) connections:** Between the conduction axis and the crest of the ventricular septum — not amenable to surgical division.
* **Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome:** Caused by accessory AV muscular strands joining atrial and ventricular myocardium outside the specialised conduction tissues. These are amenable to surgical division (now usually catheter ablation).
* **Kent bundles (misnomer):** The term is inappropriate for WPW accessory pathways. True Kent structures are node-like remnants of the embryonic AV ring that rarely give rise to anomalous connections.
* **Ohnell's description:** Accessory pathways extend through the fat pad on the epicardial aspect of the valvar annulus, bearing no resemblance to Kent's node-like remnants.

### Anatomical Distribution of Accessory Pathways
* **Left-sided pathways:** Found anywhere around the mural component of the mitral orifice. They cross outside a well-formed fibrous annulus. Bundles are 1-2 mm in diameter, made of working cardiomyocytes. Surgical ablation requires dissection within the epicardial fat pad or reflection of coronary vessels. Catheter ablation lesions are placed on the ventricular aspect of the mitral hinge point.
* **Right-sided pathways:** May pass through the fat pad. Frequently associated with Ebstein's malformation. Can be found from the membranous septum to the mouth of the coronary sinus. The tricuspid annulus is rarely a firm fibrous junction.
* **Paraseptal pathways:** Greatest clinical challenge. Cross between the coronary sinus mouth and the supraventricular crest. May run deep within the floor of the triangle of Koch. The AV node and bundle are within the same area. The artery to the AV node courses forward within the fibroadipose tissue plane.
* **Anterior septum (misnomer):** The area cranial to the membranous septum is actually the medial margin of the ventriculoinfundibular fold, not a septal structure.
* **Mahaim-type connections:** Nodal remnants can function as the atrial origin of specialised muscular accessory connections producing ventricular pre-excitation of the Mahaim type. These may run to the lateral margin of the supraventricular crest at the acute margin of the right ventricle.

### Substrates for Other Supraventricular Tachycardias

#### Atrial Flutter
* **Commonest type circuit:** Counter-clockwise, running down the terminal crest, ascending through the septal isthmus of the tricuspid valvar vestibule.
* **Inferior cavotricuspid isthmus:** Limited by the orifice of the inferior caval vein and the hinge of the septal leaflet of the tricuspid valve. Has three discrete areas (posterior, middle, anterior) with various combinations of fibrous and muscular tissue.
* **Surgical concern:** Care should be taken in the vestibular area to avoid damage to the right coronary artery (anterior compartment of the isthmus).

#### Atrial Fibrillation
* **Maze and corridor procedures:** Surgical techniques that create lines of block to ameliorate or cure atrial fibrillation.
* **Pulmonary vein sleeves:** Atrial myocardium extends for variable distances along the pulmonary veins from the venoatrial junctions. These sleeves contain cardiomyocyte bundles running in different directions with separating fibrous sheaths — setting the scene for focal triggering.
* **Histology:** The cardiomyocytes within pulmonary venous sleeves are working myocardial origin, NOT specialised conduction tissue.
* **Focal ablation:** A proportion of atrial fibrillation cases can be cured by focal lesions at the mouth of the pulmonary veins.

### Substrates for Outflow Tract Tachycardias
* **Myocardial crescents:** At the bases of the arterial valvar sinuses (adjacent sinuses of aorta and pulmonary trunk), the semilunar hinges of the valvar leaflets cross the anatomical ventriculoarterial junctions, creating crescents of ventricular muscle.
* **Origin of outflow tract tachycardias:** Most likely within these myocardial crescents.
* **Non-adjacent aortic sinus:** Rarely contains myocardium. An abnormal rhythm curable by ablation in this sinus suggests either a complete muscular infundibulum supporting the aortic valve (rare) or an extraventricular origin.
* **Distal ablation:** Some outflow tract tachycardias are ablated by lesions placed distal to the ventriculoarterial junction, explained by persistence of parts of the embryonic outflow tract myocardial sleeve that normally regresses during development.

### Arrhythmia Surgery in Patients with Congenital Heart Disease
* **Clinical context:** Increasing numbers of postoperative patients with congenital heart disease (functionally univentricular hearts, tetralogy of Fallot, transposition) present with arrhythmic problems as they age.
* **Therapeutic principle:** Transform areas of slow conduction to areas of no conduction by interrupting myocardial corridors or isthmuses between obstacles or scars, while preserving sinus rhythm and normal AV conduction.
* **Commonest operations:** Right atrial maze (for macro-re-entrant tachycardia) and Cox maze III (for atrial fibrillation).

#### Right Atrial Maze Procedure (Fontan Conversion)
* **Resection:** Tip of the right atrial appendage (and part of anterior surface if significantly enlarged).
* **Lines of block created:**
  - From resected appendage edge to oval fossa
  - From oval fossa across the terminal crest
  - From oval fossa edge to coronary sinus margin
  - From coronary sinus mouth to inferior caval vein mouth
  - From inferior caval vein to hinge of septal leaflet of tricuspid valve
* **Note:** The last lesion is not possible in tricuspid atresia due to absence of the right AV connection.

#### Cox Maze III Procedure
* **Left atrial roof line:** Encircles the orifices of all pulmonary veins.
* **Left atrial appendage line:** To the mouth of the appendage (appendage may be excised or encircled).
* **Mitral valve line:** From the pulmonary vein encircling line to the mitral annulus in the region of the third scallop of the mural leaflet.
* **Epicardial coronary sinus lesion:** Across the coronary sinus as it runs within the left atrioventricular groove.
* **Cryoablation parameters:** Final coronary sinus lesion — 2 minutes at -160°C; other lesions — 1 minute.

## Chapter 6: Analytical description of congenitally malformed hearts</summary>

### Principles of Sequential Segmental Analysis
* **Segmental approach:** The heart is divided into three segments — the atriums, the ventricular mass, and the arterial trunks. Each segment is analysed individually, followed by assessment of the junctions (atrioventricular and ventriculoarterial).
* **Morphological principle:** Description must be based on observed anatomy, not embryological theories. The system must be comprehensive, accurate, clear, and consistent.
* **Surgical focus:** The essence of a malformation lies in its underlying anatomy, not its presumed morphogenesis.

### Atrial Arrangement
* **Appendage morphology:** Right atrial appendage is broad and triangular. Left atrial appendage is finger-like with a narrow neck. Pectinate muscles in the right appendage extend to the crux of the heart; in the left they are confined to the appendage mouth.
* **Situs solitus:** Usual arrangement — right appendage on the right, left on the left (>95% of cases).
* **Situs inversus:** Mirror-image arrangement (rare).
* **Isomerism:** Both appendages have the same morphology.
  - **Right isomerism:** Usually asplenia, bilateral superior caval veins, totally anomalous pulmonary venous connection (always), pulmonary stenosis/atresia, common atrioventricular valve, double-inlet ventricle.
  - **Left isomerism:** Usually polysplenia, interruption of the inferior caval vein with azygos continuation, bilateral superior caval veins, common atrial chamber.
* **Sinus node in isomerism:** Right isomerism — duplicated (one in each terminal groove). Left isomerism — no terminal grooves, node is rudimentary and located in the anterior interatrial groove near the atrioventricular junction.

### Atrioventricular Junctions
* **Concordant connections:** Each atrium connects to its morphologically appropriate ventricle.
* **Discordant connections:** Each atrium connects to its morphologically inappropriate ventricle.
* **Mixed (biventricular) connections:** With isomeric appendages — one junction concordant, the other discordant.
* **Double-inlet connection:** Both atriums connect to the same ventricle (functionally univentricular heart).
* **Absent connection:** One atrium has no connection to the ventricular mass (e.g., tricuspid atresia). Two subtypes — right-sided or left-sided absence.
* **Uniatrial but biventricular connection:** When a single atrioventricular valve straddles the septum in the setting of an absent connection.

### Ventricular Morphology and Topology
* **Right ventricle:** Coarse apical trabeculations.
* **Left ventricle:** Fine criss-crossing trabeculations.
* **Right-hand vs. left-hand topology:** The palmar surface of the hand placed on the septal surface of the morphologically right ventricle — thumb in the inlet, fingers in the outlet. Right-hand topology is usual; left-hand topology occurs with discordant connections.
* **Dominant ventricle in univentricular connections:** Can be left (most common), right, or indeterminate. The incomplete ventricle is always anterosuperior (when left ventricle is dominant) or posteroinferior (when right ventricle is dominant).

### Valvar Morphology
* **Separate valves vs. common valve:** Two atrioventricular junctions may be guarded by two separate valves or by a common valve (as in atrioventricular septal defect).
* **Imperforate valve:** The connection has formed but is blocked by fused leaflets. Must be distinguished from absence of connection (fibroadipose tissue in the atrioventricular groove).
* **Straddling:** Tension apparatus attaches to both sides of the ventricular septum.
* **Overriding:** The junction is connected to both ventricles. The **50% rule** — the overriding valve is assigned to the ventricle supporting its greater part.

### Ventriculoarterial Junctions
* **Concordant connections:** Aorta from left ventricle, pulmonary trunk from right ventricle.
* **Discordant connections:** Aorta from right ventricle, pulmonary trunk from left ventricle (transposition).
* **Double-outlet connections:** Both great arteries arise from the same ventricle (most often the right ventricle).
* **Single-outlet arrangement:** Only one arterial trunk is connected to the heart (common arterial trunk or atresia of the complementary trunk).
* **Arterial valvar morphology:** No tension apparatus. Valves can be perforate, imperforate, or overriding (50% rule applies).

### Infundibular Morphology
* **Components:** Parietal and septal components (outlet/infundibular septum) + ventriculoinfundibular fold (separates arterial from atrioventricular valves).
* **Muscular subpulmonary infundibulum:** Normal in the right ventricle. In tetralogy of Fallot — muscular subaortic infundibulum.
* **Fibrous continuity:** Attenuation of the ventriculoinfundibular fold produces arterial-atrioventricular valvar continuity (normal in the left ventricle).
* **Septomarginal trabeculation (septal band):** A Y-shaped structure on the septal surface of the right ventricle with two limbs. Conduction tissue is never contained within infundibular structures.

### Arterial Valvar and Truncal Relationships
* **Description method:** Aorta relative to the pulmonary valve using right-left, anteroposterior, and superoinferior coordinates. Eight coordinates combining lateral and anterior-posterior positions suffice.
* **Truncal relationship:** Describe whether trunks spiral around each other (normal) or ascend in parallel fashion (anomalous).

### Position of the Heart
* **Site within thorax:** Left chest, right chest (dextrocardia), or midline (mesocardia).
* **Apical orientation:** Left, middle, or right.

### Catalogue of Malformations
* After describing the cardiac template, all associated intracardiac malformations must be catalogued. No lesion should be presumed to be the only lesion until the rest of the heart has been established as normal.

## Chapter 7: Lesions with normal segmental connections</summary>

### Septal Anatomy
* **Atrial septum:** Primarily the floor of the oval fossa (septum primum); superior, anterosuperior and posterior rims are infoldings of atrial walls; only inferoanterior rim is true muscular septum contiguous with atrioventricular septum.
* **Atrioventricular separating structures:** Fibrous membranous septum superiorly and muscular sandwich (fibroadipose tissue in floor of triangle of Koch); absent in atrioventricular septal defects.
* **Ventricular septum:** Small interventricular membranous septum and large muscular portion; best analysed from right ventricular aspect; includes septomarginal trabeculation with body and limbs, septoparietal trabeculations and moderator band.

### Interatrial Communications
* **Ostium secundum defects:** Most common; deficiency, perforation or absence of oval fossa floor; patch closure preferred; risk to sinus node artery (anterior rim or superior fold) and aortic root.
* **Sinus venosus defects:** Superior (usually overriding SVC with anomalous right superior pulmonary veins) and inferior (rarer); outside true atrial septum; require pulmonary vein rerouting without venous obstruction or sinus node injury.
* **Unroofing of coronary sinus:** Often with persistent left superior caval vein; coronary sinus mouth functions as interatrial communication; closure depends on left SVC drainage (ligation, Glenn shunt or left atrial channel).

### Atrioventricular Septal Defects
* **Unifying feature:** Common atrioventricular junction with absence of AV separating structures.
* **Gerbode defects:** Rare with separate junctions — indirect (via deficient tricuspid leaflet) or direct (deficiency of AV component of membranous septum).
* **Valve morphology:** Five-leaflet common AV valve with superior and inferior bridging leaflets; left AV valve is trifoliate with extensive zone of apposition between bridging components.
* **Rastelli classification:** Reflects degree of right ventricular commitment of superior bridging leaflet (Types A–C).
* **Shunting:** Atrial, ventricular or both depending on attachments of bridging leaflets to atrial and ventricular septa.
* **Conduction axis:** Displaced inferiorly to apex of new nodal triangle (base at coronary sinus); protected by specific suture strategies relative to coronary sinus and inferior bridging leaflet.
* **Left ventricular outflow tract:** Often narrow and elongated; risk of postoperative obstruction; modified single-patch technique controversial.

### Malformations of Atrioventricular Valves
* **Straddling and overriding:** Tension apparatus or valvar orifice crosses ventricular septum; anomalous conduction axis follows malaligned septum.
* **Ebstein’s malformation:** Rotational displacement of septal and mural tricuspid leaflet hinge points away from AV junction; atrialised right ventricular inlet.
* **Other lesions:** Dilation of AV junction, leaflet dysplasia, prolapse (elongated cords), true cleft of mitral aortic leaflet, parachute deformity (often in Shone’s syndrome).

### Malformations of Arterial Valves and Outflow Tracts
* **Aortic stenosis:** Unicuspid (unicommissural), bicuspid (equal or unequal with raphe), tricuspid (calcific in elderly); subvalvar (fibrous shelf, fibromuscular tunnel, dynamic) or supravalvar (sinutubular junction tethering).
* **Aortic regurgitation:** Congenital, post-endocarditis (root abscess with fistula risk), or associated with VSD and leaflet prolapse.
* **Pulmonary stenosis:** Dome-shaped fused commissures (valvar) or waist-like narrowing (supravalvar); excellent surgical relief with later regression of secondary infundibular hypertrophy.
* **Pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum:** Spectrum of right ventricular hypoplasia; usually duct-dependent.

### Tetralogy of Fallot
* **Hallmark anatomy:** Anterocephalad malalignment of outlet septum plus septoparietal trabecular hypertrophy causing subpulmonary obstruction; overriding aorta with perimembranous or muscular VSD.
* **Surgical priorities:** Secure VSD closure (tunnelling aorta to left ventricle) and complete relief of RVOT obstruction (resection of outlet septum and septoparietal trabeculations).
* **Pulmonary atresia variant:** Pulmonary supply via arterial duct or major aortopulmonary collateral arteries; success determined by adequacy of confluent intrapericardial pulmonary arteries.
* **Absent pulmonary valve syndrome:** Rudimentary leaflets with massive pulmonary arterial dilation causing tracheobronchial compression.

## Chapter 8: Lesions in hearts with abnormal segmental connections</summary>

### Double-Inlet Ventricle

* **Definition:** Greater parts of both atrioventricular junctions (right and left atrial chambers) connect to the same ventricle, either through two separate valves (usual) or a common valve. Hearts are functionally univentricular, almost always containing two chambers (one dominant, one incomplete lacking inlet component).

* **Surgical approach:** Fontan procedure (or modifications) is the treatment of choice; septation is possible only in rare, highly selected patients with competent atrioventricular valves and favourable anatomy.

* **Dominant left ventricle (most common):** Both junctions connect to dominant left ventricle; incomplete right ventricle is anterosuperior, left- or right-sided. Ventricular septal defect (VSD) — the communication between ventricles — may be restrictive and require enlargement.

* **Conduction axis in double-inlet left ventricle:** Arises from anomalous anterocephalad node beneath right atrial appendage (not at triangle of Koch). The atrioventricular bundle penetrates fibrous continuity between pulmonary and right atrioventricular valves, encircles anterior quadrants of pulmonary orifice, descends on left ventricular aspect of septum. Sutures must be placed ≥1 cm above right atrioventricular valve attachments to avoid node.

* **VSD enlargement:** Safest method is removing wedge of muscular ventricular septum closest to obtuse margin of heart. Conduction axis extends posteroinferior to defect when viewed from incomplete right ventricle.

* **Double-inlet right ventricle (rare):** Usually with double outlet from dominant right ventricle, straddling/overriding of left atrioventricular valve. Incomplete left ventricle is posteroinferior pouch. Fontan procedure indicated.

* **Solitary indeterminate ventricle:** Usually associated with atrial isomerism; coarse apical trabeculations and crossing tension apparatus make septation impossible.

### Tricuspid and Mitral Atresia

* **Tricuspid atresia:** Complete absence of right atrioventricular connection (muscular floor in right atrium). Right ventricle incomplete, lacking inlet component. Apical ventricular septum extends to acute margin, not to crux.

* **Surgical palliation:** Fontan procedure (total cavopulmonary connection, now mostly with external conduits). Preservation of sinus node and its artery (traverses interatrial groove) is critical to avoid postoperative atrial arrhythmias.

* **Conduction axis in tricuspid atresia with discordant connections:** Runs posteroinferior to VSD (as in double-inlet left ventricle). Exception: apical trabecular defect — axis is superior relative to defect.

* **Imperforate tricuspid valve (rare):** May occur with pulmonary atresia/intact ventricular septum, with VSD, or with Ebstein's malformation.

* **Mitral atresia:** Usually associated with aortic atresia (hypoplastic left heart syndrome). Norwood protocol followed by modified Fontan. When aortic outflow is patent, right atrium connects to morphologically left ventricle; pulmonary venous return crosses atrial septal defect to reach dominant ventricle.

### Transposition (Concordant AV + Discordant VA Connections)

* **Definition (default option for "transposition"):** Concordant atrioventricular with discordant ventriculoarterial connections. The term "d-transposition" is inadequate because left-sided aorta can occur with usual atrial arrangement.

* **Atrial redirection procedures (Mustard, Senning):** Sinus node location unaffected by discordant VA connections. The artery to sinus node courses through superior interatrial groove, crossing superior border of oval fossa (septum secundum) — at risk during septal incision.

* **Atrial conduction:** No insulated internodal pathways; preferential conduction via anisotropic aggregation of cardiomyocytes. Preserving terminal crest and superior border of oval fossa reduces postoperative arrhythmias.

* **Ventricular septal defect in transposition:** Usually opens between outlet components with malalignment of outlet septum. Perimembranous type has fibrous continuity between tricuspid and pulmonary valves (conduction axis at risk). Muscular type (posteroinferior rim) protects axis. Doubly committed and juxta-arterial defects are rarest.

* **Subpulmonary obstruction:** Produced by same lesions that cause subaortic obstruction in normal hearts (fibrous shelf, tissue tags, anomalous tension apparatus, outlet septum deviation). Fibrous shelf overlies left bundle branch; resection hazardous.

* **Coronary arteries in transposition:** Always arise from aortic sinuses adjacent to pulmonary trunk. Naming convention (Leiden): observer stands in non-adjacent aortic sinus, looks toward pulmonary trunk — right-hand sinus = #1, left-hand sinus = #2. Seven of eight possible patterns observed. Intramural course and high origin create surgical challenges but are manageable with appropriate technique.

### Congenitally Corrected Transposition (Discordant AV + Discordant VA Connections)

* **Definition:** Discordant atrioventricular and discordant ventriculoarterial connections. Can exist with usual or mirror-imaged atrial arrangement (not with isomerism).

* **Septal malalignment:** Inlet part of muscular ventricular septum and atrial septum diverge; subpulmonary outflow tract from morphologically left ventricle wedged into gap.

* **Conduction axis (anomalous anterolateral node):** Normal node at triangle of Koch cannot penetrate fibrous junction. Anomalous node gives rise to bundle that penetrates lateral to pulmonary-mitral fibrous continuity, runs around anterior quadrants of subpulmonary outflow tract, descends on muscular septum. Left bundle branch distributes in right-sided morphologically left ventricle; right bundle penetrates septum to left-sided morphologically right ventricle.

* **Associated lesions (requiring surgery):** VSD (up to 75%, usually perimembranous, inlet type), pulmonary stenosis, left-sided (morphologically tricuspid) valve anomalies.

* **VSD closure:** Safest approach — sutures placed from left-sided morphologically right ventricular aspect. Conduction axis runs around subpulmonary outflow tract to descend on anterosuperior margin of defect.

* **Ebstein's malformation of left-sided tricuspid valve:** Downward displacement of septal and mural leaflets. Conduction tissues in right-sided junction are out of danger area.

* **Double switch procedure (current trend):** Atrial redirection combined with arterial switch or interventricular tunnel to aorta, plus conduit from morphologically right ventricle to pulmonary arteries. Restores morphologically left ventricle to systemic circulation.

### Double-Outlet Ventricle

* **Definition (50% rule):** More than half of the circumference of both arterial valves originates from the same ventricle. Bilateral infundibulums are not required — fibrous continuity between arterial and atrioventricular valves can exist.

* **Interventricular communication (preferred term over "VSD"):** Location relative to great arteries determines surgical approach. Outlet septum (always devoid of conduction tissue) separates two outlets from right ventricle.

* **Subtypes:** Subaortic defect (most common — aorta posterior and to right, spiralling trunks); Taussig-Bing variant (subpulmonary defect, parallel trunks, aorta anterior or side-by-side); non-committed defect (between inlets or in AV septal defect — may preclude biventricular repair).

* **Conduction axis:** Protected by muscular inferior rim when caudal limb of septomarginal trabeculation fuses with ventriculoinfundibular fold. Perimembranous defects have axis at risk.

* **Surgical rules:** Outlet septum can be resected or used for suture anchorage. Ventriculoinfundibular fold contains right coronary artery — avoid extensive dissection.

### Common Arterial Trunk

* **Definition:** Solitary trunk supplies systemic, pulmonary, and coronary arteries; common ventriculoarterial junction guarded by common arterial valve (distinguishes from aortopulmonary window). Solitary trunk (no intrapericardial pulmonary arteries) is best classified with tetralogy of Fallot with pulmonary atresia.

* **Truncal valve:** Usually overrides septum; can be predominantly or exclusively from right ventricle. Dysplasia, quadrifoliate valves (often unequal leaflets), and incompetence/stenosis are significant post-repair (valve becomes aorta).

* **Interventricular communication:** Usually juxta-arterial with fibrous continuity between truncal and mitral valves. Muscular posteroinferior rim (fusion of posterocaudal limb with ventriculoinfundibular fold) protects conduction axis. Perimembranous type (truncal-tricuspid continuity) puts axis at risk.

* **Classification (simplified — aortic vs. pulmonary dominance):** Most are aortic-dominant; pulmonary arteries arise adjacent from leftwards/posterior component, often with very short confluent segment. Pulmonary-dominant trunk (rare) associated with aortic arch interruption or coarctation.

* **Coronary arteries:** Left coronary artery often arises above sinutubular junction, may have transmural (intramural) course. At risk when pulmonary arteries are separated from trunk.

### Concordant Ventriculoarterial Connections with Parallel Arterial Trunks

* **Definition:** Arterial trunks arise from morphologically appropriate ventricles but extend in parallel (not spiralling). Extremely rare.

* **Normal spiralling:** Counter-clockwise (right-hand topology) in normal heart; clockwise (left-hand topology) in mirror-imaged arrangement.

* **With concordant AV connections (usual atrial arrangement):** Aorta left-sided and anterior, often supported by complete muscular infundibulum in left ventricle. Can occur with double-inlet left ventricle (Holmes heart variant with parallel trunks) — incomplete right ventricle left-sided, pulmonary trunk parallel to aorta.

* **With discordant AV connections:** Produces haemodynamics of transposition (concordant VA but discordant AV). Left-hand topology; optimal therapy is atrial redirection to restore morphologically left ventricle to systemic role. Conduction axis from anterolateral node, descending anterocephalad relative to perimembranous VSD.

## Chapter 9: Anomalous Systemic Venous Drainage and Abnormalities of the Great Vessels</summary>

### Systemic Venous Anomalies

* **Persistent left superior caval vein (PLSVC):** Most common caval vein malformation. In three-fifths of cases, a brachiocephalic vein connects the left vein to the right superior caval vein — simple clamping or ligation is then possible. When isolated, the vein receives the hemiazygos vein, passes between the left atrial appendage and left pulmonary veins, then connects to the coronary sinus (through an enlarged orifice) and drains into the right atrium. The Thebesian valve is often attenuated or absent.
* **Levoatrial cardinal vein:** Rare channel connecting the left atrium to the systemic venous system. Found with mitral atresia where it functions as the only route for pulmonary venous return — produces "snowman" configuration comparable to anomalous pulmonary venous connection.
* **Inferior caval vein (ICV) anomalies:** Interruption of the terminal segment with return of lower body venous blood through the azygos or hemiazygos systems — most frequent anomaly of the ICV, seen most commonly with isomerism of the left atrial appendages. Hepatic veins drain independently into the right- or left-sided atrium.
* **Eustachian valve persistence:** Can direct flow from the inferior caval vein across an oval fossa defect into the left atrium, producing right-to-left shunting. Can also be iatrogenic after improper closure of low-lying atrial septal defects.
* **Right superior caval vein anomalies:** Extremely rare. May be diminished or completely absent, with head/neck/arm venous return passing through a persistent left superior caval vein to the right atrium via the coronary sinus (or rarely directly into the left atrium). Only the last situation requires surgical intervention.

### Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection

* **Definition:** Pulmonary veins connect anomalously when they do not join the morphologically left atrium. Unilateral totally anomalous connection — all veins from one lung connect elsewhere. Bilateral totally anomalous connection — all veins from both lungs connect elsewhere (necessarily implies total anomalous pulmonary venous connection, TAPVC).
* **Supracardiac drainage (most common):** Connection to brachiocephalic vein → superior caval vein. Produces classic "snowman" configuration on chest radiography. Obstruction occurs when the vertical vein crosses in front of the left bronchus, or more typically when the venous channel is caught between the left pulmonary artery and left bronchus (bronchopulmonary vice).
* **Azygos vein drainage:** Left pulmonary veins cross vertically beneath the heart, ascend in the right paravertebral gutter, receive right pulmonary veins, then drain to the superior caval vein via the azygos vein.
* **Cardiac drainage (coronary sinus):** All four pulmonary veins connect to the coronary sinus, which drains to the right atrium. Totally anomalous connection directly to the right atrium (other than via coronary sinus) occurs only in isomerism of the right atrial appendages.
* **Infradiaphragmatic drainage:** Common channel lies outside the pericardium posterior to the left atrium. A vertical vein passes through the diaphragm as a single channel to enter the portal vein or ductus venosus. Obstruction occurs after ductus venosus closure. Occasionally the channel fragments into branches connecting to gastric veins.
* **Scimitar syndrome:** Partial right-sided anomalous connection to the inferior caval vein — named for its Turkish sword appearance on chest radiograph.
* **Mixed drainage:** Any combination possible — e.g., upper pulmonary veins drain supracardially to superior caval vein while inferior veins drain to coronary sinus.
* **Surgical considerations for coronary sinus drainage:** The coronary sinus has its own myocardial wall separate from the left atrium. Both walls can be incised to unroof the sinus. The sinus orifice can be made confluent with the atrial septal defect (typically within the oval fossa). Important to avoid the atrioventricular node at the apex of Koch's triangle. Incision creates a route to the inferior atrioventricular groove — potential opening to extracardiac spaces.
* **Surgical considerations for infradiaphragmatic drainage:** The extrapericardial common pulmonary venous trunk is farther from the posterior left atrial wall than expected. The anastomosis between trunk and left atrium is vulnerable to obstruction, particularly at its lateral extreme.

### Aortic Anomalies — Coarctation

* **Definition:** Congenitally derived discrete shelf-like (Figure 9.24) or waist-like (Figure 9.25) lesion within the aorta causing obstruction. Most often adjacent to the patent arterial duct opening or arterial ligament.
* **Ductal tissue:** Evidence supporting its inclusion in the coarctation shelf is unequivocal. All ductal tissue must be removed during repair.
* **Tubular hypoplasia:** Usually accompanies coarctation but is anatomically independent. When involving the isthmic segment, represents part of a spectrum leading to atresia or interruption of the aortic arch.
* **Patent vs. closed duct:** Patent duct indicates associated anomaly promoting increased pulmonary trunk flow and diverting blood from proximal aorta — the associated anomaly determines surgical therapy. Closed duct coarctation is likely an isolated lesion (except occasional association with bicuspid aortic valve).
* **Collateral circulation:** Well-developed collaterals (Figure 9.27) — little danger with aortic cross-clamping. Poorly developed collaterals (Figure 9.28) — clamping may cause: left ventricular strain from proximal hypertension (cerebrovascular accident from berry aneurysm rupture); distal aortic hypotension (<50 mmHg) endangering splanchnic and spinal vascular beds.
* **Spinal cord protection:** Best preserved by interrupting none, or as few as possible, of the intercostal vessels.
* **Abbott's artery:** Anomalous artery arising from posteromedial aspect of proximal descending aorta. If not properly managed, can lead to substantial bleeding during repair.

### Interruption of the Aortic Arch

* **Definition:** Discontinuity of the aorta — may be a fibrous cord/bridge or absolute gap. Interruption at three possible positions: at the isthmus; between left subclavian and left common carotid arteries; between left common carotid and brachiocephalic arteries (Figure 9.34).
* **Associated anomalies:** Almost always a patent connection to the distal aorta through the duct. A proximal septal defect (usually VSD, occasionally aortopulmonary window) is the rule. VSD typically with posterior and leftward displacement of the outlet septum, producing subaortic stenosis.
* **Aberrant retro-oesophageal right subclavian artery:** Fairly frequent associated anomaly (Figure 9.32). Symptoms of vascular ring are unlikely unless a ring is created while reconstructing the arch.

### Vascular Rings

* **Embryology:** Initially bilaterally symmetrical arterial system with five sets of arches encircling the gut. Only third, fourth, and sixth arches remain postnatally. Normal regression of right-sided structures leaves a left arch and left-sided aorta. Failure of regression leaves a double arch forming a ring around the trachea and oesophagus.
* **Hypothetical double aortic arch (Stewart):** Midline anterior aorta continued anteriorly on each side by fourth arches to bilateral posterior aortic segments joining to form a midline descending aorta. Common carotid and subclavian arteries arise from each fourth arch. An arterial duct connects each arch to its companion pulmonary artery (Figure 9.38).
* **Double aortic arch:** Persistent patency of entire double arch causes compression of the tracheo-oesophageal pedicle (Figures 9.39, 9.40). Size of component parts is as important as anatomical arrangement in producing symptoms — accounts for high incidence of symptoms with double arch.
* **Right aortic arch with aberrant left subclavian:** Duct arising from left subclavian artery (from right arch) produces a vascular ring (Figures 9.43, 9.44). Compression is not obvious until the duct is divided and ends spring apart (Figure 9.45).
* **Right aortic arch significance:** Associated with ~25% of tetralogy of Fallot cases and ~50% of patients with common arterial trunks. Usually has mirror-imaged brachiocephalic arteries.
* **Kommerell diverticulum:** Proximal portion of the atretic right arch (Figure 9.41).
* **Isolated brachiocephalic arteries:** Arise from a pulmonary artery via a persistently patent arterial duct. Can involve the brachiocephalic artery, common carotid, or subclavian arteries.

### Pulmonary Arterial Anomalies

* **Most common malformation:** Right or left pulmonary artery of aortic origin, usually via the arterial duct. With time, the duct closes → apparent unilateral absence of that pulmonary artery. Common in tetralogy of Fallot or double-outlet right ventricle; half of patients have otherwise normal hearts. True absence of the hilar pulmonary artery is rare.
* **Direct aortic origin (hemitruncus — term not recommended):** Usually the right pulmonary artery arises directly from the ascending aorta (Figure 9.49). Almost always two normally formed arterial valves. Surgical repair: detachment from aorta and reattachment to pulmonary trunk.
* **Ductal tissue caution:** During reconstruction, ensure ductal tissue is not incorporated into anastomosis — may subsequently constrict and produce stenosis.
* **Unilateral absence of pulmonary artery:** Pulmonary hypertension and hypertensive vascular disease in the normally connected lung — receives entire right ventricular output due to disproportionately small vascular bed.
* **Pulmonary arterial sling:** Left pulmonary artery arises from right pulmonary artery (Figures 9.50, 9.51), passes between trachea and oesophagus to the left pulmonary hilum (Figure 9.52) — causes repeated respiratory problems, requires division and transplantation of the offending artery.
* **Right lung agenesis:** Displaced heart pulls on left pulmonary artery, drawing the ligament and descending aorta across the left bronchus, compressing bronchus against spinal column. Division of ligament and aortic grafting may be necessary.

### Persistent Arterial Duct and Aortopulmonary Window

* **Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA):** First congenital malformation cured by operative intervention (Gross, 1939). Anatomy almost always predictable, results uniformly excellent. May be right- or left-sided (left overwhelmingly more common).
* **Surgical anatomy (left lateral thoracotomy approach):** Duct arises from posterosuperior aspect of pulmonary trunk-left pulmonary artery junction. Courses posteriorly and leftwards to join the aortic arch-descending aorta junction, just distal to and opposite the left subclavian artery origin. Pulmonary end covered by pericardial fold; aortic end by parietal pleura.
* **Vagus nerve and recurrent laryngeal nerve:** Best anatomical guide. Vagus passes along subclavian artery, crosses aortic arch, then heads posteriorly. At the level of the duct, it gives off the recurrent nerve, which curves beneath the inferomedial wall of the duct before ascending along the posteromedial aspect of the aorta into the tracheo-oesophageal groove (Figure 9.55).
* **Thoracic duct caution:** Division of major lymph vessels leads to chylothorax — must be ligated if inadvertently divided.
* **Ductal dimensions (infant/child):** 1–15 mm in length and diameter. Rarely aneurysmally dilated (Figure 9.56).
* **Surgical technique:** Short, fat duct — cross-clamp, divide, oversew. Long, thin duct — triple ligation technique is safe and effective.
* **Aortopulmonary window:** Always associated with separate aortic and pulmonary valves (Figure 9.57) — distinguishes from common arterial trunk (solitary arterial valve). Defect located in right lateral wall of pulmonary trunk, anterior and opposite to the right pulmonary artery origin. Opening into left side of ascending aorta just distal to sinutubular junction. Surgical repair via incision in ascending aorta using cardiopulmonary bypass — closed methods not advisable.

### Coronary Artery Anomalies

* **Classification (descriptive, not major/minor):** Anomalous origin from arterial roots; anomalous epicardial course; anomalous communications with other cardiac structures; or combinations.
* **Anomalous origin from pulmonary trunk — ALCAPA (Bland-White-Garland syndrome):** Left coronary artery from pulmonary trunk (Figures 9.59–9.61). Presents in infancy with left ventricular ischaemia (Figure 9.62). Well-developed collaterals may delay presentation to childhood or adolescence. Surgical repair: transfer the artery with a button of pulmonary sinus back to the left-hand adjacent sinus of the aorta. Aortopulmonary tunnel (Takeuchi) now rare. Simple ligation usually inferior.
* **Anomalous right coronary artery from pulmonary trunk:** Less symptomatic, often not requiring surgery. If encountered, readily transferable to aortic root.
* **Anomalous aortic origin (intramural course):** Origin from inappropriate sinus (right coronary from left sinus or vice versa). Artery courses between arterial trunks, crossing the peripheral end of the zone of apposition between valvar leaflets (Figure 9.63) — established cause of sudden cardiac death.
* **Single coronary artery:** All three arteries from same aortic sinus (usually right-hand sinus). Left coronary branches have anomalous epicardial course — may involve anterior interventricular branch tracking between aorta and pulmonary trunk (potential constriction) or main stem crossing subpulmonary infundibulum towards left atrial appendage (Figure 9.64).
* **Congenital atresia of left main stem:** Substrate for sudden death in adolescent or young adult (Figure 9.66). Rarely diagnosed prior to event.
* **Muscular bridging:** May be of relevance for sudden death, though significance not fully established (Figure 9.68).
* **Coronary artery fistulas:** Most commonly involve right coronary artery. May connect to right atrium (Figures 9.69, 9.70), superior caval vein, coronary sinus, right ventricle, pulmonary trunk, pulmonary vein, or left ventricle. Can also originate from left coronary artery (Figure 9.71). Communication may be large solitary orifice or complex worm-like aneurysmal cavity. Large shunts cause marked artery dilation and coronary steal with ischaemia.
* **Aortoventricular tunnels:** Rare lesions bypassing the hinge of an aortic valvar leaflet (Figure 9.72). Typically between aorta and left ventricle (aorto-right ventricular tunnels exist — Figure 9.73). Channel courses from left ventricle between the base of the afflicted aortic sinus and the leaflet, extending through the tissue plane separating aortic and pulmonary roots. Base supported by ventricular myocardium; roof made of arterial wall.

## Chapter 10: Positional anomalies of the heart</summary>

### Principles of Cardiac Malposition
* **Cardiac malposition is not a diagnosis:** Any normal or abnormal segmental combination can be found in an abnormally located heart. The presence of malposition mandates complete sequential segmental analysis (see Chapter 6).
* **Two independent features:** Account must be taken separately of (1) the position of the cardiac mass relative to the chest silhouette, and (2) the direction of the cardiac apex.
* **Descriptive system:** Heart described as mostly in left chest, mostly in right chest, or symmetrical. Apical orientation described as left, right, or middle. This avoids confusing terms like dextrocardia, dextroposition, dextrorotation.

### Dextrocardia and Mirror-Imaged Arrangement
* **Normal:** Apex left, two-thirds of mass left of midline.
* **Mirror-imaged atrial arrangement:** Usually accompanied by mirror-imaged cardiac arrangement — apex points right, mass in right hemithorax (Figure 10.1).
* **Isomerism:** No norm for position or apex orientation.

### Ectopia Cordis (Exteriorisation of the Heart)
* **Thoracic type (most common):** Sternal defect, absence of parietal pericardium, heart protrudes directly from thorax. Associated omphalocele, small thoracic cavity.
* **Thoracoabdominal type:** Sternal defect confluent with abdominal wall and diaphragmatic deficiencies. Heart and abdominal organs displaced into omphalocele.
* **Cantrell syndrome:** Cleft of lower sternum, heart beneath skin in upper epigastrium, omphalocele. Frequently associated with complex intracardiac defects and left ventricular diverticulum.

### Criss-Cross and Superoinferior Ventricles
* **Criss-cross heart (twisted atrioventricular connections):** Ventricular relationships (or rarely topology) not as anticipated for given atrioventricular connections. Consequence of rotation of ventricular mass around its long axis.
* **Congenitally corrected transposition with criss-cross:** Morphologically right ventricle is right-sided (not left-sided as expected). Anterior interventricular coronary artery arises from right-sided coronary artery — key diagnostic clue.
* **Concordant connections with criss-cross:** Morphologically right ventricle is left-sided (Figures 10.5, 10.6).
* **Superoinferior ventricles:** Cardiac mass tilted as well as rotated. Usually associated with criss-cross. Either ventricle can be uppermost (most often right ventricle superior).
* **Conduction tissue:** Governed by connections between chambers, not by position.
* **Segmental disharmony (exceedingly rare):** Ventricular topology not as expected for atrioventricular connections (Figure 10.8). Requires description of ventricular topology in addition to atrioventricular connections.

### Isomerism of the Atrial Appendages — Surgical Significance
* **Classification based on appendage morphology (not splenic status):** Surgeon can readily distinguish right vs. left appendages intraoperatively (Figure 10.9). Splenic morphology does not always correspond to atrial anatomy.
* **Right isomerism (Figures 10.12–10.19):** Almost always severe intracardiac anomalies — totally anomalous pulmonary venous connection (even when veins join heart), major systemic venous anomalies, common atrioventricular valve, double-inlet ventricle, pulmonary stenosis/atresia, bilateral sinus nodes. Usually asplenia.
* **Left isomerism (Figures 10.20–10.21):** May present undiagnosed. Sinus node hypoplastic, located near atrioventricular junction. Interruption of inferior caval vein with azygos continuation is frequent. May have common atrioventricular valve (in severe cases). Pulmonary stenosis/atresia and univentricular connection not typical. Aortic coarctation frequent. Usually polysplenia.
* **Conduction tissue in isomerism with common AV valve and biventricular connections:**
  - **Right-hand ventricular topology (Figure 10.22, left panel):** Atrioventricular conduction axis in usual posterior position.
  - **Left-hand ventricular topology (Figure 10.22, right panel):** Dual atrioventricular nodes (posterior and anterior) connected by a sling of conduction tissue along the crest of the muscular ventricular septum — entire septal edge at risk during surgery.
