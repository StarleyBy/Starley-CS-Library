## 10. Non-Cardiovascular Comorbidities</summary><div class="details-content">

Non-cardiovascular comorbidities are exceptionally prevalent in patients with heart failure across all LVEF phenotypes. They impair functional capacity, deteriorate health-related quality of life, accelerate disease progression, increase non-cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, and frequently confound the assessment of circulatory congestion.

### 10.1 Obesity
Obesity contributes directly to systemic hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and type 2 diabetes mellitus, while exerting detrimental mechanical, metabolic, and pro-inflammatory effects on myocardial structure and microvascular endothelium. Obesity is particularly prevalent in HFpEF (accounting for 30%–40% of European cases and up to 60%–70% in North America). Circulating natriuretic peptide concentrations are paradoxically lower in obese patients due to augmented clearance receptor expression and adipose tissue degradation, necessitating lower diagnostic thresholds.

In patients with symptomatic heart failure and obesity, structured lifestyle modification targeting weight reduction is foundational. Following the landmark STEP-HFpEF and STEP-HFpEF DM trials, the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) **semaglutide** (or dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonist **tirzepatide**) substantially improves physical symptoms, Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) clinical summary scores, 6-minute walk distance, and body weight, while reducing HF hospitalizations.

### Recommendation Table 18 — Recommendations for obesity in patients with heart failure

| Recommendations | Class | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Semaglutide or tirzepatide should be considered for patients with symptomatic HF, LVEF ≥45%, and BMI ≥30 kg/m², regardless of diabetes status, to reduce body weight, and improve exercise capacity and QoL. | **IIa** | **B1** |
| Bariatric surgery may be considered in obese patients with HF and a BMI ≥35 kg/m² to reduce body weight when repetitive and structured lifestyle changes combined with weight-reducing medications do not result in maintained weight loss. | **IIb** | **C** |

### 10.2 Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus
Type 2 diabetes mellitus is present in 35%–45% of patients with chronic HF. SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) are recommended for all patients with HF and T2DM, irrespective of LVEF, providing simultaneous cardioprotection, renoprotection, and glycaemic control. Metformin remains recommended as a safe foundational agent in patients with eGFR $\ge 30\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2$. Thiazolidinediones (glitazones) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin are contraindicated due to increased risk of fluid retention and HF hospitalization.

### 10.3 Chronic Kidney Disease and Hyperkalaemia
Renal dysfunction and heart failure coexist bidirectionally as cardiorenal syndrome. Moderate-to-severe CKD (eGFR $< 60\text{ mL/min/1.73 m}^2$) is found in $> 50\%$ of HF cohorts. Foundational neurohormonal blockers (ARNI/ACE-I, MRAs, SGLT2-Is) should not be withheld or discontinued for modest transient declines in eGFR ($< 30\%$) upon initiation, provided volume status and serum potassium remain acceptable. To prevent hyperkalaemia and allow optimal titration of MRAs and ARNI, modern potassium binders (patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate) are recommended. In patients with T2DM and CKD, the non-steroidal MRA **finerenone** reduces incident HF hospitalizations and slows renal disease progression.

### 10.4 Iron Deficiency and Anaemia
Iron deficiency (ID) occurs in up to 50% of patients with ambulatory HF and $> 70\%$ with decompensated HF, independent of anaemia. It is biologically defined as **serum ferritin $< 100\text{ \mu g/L}$**, or **ferritin $100\text{--}299\text{ \mu g/L}$ with transferrin saturation (TSAT) $< 20\%$**. Iron deficiency impairs skeletal and cardiac mitochondrial energetics, reduces peak exercise capacity ($pVO_2$), and increases hospitalizations.

Intravenous iron repletion with **ferric carboxymaltose (FCM)** or **ferric derisomaltose (FDI)** is recommended to alleviate symptoms, improve functional capacity, and reduce HF hospitalizations in symptomatic HFrEF/HFmrEF patients with confirmed iron deficiency. Oral iron administration is ineffective and not recommended.

### Recommendation Table 19 — Recommendations for iron deficiency in patients with heart failure

| Recommendations | Class | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Intravenous iron supplementation is recommended in patients with symptomatic HFrEF and iron deficiency to alleviate HF symptoms and improve QoL. | **I** | **B1** |
| Intravenous iron supplementation should be considered in patients with symptomatic HFrEF and iron deficiency to reduce the risk of HFH. | **IIa** | **B1** |

### 10.5 Cancer and Cardio-Oncology
Cancer and heart failure share common epidemiological risk factors and biological pathways. Antineoplastic regimens—particularly anthracyclines (doxorubicin), HER2 inhibitors (trastuzumab), VEGF signaling inhibitors, proteasome inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors—can induce cancer therapy-related cardiac dysfunction (CTRCD). Multidisciplinary evaluation by a cardio-oncology team is recommended, including baseline risk assessment, serial echocardiography with global longitudinal strain (GLS), and biomarker monitoring (hs-cTn, NT-proBNP). Pre-emptive neurohormonal therapy with ACE-I/ARB and beta-blockers is indicated in high-risk patients.

### 10.6 Lung Disease and Sleep-Disordered Breathing
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and asthma co-occur in 20%–30% of HF patients. Cardioselective beta-1 blockers (bisoprolol, metoprolol succinate, nebivolol) are safe, well tolerated, and not contraindicated in COPD. Both obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) and central sleep apnoea (CSA, including Cheyne-Stokes breathing) are highly prevalent in HFrEF. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is indicated for symptomatic OSA. Conversely, **adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) is strictly contraindicated in patients with HFrEF and predominant central sleep apnoea**, following the SERVE-HF trial which demonstrated increased cardiovascular and all-cause mortality.

### Recommendation Table 20 — Recommendation Table 20 — Recommendations for lung disease and sleep-disordered breathing

|---|---|---|

| Adaptive servo-ventilation is not recommended in patients with HFrEF and sleep-disordered breathing with predominant central sleep apnoea because of an increased risk of CV and all-cause death. | **III** | **A** |

### 10.7 Anxiety, Depression, and Cognitive Impairment
Clinically significant depressive symptoms and anxiety affect up to 40% of HF patients, correlating with poor treatment adherence, impaired self-care, frequent emergency visits, and elevated mortality. Routine screening using validated questionnaires (PHQ-9, GAD-7) is recommended. Cognitive impairment (vascular, neurodegenerative, or secondary to cerebral hypoperfusion) mandates simplified medication regimens, blister packaging, and direct family caregiver involvement.

### 10.8 Frailty
Frailty is a multidimensional clinical syndrome characterized by decreased physiological reserve and vulnerability to stressors, affecting 30%–50% of elderly HF patients. Identification using standardized tools (e.g., Clinical Frailty Scale, Fried phenotype) is recommended to tailor therapeutic goals, individualize invasive treatment decisions, and incorporate structured physical rehabilitation and nutritional support.

### Recommendation Table 21 — Recommendations for anxiety, depression, and frailty

| Recommendations | Class | Level |
|---|---|---|
| Assessment of anxiety, depression and frailty should be considered in patients with HF to support the development of personalized care plans and to identify factors that may contribute to adverse outcomes. | **IIa** | **C** |

### 10.9 Other Non-Cardiovascular Comorbidities
- **Gout and Hyperuricaemia:** Hyperuricaemia is frequently provoked by loop diuretics and impaired renal clearance. For acute gout flares, low-dose colchicine is preferred; **NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors are strictly contraindicated** as they induce sodium/water retention, blunting of diuretic response, acute kidney injury, and precipitated HF decompensation. Allopurinol or febuxostat is indicated for chronic urate-lowering.
- **Thyroid Dysfunction:** Both hyperthyroidism (precipitating high-output failure, tachyarrhythmias, and worsening ischaemia) and hypothyroidism (inducing pericardial effusion, bradycardia, and diastolic dysfunction) should be diagnosed and treated according to endocrine guidelines. Baseline and serial TSH monitoring is mandatory in patients receiving amiodarone.
- **Infections and Vaccinations:** Respiratory viral infections (influenza, SARS-CoV-2, RSV) and Streptococcus pneumoniae represent potent precipitants of acute decompensation. Routine annual influenza vaccination, pneumococcal vaccination, and COVID-19 boosters are recommended.

### Recommendation Table 22 — Recommendation Table 22 — Recommendations for other non-cardiovascular comorbidities

|---|---|---|

| NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors are not recommended in patients with HF as they increase the risk of HF worsening and HFH. | **III** | **B2** |

## 11. Multidisciplinary Team Management, Long-Term Follow-up, and Patient Care</summary><div class="details-content">

Heart failure is a complex, multisystem, chronic illness requiring an organized, multidisciplinary care delivery infrastructure that transcends acute episodic hospital care. Integrated disease management programmes deliver structured patient education, symptom monitoring, rapid medical optimization, lifestyle coaching, and psychosocial support, dramatically lowering HF rehospitalizations and improving overall survival.

### 11.2 Patient Education, Self-Care, and Lifestyle Counselling
Effective self-care reduces admissions and enhances treatment durability:
- **Symptom Monitoring & Action Plans:** Patients must be educated to recognize early signs of fluid retention (e.g., sudden weight gain $> 2\text{ kg}$ over 3 days, increasing ankle oedema, worsening orthopnoea) and follow an individualized diuretic adjustment plan.
- **Dietary Sodium & Fluid Intake:** Extreme salt restriction is unnecessary and potentially harmful; moderate sodium intake ($< 2.3\text{ g/day}$ of sodium or $< 5\text{ g/day}$ of salt) is advised. Fluid restriction ($1.5\text{--}2.0\text{ L/day}$) is reserved for severe hyponatraemia ($[\text{Na}^+] < 130\text{ mmol/L}$) or refractory volume overload.
- **Alcohol and Smoking:** Complete smoking cessation and avoidance of illicit cardiotoxic drugs is mandatory. Alcohol should be avoided entirely in alcohol-induced cardiomyopathy, and restricted to $\le 3$ standard units weekly in other forms.

### 11.3 Exercise Training and Supervised Cardiac Rehabilitation
Structured, supervised exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation is recommended for all stable patients with heart failure, irrespective of LVEF. Regular exercise improves skeletal muscle oxidative capacity, endothelial function, peak oxygen consumption ($pVO_2$), autonomic tone, and quality of life, while reducing all-cause and HF hospitalizations.

### 11.4 Remote Patient Monitoring and Telemonitoring
Telemonitoring encompasses both non-invasive strategies (daily automated transmission of body weight, blood pressure, heart rate, and symptom checklists) and invasive haemodynamic monitoring:
- **Invasive Pulmonary Artery Pressure Monitoring (e.g., CardioMEMS):** Implantable wireless PA pressure sensors detect subclinical haemodynamic congestion days to weeks before overt clinical symptoms develop. Titration of diuretics and GDMT based on remote PA pressure data significantly reduces recurrent hospitalizations in high-risk ambulatory patients with persistent symptoms.
- **Non-Invasive Telemonitoring:** Structured phone support or digital health platforms with specialized nurse oversight should be considered to detect decompensation early and facilitate medication titration.

## 12. Specific Conditions</summary><div class="details-content">

### 12.1 Heart Failure and Pregnancy
Pregnancy imposes profound haemodynamic demands, including a 40%–50% increase in blood volume, elevated cardiac output, and physiological tachycardia. Women with pre-existing heart failure (Stage B or C) face elevated risks of maternal cardiac decompensation and adverse fetal outcomes. Pre-conception counseling, risk stratification using the modified WHO (mWHO) classification, and dedicated multidisciplinary Pregnancy Heart Team management are mandatory.

#### Pharmacotherapy During Pregnancy and Lactation
- **Strictly Contraindicated (Teratogenic / Fetotoxic):** ACE inhibitors, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs, SGLT2 inhibitors, and ivabradine must be discontinued prior to conception or immediately upon pregnancy confirmation (**Class III, Level C**).
- **Safe and Recommended Agents:** Beta-1 selective blockers (metoprolol succinate; atenolol should be avoided due to fetal growth restriction), hydralazine, oral nitrates, and loop diuretics (at lowest effective doses to prevent placental hypoperfusion). Digoxin may be utilized for rate control.

|---|---|---|
| Pre-conception care and counselling is recommended for patients with HF to facilitate decision-making surrounding HF treatments, pregnancy, contraception, pre-implantation genetic screening, and assisted reproductive therapies. | **I** | **C** |
| In pregnant women with HFrEF, it is recommended that non-selective beta-blockers are switched to beta1-selective blockers (metoprolol, bisoprolol) with close mother and foetus monitoring to reduce the risk of adverse foetal events and a lower birth weight. | **I** | **C** |
| ACE-Is, ARBs, ARNIs, MRAs, ivabradine, and SGLT2-Is are not recommended during pregnancy due to the risk of foetotoxicity or teratogenicity. | **III** | **C** |

### 12.2 Cardiomyopathies and Myocarditis
- **Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM):** Diagnosis relies on unexplained LV wall thickness $\ge 15\text{ mm}$ ($\ge 13\text{ mm}$ with positive family history). In symptomatic obstructive HCM, beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers are first-line. The cardiac myosin ATPase inhibitor **mavacamten** is recommended in patients with persistent symptoms and LV outflow tract obstruction despite first-line therapy. Surgical septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation is indicated in drug-refractory severe obstruction.
- **Dilated and Arrhythmogenic Cardiomyopathies:** Comprehensive multi-gene panel testing and cascade family evaluation are indicated to identify pathogenic variants (e.g., *LMNA*, *FLNC*, *TTN*, *RBM20*, *DSP*) carrying high risks of sudden cardiac death or rapid progression.
- **Acute Myocarditis:** Diagnosis combines clinical presentation, high-sensitivity troponin, ECG, and contrast-enhanced CMR (applying updated Lake Louise Criteria). Endomyocardial biopsy is indicated in fulminant, refractory, or complicated presentations to identify giant-cell, necrotizing eosinophilic, or sarcoid myocarditis requiring immediate aggressive immunosuppressive therapy.

### 12.3 Cardiac Amyloidosis
Cardiac amyloidosis is an increasingly recognized cause of heart failure, characterized by the extracellular deposition of insoluble amyloid fibrils derived from misfolded transthyretin (**ATTR-CA**, wild-type or hereditary) or immunoglobulin light chains (**AL-CA**). Prompt identification is critical, as disease-modifying therapies halt or reverse progression when initiated early.

## 13. Key Messages</summary><div class="details-content">

The 2026 ESC Guidelines for the management of heart failure summarize the fundamental clinical imperatives into **10 Core Key Messages**:

1. **Unified Stages of Heart Failure:** Heart failure is a continuous spectrum spanning Stage A (at risk), Stage B (pre-heart failure with structural/functional changes or elevated biomarkers), Stage C (symptomatic clinical HF), and Stage D (advanced, refractory HF). Proactive intervention at Stages A and B prevents disease onset.
2. **Simplified LVEF Phenotypes:** HFrEF is redefined as LVEF $\le 50\%$ (incorporating former HFmrEF), recognizing that neurohormonal blockade confers equivalent clinical benefits across this entire range. HFpEF is defined as symptomatic HF with LVEF $> 50\%$ and objective evidence of diastolic dysfunction.
3. **Rapid Initiation of FMT Quad-Therapy:** Foundational Medical Therapy for HFrEF consists of four pillars: ARNI/ACE-I, beta-blocker, MRA, and SGLT2 inhibitor. All four drug classes should be initiated promptly—ideally during index hospitalization—and titrated rapidly to target doses within 4–6 weeks.
4. **Evidence-Based Therapy in HFpEF:** SGLT2 inhibitors and MRAs form the backbone of disease-modifying pharmacotherapy in HFpEF to reduce hospitalizations. The GLP-1 RA semaglutide (or tirzepatide) is indicated in obesity-related HFpEF to reduce symptoms and body weight.
5. **Structured In-Hospital Decongestion:** In decompensated heart failure, prompt intravenous loop diuretic administration guided by urine spot sodium ($> 50\text{--}70\text{ mmol/L}$ at 2 hours) and hourly urine output ensures complete decongestion. Inpatient optimization of FMT and early post-discharge visits (within 7–14 days) prevent readmission.
6. **Timely Advanced HF Triage:** Patients with recurring hospitalizations, worsening renal function, persistent intolerance to neurohormonal blockers, or escalating diuretic requirements must be identified early using the **I NEED HELP** mnemonic and referred promptly to an Advanced HF center for LVAD or heart transplantation evaluation.
7. **Concomitant Atrial Fibrillation Management:** Thromboembolic risk must be evaluated with the revised **CHA2DS2-VA score**, prescribing DOACs in preference to VKAs. Catheter ablation is recommended as a first-line rhythm strategy in selected HFrEF patients with tachycardiomyopathy or high arrhythmia burden.
8. **Systematic Comorbidity Screening:** Routine screening and targeted management of non-cardiovascular comorbidities—specifically iron deficiency (treated with IV ferric carboxymaltose or ferric derisomaltose), CKD, diabetes, and sleep-disordered breathing—are mandatory components of holistic HF care.
9. **Non-Invasive Diagnosis of Cardiac Amyloidosis:** Cardiac amyloidosis should be actively sought in patients with unexplained LV hypertrophy, HFpEF, or carpal tunnel syndrome, utilizing bone scintigraphy and light-chain screening without requiring invasive myocardial biopsy. Early initiation of TTR stabilizers (tafamidis, acoramidis) or silencers (vutrisiran) halts disease progression.
10. **Multidisciplinary Team Care and Rehabilitation:** Coordinated management by multidisciplinary heart failure teams, enrollment in exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation, individualized remote telemonitoring, and proactive advance care planning substantially improve clinical outcomes, functional status, and quality of life.

## 14. Gaps in Evidence</summary><div class="details-content">

Despite monumental therapeutic progress, critical gaps in clinical evidence remain to be addressed by future clinical trials and registries:

### 14.1 Definitions, Epidemiology, and Prevention
- Precise biological consensus regarding normal physiological reference ranges of LVEF across age, sex, and ethnic cohorts.
- Prospective natural history, epidemiological trajectory, and long-term prognosis of patients with heart failure with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF), including the safety of gradual medical de-escalation.
- Comparative long-term efficacy and safety of novel multi-incretin agonists, non-incretin weight-loss molecules, and bariatric/metabolic surgery in reducing incident HF.
- Definitive clinical trials assessing whether SGLT2 inhibitors and non-steroidal MRAs prevent incident HF in non-diabetic individuals with isolated hypertension, obesity, or non-albuminuric CKD.

### 14.2 Diagnostic Phenotyping and Artificial Intelligence
- Deeper biological, proteomic, and metabolomic subphenotyping of HFpEF to identify targeted therapeutic responder subcohorts.
- Clinical validation of artificial intelligence algorithms (deep-learning ECG, automated echocardiographic analysis, generative clinical modeling) in early screening, risk stratification, and trial enrollment.
- Prospectively validated, non-invasive diagnostic criteria replacing invasive exercise right heart catheterization in ambiguous early-stage HFpEF.
- Clarification of BMI-adjusted natriuretic peptide decision cut-offs across morbidly obese and cachectic patient populations.

### 14.3 Pharmacological and Device Therapies
- Head-to-head randomized trials comparing steroidal (spironolactone, eplerenone) versus non-steroidal MRAs (finerenone) in HFrEF and HFpEF.
- Optimal timing, sequencing, and combination strategies of foundational medical therapy (FMT) in hospitalized patients with acute decompensated heart failure.
- Definitive efficacy of primary prevention ICDs in non-ischaemic cardiomyopathy in the modern era of universal FMT and CRT.
- Role of conduction system pacing (His bundle and left bundle branch area pacing) compared to biventricular CRT in patients with heart failure and wide QRS complexes.
- Safety and long-term durability of transcatheter tricuspid valve interventions (TEER, replacement) in heart failure with severe functional tricuspid regurgitation.
