## 7. Decompensated Heart Failure

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>7.1. Definition, Epidemiology, and Clinical Presentations</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Decompensated heart failure (DHF) represents a life-threatening clinical syndrome defined by the rapid onset or worsening of symptoms and signs of heart failure that require urgent medical attention and hospitalization. DHF may present as acute decompensation of pre-existing chronic heart failure (worsening heart failure, accounting for ~75%–80% of presentations) or as *de novo* heart failure (accounting for ~20%–25% of presentations, frequently triggered by acute myocardial infarction, acute myocarditis, or acute valvular disruption).

#### Clinical Categories of Decompensated Heart Failure

Patients with DHF present along a continuum of fluid overload and tissue perfusion. Four major clinical presentations are recognized:
1. **Acute Decompensated Left-Sided Heart Failure:** Characterized predominantly by pulmonary congestion, dyspnoea, orthopnoea, and elevated left ventricular filling pressures, with or without systemic congestion.
2. **Acute Decompensated Right-Sided Heart Failure:** Characterized predominantly by systemic venous congestion, elevated jugular venous pressure, hepatomegaly, ascites, and peripheral pitting oedema, frequently complicated by cardio-hepatic and cardiorenal syndromes.
3. **Acute Pulmonary Oedema:** Severe respiratory distress, tachypnoea, orthopnoea, bilateral diffuse pulmonary rales, and hypoxaemia resulting from rapid hydrostatic fluid shift into lung alveoli.
4. **Cardiogenic Shock:** Severe tissue and end-organ hypoperfusion (hypotension with SBP <90 mmHg or MAP <65 mmHg, cold clammy extremities, oliguria <0.5 mL/kg/h, elevated serum lactate >2.0 mmol/L, altered mental status) resulting from primary cardiac dysfunction.

![Diagnosing decompensated heart failure](images/fig11_diagnosing_decompensated_hf.jpg)

#### Diagnostic Evaluation in the Emergency Setting

All patients presenting with suspected DHF require immediate diagnostic evaluation:
- **12-lead ECG:** To assess rhythm (atrial fibrillation, sustained ventricular tachycardia), ischaemic changes (ST-elevation, pathological Q waves), conduction delay (QRS duration, bundle branch block), or signs of chamber hypertrophy.
- **Continuous Pulse Oximetry and Blood Pressure:** Immediate assessment of respiratory compromise and haemodynamic stability.
- **Laboratory Testing:** Plasma natriuretic peptides (BNP or NT-proBNP) to exclude non-cardiac causes of dyspnoea, cardiac troponin (high-sensitivity), serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, electrolytes (sodium, potassium), liver function tests, complete blood count, and arterial/venous blood gases in distressed or shocked patients.
- **Transthoracic Echocardiography:** Mandatory bedside echocardiography in haemodynamically unstable patients or *de novo* presentation to evaluate LV/RV systolic function, chamber dimensions, regional wall motion abnormalities, valvular structure/regurgitation/stenosis, pericardial effusion/tamponade, and estimated pulmonary artery pressures.
- **Chest Radiography and Lung Ultrasound:** To assess pulmonary venous congestion, alveolar interstitial oedema (B-lines on lung ultrasound), pleural effusions, and exclude non-cardiovascular pulmonary pathology (pneumothorax, pneumonia).

<summary>7.2. In-Hospital Phases and Management Pathways</summary>
<div class="details-content">

In-hospital care of DHF is structured into three continuous phases:
- **Phase 1: Urgent / Initial Management (First 0–6 Hours):** Immediate triage, stabilization of airway, oxygenation, haemodynamic support, relief of congestion, and identification of life-threatening precipitants (*CHAMPIT*: Coronary syndrome, Hypertensive emergency, Arrhythmia, Acute Mechanical cause, Pulmonary embolism, Infection, Tamponade).
- **Phase 2: In-Hospital Stabilization (Days 1–5):** Continued decongestion, daily monitoring of volume status, monitoring renal function and electrolytes, initiation and uptitration of foundational medical therapy (FMT).
- **Phase 3: Pre-Discharge and Early Post-Discharge (Days to Weeks):** Formal assessment of complete decongestion, safety-guided uptitration of GDMT to target doses, patient education, planning early outpatient follow-up within 1–2 weeks, and enrollment in multidisciplinary disease management programmes.

<summary>7.3. Pharmacological and Interventional Treatments in Decompensated Heart Failure</summary>
<div class="details-content">

#### Oxygen and Ventilatory Support
Oxygen therapy is indicated only when hypoxaemia is documented ($SpO_2 <90\%$ or $PaO_2 <60	ext{ mmHg}$). Routine oxygen therapy in non-hypoxaemic patients causes hyperoxia-induced vasoconstriction and reduction in coronary blood flow. Non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (CPAP or BiPAP) should be initiated early in patients with acute pulmonary oedema and respiratory distress (respiratory rate >25/min, $SpO_2 <90\%$) to reduce work of breathing, improve lung compliance, and avoid endotracheal intubation.

#### Loop Diuretics and Decongestion Protocols
Intravenous loop diuretics (furosemide, bumetanide, or torasemide) remain the cornerstone of decongestion therapy:
- For loop diuretic-naïve patients: Initial starting dose is 20–40 mg intravenous furosemide.
- For patients on chronic oral loop diuretics: Initial intravenous dose should be at least 1–2 times the daily oral home dose administered as intravenous boluses or continuous infusion.
- Early assessment of diuretic response at 2 hours (spot urinary sodium content, target $\ge 70	ext{ mEq/L}$) and at 6 hours (hourly urine output, target $\ge 100	ext{ mL/h}$) is recommended to identify diuretic resistance early.
- If diuretic response is inadequate, the intravenous loop diuretic dose should be doubled, and combination nephron blockade (addition of intravenous acetazolamide 500 mg daily or oral hydrochlorothiazide 25–50 mg daily) should be considered.

![Tools for assessment of decongestion](images/fig14_tools_assessment_decongestion.jpg)

![Management of decongestion: diuretic protocols](images/fig15_management_decongestion.jpg)

#### Vasodilators, Inotropes, and Vasopressors
- **Vasodilators (Nitroglycerin, Isosorbide Dinitrate, Nitroprusside):** May be considered in patients with SBP >110 mmHg to reduce cardiac filling pressures, systemic vascular resistance, and pulmonary congestion.
- **Inotropes (Dobutamine, Levosimendan, Milrinone):** Reserved strictly for patients with marked peripheral hypoperfusion, symptomatic hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg), or low cardiac output despite adequate filling pressures. Routine use in non-hypoperfused patients increases myocardial oxygen demand, tachyarrhythmias, and mortality.
- **Vasopressors (Norepinephrine):** Recommended as the vasopressor of first choice in cardiogenic shock to maintain coronary and cerebral perfusion pressure while minimizing tachyarrhythmias compared to dopamine or epinephrine.

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>7.4. Temporary Mechanical Circulatory Support in Cardiogenic Shock</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Temporary mechanical circulatory support (tMCS) devices are indicated to maintain systemic perfusion, reduce cardiac filling pressures, and unload the failing ventricle in patients with refractory cardiogenic shock. Device options include:
- **Microaxial Flow Pumps (Impella):** Provide transvalvular LV unloading, reduce LV end-diastolic pressure, and increase systemic forward flow up to 3.5–5.5 L/min. The DanGer Shock trial demonstrated that microaxial flow pumps significantly reduce 180-day all-cause mortality in selected patients with STEMI-related cardiogenic shock without anoxic brain injury.
- **Venoarterial Extracorporeal Life Support (VA-ECLS / VA-ECMO):** Provides biventricular circulatory and pulmonary support; however, retrograde aortic inflow increases LV afterload, requiring proactive LV venting (e.g., ECPELLA with concomitant Impella or atrial septostomy).
- **Intra-Aortic Balloon Pump (IABP):** Provides counterpulsation with modest afterload reduction and diastolic coronary augmentation, but does not actively pump blood. Multiple randomized trials (IABP-SHOCK II) demonstrated no mortality benefit for routine IABP in post-MI cardiogenic shock (Class III: Not Recommended).

## 8. Advanced Heart Failure

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>8.1. Definition, Triage, and Evaluation of Advanced Heart Failure</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Advanced heart failure (Stage D) is characterized by persistent, severe symptoms (NYHA class III–IV) and recurrent decompensation despite maximum tolerated guideline-directed medical therapy (FMT), cardiac resynchronization therapy, and surgical/interventional procedures. Timely identification and referral to a specialized Advanced Heart Failure Center are vital before the onset of irreversible end-organ damage (severe renal failure, cirrhosis, fixed pulmonary hypertension).

![Triage and timely referral for advanced heart failure](images/fig16_triage_timely_referral_advanced_hf.jpg)

![INTERMACS clinical profiles](images/fig17_intermacs_classification.jpg)

#### Table 14: Criteria for Definition of Advanced Heart Failure (HFA-ESC Criteria)

All the following criteria must be present despite optimal medical and device therapy:
1. **Severe and Persistent Symptoms:** NYHA class III (advanced) or class IV dyspnoea and/or fatigue at rest or on minimal exertion.
2. **Severe Cardiac Dysfunction:** Severe impairment documented by at least one of the following:
   - Severely reduced LVEF ($\le 30\%$).
   - Isolated right ventricular systolic failure (e.g. TAPSE <14 mm, FAC <35%).
   - Inoperable severe valvular disease or severe congenital heart disease.
   - Persistent severe diastolic dysfunction or structural abnormalities with elevated filling pressures.
3. **Severe Episodes of Congestion and/or Hypoperfusion:** At least one of the following within the past 12 months:
   - $\ge 2$ hospitalizations or emergency department visits for decompensated heart failure requiring intravenous therapy.
   - Requirement for continuous or repeated outpatient inotropic support.
   - Need for temporary mechanical circulatory support.
   - Malignant ventricular arrhythmias triggering ICD shocks.
4. **Severe Exercise Impairment:** Inability to perform cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) or objective demonstration of severely reduced peak oxygen consumption:
   - Peak $VO_2 <12	ext{ mL/kg/min}$ (or $<14	ext{ mL/kg/min}$ in patients on beta-blockers), or $<50\%$ predicted.
   - 6-minute walk distance <300 m.

## 9. Cardiovascular Comorbidities

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>9.1. Atrial Fibrillation and Arrhythmias</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained arrhythmia in patients with heart failure, affecting up to 30%–50% of individuals depending on disease severity. AF and HF share common risk factors and promote each other through adverse structural and electrical remodeling ("AF begets HF, and HF begets AF").

#### Anticoagulation in Atrial Fibrillation
Patients with AF and heart failure are at elevated risk of stroke and systemic thromboembolism. In the 2026 ESC Guidelines, thromboembolic risk assessment has been streamlined using the updated **$CHA_2DS_2	ext{-}VA$ score**, which removes the female sex category as an independent risk modifier.

#### Table 18: Updated Definitions for the $CHA_2DS_2	ext{-}VA$ Score

| Acronym | Clinical Risk Factor | Points |
| :--- | :--- | :---: |
| **C** | Chronic Heart Failure (Stage B, C, or D; symptomatic or asymptomatic LV dysfunction) | 1 |
| **H** | Hypertension (or current antihypertensive therapy) | 1 |
| **$A_2$** | Age $\ge 75$ years | 2 |
| **D** | Diabetes mellitus (type 1 or type 2, or fasting glucose >7.0 mmol/L) | 1 |
| **$S_2$** | Prior Stroke, transient ischaemic attack (TIA), or systemic thromboembolism | 2 |
| **V** | Vascular disease (prior MI, peripheral artery disease, or complex aortic plaque) | 1 |
| **A** | Age 65–74 years | 1 |

*Risk thresholds:*
- $CHA_2DS_2	ext{-}VA$ score $\ge 2$: Oral anticoagulation is **recommended (Class I)**.
- $CHA_2DS_2	ext{-}VA$ score = 1: Oral anticoagulation **should be considered (Class IIa)**.
- Direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs: apixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban) are recommended in preference to Vitamin K Antagonists (VKAs) in all non-valvular AF patients.

#### Rate vs Rhythm Control Strategies
- **Rate Control:** Recommended first-line approach in stable patients. Beta-blockers are the first choice in HFrEF. Digoxin or digitoxin is indicated when ventricular rates remain elevated despite beta-blockers or when beta-blockers are contraindicated. Lenient resting heart rate target (<110 bpm) is appropriate initially, with tighter control (<80 bpm) if symptoms persist.
- **Rhythm Control:** Urgent electrical cardioversion is mandatory in haemodynamically unstable patients or acute decompensation with rapid ventricular rate. Amiodarone is the only safe antiarrhythmic drug for pharmacological cardioversion and sinus rhythm maintenance in HFrEF. Class IC antiarrhythmics (flecainide, propafenone) and dronedarone increase mortality and are strictly contraindicated in HFrEF.
- **Catheter Ablation:** Catheter ablation for AF should be considered in selected HFrEF patients (high AF burden, paroxysmal or short-duration persistent AF <1 year, and clear tachycardiomyopathy) to restore LV function, improve QoL, and reduce hospitalizations.
- **AV Node Ablation + CRT ("Abate and Pace"):** Should be considered in severely symptomatic permanent AF with refractory rapid ventricular rates despite maximal medical therapy.

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>9.2. Chronic Coronary Syndromes and Revascularization</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD) is the underlying aetiology in over 50%–60% of patients with HFrEF. The management of CAD in patients with heart failure encompasses optimal medical antianginal therapy integrated with foundational HF medications, alongside selective myocardial revascularization evaluated by a multidisciplinary Heart Team.

![Management of coronary artery disease in heart failure](images/fig19_management_cad_in_hf.jpg)

#### Medical Therapy for Angina in Heart Failure
- **First-Line:** Guideline-directed beta-blockers (bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate) provide dual benefit for prognosis and angina relief.
- **Second-Line:** Ivabradine is indicated for persistent angina in sinus rhythm with resting heart rate $\ge 70	ext{ bpm}$ despite beta-blockers. Oral nitrates, amlodipine, and trimetazidine may be added for symptom control.
- **Contraindicated Drugs:** Non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem) have negative inotropic effects and worsen heart failure outcomes in HFrEF.

#### Myocardial Revascularization: CABG vs PCI
Long-term data from the STICH trial (10-year follow-up) demonstrated that in patients with severe LV systolic dysfunction (LVEF $\le 35\%$) and multivessel CAD suitable for surgery, Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG) added to medical therapy produces a significant reduction in all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death compared to medical therapy alone. In the REVIVED-BCIS2 trial, routine PCI did not reduce all-cause mortality or HF hospitalizations in patients with ischaemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF $\le 35\%$) and extensive CAD without refractory angina. Therefore, CABG is the preferred revascularization modality when outcome improvement is pursued in multivessel CAD suitable for surgery.

<details class="med-details" open>
<summary>9.3. Valvular Heart Disease in Heart Failure</summary>
<div class="details-content">

Valvular heart disease can be either the primary cause of heart failure or a secondary consequence of LV/RV remodeling and dilatation.

#### Severe Aortic Stenosis
Severe aortic stenosis imposes chronic pressure overload, inducing LV hypertrophy, fibrosis, and eventual systolic and diastolic failure. Aortic valve intervention is strongly recommended in all patients with severe symptomatic aortic stenosis and heart failure:
- **Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation (TAVI):** Preferred in older patients ($\ge 75$ years), patients with high surgical risk, or transfemoral anatomical suitability.
- **Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement (SAVR):** Preferred in younger patients (<75 years), low surgical risk, or those requiring concomitant CABG or aortic root surgery.
- **Low-Flow, Low-Gradient Aortic Stenosis:** Low-dose dobutamine stress echocardiography is indicated in patients with reduced LVEF and low transvalvular gradient to distinguish true severe aortic stenosis (valve area remains $\le 1.0	ext{ cm}^2$ with increasing flow) from pseudo-severe stenosis, and to assess contractile flow reserve.

#### Secondary (Functional) Mitral Regurgitation
Secondary mitral regurgitation (SMR) develops due to LV chamber dilatation, papillary muscle displacement, and tethering of structurally normal mitral leaflets. Medical therapy with FMT and CRT must be optimized first:
- **Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair (TEER / MitraClip):** In the COAPT trial, mitral TEER added to maximal GDMT significantly reduced HF hospitalizations and all-cause mortality. Strict selection criteria must be fulfilled to achieve outcome benefit (disproportionate MR).
