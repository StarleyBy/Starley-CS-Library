# 2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of chronic coronary syndromes

##### Developed by the task force for the management of chronic coronary syndromes of the European Society of Cardiology (ESC). Endorsed by the European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS).

## Executive Summary</summary><div class="details-content">

### Definition and Pathophysiology of CCS
* **Chronic Coronary Syndromes (CCS):** A range of clinical presentations arising from structural and/or functional alterations of the coronary arteries and microcirculation, leading to transient myocardial hypoperfusion (ischaemia) provoked by stress or occurring spontaneously. The term replaces “stable CAD” and acknowledges that the condition can destabilise into ACS at any time.
* **Key pathophysiological mechanisms:** Not only fixed obstructive epicardial stenoses, but also diffuse atherosclerosis without focal narrowing, myocardial bridging, congenital anomalies, epicardial vasospasm, and **coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD)**. CMD is prevalent across the entire CCS spectrum, including **ANOCA/INOCA** (angina/ischaemia with non‑obstructive coronary arteries).
* **Risk factors for microvascular disease:** Smoking, age, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, inflammatory rheumatic diseases, and psychosocial stress. CMD is more common in women after menopause.

### Clinical Presentation and Initial Assessment (Step 1)
* **Symptoms:** Only 10–25% of patients present with classic exertional angina; 57–78% have atypical symptoms, 10–15% have exertional dyspnoea. Anginal equivalents include chest pain triggered by emotional stress, dyspnoea, dizziness, arm/jaw/neck/back pain, or fatigue.
* **Canadian Cardiovascular Society (CCS) grading:** Class I (angina only with strenuous exertion) to Class IV (angina at rest). Symptom severity correlates poorly with obstructive CAD severity.
* **Physical examination:** BP, BMI, search for anaemia, valvular disease, LV hypertrophy, arrhythmias, peripheral vascular disease, thyroid/renal disease, diabetes.
* **Resting ECG:** Recommended in all patients with chest pain. Dynamic ST‑segment changes during pain suggest vasospastic angina. ST‑segment depression during supraventricular tachyarrhythmias should not be taken as evidence of obstructive CAD.
* **Basic laboratory tests:** Lipid profile (LDL‑C calculation), full blood count, creatinine (eGFR), HbA1c/fasting glucose. Thyroid function at least once. hs‑CRP and/or fibrinogen may be considered. Elevated lipoprotein(a) – single measurement sufficient.
* **Rule out ACS:** If ACS suspected, high‑sensitivity troponin and referral to ED.

### Pre‑test Likelihood and Step 2 Evaluation
* **RF‑CL model (Risk Factor‑weighted Clinical Likelihood):** Recommended to estimate pre‑test likelihood of obstructive epicardial CAD using sex, age, angina symptoms (typical/atypical/non‑anginal), number of risk factors (family history, smoking, dyslipidaemia, hypertension, diabetes). Very low ≤5%, low >5–15%, moderate >15–50%, high >50–85%, very high >85%.
* **Role of CACS (coronary artery calcium score):** In low‑likelihood patients (5–15%), CACS can reclassify many to very low (≤5%) likelihood, allowing deferral of further testing. CACS = 0 has NPV >95% for obstructive CAD. The CACS‑CL model (CACS + RF‑CL) is superior for predicting MI and death.
* **Resting echocardiography/CMR:** Measure LVEF, volumes, diastolic function, regional wall motion; detect non‑coronary cardiac disease. LVEF is the strongest predictor of long‑term survival.
* **Exercise ECG:** Low sensitivity (58%) and specificity (62%); not recommended as first‑line if CCTA or functional imaging available. May be used for risk stratification (Duke Treadmill Score < –10 indicates high risk) and to assess exercise tolerance, arrhythmias, BP response.
* **Chest X‑ray:** For suspected heart failure, pulmonary disease, or other thoracic causes.
* **Ambulatory ECG:** For suspected vasospastic angina and arrhythmias.

### Non‑invasive Diagnostic Tests (Step 3)
* **CCTA (first‑line for low/moderate likelihood 5–50%):** Recommended to diagnose obstructive CAD and estimate MACE risk. Excellent negative predictive value. FFR‑CT can complement CCTA. Contraindications: severe renal failure (eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73 m²), decompensated HF, extensive calcification, fast irregular heart rate, inability to breath‑hold.
* **Stress echocardiography (first‑line for moderate/high likelihood 15–85%):** Use contrast (microbubbles) if ≥2 segments not visualised (Class I B). Myocardial perfusion imaging with contrast improves accuracy and risk stratification. Doppler LAD flow reserve may be considered (IIb B).
* **SPECT/PET myocardial perfusion imaging (moderate/high likelihood 15–85%):** PET preferred for absolute MBF quantification and lower radiation. Measuring CACS from unenhanced CT (attenuation correction) is recommended.
* **Stress CMR (moderate/high likelihood 15–85%):** Recommended to diagnose and quantify ischaemia/scar and estimate MACE risk.
* **Non‑invasive testing for CMD:** PET‑derived MFR <2.0 (or <2.5 for non‑obstructive CAD) indicates microvascular dysfunction. CMR quantitative perfusion and myocardial contrast echocardiography are emerging.

### Invasive Tests and Functional Assessment
* **Indications for ICA:** Very high clinical likelihood (>85%), severe symptoms refractory to GDMT, angina at low level of exercise, high event risk (e.g. left main ≥50% stenosis, three‑vessel ≥70%, proximal LAD ≥70% with FFR‑CT ≤0.8, or ischaemia ≥10% of LV).
* **Radial access:** Preferred (Class I A).
* **Functional assessment of intermediate stenoses (40–90% diameter):** FFR (significant ≤0.80) or iFR (≤0.89) – Class I A. QFR (≤0.80) – Class I B. CFR/HSR/CFC – complementary (IIa B). Systematic wire‑based assessment of all vessels – not recommended (III A).
* **Microvascular assessment:** IMR ≥25 indicates microvascular dysfunction. Continuous thermodilution more reproducible than bolus.
* **Vasospasm testing:** Intracoronary acetylcholine (Ach) bolus up to 200 μg – positive if symptoms, ischaemic ECG changes, and angiographic ≥90% lumen reduction (macrospasm) or <90% with ischaemia (microspasm). Safe when performed by experienced operators.

### Diagnostic Algorithm and Risk Stratification
* **Sequential testing:** In moderate likelihood, CCTA alone often insufficient to rule in ischaemia; add functional imaging (e.g. dobutamine stress echo) to raise post‑test probability to >80%.
* **High risk of adverse events (cardiac mortality >3%/year):** Defined by – Duke Treadmill Score < –10; stress‑induced ischaemia ≥10% of LV (SPECT/PET); ≥3/16 segments with stress‑induced hypokinesia (echo); ≥2/16 stress perfusion defects or ≥3 dobutamine‑induced dysfunctional segments (CMR); CCTA – left main ≥50%, three‑vessel ≥70%, two‑vessel including proximal LAD ≥70%, or one‑vessel proximal LAD ≥70% with FFR‑CT ≤0.8.
* **Initial therapy (Step 4):** Sublingual nitrates for acute symptoms. Start GDMT (lifestyle, risk factor modification, antianginal drugs, disease‑modifying agents). Revascularisation considered if refractory symptoms or high‑risk CAD. If symptoms persist after excluding obstructive CAD, consider microvascular disease and vasospasm.

### Guideline‑Directed Therapy – Lifestyle and Exercise
* **Smoking cessation:** Pharmacological + behavioural interventions. Avoid e‑cigarettes.
* **Weight management:** Target BMI 18.5–25 kg/m². In overweight/obese CCS without diabetes, semaglutide 2.4 mg/week reduces CV death, MI, stroke (SELECT trial, HR 0.80).
* **Diet:** Mediterranean diet; limit alcohol to <100 g/week.
* **Mental health:** Treat depression/anxiety with psychotherapy and/or pharmacotherapy.
* **Physical activity:** At least 150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 min/week vigorous activity. Reduce sedentary time.
* **Exercise therapy:** Multidisciplinary cardiac rehabilitation reduces hospitalisations and CV mortality. Home‑based and mHealth interventions (IIa B). Aerobic training + resistance exercise.

### Antianginal Drugs
* **Tailored selection** based on haemodynamics, comorbidities, pathophysiology (obstructive, vasospastic, microvascular). Short‑acting nitrates for immediate relief.
* **First‑line:** Beta‑blocker and/or CCB for most patients. Beta‑blocker aim: resting HR 55–60 bpm. After uncomplicated MI with LVEF >40%, long‑term beta‑blocker benefit is uncertain (ongoing trials).
* **Combination therapy:** Beta‑blocker + dihydropyridine CCB. Add long‑acting nitrates or ranolazine if symptoms uncontrolled. Ivabradine as add‑on for LVEF <40% (IIa B); not recommended if LVEF >40% and no clinical HF (III B). Nicorandil or trimetazidine may be added (IIb B).
* **Contraindications:** Nitrates with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy or PDE‑5 inhibitors; ivabradine with verapamil/diltiazem.

### Antithrombotic Therapy for Event Prevention
* **Long‑term SAPT in CCS without OAC:** Aspirin 75–100 mg daily lifelong (prior MI or remote PCI – I A; after CABG – I A; significant obstructive CAD without prior MI – I B). **Clopidogrel 75 mg daily** is a safe and effective alternative to aspirin (I A).
* **DAPT post‑PCI (default 6 months):** Aspirin + clopidogrel (I A). In high bleeding risk (PRECISE‑DAPT ≥25 or ARC‑HBR), discontinue DAPT after 1–3 months (I A).
* **Extended intensified therapy (high ischaemic risk, not high bleeding risk):** Aspirin + ticagrelor 60 mg b.i.d. (PEGASUS‑TIMI 54) or aspirin + rivaroxaban 2.5 mg b.i.d. (COMPASS) should be considered (IIa A/IIa B). Also DAPT prolongation with clopidogrel/prasugrel (DAPT Study).
* **CCS with AF undergoing PCI:** DOAC (preferred over VKA) + clopidogrel (double therapy) for up to 12 months, after initial ≤1 week of aspirin (I A). Triple therapy (aspirin + clopidogrel + OAC) generally not recommended; ticagrelor/prasugrel not recommended as part of triple therapy (III C).
* **Proton pump inhibitors:** Recommended for duration of combined antithrombotic therapy in patients at increased GI bleeding risk (I A); consider with single antithrombotic drug (IIa A).

### Lipid‑Lowering Therapy
* **LDL‑C goal:** <1.4 mmol/L (55 mg/dL) and ≥50% reduction from baseline (I A). For recurrent events within 2 years on maximally tolerated statin, consider <1.0 mmol/L (40 mg/dL).
* **High‑intensity statin** (atorvastatin ≥40 mg or rosuvastatin ≥20 mg) first‑line (I A). Add ezetimibe if goal not reached (I B). Add PCSK9 inhibitor (alirocumab/evolocumab) if still not reached (I A).
* **Statin‑intolerant:** Bempedoic acid + ezetimibe (I B for bempedoic acid after ezetimibe failure; IIa C for add‑on bempedoic acid when statin+ezetimibe insufficient).

### RAAS Blockade, SGLT2 Inhibitors, GLP‑1 RA, Colchicine
* **ACE‑I/ARB:** Recommended in CCS with hypertension, diabetes, or HF (I A). Consider in very high CV risk (IIa A).
* **SGLT2 inhibitors with proven CV benefit:** Recommended in CCS with type 2 diabetes (I A). In HF (HFrEF/HFpEF), dapagliflozin/empagliflozin are indicated irrespective of diabetes.
* **GLP‑1 receptor agonist semaglutide:** Should be considered in overweight/obese (BMI ≥27 kg/m²) CCS patients without diabetes to reduce CV mortality, MI, stroke (SELECT, HR 0.80; IIa B).
* **Low‑dose colchicine (0.5 mg daily):** Should be considered in CCS with atherosclerotic CAD to reduce MI, stroke, and revascularisation (IIa A).

### Revascularisation in CCS
* **Indications for revascularisation to improve outcomes:** Functionally significant left main stenosis (I A), three‑vessel disease (I A), single‑/two‑vessel involving proximal LAD (I B) – all in LVEF >35%. For LVEF ≤35%, individualised decision by Heart Team (I C); CABG recommended over medical therapy alone (I B).
* **To improve symptoms:** Revascularisation of functionally significant obstructive CAD in patients with persistent angina despite GDMT (I A).
* **Choice of revascularisation modality:**
  * **Left main low complexity (SYNTAX ≤22):** CABG recommended as overall preferred (I A). PCI recommended as alternative (I A). For intermediate complexity (23–32), PCI should be considered (IIa A).
  * **Multivessel disease with diabetes:** CABG recommended over medical therapy alone and over PCI (I A).
  * **Three‑vessel disease without diabetes, low‑to‑intermediate complexity:** PCI is recommended (I A).
  * **Single‑/double‑vessel proximal LAD:** CABG or PCI recommended (I A). For complex lesions less amenable to PCI, CABG is recommended (I B).
  * **Single‑/double‑vessel non‑proximal LAD:** PCI recommended to improve symptoms (I B); CABG may be considered if not amenable to PCI (IIb C).
* **Procedural guidance:** Intracoronary imaging (IVUS/OCT) for complex lesions (left main, true bifurcations, long lesions) – I A. FFR/iFR/QFR to guide lesion selection in multivessel disease – I A.
* **Heart Team:** For complex cases when CABG and PCI hold same level of recommendation (I C). Shared decision‑making essential.

### Special Groups
* **ANOCA/INOCA:** Prevalence of CMD ~40–50%, vasospasm ~40%. Invasive coronary functional testing (Ach + adenosine) recommended for persistent symptomatic patients with poor QoL (I B). Treatment based on endotype: CCBs for vasospastic angina (I A); ACE‑I for endothelial dysfunction (IIa B); beta‑blockers for reduced flow reserve (IIa B).
* **Heart failure:** In HFrEF (LVEF ≤35%) with suspected obstructive CAD, ICA with view to CABG (I B). SGLT2 inhibitors, ACE‑I/ARNI, beta‑blockers, MRA, and CRT/ICD as indicated. In HFpEF, SGLT2 inhibitors recommended; consider PET/CMR or invasive testing for CMD.
* **Older adults (≥75 years):** Care with drug side effects, interactions, overdosing, procedural complications. Diagnostic and revascularisation decisions based on symptoms, ischaemia extent, frailty, life expectancy, comorbidities, patient preferences.
* **Women:** Same guideline‑directed preventive therapy as men. Post‑menopausal hormone therapy not recommended (III A).
* **High bleeding risk:** Assess with PRECISE‑DAPT or ARC‑HBR (I B).
* **Chronic kidney disease:** Careful risk‑benefit before ICA/CCTA. ISCHEMIA‑CKD showed no benefit of invasive over conservative strategy in advanced CKD.
* **HIV:** Attention to drug interactions between antiretroviral therapy and statins (simvastatin/lovastatin contraindicated with protease inhibitors).
* **Screening in asymptomatic individuals:** Opportunistic screening with SCORE2/SCORE2‑OP recommended (I C). If CAC from previous chest CT, use to enhance risk stratification (IIa C). CACS may be considered around treatment decision thresholds (IIb C).

### Long‑term Follow‑up, Adherence, and Future Directions
* **Follow‑up:** Annual clinical evaluation. Stress imaging for new/worsening symptoms (I C). ICA for refractory symptoms or high‑risk features (I C).
* **Adherence:** mHealth interventions (text messages, apps, wearables) recommended to improve adherence (I A). Behavioural interventions (I B), simplifying regimens (fixed‑dose combinations) (I B). Multiprofessional and family involvement (I C).
* **Refractory angina:** Invasive functional testing for suspected ANOCA/INOCA (I B). Coronary sinus reducer may be considered in experienced centres for debilitating obstructive CAD refractory to optimal therapy (IIb B).

## 1. Preamble</summary><div class="details-content">

Guidelines evaluate and summarize available evidence with the aim of assisting health professionals in proposing the best diagnostic or therapeutic approach for an individual patient with a given condition. Guidelines are intended for use by health professionals, and the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) makes its guidelines freely available.

ESC Guidelines do not override the individual responsibility of health professionals to make appropriate and accurate decisions in consideration of each patient’s health condition and in consultation with that patient or the patient’s caregiver where appropriate and/or necessary. It is also the health professional’s responsibility to verify the rules and regulations applicable in each country to drugs and devices at the time of prescription and to respect the ethical rules of their profession.

ESC Guidelines represent the official position of the ESC on a given topic and are regularly updated when warranted by new evidence. ESC Policies and Procedures for formulating and issuing ESC Guidelines can be found on the ESC website (https://www.escardio.org/Guidelines/Clinical-Practice-Guidelines/Guidelines-development/Writing-ESC-Guidelines). These guidelines update and replace the previous 2019 and partly replace the myocardial revascularization guidelines from 2018.

The Members of this task force were selected by the ESC to include professionals involved in the medical care of patients with this pathology, as well as patient representatives and methodologists. The selection procedure included an open call for authors and aimed to include members from across the whole of the ESC region and from relevant ESC Subspecialty Communities. Consideration was given to diversity and inclusion, notably with respect to gender and country of origin. The task force performed a critical review and evaluation of the published literature on diagnostic and therapeutic approaches including assessment of the risk-benefit ratio. The strength of every recommendation and the level of evidence supporting them were weighed and scored according to predefined scales as outlined in Tables 1 and 2 below. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) were also evaluated as the basis for recommendations and/or discussion in these guidelines. The task force followed ESC voting procedures and all approved recommendations were subject to a vote and achieved at least 75% agreement among voting members. Members of the task force with declared interests on specific topics were asked to abstain from voting on related recommendations.

The experts of the writing and reviewing panels provided declaration of interest forms for all relationships that might be perceived as real or potential sources of conflicts of interest. Their declarations of interest were reviewed according to the ESC declaration of interest rules, which can be found on the ESC website (http://www.escardio.org/guidelines) and have been compiled in a report published in a supplementary document with the guidelines. Funding for the development of ESC Guidelines is derived entirely from the ESC with no involvement of the healthcare industry.

The ESC Clinical Practice Guidelines (CPG) Committee supervises and co-ordinates the preparation of new guidelines and is responsible for the approval process. In addition to review by the CPG Committee, ESC Guidelines undergo multiple rounds of double-blind peer review by external experts, including members from across the whole of the ESC region, all National Cardiac Societies of the ESC and from relevant ESC Subspecialty Communities. After appropriate revisions, the guidelines are signed off by all the experts in the task force. The finalized document is signed off by the CPG Committee for publication in the European Heart Journal.

ESC Guidelines are based on analyses of published evidence, chiefly on clinical trials and meta-analyses of trials, but potentially including other types of studies. Evidence tables summarizing key information from relevant studies are generated early in the guideline development process to facilitate the formulation of recommendations, to enhance comprehension of recommendations after publication, and reinforce transparency in the guideline development process. The tables are published in their own section of the ESC Guidelines and are specifically related to the recommendation tables.

Off-label use of medication may be presented in these guidelines if a sufficient level of evidence shows that it can be considered medically appropriate for a given condition. However, the final decisions concerning an individual patient must be made by the responsible health professional giving special consideration to:

· The specific situation of the patient. Unless otherwise provided for by national regulations, off-label use of medication should be limited to situations where it is in the patient’s interest with regard to the quality, safety, and efficacy of care, and only after the patient has been informed and has provided consent.
· Country-specific health regulations, indications by governmental drug regulatory agencies and the ethical rules to which health professionals are subject, where applicable.
